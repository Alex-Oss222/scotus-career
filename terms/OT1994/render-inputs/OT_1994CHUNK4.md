## Holdings

### The Director's general administrative interest does not confer independent review

**Holding and operative rule:** The Director's general interest in correct administration of the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act does not by itself make the Director adversely affected or aggrieved under 33 U.S.C. §921(c) by this employee's unappealed benefits award. The refusal to entertain that challenge is affirmed; no rule of categorical governmental incapacity is adopted, and the separate Special Fund disposition remains undisturbed.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer: eight direct rationale votes. Justice Ginsburg concurs in the judgment and agrees with the case-specific statutory result, but does not join this explanation. No fractured-opinion synthesis is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that administrative responsibility does not itself satisfy the condition Congress placed on judicial review. Section 921(c) requires a person adversely affected or aggrieved; §921(b)(3)'s administrative party-in-interest route and §939's claimant-assistance duties do not transfer Harcum's appellate entitlement to the Director. Germain requires respect for the particular statutory review route, and Gardner requires fidelity to enacted conditions rather than an administrator's preferred policy. The Director identifies no impairment of a specific entrusted function here. Harcum did not appeal, and disagreement with his award does not establish an independent adverse interest. Possible employer insolvency presents speculative Fund exposure that the requested benefits change would not reduce. Perini's treatment of respondent participation does not decide who may initiate review. Greenwich Collieries governs persuasion in covered adjudications, not this appellate entitlement. Uniform administration is important, but accepting that interest alone would erase the statutory condition. The Court reserves express governmental review authority, genuine financial or independently statutory injury, and the Director's respondent role; it decides neither compensation nor Fund liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249: applies respect for the requirements of the particular statutory route; its bankruptcy appellate holding remains unchanged.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to enacted conditions rather than administrative additions; its benefits-causation holding is not expanded.
- Director, OWCP v. Perini North River Associates, 459 U.S. 297: distinguishes respondent participation from a Director-initiated appeal; the respondent issue remains reserved.
- Director, OWCP v. Greenwich Collieries, June 20, 1994: preserves its persuasion-burden rules and treats them as distinct from statutory appellate entitlement.
- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992: leaves its Article III holdings unchanged; the statutory disposition makes a new constitutional rule unnecessary.

## Event

Anderson v. Edwards, No. 93-1883. Argued January 18, 1995; decided March 22, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing Edwards v. Healy, 12 F.3d 154 (December 1, 1993). The question is whether federal Aid to Families with Dependent Children law permits California's challenged common-caretaker assistance-unit grouping of eligible applicants, including nonsibling children, and whether the separate availability, mandatory-inclusion, equitable-treatment, and proration objections invalidate that grouping.

The class includes applicants or recipients in households with two or more nonsibling dependent children and one caretaker relative or a relative married couple. The litigation principally concerns the single-caretaker provision. Verna Edwards herself receives no AFDC. The district court found reduced aggregate benefits and hardship, using the then-current $901 versus $630 example in its April 24, 1992 order. It relied on Beaton v. Thompson, 913 F.2d 701, as binding. Its May 8, 1992 permanent injunction, signed May 7, prohibited the nonsibling consolidation policy, required separate grants for children not required together by federal law, and included implementation, corrective-payment, and notice provisions. The Ninth Circuit affirmed on Beaton's categorical availability analysis without deciding the independent equitable-treatment ground. That ground is preserved and is decided here. Different later benefit figures are not substituted for the 1992 findings.

Successor MPP §82-824.1 combines co-resident units when caretaker relatives are married to one another, when two caretakers have separate children and a child in common, or when there is only one caretaker relative. Predecessor §44-205.31 likewise groups eligible children under the single-caretaker rule; the supplied exhibit does not establish every former exception. The judgment addresses the challenged common-caretaker grouping, not every unproved multi-caretaker application.

## Holdings

### A lawful common-caretaker unit does not itself impute unavailable income

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal AFDC law permits California to apply its benefit schedule to the otherwise eligible applicants and recipients lawfully grouped under the challenged common-caretaker rule; a grant smaller than the sum of separate grants does not itself establish forbidden assumed support. Federal membership, exclusion, availability, disregard, and equitable-treatment requirements remain operative, and neither household residence nor a unit label makes an outsider's unavailable resources countable.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that defining the aided unit and deciding what income is available are distinct inquiries. King, Lewis, and Van Lare protect against fictitious support from persons outside the relevant legally protected relationships; they do not establish an invariable separate-unit entitlement for nonsibling children. California measures the needs of applicants grouped through the common caretaker and applies its unit-size schedule. Verna Edwards herself receives no aid, so the Court does not sustain the rule by assuming her membership or contribution. The recipients correctly insist that a label cannot evade the availability regulations. Those regulations retain their full force, including the substitute-parent prohibition, protection against reductions solely from a nonresponsible person's presence, and actual or qualified legal availability requirements. But a difference from hypothetical separate grants is not itself a finding of outside income. Dandridge preserves bounded state choice over benefit levels without repealing these safeguards. The Court rejects the categorical challenge and leaves concrete forbidden attributions for their actual facts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- King v. Smith, 392 U.S. 309: distinguishes the invalid substitute-parent assumption while preserving its protection against fictitious support.
- Lewis v. Martin, 397 U.S. 552: preserves the actual-support limitations; it does not prescribe a universal nonsibling unit boundary.
- Van Lare v. Hurley, 421 U.S. 338: distinguishes reduction attributed to an unaided nonresponsible person's presence from measurement of this lawful aided unit; its outside-support protection remains.
- Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471: applies bounded state discretion over benefit levels without dispensing with federal statutory or regulatory conditions.

### Mandatory family inclusion does not exhaust lawful unit composition

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 602(a)(38)'s qualified mandatory inclusion of co-resident parents and qualifying siblings does not make those relationships the exclusive permissible assistance unit. Optional nonparent caretakers or nonsiblings may decline aid only when mandatory inclusion does not require membership, and §605 creates no duty to transfer non-AFDC income between nonsiblings or other unit members.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's required inclusions are not an unwritten prohibition on every other lawful grouping. Section 602(a)(38) directs inclusion of the co-resident parent and qualifying sibling, subject to its stated exceptions, dependency conditions, and Title II proviso. Gilliard addresses that congressional family rule and associated child-support treatment; it does not establish the asserted ceiling on state unit definition. The contrary argument gives the mandatory list exclusive force its terms do not supply. Nor is the answer that every individual can freely withdraw: an optional nonparent caretaker or nonsibling can decline only outside mandatory inclusion, and the cited sibling example may require both children to decline together. Income legally considered in a valid unit need not be transferred separately between every recipient, but unavailable resources do not become countable through that observation. Section 605 concerns proper use of the AFDC grant, not a mutual private support duty or non-AFDC transfer obligation. Restricted awards, payee arrangements, and claimed unlawful opt-out barriers remain fact-dependent questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bowen v. Gilliard, 483 U.S. 587: applies the distinction between Congress's required family inclusion and an asserted exclusive unit definition; its child-support holding remains limited to its own statutory setting.
- King v. Smith, 392 U.S. 309: retains its unavailable-outside-support protection notwithstanding lawful membership and grant computation.
- Lewis v. Martin, 397 U.S. 552: retains its unavailable-outside-support protection notwithstanding lawful membership and grant computation.
- Van Lare v. Hurley, 421 U.S. 338: retains its unavailable-outside-support protection notwithstanding lawful membership and grant computation.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to enacted conditions without inserting an additional exclusivity command.

### The classwide equitable-treatment objection fails on its merits

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying the same unit-size schedule to eligible applicants lawfully grouped under the common-caretaker rule does not violate §§233.10(a)(1) and 233.20(a)(1)(i) merely because consolidation reduces aggregate aid or an unaided caretaker's needs are absent from a grant. Classifications must remain reasonable, statewide, objective, equitable in light of the Act, and consistent with eligibility requirements; the Court assumes without deciding that the cited provisions are enforceable under §1983.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that equitable treatment does not require summing the grants of hypothetical separate units. Both regulations have independent force: classifications may not arbitrarily exclude persons or conflict with the Act's provisions and purposes, and need and assistance must be determined objectively and equitably. The Court accepts the district court's hardship finding and the argument that reduced aid may discourage relatives from caring for children. Section 601's family-support purposes nevertheless do not prescribe separate-unit totals as the only lawful measure. A common schedule for the same number of aided members supplies an objective comparison. When the caretaker does not claim aid, that grant does not include the caretaker's own needs; this distinction alone does not establish arbitrary treatment of poor relatives. Dandridge preserves bounded benefit-level choices, while King, Lewis, and Van Lare continue to prohibit fictitious support. Administrative convenience cannot excuse an inconsistent eligibility restriction. The Court resolves this preserved classwide merits ground, reserving specific inequitable administration and making no §1983 enforceability ruling.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471: applies bounded benefit-level discretion, not a replacement of the regulations' equity commands with constitutional rational-basis review.
- King v. Smith, 392 U.S. 309: preserves its substantive availability limits within the equitable scheme.
- Lewis v. Martin, 397 U.S. 552: preserves its substantive availability limits within the equitable scheme.
- Van Lare v. Hurley, 421 U.S. 338: preserves its substantive availability limits within the equitable scheme.
- Suter v. Artist M.: its enforcement-channel treatment does not decide this substantive AFDC construction; enforceability of the cited provisions is assumed without decision here.

### The outsider-proration option does not require fragmentation of this lawful unit

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 612's optional reasonable proration of shelter, utilities, and similar needs when household members are not claiming aid together does not require dividing this otherwise lawful assistance unit. A unit-size schedule is not itself an election of that option; when a State elects proration, the statutory SSI qualification and §233.20(a)(5)'s separate SSI, landlord-tenant, both-standards, and plan requirements remain binding.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's proration option concerns a different relationship from the aided unit sustained here. Section 612 addresses a claimant sharing a household with people not claiming aid together. Its detailed protections matter when that option is used; they do not command fragmentation of every lawful unit so the State can recombine it through proration. The recipients' strongest objection is that a State could evade those safeguards merely by relabeling separate claims. The answer is the bounded membership ruling, not an unlimited labeling power: federal inclusions and exclusions, availability, and equitable treatment first constrain formation of the unit. Once those requirements permit this grouping, applying its size-based schedule does not itself invoke outsider proration. Section 233.20(a)(5) remains independently operative, including its landlord-tenant bar and conditional requirement to prorate both standards reasonably under a specified plan. No particular percentage, SSI household, or landlord-tenant application is approved. This construction preserves both unit formation and the distinct optional proration mechanism.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bowen v. Gilliard, 483 U.S. 587: retains the mandatory-family-grouping rule without making a household of nonclaimants identical to an aided unit.
- Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471: preserves lawful benefit-level choices, subject to the independently operative proration conditions.

### Independent construction supplies the result without a binding-guidance holding

**Holding and operative rule:** The statutes and binding regulations independently permit this bounded common-caretaker rule and defeat the preserved classwide objections. ACF-AT-94-6 may confirm that reading at its actual scope, but guidance does not create authority, override a regulation, or resolve an objection it does not address; the maximum deference owed to the guidance is left undecided.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the governing texts supply the necessary authority. Gardner requires attention to the conditions actually enacted; NationsBank concerns an actual statutory delegation and does not establish the force of every administrative instrument. Guernsey likewise requires separate examination of the regulations and the issue the guidance addresses. The 1994 transmittal distinguishes unit membership from available income and illustrates both consolidated and separate units. In either example an outsider's unavailable resources remain excluded; where state policy retains separate units, sharing cannot be presumed across them. That account confirms the Court's construction. It cannot replace analysis of §233.10's Act-purpose requirement, which the transmittal does not separately examine, and the Court has decided that ground itself. The reproduced 1986 instructions are an excerpt, not proof of an entire document. The Court neither accepts an administrative trump over availability protections nor decides a broader deference question unnecessary to the judgment. No finding of actual grant misuse follows from the guidance's remedial discussion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies independent fidelity to the enacted conditions.
- NationsBank of North Carolina, N.A. v. Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co., January 18, 1995: distinguishes its particular delegation and agency choice; no general guidance deference rule is imported.
- Shalala v. Guernsey Memorial Hospital, March 6, 1995: applies separation of distinct regulatory questions and the actual instrument's function; no AFDC regulation is displaced.

### Operative statutory and regulatory qualifications

Section 602(a)(38), except as otherwise provided in the same part, requires inclusion of a dependent child's parent and a brother or sister satisfying §606(a)(1)–(2) or §607(a) when living in the same home. Their income or available income enters the determination under paragraphs (7) and (8), notwithstanding §405(j) for Title II benefits. The reproduced 1986 instructions cover otherwise eligible natural/adoptive parents, dependent blood or adoptive siblings within the State's age limit, and a married stepparent subject to a generally applicable state duty equal to a natural parent's support duty. A stepbrother or stepsister is not automatically included merely because the stepparent is. In incapacity cases, and under a State's unemployment-of-the-principal-earner program, both parents are included if otherwise eligible.

Those instructions retain exclusions for SSI recipients, persons failing citizenship/alienage requirements, aliens ineligible because of sponsor income/resources or agency or organizational sponsorship, and lump-sum-income ineligibility. Required overlapping co-resident memberships consolidate. A family need not apply for a grandchild in the minor-mother example, but applying triggers the linked mandatory grouping. A nonparent caretaker or nonsibling can decline aid only when not mandatorily included; a required sibling cannot withdraw alone while leaving the required sibling grouping intact, and the supplied example may require both siblings to decline. The reproduced instructions distinguish Title II payments to a representative payee in the household from payments to an outside payee, with the latter counted only to the extent made available for the beneficiary. No hypothetical restricted award or payee dispute is adjudicated as a named family's fact.

Section 233.90(a)(1) determines deprivation through death, continued absence, incapacity, or a covered State-plan unemployment case by reference to a natural/adoptive parent or the qualifying married stepparent with the generally applicable equal support duty. Its second sentence independently forbids using a substitute parent's, man-in-the-house's, or other undescribed person's presence to find ineligibility or assume income. The Court does not reduce that regulation to parental deprivation alone.

Section 233.20(a)(2)(viii) forbids reducing a need item solely because a non-legally-responsible individual is present and forbids assumed contributions, subject separately to §233.20(a)(3)(xiv)'s stepparent-income provision, §233.20(a)(5)'s authorized proration, and §233.51's sponsored-alien regime. These are distinct qualified provisions, not permission to presume every resident's contribution. For a co-resident married stepparent without the generally applicable equal support duty, paragraph (a)(3)(xiv) counts income after deducting the first $90 of gross earnings; the State need-standard allowance for the stepparent and qualifying co-resident tax dependents whose needs are not otherwise included, excluding sanctioned or required-but-noncooperating persons; amounts actually paid to qualifying dependents outside the home; and outside-household alimony or child support. If the stepparent is in the unit, paragraph (a)(11)(i)–(ii)'s disregards apply instead. Sponsored-alien deemed income does not count toward unsponsored family members' need unless actually available. No individual stepparent or sponsorship calculation is made.

Section 233.20(a)(3)(ii)(D) considers income after applicable disregards, subject to paragraph (a)(3)(xiii), and resources available for current use. Consistently with other provisions of the chapter, actual availability suffices; the alternative requires both a legal interest in a liquidated sum and legal ability to make it available for support and maintenance. No sum unavailable to every unit member becomes available merely through grouping. The separate gross-income limit denies aid for a month when non-assistance income exceeds 185 percent of the State need standard, including special needs, for the same family composition, without the specified paragraph (a)(11)(i), (ii), and (viii) earned-income and tax-credit disregards except as paragraph (a)(3)(xix) permits. That permission allows a State-plan full-time-dependent-student earned-income disregard for no more than six months per calendar year; JTPA income disregarded under that paragraph, paragraph (a)(3)(xvii), or both has a combined six-month limit. The Court recalculates no family's eligibility.

Section 233.10(a)(1) requires reasonable program classifications and stated eligibility conditions, prohibits arbitrary or unreasonable exclusions and inequitable treatment in light of the Act's provisions and purposes, and requires every statutory eligibility condition. Narrower coverage is permitted only where the Act or its legislative history authorizes it. Administrative-efficiency or independent state-welfare conditions remain permissible only when consistent with the Act's provisions and purposes. The plan must distinguish groups inside and outside federal financial participation, apply conditions consistently and equitably statewide, preserve the opportunity to apply and obtain an eligibility determination, and use methods consistent with assisting eligible persons to qualify. Its separate title XVI uniformity condition is not converted into an additional AFDC holding.

Section 233.20(a)(1)(i) independently requires objective and equitable determination of need and assistance and consideration of all types of income in the same way except where federal statute specifically authorizes otherwise. The income-uniformity exception does not erase the separate equity requirement or decide whose income belongs to a unit. These two equity provisions are applied on their merits, without a ruling whether they confer an enforceable §1983 right.

Section 612 permits reasonable proration of shelter, utilities, and similar needs when individuals share a household without claiming aid together; an SSI recipient subject to the statutory one-third reduction is excluded from that calculation. Section 233.20(a)(5) separately prohibits proration with respect to such an SSI recipient and where a bona fide landlord-tenant relationship exists. If a State elects this option, it must prorate both need and payment standards reasonably; the plan must identify allowances, procedure, and circumstances, including which other persons count as sharing the household. Election is permitted, not compelled. None of these conditions authorizes a new proration percentage here.

Section 605 concerns proper use of the AFDC grant for its beneficiaries. It creates no duty to transfer a child's or caretaker's non-AFDC income and changes no general state-law support obligation. Counseling and guidance, advice about protective payments or guardianship, and authorized penalties remain qualified permitted responses rather than mandatory cumulative remedies. No caretaker misuse is found. The disposition rests on lawful membership and actual income rules, not an assumed transfer duty.

The 1994 transmittal permits statewide unit policies within these limits. Its consolidated-grandmother-and-five-children example excludes a surviving male friend's unavailable funds; its separate-unit example forbids presumed sharing across those separate units. The instrument discusses §233.20 equity but does not separately decide §233.10's Act-purpose language. The Court supplies that analysis independently. The 1986 transmittal is known here only through its reproduced excerpt.

## Holdings

### RFRA requires a distinct inquiry into this application of the housing laws

**Holding and operative rule:** For prospective enforcement of the marital-status housing prohibitions against Swanner, Swanner must establish a substantial burden on his religious exercise; if he does, the government must produce evidence and persuade that applying the burden to him furthers a compelling governmental interest by the least restrictive means. The adverse federal prospective-enforcement determination is vacated for that inquiry because commercial participation and a general prohibition of discrimination do not by themselves complete it; no exemption or ultimate statutory justification is decided.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas: five direct rationale and judgment votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer dissent from the vacatur. Their agreement that RFRA governs does not turn their distinct application into Court law.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that RFRA requires separate attention to burden and justification in this application. Clearwater requires that inquiry for future enforcement of preexisting law, with the claimant proving substantial burden and the government bearing production and persuasion on compelling interest and least restrictive means. The Alaska court recognized the harm of discriminatory denials, but its reliance on voluntary commercial participation and its conclusion that prohibition is the most effective tool do not complete the statutory inquiry. Lee gives substantial weight to burdens an exemption would impose on others; Roberts recognizes the injury of exclusion from protected opportunities. Those concerns must be evaluated here, not replaced by an assumption that commercial activity forfeits religious protection. Conversely, availability of other housing does not erase the independent transactional and dignitary injury. The remand permits that injury to support justification while requiring assessment of the actual burden, any legally comparable exemption, and less restrictive means. No constitutional ruling or automatic landlord exemption follows from the need for this statutory examination.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Church of Scientology Flag Service Organization, Inc. v. City of Clearwater, December 5, 1994: applies its prospective, claimant-specific RFRA inquiry and distinct burdens to the present housing enforcement; its reservations concerning RFRA's constitutionality and retrospective monetary liability remain.
- United States v. Lee, 455 U.S. 252: preserves its recognition that exemptions can impose religious limitations on third parties; commercial activity is not treated as categorical forfeiture under RFRA.
- Roberts v. United States Jaycees, 468 U.S. 609: recognizes the weight of protected access and injury from exclusion; it does not automatically decide justification under every antidiscrimination law.
- Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872: its constitutional neutral-law rule does not replace the enacted RFRA inquiry; its constitutional holding remains unchanged.
- Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, June 11, 1993: preserves objective neutrality, comparable secular harm, and strict-scrutiny rules without deciding the constitutional claim here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No final substantial-burden or justification finding is made. The Anchorage ordinance's distinct individual-home exception applies when the renter would share common living areas with the owner, lessor, manager, agent, or other person; no finding places Swanner within it, and its legal comparability cannot be assumed either way. The Court neither invalidates the housing provisions nor ranks all marital-status protections. It does not decide religious teaching or advocacy, religious-mission housing, intimate owner-occupied arrangements, RFRA's constitutionality, or retrospective monetary liability. Alaska statutory construction, the state constitutional ruling, and separate notice/delegation due-process rulings are outside reconsideration. The federal constitutional exemption claim is reserved.

Commercial participation is not forfeiture, antidiscrimination labeling is not automatic justification, and alternate housing is not an automatic answer to direct injury. This is a bounded application/remand rule. It does not establish that the government will fail or that Swanner must receive an exemption. The Court preserves ordinary standing and appropriate-relief limits and the statute's protection of belief and preservation of the Establishment Clause.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer. They would affirm rejection of the federal exemption for these commercial refusals. Assuming without deciding a substantial burden, they conclude that the government has justified this application: each identified applicant was denied an otherwise offered housing opportunity on the prohibited ground, and an exemption permitting that denial would remove the applicant's protection in the transaction. The interests are protecting each covered person from that denial and preserving equal access to the covered rental market without making applicants absorb individual landlords' religious objections. Referral or notice would leave the same unequal treatment by the landlord who offered the unit. No less restrictive accommodation preserving that protection has been shown on this record; this conclusion does not shift RFRA's production and persuasion burden from the government.

The dissent rejects a rule that entry into commerce forfeits religious protection. The Commission regulates the terms of offered commercial housing, not belief, worship, teaching, or public expression. Lee supports attention to the religious restrictions an exemption imposes on others, and Roberts supports the direct access and dignitary interests. The dissent distinguishes an application-specific justification from a conclusion that the statutory label alone makes every prohibition compelling. It reserves an accommodation preserving the applicant's full protection without material transferred cost or lost access.

The dissent also rejects the federal constitutional claim independently under Smith and Lukumi: the admitted provisions do not target religion, make religious motivation an element, or allow comparable secular commercial refusals while forbidding religious ones. The individual-home exception for shared common living areas addresses a different intimate setting on this record; it does not establish a comparable exception for Swanner's offered commercial tenancies. This view does not decide regulation of religious teaching or advocacy, housing operated as part of a religious institution's mission, owner-occupied arrangements implicating intimacy or privacy, or a scheme granting comparable secular exemptions while withholding a religious one. ## Holdings

### Color may identify source under the Lanham Act

**Holding and operative rule:** A color is eligible for trademark protection when it has acquired source-identifying significance and is nonfunctional, subject to the ordinary Lanham Act requirements, including likelihood of confusion where required. Color alone is not categorically excluded, but neither bare color without secondary meaning nor ownership of color in the abstract is protected.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a color can perform the source-identifying function the Lanham Act protects. Sections 1052 and 1127 describe marks by that function and contain no categorical exclusion for color. Gardner requires applying the enacted conditions, not adding a policy restriction absent from the text. Two Pesos protects qualifying trade dress and keeps distinctiveness, functionality, and confusion separate; it does not establish that bare color is inherently distinctive. Here the district court found that Qualitex's green-gold press-pad color had acquired secondary meaning and that Jacobson's copying was likely to confuse purchasers. Those findings are not replaced with new findings by this Court. Shade comparisons can be addressed through ordinary proof of source significance and confusion. The possible depletion of useful colors requires attention to functionality and competitive need, not rejection of every color mark. The Ninth Circuit's contrary category rule and cancellation direction therefore cannot stand. The independently sustained unfair-competition judgment remains intact, and ordinary statutory requirements govern further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: applies the separation of qualifying source identification, nonfunctionality, and confusion; its inherent-distinctiveness rule for restaurant trade dress is not extended to bare color.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to statutory terms without an invented category exclusion; it supplies no separate trademark proof rule.

### Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features

**Holding and operative rule:** Acquired source significance does not make a functional color protectable: a feature essential to the product's use or purpose or affecting its cost or quality remains outside trademark protection, and protection cannot foreclose color features shown to be competitively necessary. The record's absence of a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade defeats a categorical functionality objection to its eligibility; no general rule about every aesthetic feature is decided.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes on this operative limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that trademark law protects source identification without granting control of useful product features. Inwood identifies functionality when a feature is essential to use or purpose or affects cost or quality. That boundary remains independent of secondary meaning: consumers' recognition does not justify excluding competitors from features they need for reasons unrelated to source identification. A color may sometimes perform such a function, and the availability of alternative colors must be considered in the actual product setting. The lower findings distinguish the usefulness of color generally in concealing stains from a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade. They identify no improvement in use, quality, or longevity attributable to the shade and describe numerous available tones. The Court relies on those findings without creating a new market definition or evidentiary presumption. The possibility that another color or market presents different competitive needs therefore does not support a universal color bar. It remains a reason to apply functionality in each proper case, not to dispense with that requirement here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844, 850 n.10: applies the essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality functionality boundary to the asserted color feature; functionality remains independent of source significance.
- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: preserves the separate nonfunctionality requirement; neither its holding nor this decision confers rights in useful designs or generic subject matter.

## Precedent Treatment

Two Pesos retains its protection of qualifying trade dress and its separate requirements; its restaurant-trade-dress rule does not establish inherent distinctiveness of bare color. Inwood's functionality boundary remains controlling and is applied to the claimed color feature. Gardner supports applying the enacted statutory conditions. No general aesthetic-functionality test or new evidentiary presumption is adopted.

## Law After Decision

Effective March 28, 1995, the Lanham Act permits protection of source-identifying, nonfunctional color subject to ordinary statutory requirements. The categorical color exclusion is rejected; functionality, acquired source significance for bare color, and confusion where required remain distinct. Protection does not extend to color in the abstract, functional color, or color lacking secondary meaning. This decision does not resolve every color-market competition question or grant rights in useful or generic product features.

## Event

Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Jefferson Lines, Inc., No. 93-1677. Argued November 28, 1994; decided April 3, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, reviewing its January 21, 1994 judgment, 15 F.3d 90, affirming disallowance of Oklahoma's sales-tax claims in Jefferson's Chapter 11 proceeding. ## Holdings

### The completed local sale and carrier operations supply substantial nexus

**Holding and operative rule:** A physically present carrier's completed Oklahoma sale of a ticket for a trip originating in Oklahoma supplies the substantial nexus required for this sales tax. The ruling does not decide remote collection obligations or a ticket for transportation wholly outside the State.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg: five direct rationale votes. Justices Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment and do not join this complete explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Oklahoma has the required connection to the activity it taxes. Complete Auto requires substantial nexus independently of fair apportionment, nondiscrimination, and relation to state services. Quill preserves a specific Commerce Clause boundary for a remote seller's collection obligation; it does not extend that boundary to a carrier conducting the taxed sales through established local operations. National Geographic supplies the relevant significance of actual local business activity. Jefferson is qualified to do business in Oklahoma, operates there as a common carrier, and makes the stipulated ticket sales there for trips beginning there. Its objection identifies the transportation performed outside Oklahoma, but that objection concerns the proper measure and allocation of this sale rather than the absence of a local transaction. The Court addresses allocation separately. It neither treats due process as a substitute for Commerce Clause nexus nor creates a numerical presence threshold. Tickets for wholly out-of-state trips and remote transactions remain outside this record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274: applies substantial nexus as one independent requirement; the other requirements remain necessary.
- Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, May 26, 1992: preserves its specific remote-collection boundary and separate constitutional inquiries; it does not govern this physically present seller's local transactions.
- National Geographic Society v. California Board of Equalization, 430 U.S. 551: applies the significance of actual local business operations; no remote-seller or slightest-presence rule is announced.

### Identical taxation of the completed retail sale is internally consistent

**Holding and operative rule:** A tax applied by every State to completed local retail sales of the kind at issue would tax this ticket sale once and is internally consistent. Furnishing later segments of the purchased interstate transportation does not itself constitute a second retail sale of this ticket under the identical local-sale application.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg: five direct rationale votes. Justices Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment and do not join this complete explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identical application of this local-sales tax would not multiply taxation of the ticket sale. Goldberg distinguishes internal consistency, which tests the structure on the assumption that every State uses it, from external consistency, which examines attribution to the taxing State. Jefferson invokes the statutory definition of sale, including the furnishing or rendering of taxable services, and argues that each State through which the bus passes could impose the same charge. That argument does not identify another completed retail sale of this ticket. The purchaser bought the transportation in Oklahoma, where the trip began; later performance supplies the service already purchased. The Court rejects the definition-based argument on its merits rather than treating the issue as conceded. This conclusion concerns an identical local-sale application. It does not validate every differently designed use tax or carrier levy another State might enact, nor does it substitute internal consistency for the remaining constitutional inquiries.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Goldberg v. Sweet, 488 U.S. 252: applies the distinction between internal and external consistency; passing the structural inquiry does not dispense with external allocation.
- Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274: applies fair apportionment while preserving its other independent requirements.

### The full price measures the local consumer sale without mileage apportionment

**Holding and operative rule:** The full price of this completed Oklahoma retail sale of locally originating interstate transportation may measure the consumer sales tax without mileage apportionment. The distinct carrier-business gross-receipts tax addressed in Central Greyhound remains subject to apportionment, and neither a tax's label nor nominal buyer incidence alone establishes external consistency.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg: five direct rationale votes. Justices Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment and do not join this complete explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this tax reaches the value of a localized retail transaction. Complete Auto rejects immunity or validity based on a tax's name, so the analysis must identify what Oklahoma actually taxes. The purchaser completes one sale in Oklahoma; interstate transportation is the service purchased, not a series of separately completed ticket sales. Itel Containers confirms the significance of a distinct local transaction without excusing any contested Complete Auto requirement. Central Greyhound required apportionment of receipts from the carrier's multistate transportation business. That rule remains, but it does not require dividing every consumer sale according to later performance. Jefferson's contrary approach would treat the local transaction as indistinguishable from the carrier's ongoing business. The actual event, not merely the identity of the nominal taxpayer, defeats that comparison. No actual second assessment is proved. Oklahoma provides no credit for the hypothetical competing levy, but its absence does not invalidate this single-sale application. A demonstrated competing levy or a materially different transaction remains open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274: preserves practical examination of the actual tax rather than reliance on labels.
- Central Greyhound Lines, Inc. v. Mealey, 334 U.S. 653: distinguishes the carrier-business gross-receipts tax; its apportionment requirement for that taxable activity remains in force.
- Itel Containers International Corp. v. Huddleston, February 23, 1993: applies the significance of a distinct local transaction, while preserving the fact that Complete Auto compliance was conceded there and must be decided here.
- Goldberg v. Sweet, 488 U.S. 252: applies external consistency to attribution of the actual taxable event; its telephone-service and credit setting is not a mandatory credit rule for every local sale.

### The established burden does not discriminate against interstate ticket sales

**Holding and operative rule:** Equal taxation of interstate and intrastate tickets sold locally satisfies nondiscrimination in this application because no additional discriminatory burden is established. Equal facial rates are not invariably sufficient; here the bankruptcy court's contrary practical-effect conclusion rests on the external-apportionment premise the Court rejects.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg: five direct rationale votes. Justices Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment and do not join this complete explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the established operation of this tax does not favor local transportation over interstate transportation. Complete Auto requires a separate discrimination inquiry, and Lohman requires examination of the actual burden rather than a statewide average or a supposed offset elsewhere. The stipulation establishes the same applicable rate for intrastate and interstate ticket sales. The bankruptcy court found facial neutrality and no apparent protectionist purpose, but inferred discriminatory effect because it considered the interstate portion improperly included in the tax base. The Court has rejected that allocation premise for this consumer sale. Jefferson's example of equally priced trips with different Oklahoma mileage repeats the same premise; it does not identify favoritism once the sale, rather than mileage, is the taxable activity. The Court does not infer that equal statutory rates always settle discrimination or invent a compensatory-tax defense. No actual second assessment or additional unequal burden is established here, and materially different practical effects remain reviewable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274: applies nondiscrimination independently of the other requirements.
- Associated Industries of Missouri v. Lohman, May 23, 1994: preserves comparison of the actual local burden and rejects fictional offsets; no statewide averaging or compensatory-tax defense is supplied here.

### General services supporting the local activity supply a fair relation

**Holding and operative rule:** A sales tax is fairly related to state services when broad governmental services support the locally taxed activity; the Constitution requires no dollar-for-dollar or mile-for-mile reimbursement calculation. Oklahoma's services supporting ticket sales, passenger loading, and local operations establish that relation for these transactions.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg: five direct rationale votes. Justices Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment and do not join this complete explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that fair relation concerns the connection between the taxed activity and governmental benefits, not a bill for each service the passenger receives. Commonwealth Edison rejects a requirement that the taxpayer's liability match the cost of particular benefits. Goldberg likewise recognizes the significance of broad protections supporting a taxable transaction. The stipulation and bankruptcy findings establish local ticketing and loading operations, governmental protection at those locations, and use of Oklahoma roads. Jefferson answers that Oklahoma supplies no services along the out-of-state part of the route. That objection would require the Court to price each mile's governmental support, although the occasion of this tax is the completed local sale. The transaction's price measures its value rather than the State's exact expenditure. The Court does not permit taxation of an unrelated event or hold that this factor replaces apportionment. It finds the required relation for the local activity shown and leaves remote or wholly out-of-state transactions undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Commonwealth Edison Co. v. Montana, 453 U.S. 609: applies the connection to broad governmental benefits without dollar-for-dollar accounting.
- Goldberg v. Sweet, 488 U.S. 252: applies the fair-relation inquiry to general protections supporting the taxable activity.
- Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274: keeps fair relation independent of nexus, apportionment, and nondiscrimination.

## Precedent Treatment

Complete Auto remains the governing four-part framework. Quill's remote-collection boundary is preserved; National Geographic supplies the actual-local-operations comparison. Goldberg keeps internal and external consistency distinct. Central Greyhound continues to require apportionment of the carrier-business receipts it addressed, but does not govern this distinct local retail sale. Itel Containers supplies the distinct-transaction principle without deciding this case through its conceded Complete Auto compliance. Lohman preserves actual-burden nondiscrimination analysis. ## Holdings

### No in-effect federal standard supplies the express-preemption predicate here

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1392(d)'s express restriction requires an in-effect federal motor-vehicle safety standard governing the same aspect of performance as the asserted state duty. The asserted antilock-equipment defect claims are not expressly displaced on this record because that federal predicate is absent; the Court does not decide whether common-law duties themselves are safety standards within that provision.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer: eight direct rationale votes. Justice Scalia concurs only in the judgment and gives no separate explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the express clause must be applied to the particular aspect of performance actually regulated. Easterwood requires attention to federal coverage of the duty at issue, and Cipollone requires examination of the enacted preemption language and predicate duty. Regulation of vehicle braking generally does not establish an operative antilock requirement for these claims. Paccar judicially invalidated inadequately supported stopping-distance requirements; that invalidation does not itself supply a federal safety standard forbidding state liability for omitted antilock equipment. Section 1392(d)'s condition is therefore unsatisfied without deciding whether common-law duties fall within its category of state standards. Section 1397(k) states that compliance with a federal safety standard does not exempt a person from common-law liability. The Court preserves that text but need not determine its every effect where an operative standard exists. The common-law claims return for ordinary adjudication; this statutory conclusion establishes neither a product defect nor causation and does not disturb the independent Georgia RICO judgment.

- CSX Transportation, Inc. v. Easterwood, April 21, 1993: applies its attention to the aspect of performance actually covered, without importing the separate railroad speed holding or treating every braking rule as coverage of antilock omission.

### Judicial invalidation alone establishes no actual federal conflict

**Holding and operative rule:** An express preemption clause does not categorically foreclose inquiry into an independently established actual conflict with operative federal law or a demonstrated federal objective. Here neither impossibility nor interference with such an objective is established by judicial invalidation and the absence of an operative antilock requirement, so the asserted conflict does not bar these common-law claims.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer: eight direct rationale votes. Scalia's judgment-only agreement supplies no additional rationale vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the failure of express preemption does not end every possible conflict inquiry. The Eleventh Circuit read Cipollone too broadly when it treated Congress's express clause as invariably excluding implied preemption. Gade rejects automatic displacement from regulatory overlap but supplies no physical-impossibility-only rule. The manufacturers nevertheless must identify the federal obligation or policy that their asserted state duty would frustrate. Their strongest claim treats the ability to sell trucks without antilock equipment as a protected federal choice. Paccar does not establish that premise: the court rejected the agency's evidentiary support while allowing adequately supported future standards. It did not adopt a policy insulating manufacturers from state liability. Compliance with the asserted duty is not shown to violate federal law, and regulatory silence alone does not demonstrate the claimed obstacle. A valid operative standard or demonstrated affirmative policy requiring a uniform absence of regulation could present a different case. The Court leaves that case, and the ultimate tort merits here, undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association, June 18, 1992: preserves the shared rule against automatic displacement from overlap; no physical-impossibility-only theory or broader separate theory is made Court law.
- Paccar, Inc. v. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 573 F.2d 632: distinguishes inadequate support for a mandatory standard from an affirmative policy protecting noninstallation against state duties.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 1397(k)'s compliance saving clause is not held to immunize every common-law action from an actual conflict. The Court does not decide whether tort duties are standards under §1392(d), every effect of the saving clause when a standard exists, or a federal field-wide ceiling. ## Holdings

### Regular attorney debt collectors remain covered when they use litigation

**Holding and operative rule:** The FDCPA applies to an attorney who regularly collects or attempts to collect covered consumer debts owed or asserted to be owed another, including through litigation, subject to the statute's actual definition, exceptions, elements and defenses. There is no categorical attorney-litigation exemption; the decision does not cover lawyers merely because they practice law or establish that this settlement letter violated the Act.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale and judgment votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that collection litigation is a means of collecting a debt within the enacted definition. The statute covers regular direct or indirect collection for another, and Congress deleted the former attorney exemption in 1986. Gardner supports enforcing those retained conditions without restoring an exclusion Congress removed. The strongest contrary argument is that rules regulating collection threats and communications can interact awkwardly with ordinary judicial proceedings. That concern does not justify excluding every litigating attorney from the definition. Congress retained an activity-specific exclusion for serving or attempting to serve legal process in connection with judicial debt enforcement, not a general occupational exclusion. Questions about a particular threat, communication or defense must be decided under the provision actually presented. An unsuccessful collection action is not automatically unlawful, and coverage does not prove the alleged insurance demand false or unauthorized. The complaint therefore returns for the ordinary inquiries into regularity, contractual authorization, alleged misrepresentation and defenses, without a liability or damages finding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies respect for enacted conditions and rejection of an added category restriction; it does not create a new FDCPA proof rule or resolve conduct liability.
- Green v. Hocking, 9 F.3d 18: rejects the lower-court categorical litigation-exemption approach as inconsistent with the enacted definition and attorney-exemption repeal; it supplies no continuing exemption in this Court's law.

**Operative statutory terms and questions not reached:** A covered debt is an obligation or alleged obligation of a consumer to pay money arising from a transaction whose money, property, insurance or services are primarily for personal, family or household purposes, whether or not reduced to judgment. The retained §1692a(6)(D) exception concerns a person while serving or attempting to serve legal process on another in connection with judicial enforcement of a debt; it does not create a whole-attorney exemption. No factual statutory exception is established here.

Section 1692e prohibits false, deceptive or misleading representations in debt collection; §1692f(1) addresses collection of an amount not expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt or permitted by law. The alleged $4,173 charge remains to be tested under those provisions. Section 1692e(5) concerns threatened action that cannot legally be taken or is not intended; failure to prevail alone does not necessarily satisfy it. No authoritative resolution of those conduct applications is made.

The communication and defense provisions invoked in the objection retain their exact conditions. Under §1692c(c), a consumer's written refusal to pay or written request to cease communication bars further communications except to advise that collection efforts are terminated, to notify that the collector or creditor may invoke specified remedies ordinarily invoked by that collector or creditor, or, where applicable, to notify that the collector or creditor intends to invoke a specified remedy. A mailed notice is complete on receipt. These are alternative permissions, not mandatory notices. Under §1692k(c), the collector must prove by a preponderance that a violation was unintentional and resulted from bona fide error despite procedures reasonably adapted to avoid it; no general immunity for legal error is decided. Under §1692k(e), good-faith conformity with an FTC advisory opinion protects an act or omission even if the opinion is later amended, rescinded or held invalid; staff commentary is not automatically such an opinion. Section 814(d) denies the FTC and the other agencies assigned enforcement responsibility under §814(b) authority to promulgate trade-regulation rules or other regulations concerning collection of debts by debt collectors as defined in the Act. No defense is established on these allegations, and no comprehensive litigation exception or fee entitlement is adopted.

The actual statutory exceptions operate on their stated terms, and no comprehensive litigation exemption is created.

## Event

Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, No. 94-38. Argued February 21, 1995; decided April 18, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, reviewing its April 19, 1994 judgment, 21 F.3d 1508, affirming summary judgment for Colorado in a §1983 action seeking injunctive relief. The questions concern the First Amendment limits on denying otherwise available criminal-justice information according to intended commercial solicitation and the distinct claimed constitutional right of access.

The law firm's partners and the director of a drug-and-alcohol treatment center sought names and addresses from El Paso County Court records concerning persons accused of DUI and misdemeanor traffic offenses. They intended direct-mail solicitation for legal and treatment services and refused the statutory assurance against that use. No finding establishes that their proposed speech was false or unlawful. The lower majority rejected the asserted general access entitlement but separately reviewed and sustained the commercial-speech condition; Judge Aldisert dissented on qualified-access grounds. The information's conceded availability through the El Paso County News is relevant to the claimed privacy justification.

Colorado Rev. Stat. §24-72-305.5 contains two distinct commands:

> Records of official actions and criminal justice records and the names, addresses, telephone numbers, and other information in such records shall not be used by any person for the purpose of soliciting business for pecuniary gain. The official custodian shall deny any person access to records of official actions and criminal justice records unless such person signs a statement which affirms that such records shall not be used for the direct solicitation of business for pecuniary gain.

The first sentence addresses solicitation for pecuniary gain generally; the second requires an assurance against direct solicitation before inspection. The Act includes courts with criminal jurisdiction in its definition of criminal-justice agency. It covers agency documentary materials used in legally authorized functions and official actions including arrest, charging, disposition, release, judicial condition determinations, probation or parole decisions, correctional or rehabilitation participation, and formal discipline, reclassification, or relocation of persons under criminal sentence. These definitions establish covered categories; they are not findings that all such materials are unconditionally public.

## Holdings

### The intended-speech condition requires commercial-speech review

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State otherwise makes covered records available, a rule denying access and prohibiting use because the requester intends truthful, lawful commercial solicitation is subject to the governing commercial-speech standard. The absence of a general entitlement to every government record does not remove this speech-selected condition from First Amendment review; a qualified right to particular court records is left undecided.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justices O'Connor and Scalia dissent from the application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged line concerns expression as well as access. Colorado prohibits using the covered records and their information to solicit business for pecuniary gain and conditions inspection on a promise against direct solicitation. Identical record requests thus receive different treatment because of the protected communication the requester plans to make. Central Hudson governs truthful, nonmisleading commercial expression concerning lawful activity. Shapero establishes that targeted legal-service mail falls within that protection, while Discovery Network requires justification for a selected commercial category. Colorado's custody of the records does not erase these requirements. Its strongest argument distinguishes access to information from later communication of information already acquired, but the challenged enactment regulates both access and use within an existing disclosure regime. The Court need not decide whether experience and logic independently guarantee access to these particular court materials. Nor does this holding compel disclosure of confidential files, create records, or forbid neutral access conditions. It requires review of the speech condition actually imposed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies commercial-speech protection to truthful solicitation about lawful services.
- Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association, 486 U.S. 466: applies protection for truthful targeted mail while distinguishing the separate source-of-information question.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: applies review of a speech-selected commercial category notwithstanding governmental control of the facility involved.
- Houchins v. KQED, Inc., 438 U.S. 1: does not establish an absolute governmental privilege to attach this speech restriction to otherwise available records; no general access holding is added.
- Nixon v. Warner Communications, Inc., 435 U.S. 589: does not establish an absolute governmental privilege to attach this speech restriction to otherwise available records; no general access holding is added.
- Press-Enterprise Co. v. Superior Court, 478 U.S. 1: preserves the separate experience-and-logic inquiry for qualified access; its application to these records is not decided.

### Colorado has not demonstrated material advancement in this application

**Holding and operative rule:** For a restriction on truthful, lawful commercial solicitation, the State must demonstrate real harms and material advancement of its substantial interests in the relevant setting. Colorado's generalized privacy, abuse-prevention, and public-confidence justifications do not establish that this categorical use-and-access restriction materially advances those interests as applied to these plaintiffs' proposed direct mail.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justices O'Connor and Scalia dissent from the application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantial interests do not dispense with proof of the connection between those interests and the chosen burden. Edenfield requires real harms and material advancement in the particular professional setting; it does not invariably demand empirical studies. Privacy for persons merely accused of offenses, prevention of solicitation abuse, and confidence in justice are substantial concerns. But Colorado has not shown why denying these otherwise available names and addresses to these speakers materially protects those interests. The lower court inferred meaningful privacy simply from the plaintiffs' persistence in seeking access. That inference does not establish the effect of the selected restriction. The conceded availability of the information through the El Paso County News further bears on that explanation, without creating a rule that every privacy measure must cover every source. Shapero distinguishes targeted mail from coercive personal confrontation. No finding of unlawful or misleading proposed mail answers that distinction here. Supported controls on actual deception, harassment, or protected information remain available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the real-harm and material-advancement burden without requiring studies in every case.
- Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association, 486 U.S. 466: applies the distinction between targeted mail and coercive personal solicitation; the Court does not adjudicate unexamined letter-specific content.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: preserves the need for a connection between the commercial selection and the harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the requirement of justification responsive to truthful professional expression; actual deception remains regulable.

### The categorical prohibition lacks the required reasonable fit

**Holding and operative rule:** The restriction must reasonably fit the State's substantial interests and may not burden more truthful, lawful commercial speech than that fit permits; the State need not employ the least restrictive means. This categorical prohibition and access assurance lack the required fit as applied, because Colorado has not justified selecting every proposed direct solicitation over neutral access protections or measures addressing actual abuses.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justices O'Connor and Scalia dissent from the application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the wholesale speech criterion is broader than Colorado has justified. Fox requires a reasonable, proportionate fit rather than the least restrictive conceivable measure. Discovery Network requires the chosen category to correspond to the asserted harm. Colorado distinguishes the point of official disclosure from the later mailing and argues that withholding information imposes only an indirect burden. That distinction deserves consideration, but it cannot establish fit by itself when the statute also prohibits the information's solicitation use and selects otherwise identical requests by intended expression. The record does not explain why neutral redaction, delayed release, opt-out protections, retrieval fees, or direct regulation of deceptive, coercive, and harassing solicitation are inadequate to support a more fitted response. The Court does not mandate any particular replacement system or find that every alternative is required in every case. It rejects this categorical criterion on this record. Confidential records and independently justified neutral limits remain subject to their own rules.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees of State University of New York v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: applies the relationship between the selected commercial category and the harm; alternatives are relevant without requiring regulation of all sources simultaneously.
- Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association, 486 U.S. 466: preserves appropriately directed safeguards against misleading or abusive mail instead of an unsupported blanket barrier.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: retains protection for materially different professional settings and supported regulation of deception or coercion.

## Precedent Treatment

Central Hudson and Fox remain the governing framework. Discovery Network, Edenfield, and Ibanez supply the selected-category, material-advancement, and truthful-professional-speech principles applied here. Shapero protects targeted mail without alone determining access to government information. Houchins and Nixon do not turn the absence of a general access entitlement into an absolute privilege to burden speech; the Court leaves the separate qualified-access inquiry under Press-Enterprise undecided. No precedent is overruled, and no strict-scrutiny or commercial/political-speech equality rule is adopted.

## Law After Decision

Effective April 18, 1995, the challenged solicitation-use prohibition and access assurance cannot sustain withholding otherwise available information in this application under Central Hudson and Fox. Material advancement and reasonable fit remain distinct requirements; each deficient showing supports the judgment. The ruling does not require disclosure of confidential records, resolve access to every court filing or proceeding, command creation of a list, or invalidate independently justified neutral access rules. Regulation of false, deceptive, coercive, or harassing solicitation remains available. Seven Justices directly join the controlling explanation; no Marks inquiry is needed.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs while joining the Court's opinion. Protection for speaking with lawfully obtained information does not create a general First Amendment right to obtain every state criminal-justice record or require government to create or disclose every source. Ordinary access begins with enacted law and distinct historical access doctrines. The judicial-proceeding cases require their own history and structural inquiry; a qualified right to particular court filings or proceedings is not resolved here. Colorado's existing access regime nevertheless cannot select protected intended speech for the unjustified burden the Court rejects.

Official dissemination of accused persons' identifying information for targeted pecuniary solicitation presents a privacy and potential-abuse concern different from a ban on communicating information already acquired. Their position accepts First Amendment review, acknowledges both statutory commands, and would sustain this application. ### Section 27A(b) cannot reopen this completed private federal judgment

### Statutory distinctions and preserved routes

Section 1334(b) grants original but nonexclusive district-court jurisdiction over civil proceedings arising under title 11, arising in a title 11 case, or related to one, notwithstanding an Act of Congress conferring exclusive jurisdiction on a court other than the district courts. It differs from §1334(a)'s original and exclusive jurisdiction over cases under title 11 and the then-applicable §1334(d)'s exclusive jurisdiction, in the district where the case begins or is pending, over the debtor's property wherever located as of commencement and property of the estate. The 1994 amendments do not apply to this bankruptcy commenced in 1990; the later property designation (e), new jury provision, and changed appellate-panel/election provisions supply no rule here.

Section 157(a) permits a district court to refer title 11 cases and proceedings arising under title 11, arising in such a case, or related to one to its bankruptcy judges. Under §157(b)(1), referred core proceedings arising under title 11 or in a title 11 case may be heard and determined, with appropriate orders subject to §158 review. The bankruptcy court called its bond-property determination core under §157(b)(2)(A), (G), and (O); that is its classification, not this Court's final adoption. Those clauses concern estate administration; motions to terminate, annul, or modify the automatic stay; and other proceedings affecting liquidation of estate assets or adjustment of debtor-creditor or equity-security-holder relationships, except personal-injury tort and wrongful-death claims. Section 157(b)(3) places the classification question before the bankruptcy judge on the judge's own motion or timely party motion and forbids a noncore classification solely because state law may affect the outcome. It does not make the classification unreviewable.

For a noncore but related proceeding, §157(c)(1) permits hearing and proposed findings and conclusions submitted to the district court; the final order belongs to the district judge after consideration and de novo review of the matters timely and specifically objected to. De novo review is not automatically required for every unobjected proposition. ## Holdings

### Compelled identification on these political leaflets requires exacting scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-defined requirement that an individual identify herself on ordinary political issue leaflets burdens core political expression, including the choice to speak anonymously. The State must satisfy exacting scrutiny by showing narrow tailoring to an overriding state interest; viewpoint neutrality or the election setting alone does not lower that standard.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the author's choice to omit her identity is protected as part of political expression. Talley protects anonymous handbills and recognizes the place of anonymous publication in public discussion. Ohio's requirement selects publications by their electoral subject and conditions their circulation on identification. It is therefore a content-based burden on the individual's communication, not simply a neutral rule for administering a ballot. Burson preserves demanding scrutiny for campaign-specific restrictions even though its different polling-place judgment had no shared validity rationale. Ohio invokes the modest size of the identification obligation and its election-related purpose. Neither answers whether the State may impose the burden on this ordinary issue leaflet. The Court applies exacting scrutiny requiring narrow tailoring to an overriding interest. It does not decide that anonymity is always benign or require proof that this speaker faced retaliation. Candidate finance reports, organizational attribution, coordinated spending, and fraudulent impersonation present distinct questions left outside this holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection of anonymous handbills to this individual political leaflet; the Court does not establish immunity for fraudulent communications.
- Burson v. Freeman, May 26, 1992: applies the independently majority-supported scrutiny distinction; its fractured polling-place validity judgment does not settle this leaflet requirement.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780: distinguishes the election-administration setting; it does not reduce scrutiny for every election-related speech restriction.

### The asserted voter-information interest does not justify this identification demand

**Holding and operative rule:** A general interest in helping voters evaluate a source does not justify requiring this ordinary individual to place her name and residence or business address on her independently produced, truthful political leaflet. The Court leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending, and other materially distinct disclosure settings for separate analysis.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that voters' interest in source information does not justify this direct condition on an ordinary citizen's political pamphlet. Talley protects the decision to publish without personal attribution; an author need not establish that identity has no possible informational value. Ohio relies on Buckley's campaign-finance disclosure and Bellotti's discussion of identifying corporate advertising. Those sources concern different mechanisms and interests. A report identifying financial participation in a campaign is not the same requirement as putting a private individual's name and address on every issue leaflet she distributes. Corporate spending likewise supplies no automatic rule for this record, which establishes neither a campaign organization nor coordinated financing. The Court does not hold that source information is useless or that all disclosure is forbidden. It holds that the general informational rationale is inadequate for the burden imposed here. Ohio's committee alternatives and personal-correspondence exception do not transform Margaret McIntyre's independently produced leaflets into those different statutory categories or justify this sanction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection for the ordinary author's choice of anonymous publication.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: distinguishes campaign-finance reporting and its specific interests; those disclosure holdings remain undisturbed.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: distinguishes corporate-advertising source information; its observation does not resolve this private individual's leaflet.

### Fraud prevention does not justify this application to truthful independent advocacy

**Holding and operative rule:** Although preventing electoral fraud is a weighty interest, Ohio may not impose this categorical identification requirement on truthful, independently produced individual issue leaflets without the necessary narrow fit to that interest. The enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside; properly tailored fraud, impersonation, and materially different disclosure rules remain available.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ohio's fraud-prevention interest does not supply the missing fit. Talley protects anonymous expression without preventing laws directed at false or fraudulent communications. Ohio's identification requirement reaches ordinary truthful advocacy regardless of any finding of deception, false attribution, or corrupt financing. The record contains no adjudicated fraud in McIntyre's leaflet. The State argues that identification helps investigators find wrongdoers and deters unlawful claims before they affect an election. That practical benefit does not explain why every individual issue pamphlet must carry a name and address when existing laws address fraud and false attribution more directly. The personal-correspondence exception and permission to exempt impractical small items do not cure this application to printed leaflets; no implementing exemption rule resolves the fine presented. The Court neither assumes that anonymity guarantees honest speech nor invalidates all disclosure provisions. It sets aside this sanction, preserves properly tailored enforcement against actual abuse, and leaves organizational and financial-reporting requirements outside the judgment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies anonymous-publication protection while distinguishing measures directed at fraud.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: leaves candidate-finance disclosure and corruption-related interests intact rather than treating them as dispositive of this ordinary leaflet.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: leaves materially different corporate source-disclosure questions outside the individual sanction decided here.
