## Controlling holdings

### Separable legal review of the categorical evidence rule

**Controlling proposition:** An interlocutory qualified-immunity appeal may resolve whether the law permits a categorical direct-evidence condition without deciding disputed motive, participation or causation. Review of that separable legal condition does not authorize appellate selection between contested factual accounts.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer; all nine support this legal-review proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged evidence rule presents a question of law distinct from the truth of Kimberlin's allegations. Mitchell v. Forsyth permits interlocutory review of a legal entitlement to immunity; it does not supply a trial of disputed facts on appeal. Elder v. Holloway requires independent examination of relevant contemporaneous law while preserving the proper summary-judgment record. Digital Equipment preserves finality by demanding a genuinely separable legal question. The D.C. Circuit made the form of motive evidence dispositive. That prerequisite can be examined without deciding who ordered a detention, why an official acted, or which witness should be believed. The officials' entitlement to protection from unsupported litigation remains important, but it does not make their contested account the appellate record. The Court therefore reaches the categorical legal rule alone. It leaves actual motive, personal responsibility, causal sufficiency and each episode's independently available immunity defense for lawful consideration below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 — applied to separable legal immunity review, without authorizing appellate factfinding.
- Elder v. Holloway — applied to independent review of contemporaneous legal authority on the proper record; no ultimate immunity ruling follows.
- Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc. — applied to preserve the requirement of legal separability and finality.

### Circumstantial proof remains available in motive-dependent claims

**Controlling proposition:** Qualified immunity does not impose a categorical requirement of direct evidence of unconstitutional motive. A plaintiff must establish the actual constitutional elements through legally competent direct or circumstantial proof and satisfy ordinary procedural burdens; specific nonconclusory support, lawful discovery limits and the clearly-established-law defense remain available.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer; nine votes support the rule and its operative limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the form of evidence cannot alone disqualify proof of unconstitutional motive. Harlow v. Fitzgerald makes qualified immunity an objective legal defense; it does not delete a mental-state element of the underlying constitutional claim. Anderson v. Creighton preserves particularized notice and permits appropriately tailored inquiry. Siegert v. Gilley resolved the absence of a constitutional deprivation and did not adopt a direct-evidence prerequisite. Leatherman concerns municipal pleading and expressly reserves individual-immunity pleading; its source-based procedural reasoning is an analogy, not a decision of every heightened-screen question here. Rules 8, 9(b), 26 and 56 retain supported allegations, controlled discovery and ordinary evidentiary sufficiency. Holland explains why circumstantial form does not itself establish weakness. Protecting officials from intrusive unsupported claims therefore supplies no reason to demand an admission or eyewitness account. The Court rejects that categorical barrier and adopts no new clear-and-convincing burden. It neither finds retaliation nor orders trial or unlimited discovery; independently sustainable episode-specific judgments remain possible.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 — preserved as the objective clearly-established-law defense, distinguished from the underlying motive element.
- Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635 — applied to particularized notice and appropriately tailored discovery.
- Siegert v. Gilley, 500 U.S. 226 — distinguished; its constitutional-injury ruling established no direct-only proof rule.
- Leatherman v. Tarrant County, 507 U.S. 163 — used by analogy only; its individual-immunity reservation remains.
- Holland v. United States, 348 U.S. 121 — applied for the absence of a categorical inferiority of circumstantial evidence.
