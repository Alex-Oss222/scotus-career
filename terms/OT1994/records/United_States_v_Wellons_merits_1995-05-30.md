## Controlling holdings

### Vehicle privacy and the rental agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** Absence from a rental agreement does not by itself defeat a driver’s Fourth Amendment privacy claim when he possesses and controls the vehicle with the authorized renter’s permission and without theft, fraud or comparable wrongful possession. Courts must evaluate the actual legitimate relationship, including the right to exclude, the owner’s retained rights and any effective termination of possession; renter permission alone does not establish privacy.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined in this proposition by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; all nine directly adopt it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a private driver restriction is relevant but does not conclusively decide constitutional privacy. Rakas requires the defendant’s own legitimate interest; Rawlings requires attention to the actual circumstances rather than an automatic property formula. Olson shows that invitation and recognized use can support personal privacy without title. The inquiry here therefore considers the authorized renter’s permission, legitimate possession, practical control, ability to exclude and the owner’s retained rights. Padilla likewise requires individualized rights rather than a proxy claim. The rental company’s request for impoundment may bear on whether legitimate possession continued at search time; the Court does not disregard that fact or decide its legal effect without findings. The Government cannot replace this inquiry with the contract’s name list alone. Nor may a driver prevail merely by showing permission if theft, fraud, comparable wrongful possession or effective repossession defeats the claimed relationship. The remand determines privacy; it does not establish it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rakas v. Illinois, 439 U.S. 128: applies its requirement of the claimant’s own legitimate interest, rejecting proxy rights while refusing a contract-name shortcut.
- Rawlings v. Kentucky, 448 U.S. 98: applies the circumstance-specific privacy inquiry; possession or property alone is not conclusive.
- Minnesota v. Olson, 495 U.S. 91: extends its recognition that an invited user can have personal privacy without title to this bounded inquiry; it supplies no automatic rental-driver privacy.
- United States v. Padilla, May 3, 1993: applies the individualized search/seizure inquiry; no automatic derivative rights are created.

### Independent privacy in closed personal luggage

**Holding and operative rule:** A person’s lack of vehicle privacy does not by itself extinguish a legitimate expectation of privacy in that person’s closed luggage inside the vehicle. Ownership or lawful control and measures preserving privacy must be established independently, and a valid warrant exception reaching the container may still authorize its search.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined on this separate proposition by all eight Associate Justices; nine directly adopt it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the vehicle does not absorb every constitutional interest in an object placed inside it. Chadwick recognizes personal privacy in closed luggage as distinct from the characteristics of its surrounding vehicle. Rawlings requires the claimant to demonstrate his own interest, so a bare assertion about every bag cannot substitute for ownership, lawful control and circumstances preserving privacy. The Fourth Circuit’s categorical derivative rule eliminated this separate inquiry solely because Wellons was absent from the rental agreement. That ground cannot resolve a personal container claim. Acevedo nevertheless permits an automobile probable-cause search to reach containers within its lawful scope, and Sanders’s contrary automobile warrant restriction is not restored. Privacy establishes the personal interest whose invasion may be challenged, not the unlawfulness of every search. On remand the courts must determine each claimed container’s status, any disclaimer, and the actual search authority; the Court makes no ownership finding or container-specific probable-cause determination.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Chadwick, 433 U.S. 1: preserves personal closed-luggage privacy as distinct from vehicle mobility, subject to the later applicable automobile rule.
- Rawlings v. Kentucky, 448 U.S. 98: applies the requirement of a demonstrated personal privacy interest; ownership or lawful control and privacy measures require assessment.
- California v. Acevedo, 500 U.S. 565: preserves automobile probable-cause authority to search containers within its scope; personal container privacy does not impose a categorical warrant requirement.
- Arkansas v. Sanders, 442 U.S. 753: retains container privacy only consistently with Acevedo’s displacement of its contrary automobile warrant rule; that displaced rule is not revived.

### Remedy after rejection of the categorical grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** When the reviewed categorical privacy grounds are erroneous but personal interests and preserved search justifications remain unresolved, the judgment must be vacated and remanded for those determinations before a suppression consequence is imposed. A recognized interest does not foreclose lawful probable-cause search, consent, standardized noninvestigatory inventory after lawful custody, or another preserved warrant exception.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight Associate Justices; nine directly adopt this case’s remedial holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correcting the privacy rules does not establish the final suppression result. Acevedo permits searches supported by lawful automobile probable cause within their actual scope; Padilla separates the individual protected interest from relief requiring additional determinations. The rental company’s request is not itself a finding of lawful impoundment or consent to open every personal bag. The dog alert is an event in the record, not proof that the preceding detention and sniff were lawful or that probable cause extended without limit. Inventory authority requires lawful custody and standardized noninvestigatory procedures, and consent requires lawful consent within its scope. These questions were not resolved by the categorical ground supporting the conviction affirmance. The lower courts must address them on supported findings, together with luggage ownership and any applicable ordinary harmlessness. Vacatur and remand therefore correct the legal premise while preserving lawful admission grounds. The Court orders neither automatic exclusion nor resentencing.

**Precedent treatment:**

- California v. Acevedo, 500 U.S. 565: applies the distinction between an interest and a lawful probable-cause search reaching a container; no automatic suppression follows recognition of privacy.
- United States v. Padilla, May 3, 1993: applies its individual-interest remand discipline without deciding unproved exception predicates.

Privacy establishes the personal interest whose invasion may be challenged, not the unlawfulness of every search. On remand the courts must determine each claimed container’s status, any disclaimer, and the actual search authority; the Court makes no ownership finding or container-specific probable-cause determination.

### Remedy after rejection of the categorical grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** When the reviewed categorical privacy grounds are erroneous but personal interests and preserved search justifications remain unresolved, the judgment must be vacated and remanded for those determinations before a suppression consequence is imposed. A recognized interest does not foreclose lawful probable-cause search, consent, standardized noninvestigatory inventory after lawful custody, or another preserved warrant exception.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight Associate Justices; nine directly adopt this case’s remedial holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correcting the privacy rules does not establish the final suppression result. Acevedo permits searches supported by lawful automobile probable cause within their actual scope; Padilla separates the individual protected interest from relief requiring additional determinations. The rental company’s request is not itself a finding of lawful impoundment or consent to open every personal bag. The dog alert is an event in the record, not proof that the preceding detention and sniff were lawful or that probable cause extended without limit. Inventory authority requires lawful custody and standardized noninvestigatory procedures, and consent requires lawful consent within its scope. These questions were not resolved by the categorical ground supporting the conviction affirmance. The lower courts must address them on supported findings, together with luggage ownership and any applicable ordinary harmlessness. Vacatur and remand therefore correct the legal premise while preserving lawful admission grounds. The Court orders neither automatic exclusion nor resentencing.

- United States v. Padilla, May 3, 1993: applies its individual-interest remand discipline without deciding unproved exception predicates.

## Law After Decision

A rental agreement’s name list does not alone settle a driver’s personal Fourth Amendment interest. Permission, legitimate possession and practical control must be assessed, including the owner’s retained rights and any actual termination of possession. A driver lacking vehicle privacy may still have an independent interest in his own closed luggage. A recognized interest does not defeat a valid search exception. No thief, fraudulent possessor or person remaining after effective repossession obtains privacy merely through this decision; permission alone is insufficient when the full circumstances show no legitimate or socially recognized control.

## Procedure After Action

The judgment affirming the conviction is vacated and the case remanded. The lower courts shall determine Wellons’s legitimate possession and control, the renter’s permission, any theft, fraud, comparable wrongful possession or effective repossession, and his ownership or lawful control of the searched closed luggage. They shall determine lawful seizure/impoundment; legality and consequences of the detention and dog sniff; probable cause and its container scope; lawful consent and its scope; standardized noninvestigatory inventory authority; or another preserved warrant exception. Suppression follows only the supported findings and legal determinations, with ordinary harmlessness considered if applicable. No acquittal, automatic suppression, new exclusionary exception, or resentencing is ordered.
