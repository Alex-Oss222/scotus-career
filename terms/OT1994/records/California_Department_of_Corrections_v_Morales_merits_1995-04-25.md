Requiring support for the State’s own answer neither transfers ultimate constitutional proof nor demands proof that parole is impossible. The Court prescribes no universal filing procedure or response deadline and does not decide whether a demonstrated different mechanism would suffice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993: preserves meaningful opportunity loss and the absence of an actual-release prerequisite; this decision expressly clarifies substantiation of an asserted replacement.
- Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981): applies its refusal to assume discretionary alternatives compensate for guaranteed benefits; it did not itself prescribe this parole evidentiary direction.
- In re Jackson, 39 Cal.3d 464 (1985): confines review and conceivable advancement to what the opinion establishes; initial review does not become later reconsideration.

### Limits and questions not reached

The holding concerns Morales’s demonstrated meaningful loss under the challenged 1981 provision. It does not invalidate every individualized postponement, require an interim hearing in every possible individualized scheme, or constitutionalize every scheduling detail. Maximum deferral is permission, not a statutory command or a proved near-uniform cohort practice. The Court neither requires wholly different facts for the two decisions nor treats the initial forecast as unreviewable.

No universal probability threshold, general presumption transferring constitutional proof to the State, numerical response deadline, mandatory filing procedure, suitability finding, release date, damages award, or right to favorable parole is adopted. Adequacy of a demonstrated materially different arrangement, later amendments and their transition or mootness effects are not decided. The false-information and sentence claims are not reopened. Unresolved credit and matrix calculations establish neither release entitlement nor legal impossibility of earlier release.

## Precedent Treatment

Cavanaugh remains controlling and its opportunity rule extends to the meaningful withdrawal established here, notwithstanding the genuine individual-prediction constraints. Its reservation is resolved for this record, not erased for every individualized arrangement. Weaver’s protection of legally available opportunities and Miller’s focus on binding adverse operation remain intact; Collins excludes a general procedural-disadvantage rule. Jackson’s separate-reasons construction and initial review are recognized, but its federal characterization cannot override this Court’s intervening law. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Effective April 25, 1995, California may not impose the challenged three-year interval on Morales on this record. His offense-date annual-consideration relief remains operative, with lawful suitability standards and release discretion preserved. Cavanaugh is unchanged within its original scope; this decision adds its application to the reserved individualized setting established here and the express claimant/substitute substantiation direction. It rejects an automatic rule against every frequency reduction and adopts neither the concurrence’s complete enforceable-substitute formulation for other systems nor the dissent’s view that the prediction suffices here.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela fully joins the Court’s opinion and the opportunity-protecting position joined by Justice Stevens. He adds that when a State removes a legally guaranteed meaningful avenue to earlier release, a replacement must preserve the opportunity in substance. It must be legally available, practically accessible, timely enough to permit consideration before the postponed hearing while earlier release remains legally possible, and enforceable as a usable protection. Discretionary authority to advance a hearing does not, by itself, establish an adequate substitute. Weaver’s treatment of discretionary credits supports distinguishing an enforceable opportunity from officials’ abstract power to act. Lindsey and Miller explain why a possibly identical punishment under former law does not defeat the claim; Cavanaugh supplies the direct opportunity rule.

The prisoner must identify the former entitlement, its retrospective withdrawal and its concrete link to possible earlier release. The State must then substantiate any replacement it relies on, including legal basis, practical availability and timely capacity; theoretical authority requires evidence where operation matters. Uncertainty about that answer cannot justify denying otherwise established relief. Ultimate constitutional proof remains with the prisoner. He accepts the annual default, narrow class, permissive three-year maximum, separate findings, lawful factual overlap and initial review, but finds they do not preserve later rehabilitation, changed circumstances or correction of a materially mistaken forecast. A different arrangement may suffice if it actually preserves the opportunity through an adequate enforceable substitute; no hypothetical system, universal filing procedure or numerical deadline is approved. His full joinder remains intact; the broader formulation has no additional joins and is noncontrolling.

Justice Kennedy dissents, joined throughout by Justices Scalia, Thomas, and Breyer. They would reverse the hearing-frequency judgment and remand consistently with rejecting the claim. They accept Cavanaugh’s binding opportunity protection and absence of an actual-parole proof requirement, but give weight to its individualized-postponement reservation. California retains annual consideration unless a hearing produces a separate, reviewable prediction directed to the intervening years. Permission is restricted to multiple life-taking convictions, requires stated bases, permits a shorter interval and is capped at three years. Jackson requires separately directed reasons while allowing underlying facts to overlap suitability reasons. Morales’s continued-risk findings supply an additional legal decision absent from automatic deletion of alternate hearings.

They acknowledge that forecasts can become outdated and initial review is not later reconsideration. Their sufficient distinction relies on neither unproved ordinary reopening, a binding nineteen-year minimum, aggregate probabilities nor legal impossibility of earlier release. Collins’s punishment boundary and Cavanaugh’s reserved category support their conclusion; a procedural label alone would not suffice. They accept substantiation of an asserted substitute without making equivalent interim review indispensable to every justified delay. Kennedy accepts a bounded method of testing such assertions without joining the Court’s application; Scalia, Thomas and Breyer do not join an independently reusable enumerated allocation rule. Defective, perfunctory, categorical or materially different punitive implementation remains open. They grant no parole and leave rejected claims and later enactments untouched. Their distinction has four votes and does not control.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit’s judgment is affirmed on the reviewed hearing-frequency claim. Its remand requiring offense-date parole-consideration frequency remains in force. The Board must provide annual consideration under that rule, but remains free to deny parole under lawful suitability standards. No particular hearing or release date, favorable suitability finding, damages award, or reopening of the rejected false-information and sentence claims is ordered. This Supreme Court review is complete; no new hold, operational-fact remand, hearing-status report or retained return condition is imposed.
