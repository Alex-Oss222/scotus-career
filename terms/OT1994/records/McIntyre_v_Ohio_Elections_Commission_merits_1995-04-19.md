### The asserted voter-information interest does not justify this identification demand

**Holding and operative rule:** A general interest in helping voters evaluate a source does not justify requiring this ordinary individual to place her name and residence or business address on her independently produced, truthful political leaflet. The Court leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending, and other materially distinct disclosure settings for separate analysis.

### Fraud prevention does not justify this application to truthful independent advocacy

**Holding and operative rule:** Although preventing electoral fraud is a weighty interest, Ohio may not impose this categorical identification requirement on truthful, independently produced individual issue leaflets without the necessary narrow fit to that interest. The enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside; properly tailored fraud, impersonation, and materially different disclosure rules remain available.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ohio's fraud-prevention interest does not supply the missing fit. Ohio's identification requirement reaches ordinary truthful advocacy regardless of any finding of deception, false attribution, or corrupt financing. The record contains no adjudicated fraud in McIntyre's leaflet. The State argues that identification helps investigators find wrongdoers and deters unlawful claims before they affect an election. That practical benefit does not explain why every individual issue pamphlet must carry a name and address when existing laws address fraud and false attribution more directly. The personal-correspondence exception and permission to exempt impractical small items do not cure this application to printed leaflets; no implementing exemption rule resolves the fine presented. The Court neither assumes that anonymity guarantees honest speech nor invalidates all disclosure provisions. It sets aside this sanction, preserves properly tailored enforcement against actual abuse, and leaves organizational and financial-reporting requirements outside the judgment.

## Separate Writings

Justice Thomas concurs in the judgment. Talley's protection and the historical expressive tradition it recognizes support an individual's anonymous political pamphlet. The supplied justification cannot sustain a penalty imposed solely for missing identification on this ordinary leaflet. He does not adopt the Court's modern value-balancing and content-based-scrutiny methodology as his own ground. His agreement in this judgment therefore adds no vote to the Court's complete scrutiny explanation, and his writing does not invalidate every financing or organizational disclosure rule.

Justice Scalia dissents. Election-specific attribution is a limited accountability requirement that helps voters identify a communication's source and assists enforcement against electoral wrongdoing. Buckley's disclosure principles and Bellotti's source-information discussion support that safeguard in his view. Talley's general prohibition covering all anonymous handbills is distinguishable from Ohio's election category. He recognizes the expressive burden but regards the State's justification as sufficient here; he would sustain the fine. He preserves appropriate protection where a speaker establishes threats or reprisals, without finding that McIntyre experienced either. He does not endorse a general power to forbid anonymous discussion outside the election setting.

## Procedure After Action

The Supreme Court of Ohio's judgment is reversed. Further proceedings must set aside the enforcement order and $100 fine against Margaret McIntyre, whose estate's executor maintains the challenge. Ohio may enforce valid disclosure rules consistent with the holding. The Court orders no damages, replacement election procedure, blanket statutory repeal, or retained Supreme Court proceeding.
