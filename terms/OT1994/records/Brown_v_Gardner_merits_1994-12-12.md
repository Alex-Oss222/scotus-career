## Controlling holdings

### Section 1151 requires causation, not provider fault or accident

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1151 requires an injury or aggravation resulting from covered VA hospitalization, medical or surgical treatment, chapter 31 vocational rehabilitation, or examination, producing additional disability or death and not resulting from the veteran's own willful misconduct; it does not additionally require negligence, other provider fault, or an unforeseen untoward accident. Section 3.358(c)(3)'s added fault-or-accident prerequisite is invalid, and Gardner's claim must be redetermined under the surviving statutory conditions without an automatic benefits award.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes on the causal rule and invalidity of the additional prerequisite. Stone-Zsela also concurs separately on the rule's limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that causal connection and fault are different requirements. Section 1151 speaks in terms of injury or aggravation resulting from the listed services and expressly excludes the veteran's own willful misconduct. Congress did not impose the regulation's additional demand for deficient care or an unforeseen accident. King supplies contextual interpretation that respects enacted limits, with protective construction as confirmation. Chevron requires the agency first to respect Congress's answer, and MCI confirms that administrative expertise cannot enlarge a statutory delegation. The Secretary's reliance on duration of the regulation, costs, hearing examples, reenactment, and legislative silence does not overcome that text. Removing fault does not make every later disability compensable: actual causation and the other statutory conditions remain. The existing natural-progress provision is undisturbed, and a necessary consequence of consented treatment is not automatically compensable. The Court neither finds that surgery caused Gardner's claimed disability nor resolves a comprehensive consent or necessary-consequences doctrine. The existing administrative redetermination must proceed under the lawful standard.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984): applies the statutory-meaning inquiry before deference; the clear causal standard leaves no authority to add provider fault, without abolishing deference in genuine interpretive gaps.
- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies contextual reading of protective veterans legislation and respect for express rather than invented limits; the veterans-protective canon confirms the text instead of replacing it.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., 512 U.S. 218 (1994): applies the limit that administrative expertise cannot supply statutory power Congress did not grant; the distinct tariff-modification rule is not extended into a general rejection of agency discretion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The natural-progress provision is left undisturbed. No complete rule governing consent or necessary consequences is adopted. The separate FTCA coordination sentence remains: for a qualifying judgment under 28 U.S.C. §1346(b), or settlement or compromise under §§2672 or 2677, on or after December 1, 1962, for a disability, aggravation, or death treated as service connected under §1151, benefits for months after finality are withheld until the aggregate otherwise payable equals the total recovery. That is a coordination rule, not a provider-fault requirement; no qualifying recovery is found here.
