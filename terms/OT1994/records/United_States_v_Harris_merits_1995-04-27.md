#### Case-specific reasoning

This is the first major test of whether Lopez means federal criminal statutes now automatically fail whenever conduct happens locally — Stone says no. The particular vehicle is an interstate transportation instrumentality/thing in commerce, and the offense directly attacks possession and control of that vehicle; the statute requires a vehicle-specific interstate connection, which is constitutionally different from attaching a remote historical interstate fact to an unrelated local crime.

#### Disposition, application and preserved limits

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** AFFIRM the Fifth Circuit’s judgment.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The convictions and consecutive sentences remain in force. No separate remedial instruction is required.

#### Issue 1: Commerce Clause authority

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional question and affirms on a rationale narrower than the Fifth Circuit’s aggregate-effects theory.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning that carjacking is federal because violent crime affects travel, insurance, or the national economy cannot survive Lopez. Section 2119 is constitutional here for a different reason. Congress may directly protect an instrumentality of interstate commerce from an intrastate attack, and the enacted offense requires the Government in every prosecution to establish that the particular motor vehicle was transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce. The prohibited conduct directly attacks possession and control of the vehicle itself. The instrumentality and the offense-specific interstate element therefore perform actual constitutional work rather than merely adding a remote interstate fact to ordinary local crime.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The Commerce Clause permits Congress to protect an instrumentality of interstate commerce against a direct intrastate forcible taking when the federal offense requires proof of the particular instrumentality’s interstate connection. Section 2119 is constitutional as applied to the forcible taking of the Buick proved here. The judgment is affirmed without adopting the Fifth Circuit’s aggregate-effects rationale.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** An automobile is an instrumentality of transportation capable of carrying persons and property through interstate channels. The defendants did not merely commit an offense in which a car appeared incidentally; they forcibly seized possession and control of the vehicle itself. The statute required proof of the Buick’s interstate history, and the record established that it was manufactured in Michigan and titled in Arkansas and Mississippi. That combination places this prosecution within Lopez’s instrumentality category without reclassifying violent crime as economic activity.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not hold that every local offense involving an object that once crossed a state line is federal, that every crime committed in or near a car is federal, or that insurance and productivity effects supply federal authority. Past interstate movement alone establishes no universal jurisdictional rule. The holding rests on the direct attack on a transportation instrumentality and the offense-specific interstate element enacted in § 2119.

#### Issue 2: Mens rea and notice

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory and due-process arguments because they bear on the convictions.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 2119 requires knowing participation in the forcible taking while the firearm element and interstate-vehicle element are proved as enacted. It does not require knowledge of federal jurisdiction or an intent permanently to deprive. The conduct is inherently wrongful, and the statute gives fair notice that forcibly taking a covered motor vehicle with a firearm is criminal.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The then-operative carjacking statute requires knowing participation in the taking by force, violence, or intimidation and proof of the enacted firearm and interstate-vehicle facts; it does not require knowledge that the vehicle traveled interstate or a separate intent permanently to deprive.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Ratzlaf’s willfulness construction is tied to separate statutory language absent here. Staples protects against criminalizing apparently innocent possession based on an unknown physical characteristic. Pointing a shotgun at occupants and ordering them from a vehicle is not innocent conduct whose criminality turns on an obscure characteristic. ## Controlling holdings

### The vehicle-taking economic class supplies the commerce connection

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may apply original §2119 to the armed forcible acquisition of the vehicle proved here as regulation of the economic vehicle-taking class within the interstate motor-vehicle theft-prevention scheme. The statute's separately enacted requirement that the particular vehicle have been transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce must be proved; neither remote movement alone nor generalized crime, insurance, or productivity costs independently supplies this holding's constitutional ground.

**Authority:** Justice O’Connor's opinion of the Court, joined on this ground by Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: eight direct votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment on a distinct instrumentality ground and does not join the economic-class rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the regulated taking has a commercial connection missing from the stand-alone possession offense in Lopez. Congress addresses forcible acquisition of the vehicle itself, a form of theft within the interstate vehicle market. Wickard and Perez permit regulation of an economic class without requiring each instance to produce a substantial interstate effect. Lopez preserves that authority while rejecting a chain through general crime costs, education, and productivity. The defendants' asserted retaliatory motive does not change the enacted acquisition class or create a profit-intent exception. The Buick's Michigan manufacture and Arkansas and Mississippi titling satisfy the existing vehicle-history condition; the Court inserts no new element and finds no intended interstate resale. Allied-Bruce cautions against treating remote object history as constitutionally conclusive. That caution is respected because the taking class, rather than manufacture alone, supplies the selected connection. The holding neither federalizes every property crime nor decides an independent instrumentality theory. Both the carjacking predicate and the dependent firearm count survive this commerce challenge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: applies its retained economic-class boundary and distinguishes its inadequate stand-alone possession/downstream-effects chain; no missing element is supplied.
- Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942): applies aggregation within a regulated economic class rather than demanding a substantial effect from each individual taking.
- Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146 (1971): applies Congress's authority over the economic class despite an individual instance's local character.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: preserves its distinction between actual commercial operation and remote object history; prior manufacture alone is not the constitutional ground here.

### Knowing participation and intentional assistance give the original offense definite content

**Controlling proposition:** Original §2119 requires knowing armed participation in taking or attempting to take the covered vehicle from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation; aiding-and-abetting liability requires intentional assistance in that taking, not mere presence, association, or awareness of another's plan. Read with that ordinary criminal fault, the statute gives fair notice of the charged conduct and defeats the presented lack-of-fault and vagueness challenges without adding a profit motive, interstate-resale intention, knowledge of federal law, or the later intent-to-kill-or-seriously-injure element.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer, directly adopts the complete construction and its bounded application. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the knowing-participation and fair-notice propositions and rejection of a general legal-knowledge requirement; no additional Stone join is attributed to the separately stated intentional-assistance formulation. His separate conclusions concerning knowledge of the vehicle's interstate history and permanent deprivation have no additional joins and are not holdings of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory silence does not make this serious forcible-taking offense a crime of unwitting involvement. Staples preserves ordinary factual culpability, and X-Citement Video requires attention to the whole statutory setting rather than automatic strict liability. The taking and force terms identify the wrongful conduct; participation in that taking must be knowing, and assistance must be intentional. Shabani's rejection of guilt by association supplies a consistent boundary, without converting aiding and abetting into conspiracy. Ratzlaf's known-illegality rule rested on distinct willfulness language and does not add a universal legal-knowledge element here. A claimed retaliatory motive does not negate participation in the taking as a matter of law. The lower record treats Harris's claimed ignorance as a defense, not an accepted finding. No supplied original charge or preservation record establishes an omitted-fault instruction. **Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the clear cumulative direction resolves the double-jeopardy objection under Hunter. ## Event

United States v. Harris, No. 94-297. Merits decision, April 27, 1995. On review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit's June 29, 1994 judgment in United States v. Harris, 25 F.3d 1275, No. 93-7554. The questions concern Congress's commerce authority to enact the original armed-carjacking offense, its criminal-fault and fair-notice requirements, and cumulative convictions and consecutive punishment under §§2119 and 924(c). The defendants seek relief from their convictions and sentences; the Government seeks affirmance.

Render form: full. The unanimous judgment rests on different commerce grounds, and the fault and notice discussion has proposition-specific partial joins.

The offense occurred December 12, 1992. The borrowed 1976 Buick Electra was manufactured in Flint, Michigan and titled in Arkansas and Mississippi. The trial evidence recounted by the Fifth Circuit placed Harris with a sawed-off shotgun and Hoskins with an ax handle at the confrontation: the occupants were ordered out, Hoskins drove away, and Harris pursued the occupants. The defendants' retaliation/afterthought account and Harris's claimed ignorance were defenses, not accepted contrary findings. Each defendant was convicted of aiding and abetting carjacking and of using or carrying a firearm during a crime of violence, receiving forty-one months on Count One and sixty months on Count Two, consecutive.

The December 12, 1992 offense is governed by original §2119. A person possessing a firearm as defined in §921 who takes or attempts to take a motor vehicle previously transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation is subject to a fine, imprisonment up to fifteen years, or both. Resulting serious bodily injury, as defined in §1365, raises the maximum to twenty-five years; resulting death permits imprisonment for any number of years up to life, with the fine-or-both alternatives retained. Neither enhanced tier is decided. The later intent-to-cause-death-or-serious-injury language is not part of this offense. The enactment's encouragement of federal/state cooperation and appropriate prosecution consistent with prosecutorial discretion is neither an additional element nor an automatic constitutional limit.

Offense-date §924(c)(1) requires use or carrying of a firearm during and in relation to a crime of violence or drug-trafficking crime for which the person may be prosecuted in federal court. Its inclusive parenthetical reaches predicates with their own deadly-weapon enhancements. The ordinary term is five years in addition to the predicate punishment, consecutive to every other imprisonment term, expressly including the predicate term. Probation and suspended sentence are barred, and the then-effective provision denies parole during its term. A crime of violence under §924(c)(3) is a felony having as an element use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against another's person or property, or by its nature involving a substantial risk that such force may be used in committing it. No later amendment of that definition is adjudicated.

The then-effective separate firearm tiers prescribed ten years for a short-barreled rifle or shotgun; thirty years for a machinegun, destructive device, or firearm equipped with a silencer or muffler; twenty years for a second or subsequent conviction; and life without release for such a repeat conviction involving the listed machinegun, destructive-device, or silencer/muffler category. A sawed-off description in this record establishes neither statutory barrel length nor a basis for increasing the reviewed sentence. The later semiautomatic-assault-weapon tier and later deletion of the parole sentence are not applied to the 1992 conduct.

## Holdings

### The vehicle-taking economic class supplies the commerce connection

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may apply original §2119 to the armed forcible acquisition of the vehicle proved here as regulation of the economic vehicle-taking class within the interstate motor-vehicle theft-prevention scheme. The statute's separately enacted requirement that the particular vehicle have been transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce must be proved; neither remote movement alone nor generalized crime, insurance, or productivity costs independently supplies this holding's constitutional ground.

**Authority:** Justice O’Connor's opinion of the Court, joined on this ground by Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: eight direct votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment on a distinct instrumentality ground and does not join the economic-class rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the regulated taking has a commercial connection missing from the stand-alone possession offense in Lopez. Congress addresses forcible acquisition of the vehicle itself, a form of theft within the interstate vehicle market. Wickard and Perez permit regulation of an economic class without requiring each instance to produce a substantial interstate effect. Lopez preserves that authority while rejecting a chain through general crime costs, education, and productivity. The defendants' asserted retaliatory motive does not change the enacted acquisition class or create a profit-intent exception. The Buick's Michigan manufacture and Arkansas and Mississippi titling satisfy the existing vehicle-history condition; the Court inserts no new element and finds no intended interstate resale. Allied-Bruce cautions against treating remote object history as constitutionally conclusive. That caution is respected because the taking class, rather than manufacture alone, supplies the selected connection. The holding neither federalizes every property crime nor decides an independent instrumentality theory. Both the carjacking predicate and the dependent firearm count survive this commerce challenge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: applies its retained economic-class boundary and distinguishes its inadequate stand-alone possession/downstream-effects chain; no missing element is supplied.
- Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942): applies aggregation within a regulated economic class rather than demanding a substantial effect from each individual taking.
- Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146 (1971): applies Congress's authority over the economic class despite an individual instance's local character.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: preserves its distinction between actual commercial operation and remote object history; prior manufacture alone is not the constitutional ground here.

### Knowing participation and intentional assistance give the original offense definite content

**Controlling proposition:** Original §2119 requires knowing armed participation in taking or attempting to take the covered vehicle from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation; aiding-and-abetting liability requires intentional assistance in that taking, not mere presence, association, or awareness of another's plan. Read with that ordinary criminal fault, the statute gives fair notice of the charged conduct and defeats the presented lack-of-fault and vagueness challenges without adding a profit motive, interstate-resale intention, knowledge of federal law, or the later intent-to-kill-or-seriously-injure element.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer, directly adopts the complete construction and its bounded application. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the knowing-participation and fair-notice propositions and rejection of a general legal-knowledge requirement; no additional Stone join is attributed to the separately stated intentional-assistance formulation. His separate conclusions concerning knowledge of the vehicle's interstate history and permanent deprivation have no additional joins and are not holdings of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory silence does not make this serious forcible-taking offense a crime of unwitting involvement. Staples preserves ordinary factual culpability, and X-Citement Video requires attention to the whole statutory setting rather than automatic strict liability. The taking and force terms identify the wrongful conduct; participation in that taking must be knowing, and assistance must be intentional. Shabani's rejection of guilt by association supplies a consistent boundary, without converting aiding and abetting into conspiracy. Ratzlaf's known-illegality rule rested on distinct willfulness language and does not add a universal legal-knowledge element here. A claimed retaliatory motive does not negate participation in the taking as a matter of law. The lower record treats Harris's claimed ignorance as a defense, not an accepted finding. No supplied original charge or preservation record establishes an omitted-fault instruction. The Court rejects the presented statute and notice challenge without approving unknown instructions, finding harmless error, or ordering a new trial. Knowledge of interstate history and deprivation duration receive no majority resolution.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Staples v. United States, May 23, 1994: applies contextual factual culpability despite silence while retaining its distinct regulated-characteristic holding.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: applies contextual criminal-fault construction without transplanting its depiction elements or deciding every jurisdictional-knowledge question.
- United States v. Shabani, November 1, 1994: preserves the distinction between knowing participation and mere association; its conspiracy holding does not itself define this separate offense.
- Ratzlaf v. United States, January 11, 1994: distinguishes its specific willfulness and known-illegality language rather than converting it into a universal ignorance-of-law defense.
- Lopez: preserves its refusal to insert an absent commerce element; that limit does not eliminate ordinary criminal-fault construction of the enacted taking offense.

### Section 924(c) expressly adds a consecutive term for this armed carjacking

**Controlling proposition:** The offense-date §924(c) authorizes its additional, consecutive punishment for the original §2119 carjacking predicate even though firearm possession is integral to that predicate's definition. The Government must still establish firearm use or carrying during and in relation to a qualifying federally prosecutable predicate; this cumulative-authorization holding does not equate possession with every §924(c) condition or determine universal element overlap.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined on this statutory holding by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes. The holding sustains the reviewed forty-one-month carjacking terms and additional sixty-month firearm terms, not an unreviewed weapon enhancement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted punishment directions encompass this pairing. Section 924(c) reaches any qualifying federal crime of violence, includes predicates with their own deadly-weapon enhancement, adds punishment to the predicate, and expressly forbids concurrent service. The defendants distinguish an offense defined by firearm possession from an otherwise complete offense carrying an enhancement. But the parenthetical's inclusion is nonexclusive; it does not narrow the general command to the examples it names. Original §2119's later enactment and silence therefore supply no exception for armed carjacking. Ordinary construction resolves this question before residual lenity, which Ratzlaf preserves for genuine remaining ambiguity. Smith keeps firearm use or carrying and relation to the predicate distinct; the Court does not deem incidental possession sufficient. Deal addresses a different question about additional convictions in one prosecution and does not independently decide this statutory pair. The existing five-year firearm terms stand against the presented challenge. No different weapon classification, higher sentence, merger, or concurrent conversion is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ratzlaf: preserves residual lenity after ordinary construction; the inclusive, additional, and consecutive directions leave no residual ambiguity for this pairing.
- Smith v. United States, June 1, 1993: preserves the separate firearm-use and relation requirements; a predicate's possession element does not automatically establish all §924(c) elements.
- Deal v. United States, May 17, 1993: distinguishes its conviction-sequence question, which does not itself decide cumulative punishment under these two statutes.

### Hunter permits the clearly authorized punishment in this single prosecution

**Controlling proposition:** Applying Missouri v. Hunter, the clearly authorized §2119 and §924(c) convictions and consecutive punishments imposed in this single prosecution do not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause even assuming overlap under Blockburger. This holding applies the governing same-trial precedent to the clear statutory direction and neither decides universal element overlap nor changes the rules for successive prosecutions.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this bounded application. Stevens joins the application of controlling Hunter while preserving his earlier objection to legislative multiplication of convictions; his reservation does not subtract a vote from this limited holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the clear cumulative direction resolves the double-jeopardy objection under Hunter. Blockburger supplies a method for discerning whether two offenses carry distinct legislative punishment, and Whalen applies that construction principle when contrary authorization is absent. Hunter establishes the governing consequence when the legislature instead clearly authorizes both punishments at one trial. Section 924(c)'s additional and consecutive commands provide that authorization here. The Court therefore need not decide whether every possible original carjacking violation satisfies every condition of the firearm offense. The defendants' overlap argument cannot displace the clear direction in this same-trial setting. Dixon preserves Grady's distinct successive-prosecution rule; neither a common episode nor the present judgment silently changes it. This decision makes no independent claim that multiple convictions lack stigma or collateral consequences and does not endorse every possible multiplication of charges. It applies the existing precedent to this pairing. Both convictions and their supplied consecutive terms remain in force without resentencing.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Missouri v. Hunter, 459 U.S. 359 (1983): applies its governing same-trial rule to the clear cumulative authorization without reconsidering its constitutional foundation.
- Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932): applies its construction role rather than treating possible overlap as an absolute bar to this expressly authorized same-trial punishment.
- Whalen v. United States, 445 U.S. 684 (1980): preserves the presumption against cumulative punishment absent contrary legislative intent and distinguishes the clear direction here.
- United States v. Dixon, June 28, 1993: preserves its successive-prosecution holdings, including the continued operation of Grady.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 (1990): remains governing law for its essential-conduct successive-prosecution setting; this single prosecution does not invoke or displace that rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides no independent instruction, indictment, preservation, harmlessness, sufficiency, or intradistrict-venue question. It determines neither knowledge of the vehicle's interstate history nor an independent permanent-deprivation requirement by a majority. No later carjacking intent amendment, later firearm tier, new short-barrel classification, serious-injury or death enhancement, or different charging pair is adjudicated.
