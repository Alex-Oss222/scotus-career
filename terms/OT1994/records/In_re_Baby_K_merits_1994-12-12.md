## Controlling holdings

#### The acute respiratory emergency triggers stabilization or lawful transfer

**Holding and operative rule:** When a participating hospital determines that an individual has an emergency respiratory condition, §1395dd(b)(1) requires the examination and treatment within its available staff and facilities necessary to prevent likely material deterioration, within reasonable medical probability, resulting from or during transfer, or transfer complying with subsection (c). Inability to cure the patient's underlying anencephaly, an ethical or medical objection to continued life support, uniform withholding from comparable anencephalic patients, and the hospital's lack of an intention to transfer do not excuse this duty where the acute emergency and effective stabilizing capability are established.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 9 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the immediate respiratory emergency is the condition to be stabilized. Section 1395dd(e)(1)(A) addresses acute symptoms sufficiently severe that absence of immediate attention could reasonably cause serious jeopardy to health, serious impairment of bodily functions, or serious dysfunction of an organ or part. The hospital concedes that Baby K's episodes meet that standard and that ventilation prevents deterioration. Incurable anencephaly does not negate those concessions. King requires reading the whole statute without adding an unexpressed restriction; Suter keeps the enacted duty distinct from an inferred remedy. Uniform screening under subsection (a) therefore cannot replace the separate stabilization obligation after an emergency is found. Nor may the hospital erase that obligation by retaining the patient without intending transfer. Ethical objection does not establish that otherwise available staff or facilities are unavailable. The holding concerns these acute presentations, not all inpatient, nonemergency or end-of-life care, and creates no entitlement to every preferred treatment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies contextual reading and respect for expressed statutory coverage and limits; its military-leave rule does not decide a medical question.
- Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992): preserves separate inquiries into enacted duty, cause of action and remedy; its rejection of particular child-welfare claims supplies no categorical exemption from express hospital duties.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Screening under subsection (a) is requested examination of an individual coming to the emergency department within that department's capability, including routinely available ancillary services. The diagnosed-emergency duty under subsection (b) is distinct. Transfer includes hospital-directed movement and discharge, but excludes movement after declaration of death or departure without hospital-affiliated permission. The separate labor provisions are not decided; Congress remains free to enact a defined futility exception.

#### Unstabilized transfer remains a fully conditional alternative

**Holding and operative rule:** An unstabilized transfer requires either a written request by the individual or legally responsible person after explanation of the hospital's obligations and transfer risks, or a physician-signed certification, based on then-available information, that expected medical benefits at the other facility outweigh increased transfer risks; if no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a qualified medical person may sign only after consulting a physician who makes that determination and later countersigns, and every certification must summarize risks and benefits. Every route also requires available treatment minimizing risk; receiving space, qualified personnel and acceptance of appropriate care; transmission of all available emergency-related records, the request or certification, and the name and address of a required on-call physician who refused or failed timely to appear; qualified transportation personnel and equipment with necessary appropriate life support; and the Secretary's applicable additional health-and-safety requirements.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 9 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress provided a lawful transfer route and specified what makes it lawful. The treatment-or-transfer structure cannot be restated as treatment at this hospital under all future circumstances. King supports respecting those expressed alternatives rather than constructing an absolute obligation from the statute's protective purpose. The present record separately lacks a benefits-over-risks certification and a qualified receiving facility that has agreed to accept Baby K. The mother's opposition is not a substitute for either missing condition. Conversely, opposition alone does not defeat a future transfer supported by the statutory physician certification and every appropriate-transfer safeguard. Available records include the emergency observations, preliminary diagnosis, treatment and test results; the hospital must send what is available rather than invent missing records. The alternative is permission for a compliant transfer, not a command to transfer or a finding that future space, personnel and acceptance exist. The prospective declaration remains confined by those statutory terms.

**Precedent treatment:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): preserves the force of express statutory alternatives and conditions; protective purpose does not erase an enacted transfer route.

#### Refusal compliance requires an actual offer and informed refusal

**Holding and operative rule:** Subsection (b)(2) deems the treatment requirement met when the hospital offers the required examination and treatment, explains its risks and benefits, the individual or representative refuses, and the hospital takes all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Subsection (b)(3) separately deems compliance when the hospital offers a transfer satisfying subsection (c), explains its risks and benefits, the individual or representative refuses, and the hospital takes all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal; the mother's opposition alone establishes neither route here.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 8 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that refusal provisions are conditional compliance routes, not exceptions triggered by disagreement alone. A treatment offer and a transfer offer perform different statutory work. The latter must itself satisfy subsection (c), including appropriate receiving capability and acceptance, before refusal can have the effect stated in subsection (b)(3). Here the record supplies neither a compliant transfer offer nor a finding that the mother refused the stabilizing treatment the hospital must offer. Her opposition to the requested declaration therefore does not release the hospital from its emergency obligation. King supports reading the alternatives within the coordinated enactment, and Suter cautions against confusing a defined duty with an assumed remedial result. The duty to take all reasonable steps to secure writing does not become an assertion that written refusal already exists. The Court preserves either route if its actual predicates arise, while making no general decision about parental or guardian authority outside this statutory controversy.

**Precedent treatment:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies the coordinated statutory structure, including the separate conditions attached to separate alternatives.
- Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992): preserves the distinction between a substantive duty and a claimed consequence; no broader family-authority or remedial rule is inferred.

#### The receiving-hospital route depends on appropriate transfer, required specialized capability and capacity

**Holding and operative rule:** If a nursing-home movement is an appropriate transfer requiring the participating hospital's specialized capabilities or facilities, §1395dd(g) prohibits the hospital from refusing acceptance when it has capacity to treat the individual. The Court does not settle every movement's classification or presume future capacity; the conditional receiving-hospital route preserves those requirements independently of the ordinary arrival-and-diagnosed-emergency route.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 8 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the uncertainty about the description of a nursing-home movement does not justify deleting the receiving-hospital provision. Subsection (g) addresses an appropriate transfer requiring specialized capabilities and expressly conditions the duty on receiving capacity. The record identifies pediatric intensive-care and ventilation capabilities, but that evidence does not establish every future transfer's appropriateness or the hospital's capacity whenever one may be proposed. King requires attention to the distinct function of each part of the enactment. The hospital's broader reading would allow the unresolved label to defeat either statutory route without examining its conditions; an unconditional acceptance rule would make the opposite error by erasing capacity. The declaration instead applies the route whose actual predicates are met. The Court neither finds that every nursing-home movement is a new emergency presentation nor decides that every such movement is a transfer requiring acceptance. Those factual predicates remain necessary when the prospective duty is applied.

**Precedent treatment:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies reading of related provisions together while retaining each provision's expressed conditions.

#### State medical law yields only to an applicable direct conflict

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §1395dd(f), a state or local requirement remains effective except to the extent it directly conflicts with an applicable EMTALA requirement; state law cannot authorize withholding the stabilizing treatment required here. Virginia §54.1-2990, a limitation within an article concerning adult directives and surrogates, does not itself establish an infant-care exemption, and the Court invalidates no wider field of state medical law.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 9 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that federal displacement extends only as far as the conflict Congress identified. The hospital invokes Virginia's provision stating that its Health Care Decisions Act does not require treatment a physician considers medically or ethically inappropriate. The article's described coverage concerns adult directives and surrogate decisions, so its language does not itself establish authority governing this infant. Even if an applicable state requirement permitted withholding the stabilizing care required by EMTALA, subsection (f) resolves that direct conflict in favor of the federal duty. King supports reading this limiting clause with the enacted emergency obligation rather than replacing either with a general medical-policy judgment. The hospital remains governed by compatible state medical law, including questions this case does not decide. The Court does not derive a general federal right to demand any treatment, adjudicate parental or guardian power, or decide the district court's independent disability-law grounds. The declaration is affirmed at the scope of the federal emergency duty.

**Precedent treatment:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies the whole-enactment approach; the limited preemption clause and affirmative duty both retain effect.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the uncertainty about the description of a nursing-home movement does not justify deleting the receiving-hospital provision. The record identifies pediatric intensive-care and ventilation capabilities, but that evidence does not establish every future transfer's appropriateness or the hospital's capacity whenever one may be proposed. #### State medical law yields only to an applicable direct conflict

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §1395dd(f), a state or local requirement remains effective except to the extent it directly conflicts with an applicable EMTALA requirement; state law cannot authorize withholding the stabilizing treatment required here. Virginia §54.1-2990, a limitation within an article concerning adult directives and surrogates, does not itself establish an infant-care exemption, and the Court invalidates no wider field of state medical law.

## Law After Decision

Effective December 12, 1994, EMTALA's determined-emergency duty applies to these acute respiratory episodes despite incurable anencephaly and broader futility objections. Screening, treatment, appropriate transfer, informed refusal, receiving capacity and direct-conflict preemption remain separate statutory rules. The Court adds this bounded stabilization application; it does not create a federal malpractice code, an indefinite post-stabilization intensive-care duty or a general right to preferred treatment.
