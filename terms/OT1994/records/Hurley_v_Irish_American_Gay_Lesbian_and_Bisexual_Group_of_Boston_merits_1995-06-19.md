## Controlling holdings

### Private parade composition and compelled inclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices adopt the as-applied private-expression holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the right to speak includes control over the composition of a private expressive presentation. Barnette prohibits compulsory affirmation, and Tornillo protects editorial choice against compelled inclusion. Turner preserves the importance of a speaker’s selection within a particular medium; its separate cable context is not mechanically imported here. A parade presents expression through the groups and messages its organizers choose, even if those selections are permissive or varied. GLIB’s bannered unit has its own protected message, but that protection creates no entitlement to insert it into another private speaker’s presentation. Use of public streets under a neutral permit does not convert this parade into a governmental forum. Roberts and PruneYard concern access without the same compelled alteration of expression. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations, with credibility deference, rejects the conclusion that absence of a single theme eliminates protection. The Court invalidates only this compelled expressive inclusion, without finding every customer identity a message or requiring proof that spectators endorse each contingent.

**Precedent treatment:**

- West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette: applies protection against compelled expression.
- Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo: applies editorial selection protection.
- Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC: applies existing medium-specific editorial protection, without importing unrelated cable results.
- Roberts v. United States Jaycees; PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins: distinguish access not materially altering the private expressive composition.
- Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union; Fiske v. Kansas: apply independent review of intertwined First Amendment characterizations while retaining credibility deference.

The respondents’ state-action/public-forum theory against the City was dismissed and not cross-appealed; it is not revived here. The importance of message misattribution in another setting remains open. No general antidiscrimination exemption, facial invalidation or judgment about the correct political message is announced.

### Scope and alternatives

**Mootness threshold — all eight associates reject dismissal and reach the merits.** Respondent's January26 suggestion argues that the Council's revised written rules and protest theme, and the January17 federal declaration protecting the 1995 parade, superseded the historical facts and made review advisory. Petitioners' February6 response insists that the permanent state injunction remains effective against the traditional parade, that they wish to return to that form, and that the new federal judgment addresses the materially different 1995 protest event. The continuing state injunction supplies the decisive live adverse legal constraint; changing speech to comply with or protest it does not itself vacate it. The present question is not merely who could attend an expired parade. W.T. Grant's continuing-controversy principle, Carroll's recognition of an order's ongoing official effect, and the entered permanent judgment support reach. Schmid's genuinely superseded rule is distinguishable because the adverse state order is not established to have been withdrawn or rendered ineffectual. The alternative lawful path would dismiss if verified superseding events actually removed the disputed injunction and every material continuing consequence; those premises are not established here. No Justice must rely on disputed characterizations of financial awards as fines, potential criminal sanctions, or an exact fee amount. O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter join this continuing-order rationale without a broad voluntary-cessation formula; Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer likewise join the same specific continuing-adversity ground. No vacatur question is reached because mootness is rejected. The later 1995 federal declaratory judgment is a verified lower-court event, not an imported Supreme Court action or a new controlling Supreme Court rule.

Reach the as-applied constitutional objection to the order requiring the private Council to include GLIB's group expression in its parade. The respondents' inspected merits account identifies denial of claims against the City and a permanent state-law injunction against the Council; no new reversal of an adjudicated state-action claim is assumed. Findings about discriminatory motive do not themselves answer whether the order alters a private expressive presentation. Conversely, the constitutional holding cannot be written as if the trial court found no discrimination. State-law construction that the parade is covered by the accommodations law is distinct from federal constitutional validity of this application.

**Path A:** reverse the compelled-inclusion judgment and remand. A parade communicates through the organized presentation of people, groups, banners and performances; coherent narrow ideology and exhaustive speaker screening are not conditions of protected expression. Compelling insertion of GLIB as an identifiable expressive contingent changes that presentation. Barnette and Tornillo protect a private speaker's control of the expression it conveys. Turner preserves editorial expression while distinguishing genuinely content-neutral cable carriage and creating no general private-publication entitlement. The rule is as applied to expressive inclusion, not an immunity from ordinary nondiscrimination laws for all commercial conduct or all individual access.

**Path B:** affirm because the statute regulates discriminatory access to an open public accommodation and inclusion is attributable to GLIB rather than the organizers. Roberts v. Jaycees and PruneYard offer genuine analogies. The strongest obstacle is that an expressive procession differs from admitting customers to business premises or associational members without material alteration of the group's communication. The adopted response does not require proof that each viewer believes the organizers endorse each marcher; compelled composition of an expressive whole itself burdens the protected selection.

The public-street permit does not by itself convert the private Council into the government or create a government forum in its parade. Lamb's Chapel's viewpoint-neutral government-forum rule therefore does not transfer directly. Independent review of the constitutional expression question does not authorize rejection of ordinary historical facts merely because the Court dislikes the state court's legal conclusion.
