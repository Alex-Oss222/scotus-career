#### Issue 1: Whether thermal imaging is a search

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the preserved Fourth Amendment question whether the warrantless thermal scan was a search. He does not reach the separate sufficiency of the warrant affidavit after removal of the thermal information. Pinson did not preserve that issue on appeal, and the Eighth Circuit expressly declined to determine it. - **Stone's Legal Position:** The home's Fourth Amendment protection does not end at the physical surface of its walls. Officers deliberately used sense-enhancing technology from outside the residence to obtain information about patterns of heat associated with activity occurring inside that ordinary public observation could not reveal. The acquisition of that otherwise unavailable information about the interior of the home is a search. The absence of physical entry does not remove the surveillance from the Fourth Amendment merely because the instrument detects heat rather than producing a conventional visual image.

- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Law-enforcement use of sense-enhancing technology from outside a home to obtain information about activity within the home that ordinary public observation could not otherwise obtain constitutes a Fourth Amendment search. The warrantless thermal scan of Pinson's residence therefore was a search.

- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The home lies at the center of the security protected by the Fourth Amendment. The officers did not merely observe something exposed to ordinary public perception. They employed a technological instrument for the purpose of learning something about activity occurring behind the walls of a residence that their unaided senses could not discern. Constitutional protection cannot depend on whether information about the interior is extracted through reflected light, sound, heat, or some other physical signal. A rule treating every measurable emission escaping a home as abandoned to unrestricted governmental examination would permit increasingly capable instruments to obtain information about domestic activity without constitutional process while leaving the formal protection of the home intact only against physical entry. The Fourth Amendment protects the security of the interior, not merely the walls surrounding it.

It does not authorize Stone to decide a different question concerning the legal sufficiency of the warrant after the thermal information is removed. The proper course is accordingly to vacate the judgment resting on the erroneous no-search rule and return the case for whatever further proceedings remain lawfully available under ordinary preservation and remedial principles.

The holding concerns deliberate technological acquisition of otherwise unavailable information about activity inside a home. ## Controlling holdings

### I. The targeted examination of this closed home's heat patterns is a search

**Holding and operative rule:** A targeted technological examination of a closed home constitutes a Fourth Amendment search when, as here, its reported heat-pattern information reveals otherwise unobservable information concerning concealed domestic operation. External reception and the absence of an image identifying a person or object do not defeat protection; investigative purpose or use of equipment alone is insufficient, and the Court adopts no general rule governing every sensor or exterior emission.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. This directly controlling application concerns the covered dwelling and demonstrated differential-heat output. Stone-Zsela also states a broader formulation in his separate writing; the other six do not join that formulation merely by joining this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Fourth Amendment protects the privacy of a home against an examination that obtains otherwise concealed domestic information. Katz makes physical penetration unnecessary; Karo protects information about a home's interior that ordinary observation from outside cannot disclose. Police used the instrument to examine heat patterns at a covered third-floor window, roof, and skylight. Although it received surface radiation and did not identify an interior person or object, the resulting pattern conveyed information concerning concealed domestic operation. Treating its physical medium as dispositive would deny the protection Karo recognizes. Hicks likewise rejects an exception for supposedly unimportant concealed information. Riley's naked-eye observation through actual openings does not establish ordinary exposure here. Greenwood's purposeful disposal of garbage does not surrender all information conveyed by escaping heat, and Place's contraband-only sniff differs from information also produced by lawful activity. The Court decides this examination, without assuming greater device capabilities, public availability, particular flight conditions, or a general threshold for other sensing methods.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; privacy protection does not require physical penetration of the home.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied to the reported concealed domestic information; its protection does not depend on the information arriving as an ordinary visual image.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321: applied by analogy to the additional examination of concealed information and its rejection of an unimportant-information exemption; no physical manipulation is found here.
- Florida v. Riley, 488 U.S. 445: distinguished as naked-eye observation through actual openings; its opinions supply none of this record's missing altitude or public-flight facts.
- California v. Greenwood, 486 U.S. 35: distinguished; purposeful placement of garbage for collection does not establish surrender of domestic information conveyed by heat.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696: distinguished; the narrow contraband-information character of the sniff is absent from this heat examination.
- Dow Chemical Co. v. United States, 476 U.S. 227: distinguished; the outdoor industrial photographs do not authorize this examination of a closed dwelling.
- Minnesota v. Dickerson, decided June 7, 1993: consistent with the distinction between information obtained through authorized observation and additional unauthorized probing; no tactile search is presented.

### II. No warrant exception justifies this scan on the established record

**Holding and operative rule:** This search of protected home information required a warrant unless a recognized exception applied. The July 25 scan had no warrant, and no consent, exigency, or other recognized exception is established; the subsequent warrant for physical entry does not retroactively authorize the scan.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: six Justices. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. Kennedy and Breyer do not reach justification because they find no search.

**Controlling explanation:** Once the examination is recognized as a search of protected domestic information, the ordinary warrant requirement applies. Karo preserves recognized exceptions, including genuinely exigent circumstances; it does not authorize warrantless home surveillance merely because the receiving instrument remains outside. Nothing in this record establishes consent, exigency, or another exception for the July 25 scan. The Government's exterior-measurement theory addresses whether a search occurred and supplies no separate justification after that question is resolved. Nor does the limited detail of the output establish an exception for searches considered less revealing. The July 30 warrant authorized a later physical entry; its issuance cannot supply advance judicial authority for an earlier completed search. This conclusion concerns the scan's justification. It neither determines the validity of that later warrant nor requires exclusion of the evidence found during its execution. Those distinct questions remain subject to the record and procedural limits governing the remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; protected surveillance is subject to the warrant requirement and its established exceptions.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied; the warrant presumption for concealed home information retains recognized exceptions, including true exigency, none established here.

### III. Correct the preserved search ruling without reopening unpresented warrant questions

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth Circuit's judgment is vacated insofar as its rejection of the preserved suppression claim and affirmance of the conviction rest on the categorical no-search ground, and the case is remanded for lawful disposition of that claim under the corrected search rule. Pinson's failure to challenge affidavit sufficiency on appeal remains operative: the mandate directs no new excision-and-probable-cause hearing, revives no waived or forfeited argument automatically, and permits further warrant, suppression, or reliance questions only to the extent properly available under the existing record and ordinary preservation and remedial law.

**Authority:** Part III of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. The mandate controls directly. The remedial authorities below delimit unresolved possibilities; none supplies an independent finding admitting or excluding the evidence. Kennedy and Breyer prefer affirmance on their no-search ground; their acceptance of these procedural limits if the search holding governs does not add votes for vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** The circuit rejected the preserved suppression claim because it considered the scan no search. That premise cannot sustain the challenged conviction affirmance. But its footnote expressly records that Pinson did not challenge affidavit sufficiency on appeal, and the circuit did not decide that question. Correcting the search rule does not erase this limit. Karo's separate treatment of an untainted warrant establishes a possible legal distinction, not a finding about Pinson's affidavit. Olano distinguishes intentional waiver from forfeiture and imposes additional conditions before forfeited error may be corrected. A preserved search question does not establish those conditions for another argument. Leon requires supported objective reliance, subject to its exclusions, rather than automatic validation by a later magistrate. Evans concerns qualifying court-employee clerical error and reasonable reliance; deliberate police scanning is not that error. The lower court must determine what remaining issues are lawfully available. This Court orders neither suppression nor a particular ultimate conviction consequence and does not reopen the separate sentencing question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part IV: retained as the distinct possibility of sufficient untainted warrant information; no determination of Pinson's affidavit or entitlement to a new hearing follows.
- United States v. Olano, decided April 26, 1993: retained; intentional waiver differs from forfeiture. If Rule 52(b) is actually invoked, error, plainness, ordinarily outcome-affecting prejudice, and the separate discretionary inquiry concerning fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings must be satisfied as applicable. This mandate does not establish any predicate or revive an argument automatically.
- United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897: left available only if procedurally open and factually supported. Objective reliance affords no protection for knowing or reckless deception of the magistrate, abandonment of judicial neutrality, an affidavit so lacking probable-cause indicia that reliance is wholly unreasonable, or facial warrant deficiency precluding reasonable reliance. The inquiry includes information-obtaining and supplying officers and assumes execution within the warrant's scope; no final reliance finding is made.
- Arizona v. Evans, decided March 1, 1995: confined to qualifying non-law-enforcement court-employee clerical error plus objectively reasonable reliance. Reserved police or mixed institutional errors supply no technology exception for this deliberate scan, and no final exclusion ruling follows here.

## Holdings

### I. The targeted examination of this closed home's heat patterns is a search

**Holding and operative rule:** A targeted technological examination of a closed home constitutes a Fourth Amendment search when, as here, its reported heat-pattern information reveals otherwise unobservable information concerning concealed domestic operation. External reception and the absence of an image identifying a person or object do not defeat protection; investigative purpose or use of equipment alone is insufficient, and the Court adopts no general rule governing every sensor or exterior emission.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. This directly controlling application concerns the covered dwelling and demonstrated differential-heat output. Stone-Zsela also states a broader formulation in his separate writing; the other six do not join that formulation merely by joining this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Fourth Amendment protects the privacy of a home against an examination that obtains otherwise concealed domestic information. Katz makes physical penetration unnecessary; Karo protects information about a home's interior that ordinary observation from outside cannot disclose. Police used the instrument to examine heat patterns at a covered third-floor window, roof, and skylight. Although it received surface radiation and did not identify an interior person or object, the resulting pattern conveyed information concerning concealed domestic operation. Treating its physical medium as dispositive would deny the protection Karo recognizes. Hicks likewise rejects an exception for supposedly unimportant concealed information. Riley's naked-eye observation through actual openings does not establish ordinary exposure here. Greenwood's purposeful disposal of garbage does not surrender all information conveyed by escaping heat, and Place's contraband-only sniff differs from information also produced by lawful activity. The Court decides this examination, without assuming greater device capabilities, public availability, particular flight conditions, or a general threshold for other sensing methods.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; privacy protection does not require physical penetration of the home.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied to the reported concealed domestic information; its protection does not depend on the information arriving as an ordinary visual image.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321: applied by analogy to the additional examination of concealed information and its rejection of an unimportant-information exemption; no physical manipulation is found here.
- Florida v. Riley, 488 U.S. 445: distinguished as naked-eye observation through actual openings; its opinions supply none of this record's missing altitude or public-flight facts.
- California v. Greenwood, 486 U.S. 35: distinguished; purposeful placement of garbage for collection does not establish surrender of domestic information conveyed by heat.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696: distinguished; the narrow contraband-information character of the sniff is absent from this heat examination.
- Dow Chemical Co. v. United States, 476 U.S. 227: distinguished; the outdoor industrial photographs do not authorize this examination of a closed dwelling.
- Minnesota v. Dickerson, decided June 7, 1993: consistent with the distinction between information obtained through authorized observation and additional unauthorized probing; no tactile search is presented.

### II. No warrant exception justifies this scan on the established record

**Holding and operative rule:** This search of protected home information required a warrant unless a recognized exception applied. The July 25 scan had no warrant, and no consent, exigency, or other recognized exception is established; the subsequent warrant for physical entry does not retroactively authorize the scan.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: six Justices. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. Kennedy and Breyer do not reach justification because they find no search.

**Controlling explanation:** Once the examination is recognized as a search of protected domestic information, the ordinary warrant requirement applies. Karo preserves recognized exceptions, including genuinely exigent circumstances; it does not authorize warrantless home surveillance merely because the receiving instrument remains outside. Nothing in this record establishes consent, exigency, or another exception for the July 25 scan. The Government's exterior-measurement theory addresses whether a search occurred and supplies no separate justification after that question is resolved. Nor does the limited detail of the output establish an exception for searches considered less revealing. The July 30 warrant authorized a later physical entry; its issuance cannot supply advance judicial authority for an earlier completed search. This conclusion concerns the scan's justification. It neither determines the validity of that later warrant nor requires exclusion of the evidence found during its execution. Those distinct questions remain subject to the record and procedural limits governing the remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; protected surveillance is subject to the warrant requirement and its established exceptions.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied; the warrant presumption for concealed home information retains recognized exceptions, including true exigency, none established here.
