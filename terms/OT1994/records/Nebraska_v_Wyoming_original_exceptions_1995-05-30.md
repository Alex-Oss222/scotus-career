## Controlling holdings

### Settled allocation and discrete defenses

**Holding and operative rule:** The decree's 75/25 natural-flow allocation cannot be replaced through these amendments by a mass beneficial-use ceiling. Reject the First Counterclaim and First Cross-Claim insofar as they seek that ceiling, while preserving discrete geographic, priority, unlawful-call and relevant waste defenses not dependent on it.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that retained jurisdiction does not give a State another opportunity to replace an adjudicated allocation. Nebraska's 1945 decision supplies the percentage allocation and its geography and priorities; the 1953 decree preserves those terms as modified. The April 20, 1993 decision distinguishes enforcement of an established right from modification requiring clear and convincing evidence of real and substantial injury. Wyoming's proposed mass beneficial-use ceiling substitutes a different measure for the settled percentage. A desire for quantified certainty does not itself establish the basis for that change. But rejecting the formula does not establish that Nebraska may make calls outside the decree, bypass priorities or waste water without legal consequence. Those discrete enforcement and defensive matters do not logically depend on the new ceiling and remain available in their proper scope. No alleged violation, waste quantity or final restriction is decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Nebraska v. Wyoming, 325 U.S. 589: preserves allocation, geography and priority terms.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming, 345 U.S. 981: preserves the decree as modified in 1953.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming, April 20, 1993: applies distinct enforcement and modification burdens; modification requires clear and convincing real and substantial injury. Its Inland Lakes acquiescence alternative is not generalized here.

## Holdings

### Settled allocation and discrete defenses

**Holding and operative rule:** The decree's 75/25 natural-flow allocation cannot be replaced through these amendments by a mass beneficial-use ceiling. Reject the First Counterclaim and First Cross-Claim insofar as they seek that ceiling, while preserving discrete geographic, priority, unlawful-call and relevant waste defenses not dependent on it.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that retained jurisdiction does not give a State another opportunity to replace an adjudicated allocation. Nebraska's 1945 decision supplies the percentage allocation and its geography and priorities; the 1953 decree preserves those terms as modified. The April 20, 1993 decision distinguishes enforcement of an established right from modification requiring clear and convincing evidence of real and substantial injury. Wyoming's proposed mass beneficial-use ceiling substitutes a different measure for the settled percentage. A desire for quantified certainty does not itself establish the basis for that change. But rejecting the formula does not establish that Nebraska may make calls outside the decree, bypass priorities or waste water without legal consequence. Those discrete enforcement and defensive matters do not logically depend on the new ceiling and remain available in their proper scope. No alleged violation, waste quantity or final restriction is decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Nebraska v. Wyoming, 325 U.S. 589: preserves allocation, geography and priority terms.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming, 345 U.S. 981: preserves the decree as modified in 1953.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming, April 20, 1993: applies distinct enforcement and modification burdens; modification requires clear and convincing real and substantial injury. Its Inland Lakes acquiescence alternative is not generalized here.

### Groundwater and equitable connection

**Holding and operative rule:** Specific upstream groundwater development and alleged depletion threatening the decree allocation may be pleaded. Nebraska's own pumping creates no categorical unclean-hands bar without relevant conduct and connection to the asserted harm; supported defenses and applicable eventual proof requirements remain.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that alleged groundwater effects may be examined without deciding that any well violates the decree. The April 20, 1993 decision supplies the distinction between existing-right enforcement and new allocation, including the latter's clear-and-convincing substantial-injury burden. Kansas v. Colorado supplies proof-sensitive treatment of pumping and concrete equitable defenses, but decides neither these States' hydrology nor a universal enforcement quantum. Nebraska identifies upstream development and alleged effects on protected natural flow. These allegations warrant inquiry; they do not invite general supervision of every well. Wyoming's strongest objection invokes Nebraska's own pumping. Similar activity alone does not establish legally relevant misconduct, injury to Wyoming or connection to this claim. Wyoming remains free to prove a properly connected defense. Leave therefore neither establishes interstate injury nor immunizes Nebraska's conduct. Actual injury, causation and any permissible restriction remain for evidence before the Master.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Nebraska v. Wyoming, April 20, 1993: applies distinct enforcement and modification burdens; modification requires clear and convincing real and substantial injury.
- Kansas v. Colorado, May 15, 1995: applies proof-sensitive pumping and defense analysis without importing Compact elements or factual conclusions.

### Downstream and habitat evidence

**Holding and operative rule:** Downstream nonirrigation and habitat effects may be considered insofar as relevant to injury and lawful modification in admitted decree claims. Relevance creates no independent instream entitlement, habitat-flow quantity, free-standing environmental right or automatic priority; Count IV remains denied without prejudice.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that evidence of injury and entitlement to a new allocation are different questions. Nebraska's 1945 decision supplies the allocation's setting, and the April 20, 1993 decision supplies the proof required for enforcement or modification. Downstream conditions may demonstrate the consequences of threatened reduction of protected water. Excluding all nonirrigation consequences would remove potentially relevant evidence merely because the decree apportioned irrigation water. Nebraska's contrary risk is equally real: evidence relevant to injury cannot confer a new habitat right. The admitted claims and applicable modification burden supply the limit. Count IV's wider seasonal theory is not admitted, and no wildlife-specific quantity is adjudicated. The Master may examine relevant effects within the authorized controversy. Actual injury, changed conditions and entitlement remain for proof before a different allocation or operating obligation can issue; no independent public environmental right is established.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Nebraska v. Wyoming, April 20, 1993: applies distinct enforcement and modification burdens; modification requires clear and convincing real and substantial injury.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the pivotal reach does not exclude every supporting downstream condition from the retained proceeding. Nebraska alleges Horse Creek changes affect supply conditions and thereby threaten the existing balance. Entry below Tri-State Dam matters to that causal inquiry but does not alone resolve it. The answer is a proved material connection and the governing modification burden, not automatic admission of every remote dispute.
