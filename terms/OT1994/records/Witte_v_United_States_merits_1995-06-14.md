## Controlling holdings

### Relevant conduct is not a prior cocaine prosecution or punishment here

**Controlling proposition:** Consideration of relevant uncharged conduct in selecting a sentence within the authorized statutory range for the offense of conviction does not itself constitute prosecution or independent punishment for the uncharged offense. Witte's marijuana-attempt sentence therefore does not bar the distinct cocaine indictment under the retained Grady rule; this holding does not approve a separate disguised penalty, punishment beyond statutory authority or disregard of independent sentencing safeguards.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, constitutional-characterization discussion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six support this rationale. Scalia and Thomas affirm only in the judgment on the narrower distinct-prosecution ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the first judgment punished the marijuana attempt, although the cocaine facts materially increased its Guidelines calculation. Williams v. Oklahoma permits consideration of other criminal conduct when fixing punishment for the offense before the sentencing court; McMillan distinguishes lawful sentencing considerations from a separate offense, subject to independent safeguards. The statutory range was five to forty years, the calculated Guidelines range 292 to 365 months, and the actual substantial-assistance sentence 144 months. Those distinct quantities show why the sentencing label alone is not the answer: real aggravation occurred, but it calibrated one legally authorized marijuana punishment rather than imposed a separate cocaine penalty. Felix supplies the distinction between evidentiary use and prior prosecution, applied here by analogy rather than equating all sentencing with trial evidence. Dixon retained Grady's essential-conduct protection; the cocaine offenses were not previously prosecuted, and their conduct did not constitute the later marijuana attempt. Kurth Ranch involved a distinct punitive exaction and remains intact. The Court decides no guilt or future sentence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576 — applied to consideration of other conduct when fixing lawful offense-of-conviction punishment.
- McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 — applied within statutory authority; independent due-process and jury limits remain.
- United States v. Felix — applied by analogy for the difference between use of conduct and prior prosecution; no same-evidence bar is created.

- United States v. R.L.C. — preserved: statutory sentencing authority is not replaced by the Guidelines range.
- United States v. Harris — confined to authorized cumulative punishment in one prosecution; it does not cure a valid successive-prosecution bar.

### Future sentence coordination remains conditional and separate

**Controlling proposition:** The possibility of an overlapping later sentence does not itself bar this prosecution, and any sentence following conviction must satisfy the then-applicable coordination provisions and independent constitutional limits. Under received § 5G1.3, subsection (a) first requires the instant sentence to run consecutively to the undischarged term if the instant offense occurred during service of that term, including work release, furlough or escape, or after sentencing for that term but before service began; only if (a) does not apply does subsection (b) require concurrency when the earlier offenses were fully taken into account in the instant offense level; in all remaining cases, policy statement (c) requires consecutiveness to the extent necessary for reasonable incremental punishment, with the commentary's approximation task, not an automatic total or repeated departure.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, conditional coordination discussion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six support this statutory and remedial proposition.
