#### Issue 1: Merits

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone finds no jurisdictional, preservation, or vehicle bar and reaches the question presented.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies ordinary consent principles. Courts independently decide whether parties agreed to submit arbitrability to the arbitrator unless clear and unmistakable evidence shows that delegation. Merely arguing arbitrability to the panel while objecting to jurisdiction does not establish consent. Appellate review of the district court’s legal conclusion is ordinary, not specially deferential.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Courts decide arbitrability independently absent clear and unmistakable agreement to delegate that question, and a party does not consent merely by presenting an objection to the arbitrators while preserving it.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Arbitration rests on agreement. Silence and defensive participation cannot supply the delegation. Once the court properly determines no agreement existed, ordinary standards govern appellate review of that judicial determination.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding leaves broad delegation clauses, incorporated rules that clearly assign arbitrability, and deference to merits awards within an established arbitration agreement for future cases.

## Controlling holdings

### Antecedent assent and delegation

**Holding and operative rule:** Courts independently determine agreement to arbitrate arbitrability unless ordinary applicable contract law establishes clear and unmistakable delegation. Presenting an objection while denying panel jurisdiction does not itself establish delegation; company submission does not itself establish an owner's personal assent. Where parties actually delegate arbitrability, limited award review governs that decision.

**Authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court, Part I; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer on the antecedent-assent rule and application; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer alone on the additional conditional actual-delegation review rule (Stone-Zsela reserves that proposition) directly join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that arbitration depends on agreement, including agreement about who decides whether arbitration is required. AT&T Technologies supplies clear and unmistakable delegation before arbitrators are treated as the chosen decisionmakers on arbitrability. Volt supplies fidelity to the actual contractual arrangement. A presumption favoring coverage within an accepted arbitration agreement does not answer this antecedent decisionmaker question. MKI signed the workout document containing arbitration language and submitted its dispute, but that conduct cannot automatically establish the Kaplans' personal agreement. Their appearance to object asks the panel to decline authority rather than clearly entrusting authority to it. Independent review therefore governs their objections. Actual delegation instead entails limited review, a proposition eight Justices join. Broad clauses, incorporated rules and independent nonsignatory-binding theories remain for cases presenting them; MKI's submission is distinct. Stone-Zsela reserves merits-award deference, and the Court permits no rehearing of an agreed arbitration's merits.

### Ordinary appellate review

**Holding and operative rule:** Appellate courts independently review district-court legal conclusions and review factual findings for clear error. Arbitration supplies no additional appellate deference to the district judge's independent arbitrability determination.

**Authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court, Part II; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ordinary division between review of law and review of fact governs this appeal. A district court's independent decision about assent is a judicial decision. Arbitration gives that judge no additional authority to resolve law conclusively for the appellate court. AT&T Technologies distinguishes the decisionmaker question from underlying merits; Volt requires fidelity to the actual agreement. Those principles explain why deference to an agreed arbitrator cannot simply be transferred to a district judge when parties did not delegate arbitrability. The contrary argument treats arbitration-related judicial action as deserving special insulation, but confuses review of an award with review of the district court's own decision. Legal conclusions remain independently reviewable and findings retain clear-error review. The Court affirms the Third Circuit without creating a new vacatur ground for the merits of an actually agreed arbitration.

**Precedent treatment:**

- AT&T Technologies, 475 U.S. 643: preserves decisionmaker/merits distinctions.
- Volt, 489 U.S. 468: actual agreement explains arbitral deference; it insulates no district-court legal conclusion.
