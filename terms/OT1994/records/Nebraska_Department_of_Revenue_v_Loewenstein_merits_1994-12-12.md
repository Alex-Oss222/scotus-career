## Controlling holdings

#### The private repo return is outside the federal-obligation exemption

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3124(a) does not exempt the return on these repurchase agreements because it is owed under a separate private financing obligation, rather than on the United States' securities; actual book-entry delivery does not change the source of the negotiated earnings. The statute continues to protect direct and indirect taxation of federal obligations and their interest, except nondiscriminatory corporate franchise or substitute corporate nonproperty taxes and estate or inheritance taxes; the State prevails because this income is outside the protected subject, not because an exception covers this individual income tax.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 9 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the obligation producing the taxed return controls. Rockford distinguishes the Government's payment obligation from a private obligation supported by federal credit, and Smith v. Davis supplies the same direct-obligation boundary. Here the Trusts advance cash for a privately negotiated return unrelated to Treasury yield. The Trusts transmit the issuer's payments to the Seller-Borrower; the Seller-Borrower maintains collateral under the margin provisions; and the Trusts retain recourse for a deficiency after default liquidation. Those arrangements identify secured financing for this exemption even though securities actually passed through the Federal Reserve system and no separate note was signed. Frank Lyon respects genuine business arrangements; it does not determine whether the Government owes these particular earnings. The State therefore may tax the private return. The removal of surplus language during recodification did not abolish indirect-tax protection. The Court decides neither repo ownership under other bodies of law nor a transaction conveying genuine beneficial ownership and federal coupon income.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rockford Life Insurance Co. v. Illinois Department of Revenue, 482 U.S. 182 (1987): applies its distinction between an actual federal obligation and separate private earnings; its GNMA facts are not substituted for these delivered federal securities.
- Smith v. Davis, 323 U.S. 111 (1944): applies the boundary between the Government's own direct obligation and a separate private obligation supported by federal assets or credit.
- Frank Lyon Co. v. United States, 435 U.S. 561 (1978): distinguishes respect for a genuine transaction's form from identifying the obligation producing income under §3124; no sham or universal form-disregard rule is adopted.
- American Bank & Trust Co. v. Dallas County, 463 U.S. 855 (1983), and First National Bank of Atlanta v. Bartow County Board of Tax Assessors, 470 U.S. 583 (1985): preserve protection when the tax computation actually reaches protected obligations or their income; neither classifies this private repo return.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Subsection (b) sends the tax status of interest on Government or agency obligations, income from evidences of ownership, and disposition gain or loss to the Internal Revenue Code. An obligation that the Federal Housing Administration agreed, under a contract made before March 1, 1941, to issue at a future date retains the tax-exemption privileges provided by the authorizing law at the time of that contract. Subsection (b) does not apply to obligations and evidences of ownership issued by the District of Columbia, a territory or possession, or their departments, agencies, instrumentalities or political subdivisions; it does not erase subsection (a). No separate construction, application or validity ruling concerning a Seller-Borrower's §77-2716(1)(e)(i) add-back is made.

#### The asserted discrimination lacks a supported comparator policy

**Holding and operative rule:** A ruling that expressly addresses federal-obligation repos does not, without an identified favorable policy for comparable state or local repos, establish the discriminatory taxation alleged here. The discrimination objection therefore supplies no ground to invalidate Revenue Ruling 22-85-1 on this record; the Court does not approve actual discriminatory treatment or find every Nebraska practice neutral.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 9 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the taxpayer's comparison must concern an actual difference in treatment. Memphis Bank forbids favoring state obligations over protected federal obligations; that protection remains intact. But the taxpayer identifies no statute, ruling or other Nebraska policy exempting the return on otherwise comparable state-obligation repurchase agreements. Revenue Ruling 22-85-1 names federal transactions because those transactions are its subject. That choice of subject does not itself establish the asserted favored comparator. Nor does the private character of the repo return alone answer the separate constitutional contention: the Court examines the alleged discrimination independently. The strongest objection is that singling out federal instruments might conceal unequal treatment. The present materials do not establish the other side of that comparison, and the Court cannot supply an exemption that has not been identified. A claim supported by an actual disparate policy remains for another case. This conclusion resolves only the objection offered to sustain the reviewed judgment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Memphis Bank & Trust Co. v. Garner, 459 U.S. 392 (1983): preserves the prohibition of actual discriminatory taxation favoring state obligations; an unproved comparator policy does not establish its predicate.

#### The borrowing-power objection is not established by evidence about bankruptcy classification

**Holding and operative rule:** The constitutional borrowing-power objection fails because the supplied evidence concerning the effects of classifying repos in bankruptcy does not establish the asserted tax-specific burden on federal borrowing. Rockford's exclusion of remote or conjectural impairment applies without a finding that taxation can never affect borrowing or a numerical threshold for permissible effects.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this proposition and its stated qualifications. These 8 rationale votes control without combining separate rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the claimed impairment must rest on evidence addressing the challenged tax. Rockford denies immunity based merely on a private instrument's contribution to federal financing and requires more than a remote or conjectural burden. The Sternlight affidavit describes losses of liquidity, flexibility or institutional availability if repos receive a different legal characterization in bankruptcy and related settings. The 1984 letter likewise confines its endorsement of purchase-and-sale treatment to bankruptcy, and the reported Federal Reserve Bank brief distinguishes that issue from taxation of repo income. These sources establish why classification can matter; they do not establish that this individual income tax causes the asserted financing losses. The Court considers the practical importance of repo markets but cannot transfer an evidentiary conclusion from one legal change to another. The taxpayer therefore has not supplied a constitutional ground for preserving the exemption. A differently supported claim concerning an actual tax burden on borrowing remains open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rockford Life Insurance Co. v. Illinois Department of Revenue, 482 U.S. 182 (1987): applies the limit on remote or conjectural impairment of federal borrowing; it creates no rule that every market effect is irrelevant.
