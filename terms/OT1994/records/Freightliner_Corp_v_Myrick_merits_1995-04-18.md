- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies preemption in order. The Safety Act’s express preemption clause does not reach a subject on which no federal standard was in force, and its saving clause preserves common-law liability. The absence of regulation creates neither impossibility nor a federal policy requiring manufacturers to remain free from state duties.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When no applicable federal safety standard governs the challenged feature, the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act does not expressly or impliedly preempt state common-law defect claims merely because the agency previously suspended rulemaking.

- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** A valid federal standard or an affirmative federal policy requiring a uniform no-regulation result could present a different conflict.

## Controlling holdings

### No in-effect federal standard supplies the express-preemption predicate here

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1392(d)'s express restriction requires an in-effect federal motor-vehicle safety standard governing the same aspect of performance as the asserted state duty. The asserted antilock-equipment defect claims are not expressly displaced on this record because that federal predicate is absent; the Court does not decide whether common-law duties themselves are safety standards within that provision.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer: eight direct rationale votes. Justice Scalia concurs only in the judgment and gives no separate explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the express clause must be applied to the particular aspect of performance actually regulated. Easterwood requires attention to federal coverage of the duty at issue, and Cipollone requires examination of the enacted preemption language and predicate duty. Regulation of vehicle braking generally does not establish an operative antilock requirement for these claims. Paccar judicially invalidated inadequately supported stopping-distance requirements; that invalidation does not itself supply a federal safety standard forbidding state liability for omitted antilock equipment. Section 1392(d)'s condition is therefore unsatisfied without deciding whether common-law duties fall within its category of state standards. Section 1397(k) states that compliance with a federal safety standard does not exempt a person from common-law liability. The Court preserves that text but need not determine its every effect where an operative standard exists. The common-law claims return for ordinary adjudication; this statutory conclusion establishes neither a product defect nor causation and does not disturb the independent Georgia RICO judgment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- CSX Transportation, Inc. v. Easterwood, April 21, 1993: applies its attention to the aspect of performance actually covered, without importing the separate railroad speed holding or treating every braking rule as coverage of antilock omission.
- Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., June 24, 1992: applies clause- and duty-specific analysis; its particular cigarette holdings do not answer all Safety Act questions.
- Paccar, Inc. v. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 573 F.2d 632: treats its judicial invalidation as the regulatory-history predicate, not as a new federal right to avoid common-law liability.

### Judicial invalidation alone establishes no actual federal conflict

**Holding and operative rule:** An express preemption clause does not categorically foreclose inquiry into an independently established actual conflict with operative federal law or a demonstrated federal objective. Here neither impossibility nor interference with such an objective is established by judicial invalidation and the absence of an operative antilock requirement, so the asserted conflict does not bar these common-law claims.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer: eight direct rationale votes. Scalia's judgment-only agreement supplies no additional rationale vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the failure of express preemption does not end every possible conflict inquiry. The Eleventh Circuit read Cipollone too broadly when it treated Congress's express clause as invariably excluding implied preemption. Gade rejects automatic displacement from regulatory overlap but supplies no physical-impossibility-only rule. The manufacturers nevertheless must identify the federal obligation or policy that their asserted state duty would frustrate. Their strongest claim treats the ability to sell trucks without antilock equipment as a protected federal choice. Paccar does not establish that premise: the court rejected the agency's evidentiary support while allowing adequately supported future standards. It did not adopt a policy insulating manufacturers from state liability. Compliance with the asserted duty is not shown to violate federal law, and regulatory silence alone does not demonstrate the claimed obstacle. A valid operative standard or demonstrated affirmative policy requiring a uniform absence of regulation could present a different case. The Court leaves that case, and the ultimate tort merits here, undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., June 24, 1992: rejects the lower court's universal inference that any express provision exhausts every possible actual-conflict inquiry; its enacted-clause holdings remain intact.
- Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Association, June 18, 1992: preserves the shared rule against automatic displacement from overlap; no physical-impossibility-only theory or broader separate theory is made Court law.
- Paccar, Inc. v. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 573 F.2d 632: distinguishes inadequate support for a mandatory standard from an affirmative policy protecting noninstallation against state duties.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 1397(k)'s compliance saving clause is not held to immunize every common-law action from an actual conflict. The Court does not decide whether tort duties are standards under §1392(d), every effect of the saving clause when a standard exists, or a federal field-wide ceiling. It does not find all air-brake regulation absent, invent an agency policy from an unproduced revision, or decide defect, accident causation, consortium entitlement, wrongful-death recovery, or damages.
