## Holdings

### Review of the scope certification

**Holding and operative rule:** A Westfall Act scope-of-employment certification is judicially reviewable before substitution of the United States becomes final. Initial substitution on certification does not conclusively establish employment scope; §2679(d)(2) makes certification conclusive for removal only.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Breyer; six Justices adopt the statutory review rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress did not clearly entrust final adjudication of employment scope to the Executive. Abbott Laboratories supplies the ordinary presumption that executive action affecting legal rights remains reviewable. Section 2679 directs initial substitution when certification issues but expressly makes the certificate conclusive only for removal. Reading that limitation as finality for every purpose would confuse the litigation procedure with the predicate for employee immunity. Certification can eliminate a private tort action when the United States is immune under an FTCA exception, making impartial determination of the predicate important. United States v. Smith preserves employee exclusivity once scope is established; it does not decide who conclusively determines that scope. The express employee petition following refusal of certification does not clearly bar a plaintiff’s ordinary judicial challenge. The district court must review scope under applicable law with appropriate evidence and procedure. The Court determines neither actual scope nor liability and creates no federal damages action.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner: applies the judicial-review presumption to an ambiguous statutory finality arrangement.
- United States v. Smith: preserves employee exclusivity after scope is established; does not make the certificate conclusive.
- FDIC v. Meyer: preserves the distinction between immunity, actionable claims and employee exclusivity; supplies no certification-finality rule.

Diversity supplies jurisdiction in this case independently of certification. The Court does not decide whether Article III permits retention of a nondiverse removed case after certification is rejected, or the §904/headquarters matters outside the grant. Review waives no immunity, determines no tort merits and requires no universal discovery or specified trial mode.

## Precedent Treatment

The treatment in the holding controls. No foreign-country exception or federal immunity is displaced.

## Law After Decision

Courts review the certification’s employment predicate before substitution becomes final. The foreign-country exception and all actual defenses retain their force. No removed-case jurisdiction rule is created.

## Separate Writings

Souter, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents. Mandatory substitution, deletion of predecessor plaintiff-review language and the express employee petition on refusal indicate an asymmetric statutory scheme. The removal-finality language can clarify the end of former remand practice without creating a plaintiff challenge. The dissent also regards the possible removed-case jurisdiction anomaly as a reason for caution. It does not find actual employment scope or treat an FTCA exception as proof of it.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded. The district court shall review employment scope under applicable law, permit appropriate evidence and procedure, and determine the proper defendant before addressing immunity and merits. The Court’s merits review is complete; ordinary proceedings remain below.

## Holdings

### Private parade composition and compelled inclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices adopt the as-applied private-expression holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the right to speak includes control over the composition of a private expressive presentation. Barnette prohibits compulsory affirmation, and Tornillo protects editorial choice against compelled inclusion. Turner preserves the importance of a speaker’s selection within a particular medium; its separate broadcast context is not mechanically imported here. A parade presents expression through the groups and messages its organizers choose, even if those selections are permissive or varied. GLIB’s bannered unit has its own protected message, but that protection creates no entitlement to insert it into another private speaker’s presentation. Use of public streets under a neutral permit does not convert this parade into a governmental forum. Roberts and PruneYard concern access without the same compelled alteration of expression. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations, with credibility deference, rejects the conclusion that absence of a single theme eliminates protection. The Court invalidates only this compelled expressive inclusion, without finding every customer identity a message or requiring proof that spectators endorse each contingent.

**Precedent treatment:**

- West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette: applies protection against compelled expression.
- Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo: applies editorial selection protection.
- Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC: applies existing medium-specific editorial protection, without importing unrelated broadcast results.
- Roberts v. United States Jaycees; PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins: distinguish access not materially altering the private expressive composition.
- Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union; Fiske v. Kansas: apply independent review of intertwined First Amendment characterizations while retaining credibility deference.

The respondents’ state-action/public-forum theory against the City was dismissed and not cross-appealed; it is not revived here. The importance of message misattribution in another setting remains open. No general antidiscrimination exemption, facial invalidation or judgment about the correct political message is announced.

## Precedent Treatment

The treatment accompanying the holding controls. Ordinary nondiscrimination applications remain distinct and enforceable.

## Law After Decision

Private parade composition receives protection against compelled insertion of an identified expressive unit. Other expressive-association disputes require a comparably material expressive burden; ordinary customer identity alone is not a message.

## Separate Writings

The Court issues a single opinion. No separate writing is published.

## Procedure After Action

The Supreme Judicial Court judgment is reversed and the case remanded. The injunction compelling GLIB’s inclusion shall be dissolved. The public-accommodations law remains enforceable where its application does not compel private expression. No fee amount, fine or sanction is decided.

## Holdings

### Federal tax equity and an actually adequate legal remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1983 does not authorize injunctive or declaratory interference with state tax administration where an adequate legal remedy actually protects the claimant. The limitation arises from federal equitable principles informing §1983, not direct application of 28 U.S.C. §1341 to state courts, and does not restrict otherwise lawful state-law equitable relief.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine Justices adopt this remedial rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a federal right and the relief available to enforce it are distinct questions. Dennis v. Higgins recognizes an enforceable Commerce Clause right; it does not abolish the established limits on federal tax equity. Dows, Matthews and Great Lakes supply that tradition: courts ordinarily withhold tax injunctions and parallel declarations when legal relief affords effective protection. Section 1341 directly addresses federal district courts and therefore cannot itself withdraw this state court’s jurisdiction. Nor does a State’s preference for uniform procedure defeat federal law. Here the taxpayers obtained invalidation of the discriminatory taxes and an adequate state-law refund remedy. Additional federal equity is unnecessary on that record. The rule preserves extraordinary relief for irreparable injury, a cloud on title or a genuine risk of numerous suits between the same parties involving the same law or facts. It does not impose general exhaustion, decide damages, or permit withdrawal of an actually promised refund.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dennis v. Higgins: preserves the Commerce Clause right; distinguishes right from equitable relief.
- Dows v. Chicago; Matthews v. Rodgers; Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co. v. Huffman: apply the tax-equity tradition and its adequacy and extraordinary-injury limits.
- Reich v. Collins: preserves an actually honored postpayment remedy; the successful refunds here do not require duplicate equity.
- Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation: preserves application of governing tax law and meaningful correction, without fixing a single remedy.
- Livadas v. Bradshaw: applies the separation of federal right, enforcement channel and available relief.
- Felder v. Casey; Patsy v. Board of Regents: distinguish hostile state conditions and general exhaustion from this intrinsic federal equitable limitation.

### Fees follow an available fee-eligible federal claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the invoked §1983 equitable action affords no relief on this record, §1988 supplies no fee entitlement through that action. Success on state law does not categorically defeat fees where an independently viable fee-eligible federal claim exists.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that section 1988 does not create the federal action whose availability must be established first. Maher v. Gagne permits fees in appropriate federal enforcement proceedings without an express adjudication of federal merits, including when a court resolves the dispute on another ground. The decisive defect here is different: the federal tax-equity limitation leaves no available relief under the section 1983 action the taxpayers invoked. Their state-law refund victory therefore cannot supply a fee entitlement through an unavailable federal remedial route. The Court does not hold that every state-law judgment, or every absence of an express federal merits ruling, defeats fees. No independently viable alternative federal fee-eligible claim is established in this case. The tax invalidity and refund entitlement remain undisturbed. The independent state statutory fee ground is outside review, as are a reasonable fee amount, an unpresented damages action and any case in which the legal remedy fails to afford adequate protection.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Maher v. Gagne: distinguishes an available fee-eligible action resolved without federal merits from this unavailable federal equitable action; its rule remains intact.

An inadequate remedy, including continued unconstitutional taxation after a refund judgment, may require different equity. Mere parallel claims by many taxpayers do not themselves establish the same-party multiplicity exception. The general duty of state courts to entertain §1983 is assumed rather than decided.

## Precedent Treatment

The treatment accompanying each holding controls. Dennis remains law despite Kennedy’s separate objection. No prior right, meaningful refund requirement or generally available §1983 remedy is withdrawn.

## Law After Decision

Federal tax equity under §1983 and derivative fees are unavailable on this actually adequate-refund record. State-law equitable authority remains intact. This decision does not determine tax validity, refund amounts, a separate damages claim or the independent state fee ground.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy concurs and joins the Court in full. He preserves his view, expressed in his Dennis dissent, that the Commerce Clause was not meant to create the kind of individual statutory right §1983 enforces. Dennis nevertheless governs this case; his objection supplies no alternative controlling rule and does not alter the adopted remedial analysis.

## Procedure After Action

The Oklahoma Supreme Court’s denial of the reviewed federal relief and fees is affirmed. The existing tax invalidity and state-law refund entitlement remain in effect; lawful refund proceedings continue without an additional federal equitable or fee instruction. This merits review is complete.

## Holdings

### Legal incidence of the reviewed motor-fuels tax

**Holding and operative rule:** Without clear congressional authorization, Oklahoma cannot enforce a tax whose legal incidence falls on the Tribe or tribal members for Indian-country sales. Under the reviewed statute’s payment, remittance and credit arrangement, incidence falls on the tribal retailer, so the fuel tax is barred in that application.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices support the fuel-incidence rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that legal incidence must be identified before a State relies on economic pass-through or collection machinery. Moe and Potawatomi distinguish a direct tax on an immune tribe from collection of a valid tax on nonmembers. Yakima requires attention to the particular tax Congress authorized; Attea permits reasonable collection controls for valid nonmember taxes, not taxation of the Tribe itself. Here distributors remit on retailers’ behalf, receive credits for retailer taxes they cannot collect, and receive an exemption for the distributor’s own sale. Comparable consumer liability or a mandatory pass-through does not appear in that arrangement. These features support the lower court’s reasonable construction that the retailer bears legal liability; they are not an express statutory declaration naming incidence. The late Hayden-Cartwright argument was not presented below or in the petition and will not be decided first here. A tax lawfully shifted to non-Indians would still require its own federal-preemption and collection analysis.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Moe v. Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes; Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe: distinguish immune tribal incidence from valid nonmember taxation and collection.
- County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes: applies tax-specific congressional authorization, without transferring one tax’s permission to another.
- Department of Taxation v. Milhelm Attea & Bros.: preserves reasonable controls for valid nonmember taxes; supplies no tribal-incidence tax authority.

### Resident taxation and the surviving treaty promise

**Holding and operative rule:** Oklahoma may apply its generally applicable income tax to members residing outside Indian country who earn wages from tribal work in Indian country; the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek does not exempt that employee income. Members residing and earning tribal income in Indian country retain the ordinary protection under McClanahan and Sac and Fox.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s income portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices adopt the off-country-resident treaty construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the treaty’s protection of the Nation’s government does not immunize the income of every member residing outside Indian country. Mescalero supplies ordinary state authority over off-reservation activity absent contrary federal law. McClanahan and Sac and Fox preserve the distinct protection tied to member residence and income in Indian country. Article IV protects jurisdiction over persons and property within the Nation’s western limits and guarantees that state laws will not govern the Nation. Read as a whole, that territorial governmental promise does not prohibit the nondiscriminatory taxation of an off-country resident simply because the Tribe pays the salary. Graves supplies the distinction between taxing an employee’s income and directly taxing the employer. The favorable Indian treaty canon cannot remove the promise’s geographic context. The Court relies on the surviving treaty’s meaning, not implied repeal at statehood. It leaves nonmember wages, nontribal-source income and an independent unpressed infringement claim outside the decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission; Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation: retain the residence-and-source protection within Indian country, including statutory Indian country beyond formal reservation labels.
- Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones: applies ordinary off-reservation state authority absent contrary federal law.
- Graves v. New York ex rel. O’Keefe: distinguishes employee income taxation from taxation of government as employer.

Article IV’s retained council authority is conditioned on consistency with the federal Constitution, treaties and laws; Congress’s laws within its constitutional Indian-affairs authority remain expressly permitted. The Court does not decide Hayden-Cartwright authorization, a replacement tax, amounts, refunds, actual individual residence or an independent unpressed Williams infringement theory.

## Precedent Treatment

The treatment supplied with each holding governs. No broad off-country immunity or universal authorization of non-Indian-incidence taxes follows.

## Law After Decision

The reviewed retailer-incidence fuel tax is barred. The treaty does not bar the generally applicable income tax on the specified off-country resident wages. Indian-country residence/source protection remains. Proposed bills are not an enacted replacement; a later incidence shift requires independent analysis.

## Separate Writings

Breyer, joined by Stevens, O’Connor and Souter, agrees with the fuel holding but dissents on income. The treaty’s protection of the Nation and its descendants, read with its history and the favorable-construction canon, protects members’ wages for tribal-government work performed in Indian country despite commuter residence. Taxing that payroll burdens the protected government and its expenditures. The dissent does not revive universal intergovernmental employee immunity or exempt all off-country activities; it rests on this treaty and the member, employer and work-location nexus.

## Procedure After Action

The Tenth Circuit is affirmed on the reviewed fuel tax and reversed on the specified off-country-resident wage exemption. The case is remanded for application of these separate rulings. Oklahoma may not enforce the reviewed fuel tax against the Tribe or tax covered tribal wages of members residing in Indian country; off-country-resident wages may be taxed subject to ordinary law. No tax sum, refund or individual residence finding is entered.

## Holdings

### Substantive state constraints and meaningful prison liberty

**Holding and operative rule:** A State creates protected prison liberty when substantive law meaningfully restricts officials’ authority to impose a significant deprivation. Mandatory wording alone is insufficient: courts must examine the genuine constraint in the whole disciplinary scheme and the meaningful restraint, without making state codification necessary for directly protected constitutional liberty.

**Authority:** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that legal substance matters more than a regulation’s drafting conventions. Hewitt supplies the recognition that substantive state constraints may create protected liberty; Thompson distinguishes genuine predicates from procedures that do not require a particular substantive outcome. The Court narrows reliance on mandatory words alone without discarding real constraints simply because they operate inside prison. The complete scheme must be considered: defined misconduct, an impartial adjudicative process, valid evidence and the relationship between guilt and a significant sanction can confine official authority even when officials retain discretion to select a commensurate penalty afterward. Wolff distinguishes substantial protected liberty from routine management and supplies the separate inquiry into appropriate process. Mere mandatory administration, routine transfers or brief inconveniences do not automatically create entitlements. Nor is this an exclusive requirement of state codification for intrinsic constitutional liberty, an unexpected restraint or a sentence-duration interest. The Court adopts no new multifactor hardship test and leaves the ultimate application to the district court under the stated rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hewitt v. Helms: narrows mechanical reliance on mandatory wording while retaining genuine substantive-predicate liberty protection; no wholesale methodology replacement controls.
- Kentucky Department of Corrections v. Thompson: applies distinction between substantive constraints and procedural directions alone.
- Wolff v. McDonnell: preserves significant prison liberty and context-sensitive procedural protection.
- Meachum v. Fano: distinguishes ordinary sentence-authorized management from a genuine protected constraint.
- Vitek v. Jones; Washington v. Harper: preserve distinct directly protected, qualitatively different restraints; this state-entitlement rule is nonexclusive.
- Farmer v. Brennan; Helling v. McKinney: distinguish Eighth Amendment conditions liability from this procedural-liberty inquiry.

### The reviewed summary judgment and remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The summary judgment rejecting the reviewed liberty-and-process claim cannot stand on a mechanical reading of Hawaii’s regulatory words or dismissal of genuine punitive constraints. The district court must determine whether the rules genuinely constrain authority, whether the deprivation is meaningful rather than routine, and what process is due; the Court makes no liability or ultimate protected-interest finding.

**Authority:** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court and remedial disposition; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg join the bounded rule-and-remand ground. Four concurring Justices separately would find the interest already established.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the dispute requires application of the substantive rule to the complete disciplinary scheme and the actual deprivation. Hawaii’s direction to find guilt on substantial evidence is not rewritten as an express acquittal command; it must be read with the defined offenses, impartial committee, evidence rules and authority for serious sanctions. The record concerns thirty days of punitive isolation and material restriction of ordinary out-of-cell activity. Similarity to separately authorized administrative or protective confinement does not by itself eliminate the legal constraint on punishment, while punitive labeling alone does not create protected liberty. Later expungement of the serious charge does not retroactively supply adequate process. Discretionary parole consequences are not an inevitable sentence extension. The district court must make the necessary determinations and assess process if protected liberty is implicated. Wolff permits refusal of witnesses for irrelevance, lack of necessity or hazards to safety or correctional goals. No automatic witness right, useless-witness finding, damages, immunity or unrelated amended-complaint disposition follows.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wolff: applies witness qualifications and the separate process inquiry once protected liberty is implicated.
- Hewitt; Thompson: require whole-scheme substantive analysis rather than a one-way mandatory-word inference.
- Meachum: preserves ordinary authorized custody without making every punitive restraint legally unconstrained.

## Precedent Treatment

The treatment accompanying the holdings controls. Hewitt is narrowed, not wholesale replaced. The proposed atypical-and-significant-hardship framework has four dissenting votes; the concurrences’ positive application has four votes. Neither controls through Marks.

## Law After Decision

The five-Justice sufficient state-entitlement rule and bounded remand govern. The Court does not hold that protected liberty is absent or already established on this record. Other direct constitutional, unexpected-restraint and sentence-duration protections are not displaced; unrelated claims and defenses remain. No new multifactor prison test is adopted.

## Separate Writings

Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, concurs. They would recognize protected liberty already on the actual punitive confinement record and also preserve an independent constitutional retained-liberty ground. Severe imposed isolation and its punitive character matter without depending on how a State drafts its code; administrative similarity and later expungement do not retroactively remove the need for process. They join the Court’s sufficient state-entitlement rule because it does not make codification necessary or replace intrinsic liberty with an exclusive hardship prerequisite. Breyer, joined by Souter, also concurs. The full defined-misconduct, impartial-decision and valid-evidence scheme constrains serious punishment, and the thirty-day isolation materially restricts ordinary activity. They would find the protected interest already established, while accepting the Court’s narrower remand and leaving actual procedural adequacy open. O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. They would replace the mandatory-regulation methodology with a rule ordinarily limiting state-created interests to atypical and significant hardship relative to ordinary prison life, while preserving direct unexpected-restraint and inevitable sentence-duration interests. They find the reviewed thirty-day confinement insufficient under that rule in light of comparable administrative and protective confinement and discretionary parole. Their proposed framework receives four votes and does not control. The concurrences’ affirmative application likewise has four votes; no Marks aggregation makes it the Court’s application holding.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit is affirmed in the reviewed respect and the case remanded. The district court shall determine whether Hawaii rules genuinely constrain officials’ authority to impose punitive segregation, whether the deprivation is meaningful rather than routine, and what process is due. The Court makes no liability or final interest finding; no unrelated amended-complaint claim is swept into the disposition. Properly framed ensuing proceedings remain available.

## Holdings

### The jury decides application of the materiality element

**Holding and operative rule:** When materiality is an element of the charged criminal false-statement offense, the Fifth and Sixth Amendments require the jury to determine it beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge states statutory meaning and the governing legal standard; labeling its application a mixed question does not remove an element from the jury.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices adopt the element-allocation rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the jury must decide every element necessary to establish criminal guilt, including application of an instructed legal standard to the charged facts. Winship supplies proof beyond a reasonable doubt; the constitutional jury guarantee places that determination with the jury. Materiality asks whether a statement has a natural tendency or capacity to influence the relevant governmental decision. That application does not become an abstract legal question simply because legal instruction is needed. Historical practice and functional convenience cannot reserve an offense element to the judge. Sinclair’s contrary criminal pertinency allocation cannot stand to that extent. Kungys concerns a civil denaturalization proceeding and a different appellate allocation, not this criminal jury guarantee. Here the Government concedes materiality is an element and did not challenge the lower court’s error classifications. Those premises preserve this reversal without deciding whether every section 1001 clause includes materiality or every withheld element is structural error. Statutory interpretation and threshold evidentiary sufficiency remain judicial functions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- In re Winship: applies proof beyond a reasonable doubt to facts establishing guilt.
- Sinclair v. United States: overruled to the extent it assigns a criminal pertinency element to the judge; unrelated propositions are not displaced.
- Kungys v. United States: distinguishes civil denaturalization and its appellate allocation.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana: preserves the actual existing constitutional jury/burden holding; this case creates no universal omitted-element error classification.
- Hubbard v. United States: preserves §1001 entity coverage at its actual scope; supplies no authority to remove HUD offense elements from a jury.

The decision does not determine abstract statutory meaning by jury, classify every omitted-element error, adjudicate exact unverified trial-preservation history, or disturb a separately treated equity-skimming conviction or calculate its sentence.

## Precedent Treatment

The treatment accompanying the holding controls; only the incompatible criminal-element allocation of Sinclair is displaced.

## Law After Decision

A jury must apply the legal materiality standard beyond a reasonable doubt where materiality is an element. No universal materiality requirement for every §1001 clause or universal structural-error rule is created.

## Separate Writings

Thomas, joined by O’Connor and Breyer, concurs while joining the Court in full. The Government’s concession establishes materiality as an element for this case; the opinion need not construe every §1001 clause. The unchallenged lower error classifications do not settle harmless error, plain error or structural error for all later cases. These reservations qualify the questions decided, not the jury-element holding.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit is affirmed and the reviewed false-statement convictions remain vacated. The Government may retry under instructions submitting every element, including materiality, to the jury. This is not an acquittal or a disposition of the separately treated equity-skimming conviction. This merits review is complete.

## Holdings

### Foreign arbitration costs and COGSA nonwaiver

**Holding and operative rule:** COGSA §3(8) does not invalidate the Tokyo arbitration clause merely because the selected forum increases inconvenience or enforcement expense. The FAA does not override nonwaivable COGSA substantive protection; the statutes operate together while that protection remains available.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven participating Justices adopt this statutory coexistence ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a change in forum is not itself a surrender of the carrier obligations Congress protected. COGSA invalidates contractual relief from or lessening of specified liability; it does not guarantee the cheapest forum for enforcing those obligations. The FAA separately enforces maritime arbitration agreements. Those commands can coexist without treating either as an implied repeal or giving arbitration automatic priority over statutory rights. Mitsubishi preserves the distinction between submitting a federal claim to arbitration and prospectively waiving the protected entitlement. Indussa’s increased-cost inference for foreign courts is not adopted for this arbitration clause; its arbitration reservation matters here. Allied-Bruce, Mastrobuono and First Options retain their actual coverage, agreement and judicial-review limits, without deciding COGSA. The certified question permits rejecting the categorical foreign-arbitration objection. It permits no reopening of the excluded adhesion defense or adjudication of cargo liability. True foreign-court selection clauses, practical foreclosure on a materially different record and demonstrated substantive waiver remain outside this holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc.: applies forum/substantive-right distinction and preserves its prospective-waiver reservation.
- Indussa Corp. v. S.S. Ranborg: rejects its costs inference for this arbitration clause; reserves its application to true foreign-court clauses.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson; Mastrobuono v. Shearson Lehman Hutton; First Options of Chicago v. Kaplan: preserve actual FAA coverage, consent, choice-of-law and review limits, without overriding COGSA.

### Retained jurisdiction and a concrete statutory-waiver objection

**Holding and operative rule:** The present record does not establish that arbitration will diminish nonwaivable COGSA protection. The district court must retain the action during arbitration; the stay neither authorizes prospective waiver nor forecloses a properly supported statutory objection through the remedial authority lawfully available at enforcement.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; the same seven Justices support the retained-jurisdiction rule and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unrendered award and an uncertain choice of substantive law cannot be treated as an adjudicated surrender of federal protection. No arbitrator has yet selected the liability rule or decided the claim. The Japanese-law and stowage-defense arguments therefore do not establish actual foreign law, an arbitral finding or diminished carrier liability. Retaining the domestic action distinguishes enforcement of a forum agreement from acceptance of whatever substantive limit an award might later impose. Mitsubishi’s public-policy reservation requires confronting a proven tandem waiver when no lawful later protection remains; an already unambiguous surrender could not be cured merely by postponing review. That premise is not established here. The district court may consider a concrete statutory objection through the enforcement authority properly invoked. The Court promises neither plenary reconsideration of arbitral merits nor COGSA recovery. It determines no hypothetical award, foreign defense, final cargo liability or nondelegable-duty question on this record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mitsubishi Motors: preserves a concrete prospective-waiver/public-policy objection while distinguishing the presently speculative reduction.
- First Options: retains the actual bounds on judicial review and agreement; does not guarantee unlimited review of arbitral merits.

COGSA’s enumerated duties and notice/suit periods do not lose protection merely because ordinary forum expense differs. The Court neither dismisses the action, rewrites the agreement, determines cargo-loss merits nor decides a future award.

## Precedent Treatment

The treatment in each holding controls. No categorical FAA supremacy, automatic foreign-arbitration invalidity or general foreign-court-clause rule follows.

## Law After Decision

The statutes coexist on this present record. The district court retains the action and lawful enforcement authority; nonwaivable protection is not contracted away.

## Separate Writings

Stevens dissents. Foreign arbitration can practically lessen liability by increasing expense and placing a cargo claimant with limited bargaining power in a distant forum. Knott and COGSA’s nonwaiver protection favor preserving effective enforcement; Indussa’s cost reasoning and statutory stare decisis weigh against treating those burdens as merely procedural. His objection concerns the statutory consequence of the clause, not reopening the separately rejected adhesion issue. He would reverse the stay without adjudicating the cargo loss.

## Procedure After Action

The First Circuit’s stay is affirmed and the case remanded. The district court shall retain the underlying action while arbitration proceeds. It may address a concrete COGSA-waiver objection through the remedial authority then properly invoked. No cargo liability or arbitral award is determined, and no plenary arbitral merits review is guaranteed.
