### Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, No. 91-194

Merits decision, May 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

504 U.S. 298.

Argued January 22, 1992; decided May 26, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the North Dakota Supreme Court, 470 N.W.2d 203, which reversed summary judgment for Quill and directed enforcement of the State's collection duty. Quill challenges that duty under the Commerce and Due Process Clauses and seeks reversal of liability for tax, interest and penalties sought from July 1, 1987. The potential retroactive consequences of overruling Bellas Hess were excluded from the grant.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 8–1. Bellas Hess’s surviving Commerce Clause collection rule bars the challenged duty on this record. All nine Justices reject a physical-presence requirement under Due Process; Stone-Zsela joins that independent holding while relying on the separate Commerce rule to reverse the collection judgment.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enforceability of Quill collection duty | Reverse and remand, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | White | The challenged collection liability fails on this record under the Commerce Clause. |
| Due-process challenge | Rejected, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | None | This ruling supplies no collection liability in light of the separate Commerce disposition. |

The consumer’s use-tax duty and other taxes remain outside the judgment. The Court creates no retrospective liability, tax amount, interest award or penalty; the challenge to this duty succeeds. The state-court §1983/§1988 cross-appeal, left unaddressed below after its constitutional ruling, receives no Supreme Court merits decision or fee award and may be handled in lawful further proceedings.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Purposeful contacts can satisfy due process without physical presence

**Controlling proposition:** A mail-order seller that purposefully and continuously solicits a State’s consumers may have the minimum connection required for a use-tax collection obligation even without physical presence, when the obligation is rationally related to benefits received through that market. Quill’s sustained solicitation and approximately $1 million in annual North Dakota sales satisfy due process; Bellas Hess and earlier holdings are overruled only insofar as they make physical presence indispensable for this collection duty.

**Authority:** Parts I–III of Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine Justices support the Due Process holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deliberate, continuing participation in the State’s market supplies the fair connection required here. Miller Brothers supplies the requirement of a definite link between the State and the person or transaction, while International Shoe explains why purposeful contacts matter more than a formal presence label. Burger King confirms that commercial activity deliberately directed to a State does not escape its authority solely because the seller communicates from elsewhere; Shaffer applies the same rejection of mechanical presence rules to adjudicative jurisdiction. Comparable reasoning governs this collection obligation without equating every rule for judicial jurisdiction with every rule for legislative authority. Quill repeatedly solicits North Dakota customers and conducts substantial sales there, and the use tax relates to the benefits of access to that market. The contrary physical-presence prerequisite cannot survive that reasoning. Due process and the Commerce Clause impose distinct limits, however, so satisfying this connection does not itself authorize the collection duty that the Court rejects on the other ground.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — overruled only to the extent its due-process holding requires physical presence for use-tax collection; its Commerce rule is treated separately below.
- Miller Brothers v. Maryland, 347 U.S.340 — the minimum-connection requirement remains; any indispensable physical-entry implication is displaced at the stated collection scope.
- International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S.310 — applied for purposeful affiliation and fair connection.
- Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S.462 — its nonphysical purposeful-contact reasoning is extended by analogy to the collection obligation; every personal-jurisdiction rule is not imported.
- Shaffer v. Heitner, 433 U.S.186 — applied by comparable reasoning against a mechanical presence prerequisite.
- Moorman Manufacturing Co. v. Bair, 437 U.S.267 — the requirement that the tax relate rationally to state-connected values remains.

##### 2. The specific Commerce collection precedent remains controlling

**Controlling proposition:** Bellas Hess’s specific Commerce Clause rule continues to bar imposing a sales-or-use-tax collection duty on a seller whose customer connections are limited to mail or common-carrier commerce without the requisite local presence. Quill’s solicitation and remote deliveries do not themselves displace that rule; Complete Auto’s separate substantial-nexus requirement is not satisfied merely by purposeful market access or the due-process showing.

**Authority:** The common, narrower ground expressly supported by Stevens, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter in Part IV, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas in their concurrence, and Stone-Zsela in his concurrence in the judgment; eight Justices retain the particular precedent. The full Part IV substantive policy analysis has four joins and is not an Opinion of the Court. The software application has the separate five-Justice authority identified below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that its specific collection precedent remains in force. Complete Auto abandoned a formal immunity based on how a State labels its tax, but it preserved substantial nexus as a distinct condition and did not overrule Bellas Hess’s mail-order collection boundary. National Geographic and D.H. Holmes distinguished that precedent rather than announcing its abandonment. The State’s account of economic growth and easier calculation presents a substantial argument for change, but it does not remove the particular precedent or the reliance of sellers that arranged distribution and completed transactions under it. Adherence to precedent supports retaining that defined boundary, while Congress can make a coordinated adjustment to collection obligations. Adherence does not require every Justice to endorse the precedent’s original substantive reasoning. This ground does not extend physical presence to every tax or establish a quantitative sales threshold. It preserves the particular rule and the independent constitutional limits that any different measure must satisfy.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — retained for its specific Commerce Clause sales-and-use collection rule; no general immunity for interstate commerce is restored.
- Complete Auto Transit v. Brady, 430 U.S.274 — substantial nexus remains distinct from fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services; its rejection of Spector’s labeling rule remains.
- National Geographic Society v. California Board of Equalization, 430 U.S.551, and D.H. Holmes Co. v. McNamara, 486 U.S.24 — their express distinctions of Bellas Hess preserve reasonable reliance on the particular collection precedent rather than establish its abandonment.
- Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S.164, and Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S.808 — their congressional-correction and reliance principles support the narrower adherence ground at the stated scope.
- Prudential Insurance Co. v. Benjamin, 328 U.S.408 — preserves Congress’s power to authorize otherwise dormant-Commerce burdens; Congress cannot authorize a due-process violation.

##### 3. The limited software interests do not establish sufficient collection nexus

**Controlling proposition:** Quill’s insignificant interests in the licensed ordering software on this record do not supply the local commercial presence required to take its mail-order sales outside the retained Commerce Clause collection rule. This conclusion rejects the State’s asserted alternative nexus for this seller without prescribing a general rule for all software, licenses or in-state property.

**Authority:** Stevens, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter in Part IV, together with Stone-Zsela’s expressly matching record-specific software ground in his concurrence in the judgment; five Justices adopt this application at the same scope. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support reversal but are not counted as joining this substantive software explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the limited ordering-software interests do not establish the local commercial activity missing from this record. Bellas Hess supplies the specific collection boundary that the Court retains, and Tyler Pipe identifies the significance of activity within the State that establishes and maintains the seller’s market. Quill’s licensed ordering materials facilitate communication with its out-of-state business; the insignificant property interest does not turn those materials into the local business operation or representative activity on which the State’s alternative depends. The argument that any retained property must end the inquiry gives the bare ownership label more significance than this record warrants. The Court rejects that alternative on the record before it and does not find an unproved warehouse, employee or agent. Nor does it define a universal property-value threshold or decide that every software license is insufficient. Its holding concerns these limited interests and this challenged collection demand alone.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — applied to this mail-order collection demand after rejection of the asserted distinguishing property interest.
- Tyler Pipe Industries v. Washington Department of Revenue, 483 U.S.232 — distinguished for actual in-state market-maintaining activity, including representatives; no employee-only nexus requirement is adopted.
- National Geographic Society v. California Board of Equalization, 430 U.S.551 — Part IV’s additional rejection of a slightest-presence standard supports its four signers’ explanation; Stone-Zsela’s fifth vote adopts the stated record-specific insufficiency without a whole-opinion join. Its local-office holding remains undisturbed.

**Fractured authority:** There is an express majority for the particular surviving-precedent rule, not merely eight matching dispositions. Marks v. United States, 430 U.S.188, does not authorize importing the plurality’s broader economic-policy theory through the concurring votes. The narrower adherence ground is a genuine common ground of the reversal writings and a logical subset of Part IV’s precedent-based reason for the same result. The record-specific software application independently has the five expressly identified supporters. Neither conclusion creates a general physical-presence theory for other taxes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The consumer’s use tax, other taxes and general tax immunity are outside the holding. No numerical transaction threshold, general software-nexus rule or result under a future statute is announced. Potential retroactive effects of overruling the Commerce rule were excluded from the grant and are not reached. The Court makes no §1983 or §1988 entitlement ruling. Stone-Zsela reserves due process; the eight-Justice due-process holding nevertheless controls.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Bellas Hess has distinct present force: the due-process physical-presence prerequisite is overruled at the collection scope, while the Commerce collection rule remains. Complete Auto’s four separate requirements and rejection of Spector’s formal tax-label immunity survive. National Geographic, D.H. Holmes and Tyler Pipe retain the local-activity applications described above. The ground based on adherence to precedent does not constitutionalize a particular technology or sales threshold. Wyoming’s discriminatory-coal-quota and congressional-authorization holdings remain separate; no collection-nexus holding is taken from them. Hilton’s FELA coverage holding also remains separate; its limited treatment of precedent supplies no automatic fiscal rule. No other earlier holding is overruled here.

Effective May 26, the two constitutional inquiries must be kept separate. Purposeful sustained solicitation on this record satisfies due process without physical presence; Congress cannot dispense with that constitutional limit. Bellas Hess’s specific dormant-Commerce collection rule continues to bar this demand, including the asserted minimal software basis. Congress remains able to change dormant-Commerce constraints through valid legislation. Before this decision Bellas Hess supplied both grounds; afterward its due-process physical-presence requirement is displaced while its specific Commerce rule survives. The broad Part IV account of bright-line rules and national economic burdens has four joins; only the narrower expressly shared precedent ground controls across the eight reversal votes. Neither consumer liability nor every other state tax changes.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, joins the Court’s due-process analysis on the understanding that analogous reasoning does not equate every adjudicative and legislative jurisdiction standard. On Commerce, they retain the square precedent through adherence to precedent, justified reliance and Congress’s authority, without revisiting its substantive merits. Stone-Zsela separately retains the specific collection rule within Complete Auto and concludes that insignificant software is no substitute for local market-maintaining activity, while joining the Court's Due Process analysis because purposeful sustained market contacts satisfy that independent constitutional requirement. His reliance and congressional-adjustment reasons support adherence without extending a physical-presence condition to all taxation. White joins the due-process ruling but would overrule Bellas Hess’s Commerce rule: sustained economic exploitation and market benefits should meet nexus, and the physical distinction creates unjustified competitive differences. He also questions deciding the software issue against the State without further inquiry. The plurality’s broader defense of the Commerce boundary commands four votes only.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with reversal and remand to North Dakota for further proceedings consistent with rejection of the challenged collection duty on this record. No tax, interest or penalty amount is imposed; no retrospective authorization is created. The unaddressed state-court civil-rights and fees cross-appeal remains subject to lawful further proceedings without a Supreme Court entitlement finding. The consumer’s use-tax liability is untouched. No new filing, deadline, enforcement finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is established.

### Itel Containers International Corp. v. Huddleston, No. 91-321

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

Argued October 14, 1992. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. 1257 to the Tennessee Supreme Court, 814 S.W.2d 29 (1991), which affirmed rejection of Itel's federal objections after the Chancery Court reduced the assessment under state law. Itel seeks a federal-law refund; Tennessee seeks affirmance. The questions concern the Container Conventions and federal customs rules, the Foreign Commerce Clause and the Import-Export Clause.

Itel is a domestic container lessor whose containers are leased exclusively for international use and delivered at its Memphis and designated third-party Tennessee terminals. The tax attaches to delivery transferring possession, rather than importation. The original $382,465 assessment of tax, penalties and interest for January 1983 through November 1986 was reduced to $158,012 under state law. That reduction is not reopened. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The Court affirms, 8–1. Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support affirmance and reject the treaty and regulatory challenges; Blackmun dissents and would invalidate the tax under the Conventions. On the Foreign Commerce challenge, Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas adopt the Court's rationale, Scalia agrees in the judgment, and Blackmun would invalidate the tax. On the Import-Export challenge, Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas adopt the Court's rationale and Scalia rejects the challenge on a separate ground. Blackmun's proposed reversal rests on other grounds without an independent Import-Export disposition. No additional federal-law refund is required on any of the presented grounds; the existing state-law reduction remains.

Kennedy delivers the opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. Scalia joins Parts I–III and VI, including the treaty and regulatory reasoning and the conclusion, and concurs only in the judgment on Parts IV–V, addressing the constitutional grounds. Eight Justices therefore support the treaty and regulatory reasoning, and seven support the Foreign Commerce and Import-Export reasoning. Scalia writes separately, joined by no other Justice. Blackmun dissents alone on treaty construction and, independently, foreign-commerce uniformity. Itel accepts compliance with Complete Auto; those requirements are not independently relitigated, and the Court makes no new due-process holding.

The 1956 and 1972 Container Conventions prohibit direct or indirect taxes imposed by reason of, or in connection with, importation of qualifying temporarily admitted containers, rather than every tax involving those containers. Tennessee's nondiscriminatory tax on the transfer of possession under a local lease is not such an importation charge; the 1972 definition also excludes fees and charges limited to the approximate cost of services rendered.

Kennedy's treaty portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. These eight Justices adopt this construction and application.

The Court holds that the reason for imposing the tax controls, not the historical reason why the container is present in Tennessee. Air France v. Saks directs attention first to treaty text. Both Conventions connect the exemption to importation, and removing that connection would make their limiting words ineffective. Qualifying containers receive temporary admission subject to reexportation within three months unless customs authorities extend the period; the protection does not establish permanent tax immunity for their commercial use. Itel's lease proceeds are taxed because possession changes in Tennessee, without discrimination between domestic and foreign goods. Other nations' practices deserve attention under Factor v. Laubenheimer, but their use of different tax systems does not establish the asserted exemption. Direct and indirect taxes alike are prohibited when they attach to importation; neither form is prohibited merely because a container contributes to taxable commercial value. The Executive's supporting interpretation is persuasive support, not a substitute for construing the Conventions.

Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392 (1985): applied for treaty interpretation beginning with text while considering relevant context.

Factor v. Laubenheimer, 290 U.S. 276 (1933): applied for consideration of treaty parties' practical construction; the practice asserted here does not displace the operative importation limitation.

The Container Conventions, 19 U.S.C. 1322 and their implementing regulations do not occupy all container taxation or preempt a nondiscriminatory local lease tax that neither taxes importation nor obstructs the federal temporary-admission policy. Tennessee's tax therefore survives both the asserted field-preemption and obstacle-preemption grounds; the distinct federal rules governing bonded warehouses do not confer the same exemption on these lease transactions.

Kennedy's regulatory-preemption portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. These eight Justices adopt the stated rejection of both grounds.

The Court holds that the scope of the federal arrangement must be identified before state taxation can be displaced. Hines v. Davidowitz supplies the obstacle inquiry, but the pertinent federal objective is protected temporary admission of containers, not freedom from every ordinary local tax. McGoldrick and Xerox involved a different arrangement: goods held under continuous customs supervision for reexport, with duty treatment intended to encourage use of American facilities. Taxing those goods could defeat that particular benefit. The container rules instead concern admission, certification and specified customs treatment, and Tennessee's local lease tax leaves those protections intact. Nor does extensive federal regulation alone establish occupation of the entire field. R. J. Reynolds rejected that inference even for the more detailed warehouse regime. The Court rejects both the alleged obstacle and the asserted complete occupation without denying that another tax could conflict with federal law. Nondiscrimination is relevant here but does not by itself answer every preemption question.

Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941): applied for conflict with federal purposes; no such obstacle established here.

McGoldrick v. Gulf Oil Corp., 309 U.S. 414 (1940): distinguished because the bonded-warehouse benefit and supervision differ from container leasing.

Xerox Corp. v. County of Harris, 459 U.S. 145 (1982): distinguished on the same specific federal warehouse purpose; its protection remains.

R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Durham County, 479 U.S. 130 (1986): applied against inferring complete field occupation from detailed customs regulation.

In addition to Complete Auto's substantial nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services, a tax affecting foreign commerce must avoid a substantial risk of international multiple taxation and interference with the Nation's ability to speak with one voice. With Complete Auto compliance conceded here, Tennessee's discrete local transaction and credit for tax properly paid to another domestic or foreign jurisdiction on that same transaction, together with the demonstrated federal arrangement, satisfy those additional requirements; Executive support informs but does not decide their application.

Kennedy's foreign-commerce portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. These seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia agrees only in the judgment on this issue; Blackmun dissents.

The Court holds that potential foreign taxing authority alone does not make a local transaction constitutionally untaxable. Japan Line supplies separate multiple-taxation and national-uniformity inquiries; domestic ownership does not eliminate them. Container Corp. distinguishes unavoidable overlapping taxation from the mere possibility that another sovereign could tax, while Wardair recognizes the significance of a discrete local transaction. Tennessee taxes delivery under the lease and credits tax properly paid elsewhere on the same event, reducing the relevant duplication risk. Itel accepts the lower court's Complete Auto determination, so the Court does not independently reconsider those four requirements. The tax also fits the actual treaty, statutory and regulatory arrangement and does not conflict with a demonstrated international practice requiring exemption of this transaction. The United States' submission supports that conclusion but cannot authorize an otherwise unconstitutional tax. The absence of a demonstrated foreign conflict supports this application; it does not reduce the one-voice inquiry to express statutory preemption or dispense with apportionment in another case.

Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274 (1977): its four requirements remain mandatory; their satisfaction is conceded in this case.

Japan Line, Ltd. v. County of Los Angeles, 441 U.S. 434 (1979): retained and distinguished; foreign-owned property taxed fully abroad presented a different multiple-taxation and uniformity problem.

Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 159 (1983): applied to distinguish inevitable duplication from potential foreign taxation and to assess foreign-policy evidence.

Wardair Canada Inc. v. Florida Department of Revenue, 477 U.S. 1 (1986): applied for the significance of a discrete local transaction and the actual federal arrangement.

Under Michelin, this nondiscriminatory local lease tax does not offend national commercial uniformity, divert federal import revenue or disrupt interstate harmony, and therefore does not violate the Import-Export Clause. The tax attaches to temporary possession transferred in Tennessee, rather than to the containers or their cargo as imports or exports in transit; the Court leaves unresolved whether Michelin otherwise altered Richfield's rule for direct taxation of goods in transit.

Kennedy's Import-Export portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. These seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia concurs only in this component's judgment on a distinct export-status ground; Blackmun does not supply a separate Import-Export disposition.

The Court holds that the Import-Export Clause requires its own analysis even when the Commerce Clause challenge fails. Michelin identifies protection of national commercial policy, federal import revenue and harmony among States. The national-policy inquiry is satisfied for the reasons already stated, and Washington Stevedoring explains why a properly connected, apportioned and evenhanded charge for local commercial activity respects interstate harmony. The revenue concern also fails here because Tennessee collects revenue from a local transfer of possession, not from importing goods. Richfield involved a direct tax upon the export transaction in goods already committed to export. This lease tax falls on a distinct service and its proceeds. The fact that a container will carry goods abroad does not transform every earlier local commercial event into an import duty or export impost. The Court need not decide how Michelin affects other direct taxes on goods in transit, and it neither overrules Richfield nor grants a general exemption from constitutional review.

Michelin Tire Corp. v. Wages, 423 U.S. 276 (1976): applied through its three constitutional concerns.

Department of Revenue v. Association of Washington Stevedoring Cos., 435 U.S. 734 (1978): applied for distinct local services and the interstate-harmony inquiry.

Richfield Oil Corp. v. State Board of Equalization, 329 U.S. 69 (1946): distinguished; its direct-export rule is not overruled or finally reconciled with every implication of Michelin.

These holdings govern this transaction effective February 23, 1993. The preexisting general rules and limited temporary-admission regime now receive a controlling construction and application to a nondiscriminatory domestic container-lease transaction. Complete Auto remains mandatory; Japan Line's two additional inquiries remain independent; federal preemption remains distinct from constitutional review; and Michelin's three concerns govern the Import-Export holding. Neither domestic ownership nor Executive support eliminates the foreign-commerce inquiry. No general local-tax immunity, apportionment exemption, express-conflict-only rule or overruling of Richfield follows. No precedent is overruled.

Scalia joins the treaty and regulatory holdings but agrees only in the judgment on the constitutional grounds. He would enforce a negative Commerce Clause, as a matter of precedent, against facial discrimination and laws indistinguishable from a type previously invalidated, without extending open-ended balancing tests. This tax fits neither prohibition. His Import-Export analysis preserves Richfield's textual protection but treats these empty containers as not yet exports when delivered for use in domestic loading; he therefore does not reach Michelin's broader test. His criticism of allowing an Executive litigation position to determine state taxing authority does not alter the Court's independent constitutional holding.

Blackmun would construe the Conventions in light of their purpose and the signatories' practice to protect these recurring transfers as integral to international container movement. He distinguishes a tax on container leases from a value-added tax on imported goods and regards the local-transaction distinction as insufficient. Independently, he would find that the state tax threatens the uniform treatment protected by Japan Line. The Executive's brief cannot itself authorize a tax, and congressional silence does not supply unmistakably clear authorization to override Commerce constraints. Those treaty and foreign-commerce positions support his proposed reversal; they are not Court law.

Scalia's prospective doctrine is limited by his assumption, rather than decision, that precedent's facial-discrimination rule applies equally to foreign commerce. His writing retains the separate textual Import-Export inquiry and does not reduce it to nondiscrimination. Blackmun's treaty-purpose construction is independently sufficient for his proposed reversal, and his foreign-commerce ground remains additional. All these propositions are noncontrolling.

Supreme Court review is complete. Tennessee's judgment and the state-law reduction stand, and no additional federal-law refund is required. The Court orders no remand or new tax computation, makes no penalty ruling, and retains no proceeding. Future assessments remain subject to the stated constitutional and federal-law requirements.
