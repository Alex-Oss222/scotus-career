### Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, No. 91-194

Merits decision, May 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

504 U.S. 298.

Argued January 22, 1992; decided May 26, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the North Dakota Supreme Court, 470 N.W.2d 203, which reversed summary judgment for Quill and directed enforcement of the State's collection duty. Quill challenges that duty under the Commerce and Due Process Clauses and seeks reversal of liability for tax, interest and penalties sought from July 1, 1987. The potential retroactive consequences of overruling Bellas Hess were excluded from the grant.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The judgment is vacated and the case remanded, 5–4 on the Commerce Clause. Bellas Hess’s physical-presence collection rule is overruled; Complete Auto’s substantial-nexus requirement governs the collection duty, and Quill’s deliberate and regular service of the North Dakota market satisfies it, subject to the separate protection against excessive collection burdens and to lawful temporal and remedial requirements. All nine Justices reject a physical-presence requirement under Due Process.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enforceability of Quill collection duty | Vacate and remand, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Bellas Hess’s physical-presence rule does not bar the duty, and substantial nexus exists on this record; the remaining Commerce objections and lawful temporal and remedial requirements are for further proceedings. |
| Due-process challenge | Rejected, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | None | Due process does not bar the collection duty; the Commerce Clause inquiry is separate. |

The consumer’s use-tax duty and other taxes remain outside the judgment. The Court enters no assessment and creates no tax amount, interest award, retroactive penalty or retrospective liability. North Dakota may establish a constitutionally valid collection obligation, subject to the remaining Commerce objections and to lawful temporal and remedial requirements, including ordinary limitations periods, notice requirements and independent defenses; liability for periods before this decision is left to those further proceedings. The state-court §1983/§1988 cross-appeal, left unaddressed below after its constitutional ruling, receives no Supreme Court merits decision or fee award and may be handled in lawful further proceedings.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Purposeful contacts can satisfy due process without physical presence

**Controlling proposition:** A mail-order seller that purposefully and continuously solicits a State’s consumers may have the minimum connection required for a use-tax collection obligation even without physical presence, when the obligation is rationally related to benefits received through that market. Quill’s sustained solicitation and approximately $1 million in annual North Dakota sales satisfy due process; Bellas Hess and earlier holdings are overruled only insofar as they make physical presence indispensable for this collection duty.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine Justices join this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deliberate, continuing participation in the State’s market supplies the fair connection required here. Miller Brothers supplies the requirement of a definite link between the State and the person or transaction, while International Shoe explains why purposeful contacts matter more than a formal presence label. Burger King confirms that commercial activity deliberately directed to a State does not escape its authority solely because the seller communicates from elsewhere; Shaffer applies the same rejection of mechanical presence rules to adjudicative jurisdiction. Comparable reasoning governs this collection obligation without equating every rule for judicial jurisdiction with every rule for legislative authority. Quill repeatedly solicits North Dakota customers and conducts substantial sales there, and the use tax relates to the benefits of access to that market. The contrary physical-presence prerequisite cannot survive that reasoning. Due process and the Commerce Clause impose distinct limits, however, so satisfying this connection does not itself satisfy the separate requirements of the Commerce Clause.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — overruled only to the extent its due-process holding requires physical presence for use-tax collection; its Commerce rule is treated separately below.
- Miller Brothers v. Maryland, 347 U.S.340 — the minimum-connection requirement remains; any indispensable physical-entry implication is displaced at the stated collection scope.
- International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S.310 — applied for purposeful affiliation and fair connection.
- Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S.462 — its nonphysical purposeful-contact reasoning is extended by analogy to the collection obligation; every personal-jurisdiction rule is not imported.
- Shaffer v. Heitner, 433 U.S.186 — applied by comparable reasoning against a mechanical presence prerequisite.
- Moorman Manufacturing Co. v. Bair, 437 U.S.267 — the requirement that the tax relate rationally to state-connected values remains.

##### 2. Complete Auto’s substantial-nexus requirement governs collection duties without a physical-presence rule

**Controlling proposition:** Bellas Hess’s Commerce Clause rule barring a sales-or-use-tax collection duty on a seller whose only connection with customers in the State is by mail or common carrier is overruled. Complete Auto controls: the collection obligation must rest on substantial nexus with the taxing State and must satisfy fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to services the State provides. For a collection obligation, substantial nexus exists when a seller deliberately and regularly serves the State’s market through economically substantial transactions; accidental, isolated or incidental transactions do not suffice. Physical presence is relevant evidence of that connection but is not necessary to it. Quill’s sustained solicitation of North Dakota customers by catalogs, advertising and telephone, and its approximately $1 million in annual sales to about 3,000 customers there, satisfy substantial nexus.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun and Souter; five Justices. O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas dissent from this Part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Bellas Hess’s physical-presence line cannot be reconciled with the analysis Complete Auto adopted. Complete Auto rejected Spector’s rule that a tax’s label decides its validity and asks instead about practical effect, while keeping substantial nexus as a distinct requirement. That requirement asks whether the seller’s own activity connects it to the taxing State in a real and substantial way. A seller that deliberately and regularly directs its business to a State’s consumers and completes economically substantial transactions there has that connection whether it reaches them through a salesperson, a catalog or a telephone call. Physical presence remains strong evidence of nexus, as National Geographic, D.H. Holmes and Tyler Pipe illustrate, but making it indispensable rewards the form of a seller’s contact rather than its substance. The State’s account of economic growth and easier calculation is not the reason for the change; the doctrinal inconsistency is, and Parts III and IV separately address the compliance burdens and reliance that Bellas Hess protected. Quill’s continuous solicitation and roughly $1 million in annual sales are neither accidental nor incidental.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — overruled insofar as its Commerce Clause rule makes physical presence necessary for a sales-or-use-tax collection duty; its concern with multiple compliance obligations is addressed in Part III.
- Complete Auto Transit v. Brady, 430 U.S.274 — applied as the controlling framework; substantial nexus remains distinct from fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services, and its rejection of Spector’s labeling rule remains.
- National Geographic Society v. California Board of Equalization, 430 U.S.551; D.H. Holmes Co. v. McNamara, 486 U.S.24; and Tyler Pipe Industries v. Washington Department of Revenue, 483 U.S.232 — their local offices, employees, representatives and market-maintaining activity remain sufficient evidence of nexus; they are not read to make physical presence necessary.
- Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S.164, and Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S.808 — applied: adherence to precedent does not require retaining a constitutional rule whose underpinnings later doctrine has removed, and the reliance it created is protected through the independent remedial and temporal requirements described in Part IV.
- Prudential Insurance Co. v. Benjamin, 328 U.S.408 — Congress retains power to authorize or limit state collection duties; that power supports but does not compel adherence to the overruled rule, and Congress cannot authorize a due-process violation.

##### 3. Excessive collection burdens remain subject to independent review

**Controlling proposition:** A seller with substantial nexus may still challenge a collection obligation as an excessive burden on interstate commerce. The seller must first make a prima facie showing of substantial compliance costs, overlapping jurisdictional requirements, materially inconsistent rules or other genuine obstacles arising from the obligation as it actually operates; ordinary bookkeeping expense does not suffice. The State must then show that its requirements reasonably serve legitimate revenue and enforcement interests and that substantially less burdensome, reasonably available mechanisms would not adequately serve those interests. Courts examine actual burdens rather than hypothetical ones.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun and Souter; five Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that rejecting a physical-presence rule does not leave interstate sellers without protection against the burdens Bellas Hess identified. One State’s collection duty may be modest; the same duty multiplied across States and localities with differing definitions, rates, filing schedules and audit demands may impose costs that local sellers do not bear and that reduced computerized calculation does not eliminate. Pike v. Bruce Church already asks whether a burden on interstate commerce is clearly excessive in relation to local benefits and whether the local interest could be promoted as well with a lesser impact. Applied to collection obligations, that inquiry places the first showing on the seller, which knows its own costs, and the justification on the State, which designed the system and controls the alternatives, such as uniform definitions, consolidated filing or simplified administration. Ordinary recordkeeping that any retailer bears does not shift that burden. The inquiry concerns actual burdens shown on a record, not speculation, and the remand permits Quill to attempt the showing.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc., 397 U.S.137 — its burden and lesser-alternative inquiry is applied to collection obligations through the stated allocation of showings.
- National Bellas Hess v. Department of Revenue, 386 U.S.753 — its concern with multiple and inconsistent collection obligations is carried into an actual-burden inquiry rather than a physical-presence rule.
- Wyoming v. Oklahoma, January 22, 1992 — its requirement that a State justify discrimination by the absence of adequate reasonable alternatives informs the State’s showing; a collection duty is not thereby treated as discriminatory.
- Complete Auto Transit v. Brady, 430 U.S.274 — fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation remain separate requirements.

##### 4. Reliance limits retrospective remedies, not the governing rule

**Controlling proposition:** The Court applies its Commerce Clause holding to Quill and does not confine it to future cases. Reliance on Bellas Hess is concrete but supplies no permanent immunity from a valid collection obligation; it is protected through the remedial and temporal requirements that govern retrospective monetary consequences. Ordinary limitations periods, notice requirements and independent defenses are preserved. The substantive validity of a collection obligation is distinct from liability for taxes, interest or penalties for periods before this decision. Accrued liability was excluded from the grant and is not decided; the Court enters no assessment and authorizes no retroactive penalty.

**Authority:** Part IV of Stevens’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun and Souter; five Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that sellers that organized their distribution and completed transactions under an express collection rule have a reliance interest that matters. That interest does not require the Court to retain a constitutional rule it does not find sound, and it cannot be honored by announcing the new rule while withholding it from the litigant who obtained it; Beam rejects that kind of selective application once a rule is applied to the parties. The interest is honored where it actually operates: in the consequences of applying a collection obligation to past transactions. Whether a State may collect taxes that a seller never collected from its customers, or impose interest and penalties for conduct the governing precedent protected, is a question separate from the obligation’s validity. Ordinary limitations periods, notice requirements and independent defenses continue to apply, and McKesson recognizes that States have lawful remedial choices within constitutional limits. Because retrospective consequences were excluded from the grant, the Court decides no accrued liability. It holds that the new rule governs this case; reliance is protected by those independent requirements, not by an equitable weighing that applies the rule differently among pending cases.

**Precedent treatment:**

- James B. Beam Distilling Co. v. Georgia, 501 U.S.529 — applied: the rule announced governs the litigant before the Court, and no selective prospectivity is adopted.
- Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S.97 — its prospectivity analysis is not used to withhold the rule from this case or from other pending cases; reliance is protected only through independent remedial and temporal requirements.
- McKesson Corp. v. Division of Alcoholic Beverages and Tobacco, 496 U.S.18 — its recognition of lawful state remedial choices and procedural limits is preserved.
- Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S.808 — reliance interests are weighed in deciding whether and how to depart from precedent; here they are protected remedially.

**Authority structure:** Each Part has a direct majority: nine Justices join Part I and five join Parts II–IV. No Marks analysis is needed, and the dissenters’ agreement with Part I is a direct join.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The consumer’s use tax, other taxes and general tax immunity are outside the holding. The Court decides nexus on Quill’s actual record and does not sustain North Dakota’s three-advertisement definition as a general measure of nexus. No numerical transaction threshold, general software-nexus rule or result under future legislation is announced. Because Quill’s solicitation and sales establish substantial nexus, the Court does not decide whether its licensed ordering software would independently do so. Apportionment and discrimination were not separately contested. Accrued liability, retroactive penalties and the other retrospective consequences of overruling were excluded from the grant and are not decided. The Court makes no §1983 or §1988 entitlement ruling. No Justice reserves Due Process; the nine-Justice holding controls.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Bellas Hess is overruled at the collection scope on both grounds: its due-process physical-presence prerequisite and its Commerce Clause physical-presence collection rule. Complete Auto’s four separate requirements and its rejection of Spector’s formal tax-label immunity control. National Geographic, D.H. Holmes and Tyler Pipe remain sufficient local-activity applications of substantial nexus rather than limits on it. Pike’s burden and lesser-alternative inquiry is applied to collection obligations through Part III’s allocation of showings. Beam’s rejection of selective application is applied, Chevron Oil is not used to withhold the rule from this case, and McKesson’s remedial principles are preserved. Patterson and Payne inform the decision to overrule; Prudential preserves Congress’s power to adjust collection duties. Wyoming’s discriminatory-coal-quota and congressional-authorization holdings remain separate; its reasonable-alternatives requirement informs Part III without treating collection duties as discrimination. Hilton’s FELA coverage holding also remains separate; its limited treatment of precedent supplies no automatic fiscal rule. No other earlier holding is overruled here.

Effective May 26, the two constitutional inquiries remain separate. Purposeful sustained solicitation satisfies due process without physical presence; Congress cannot dispense with that constitutional limit. Under the Commerce Clause, Complete Auto governs sales-and-use-tax collection obligations: substantial nexus exists when a seller deliberately and regularly serves the State’s market through economically substantial transactions; physical presence is relevant evidence but not necessary; accidental, isolated or incidental transactions do not suffice. A seller that makes a prima facie showing of substantial compliance costs, overlapping jurisdictional requirements, materially inconsistent rules or other genuine obstacles shifts to the State the burden of showing that its requirements reasonably serve legitimate revenue and enforcement interests and that substantially less burdensome, reasonably available mechanisms would not adequately serve them; ordinary bookkeeping expense does not make that showing. Reliance on the overruled rule is protected through ordinary limitations periods, notice requirements, independent defenses and lawful remedial requirements, not by withholding the rule, and substantive validity remains distinct from retrospective monetary consequences. Before this decision Bellas Hess required physical presence on both grounds; afterward neither ground requires it. Congress remains able to adjust collection obligations through valid legislation. Neither consumer liability nor every other state tax changes.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by O’Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, joins Part I on the understanding that comparable reasoning does not equate every adjudicative and legislative jurisdiction standard, and dissents from Parts II–IV and the judgment. He would retain Bellas Hess’s specific Commerce collection rule through adherence to precedent, the reliance sellers placed on an express rule and Congress’s authority to adjust collection duties, without defending its substantive merits, and would reverse. In his view coordinated congressional legislation, rather than case-by-case burden litigation, is the proper means of changing the rule. No other Justice writes separately.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with vacatur of the North Dakota Supreme Court’s judgment and remand for further proceedings not inconsistent with the Court’s opinion. Quill’s physical-presence defense fails under both clauses; North Dakota may establish a constitutionally valid collection obligation subject to Quill’s remaining Commerce objections, including any excessive-burden showing, and to lawful temporal and remedial requirements. No assessment, tax, interest or penalty amount is imposed, and no retroactive penalty is authorized. The unaddressed state-court civil-rights and fees cross-appeal remains subject to lawful further proceedings without a Supreme Court entitlement finding. The consumer’s use-tax liability is untouched. No new filing, deadline, enforcement finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is established.

### Itel Containers International Corp. v. Huddleston, No. 91-321

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

Argued October 14, 1992. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. 1257 to the Tennessee Supreme Court, 814 S.W.2d 29 (1991), which affirmed rejection of Itel's federal objections after the Chancery Court reduced the assessment under state law. Itel seeks a federal-law refund; Tennessee seeks affirmance. The questions concern the Container Conventions and federal customs rules, the Foreign Commerce Clause and the Import-Export Clause.

Itel is a domestic container lessor whose containers are leased exclusively for international use and delivered at its Memphis and designated third-party Tennessee terminals. The tax attaches to delivery transferring possession, rather than importation. The original $382,465 assessment of tax, penalties and interest for January 1983 through November 1986 was reduced to $158,012 under state law. That reduction is not reopened. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The Court affirms, 8–1. Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support affirmance and reject the treaty and regulatory challenges; Blackmun dissents and would invalidate the tax under the Conventions. On the Foreign Commerce challenge, Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas adopt the Court's rationale, Scalia agrees in the judgment, and Blackmun would invalidate the tax. On the Import-Export challenge, Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas adopt the Court's rationale and Scalia rejects the challenge on a separate ground. Blackmun's proposed reversal rests on other grounds without an independent Import-Export disposition. No additional federal-law refund is required on any of the presented grounds; the existing state-law reduction remains.

Kennedy delivers the opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. Scalia joins Parts I–III and VI, including the treaty and regulatory reasoning and the conclusion, and concurs only in the judgment on Parts IV–V, addressing the constitutional grounds. Eight Justices therefore support the treaty and regulatory reasoning, and seven support the Foreign Commerce and Import-Export reasoning. Scalia writes separately, joined by no other Justice. Blackmun dissents alone on treaty construction and, independently, foreign-commerce uniformity. Itel accepts compliance with Complete Auto; those requirements are not independently relitigated, and the Court makes no new due-process holding.

The 1956 and 1972 Container Conventions prohibit direct or indirect taxes imposed by reason of, or in connection with, importation of qualifying temporarily admitted containers, rather than every tax involving those containers. Tennessee's nondiscriminatory tax on the transfer of possession under a local lease is not such an importation charge; the 1972 definition also excludes fees and charges limited to the approximate cost of services rendered.

Kennedy's treaty portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. These eight Justices adopt this construction and application.

The Court holds that the reason for imposing the tax controls, not the historical reason why the container is present in Tennessee. Air France v. Saks directs attention first to treaty text. Both Conventions connect the exemption to importation, and removing that connection would make their limiting words ineffective. Qualifying containers receive temporary admission subject to reexportation within three months unless customs authorities extend the period; the protection does not establish permanent tax immunity for their commercial use. Itel's lease proceeds are taxed because possession changes in Tennessee, without discrimination between domestic and foreign goods. Other nations' practices deserve attention under Factor v. Laubenheimer, but their use of different tax systems does not establish the asserted exemption. Direct and indirect taxes alike are prohibited when they attach to importation; neither form is prohibited merely because a container contributes to taxable commercial value. The Executive's supporting interpretation is persuasive support, not a substitute for construing the Conventions.

Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392 (1985): applied for treaty interpretation beginning with text while considering relevant context.

Factor v. Laubenheimer, 290 U.S. 276 (1933): applied for consideration of treaty parties' practical construction; the practice asserted here does not displace the operative importation limitation.

The Container Conventions, 19 U.S.C. 1322 and their implementing regulations do not occupy all container taxation or preempt a nondiscriminatory local lease tax that neither taxes importation nor obstructs the federal temporary-admission policy. Tennessee's tax therefore survives both the asserted field-preemption and obstacle-preemption grounds; the distinct federal rules governing bonded warehouses do not confer the same exemption on these lease transactions.

Kennedy's regulatory-preemption portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. These eight Justices adopt the stated rejection of both grounds.

The Court holds that the scope of the federal arrangement must be identified before state taxation can be displaced. Hines v. Davidowitz supplies the obstacle inquiry, but the pertinent federal objective is protected temporary admission of containers, not freedom from every ordinary local tax. McGoldrick and Xerox involved a different arrangement: goods held under continuous customs supervision for reexport, with duty treatment intended to encourage use of American facilities. Taxing those goods could defeat that particular benefit. The container rules instead concern admission, certification and specified customs treatment, and Tennessee's local lease tax leaves those protections intact. Nor does extensive federal regulation alone establish occupation of the entire field. R. J. Reynolds rejected that inference even for the more detailed warehouse regime. The Court rejects both the alleged obstacle and the asserted complete occupation without denying that another tax could conflict with federal law. Nondiscrimination is relevant here but does not by itself answer every preemption question.

Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941): applied for conflict with federal purposes; no such obstacle established here.

McGoldrick v. Gulf Oil Corp., 309 U.S. 414 (1940): distinguished because the bonded-warehouse benefit and supervision differ from container leasing.

Xerox Corp. v. County of Harris, 459 U.S. 145 (1982): distinguished on the same specific federal warehouse purpose; its protection remains.

R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Durham County, 479 U.S. 130 (1986): applied against inferring complete field occupation from detailed customs regulation.

In addition to Complete Auto's substantial nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services, a tax affecting foreign commerce must avoid a substantial risk of international multiple taxation and interference with the Nation's ability to speak with one voice. With Complete Auto compliance conceded here, Tennessee's discrete local transaction and credit for tax properly paid to another domestic or foreign jurisdiction on that same transaction, together with the demonstrated federal arrangement, satisfy those additional requirements; Executive support informs but does not decide their application.

Kennedy's foreign-commerce portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. These seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia agrees only in the judgment on this issue; Blackmun dissents.

The Court holds that potential foreign taxing authority alone does not make a local transaction constitutionally untaxable. Japan Line supplies separate multiple-taxation and national-uniformity inquiries; domestic ownership does not eliminate them. Container Corp. distinguishes unavoidable overlapping taxation from the mere possibility that another sovereign could tax, while Wardair recognizes the significance of a discrete local transaction. Tennessee taxes delivery under the lease and credits tax properly paid elsewhere on the same event, reducing the relevant duplication risk. Itel accepts the lower court's Complete Auto determination, so the Court does not independently reconsider those four requirements. The tax also fits the actual treaty, statutory and regulatory arrangement and does not conflict with a demonstrated international practice requiring exemption of this transaction. The United States' submission supports that conclusion but cannot authorize an otherwise unconstitutional tax. The absence of a demonstrated foreign conflict supports this application; it does not reduce the one-voice inquiry to express statutory preemption or dispense with apportionment in another case.

Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274 (1977): its four requirements remain mandatory; their satisfaction is conceded in this case.

Japan Line, Ltd. v. County of Los Angeles, 441 U.S. 434 (1979): retained and distinguished; foreign-owned property taxed fully abroad presented a different multiple-taxation and uniformity problem.

Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 159 (1983): applied to distinguish inevitable duplication from potential foreign taxation and to assess foreign-policy evidence.

Wardair Canada Inc. v. Florida Department of Revenue, 477 U.S. 1 (1986): applied for the significance of a discrete local transaction and the actual federal arrangement.

Under Michelin, this nondiscriminatory local lease tax does not offend national commercial uniformity, divert federal import revenue or disrupt interstate harmony, and therefore does not violate the Import-Export Clause. The tax attaches to temporary possession transferred in Tennessee, rather than to the containers or their cargo as imports or exports in transit; the Court leaves unresolved whether Michelin otherwise altered Richfield's rule for direct taxation of goods in transit.

Kennedy's Import-Export portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. These seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia concurs only in this component's judgment on a distinct export-status ground; Blackmun does not supply a separate Import-Export disposition.

The Court holds that the Import-Export Clause requires its own analysis even when the Commerce Clause challenge fails. Michelin identifies protection of national commercial policy, federal import revenue and harmony among States. The national-policy inquiry is satisfied for the reasons already stated, and Washington Stevedoring explains why a properly connected, apportioned and evenhanded charge for local commercial activity respects interstate harmony. The revenue concern also fails here because Tennessee collects revenue from a local transfer of possession, not from importing goods. Richfield involved a direct tax upon the export transaction in goods already committed to export. This lease tax falls on a distinct service and its proceeds. The fact that a container will carry goods abroad does not transform every earlier local commercial event into an import duty or export impost. The Court need not decide how Michelin affects other direct taxes on goods in transit, and it neither overrules Richfield nor grants a general exemption from constitutional review.

Michelin Tire Corp. v. Wages, 423 U.S. 276 (1976): applied through its three constitutional concerns.

Department of Revenue v. Association of Washington Stevedoring Cos., 435 U.S. 734 (1978): applied for distinct local services and the interstate-harmony inquiry.

Richfield Oil Corp. v. State Board of Equalization, 329 U.S. 69 (1946): distinguished; its direct-export rule is not overruled or finally reconciled with every implication of Michelin.

These holdings govern this transaction effective February 23, 1993. The preexisting general rules and limited temporary-admission regime now receive a controlling construction and application to a nondiscriminatory domestic container-lease transaction. Complete Auto remains mandatory; Japan Line's two additional inquiries remain independent; federal preemption remains distinct from constitutional review; and Michelin's three concerns govern the Import-Export holding. Neither domestic ownership nor Executive support eliminates the foreign-commerce inquiry. No general local-tax immunity, apportionment exemption, express-conflict-only rule or overruling of Richfield follows. No precedent is overruled.

Scalia joins the treaty and regulatory holdings but agrees only in the judgment on the constitutional grounds. He would enforce a negative Commerce Clause, as a matter of precedent, against facial discrimination and laws indistinguishable from a type previously invalidated, without extending open-ended balancing tests. This tax fits neither prohibition. His Import-Export analysis preserves Richfield's textual protection but treats these empty containers as not yet exports when delivered for use in domestic loading; he therefore does not reach Michelin's broader test. His criticism of allowing an Executive litigation position to determine state taxing authority does not alter the Court's independent constitutional holding.

Blackmun would construe the Conventions in light of their purpose and the signatories' practice to protect these recurring transfers as integral to international container movement. He distinguishes a tax on container leases from a value-added tax on imported goods and regards the local-transaction distinction as insufficient. Independently, he would find that the state tax threatens the uniform treatment protected by Japan Line. The Executive's brief cannot itself authorize a tax, and congressional silence does not supply unmistakably clear authorization to override Commerce constraints. Those treaty and foreign-commerce positions support his proposed reversal; they are not Court law.

Scalia's prospective doctrine is limited by his assumption, rather than decision, that precedent's facial-discrimination rule applies equally to foreign commerce. His writing retains the separate textual Import-Export inquiry and does not reduce it to nondiscrimination. Blackmun's treaty-purpose construction is independently sufficient for his proposed reversal, and his foreign-commerce ground remains additional. All these propositions are noncontrolling.

Supreme Court review is complete. Tennessee's judgment and the state-law reduction stand, and no additional federal-law refund is required. The Court orders no remand or new tax computation, makes no penalty ruling, and retains no proceeding. Future assessments remain subject to the stated constitutional and federal-law requirements.
