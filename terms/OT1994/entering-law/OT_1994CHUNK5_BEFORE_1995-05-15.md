### Kyles v. Whitley, No. 93-7927. — 1995-04-19

Authority: [Record](../records/Kyles_v_Whitley_merits_1995-04-19.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### Collective suppression undermines confidence in this conviction

**Controlling proposition:** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its collective lawful uses, considered in the whole record and in the defense’s preparation and presentation, create a reasonable probability of a different result sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict. No item must independently satisfy that standard, and the defendant need not show acquittal more likely than conviction or that the remaining evidence is legally insufficient; the established suppression here meets the standard.

**Authority:** Justice Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for the rule, application and conviction-level relief. The dissent accepts collective assessment but disputes materiality on this record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the undisclosed evidence changes the relationship among the prosecution’s apparently reinforcing proofs. Brady requires disclosure of material favorable evidence, and Bagley supplies a probability sufficient to undermine confidence rather than a demand for probable acquittal. Earlier identification accounts permit substantial impeachment; Beanie’s differing accounts support corroboration and examination of the investigation; and the garbage material bears on the provenance of tangible evidence. These uses reinforce one another. They do not all depend on calling Beanie or accepting every statement as true. The remaining identifications, ammunition, holster, pet food and credibility objections remain significant. But counting that evidence as sufficient does not answer how the defense could have challenged its apparent independence after disclosure. The plate list contributes only within its disputed completeness. The Court finds no planting, perjury or factual innocence. It resolves the established cumulative suppression and leaves later evidence and independently unadmitted constitutional claims outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83: applies the duty to disclose material favorable evidence to the established suppression.
- United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667: applies reasonable-probability materiality to the combined defense counterfactual, including lawful impeachment and preparation uses.
- Schlup v. Delo, January 23, 1995: distinguishes its gateway showing; it supplies no additional innocence prerequisite for this Brady claim.

### The prosecutor’s responsibility includes the investigating team

**Controlling proposition:** A prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to police acting on the government’s behalf in the case and disclose it when Brady requires disclosure; personal ignorance does not remove that evidence from the materiality inquiry. This case-specific responsibility does not impute all knowledge of every government office or establish unrestricted investigative or open-file duties.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. The dissent’s consideration of the police-held material does not supply additional votes for an affirmative organizational rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the prosecution cannot avoid its disclosure responsibility by separating the trial lawyer from police who worked on the case. Brady protects the fairness of the trial, and Bagley assesses the material effect of nondisclosure; that protection would fail if the same favorable information counted only when personally recalled by the prosecutor. Here the eyewitness statements, recorded accounts, plate list and investigative memorandum arose within the investigation used to prosecute Kyles. Their lawful defense uses therefore enter the same collective inquiry. The rule does not require every agency to search all government files, and it does not convert every nondisclosure into a completed constitutional violation. Materiality and the evidence’s favorable character still matter. United States v. Williams addresses a distinct federal grand-jury duty and its specified remedy conditions; its personal-knowledge limitation does not narrow trial Brady responsibility. The Court creates no open-file entitlement and excuses no lack of diligence concerning evidence already known to the defense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady and Bagley: apply their trial-disclosure protection to information held by police acting for this prosecution.
- United States v. Williams, May 4, 1992: distinguishes its confined grand-jury disclosure and dismissal rules; those limits remain effective within their own setting.

### A completed material Brady violation requires no second duplicative harmlessness showing

**Controlling proposition:** Once this suppression is found material under Brady and Bagley, a second prejudice or harmlessness test may not defeat relief merely by reassessing whether the same nondisclosure affected the verdict. This holding rests on the prejudice built into the completed Brady violation and establishes no universal harmlessness rule for other habeas claims.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for this claim-specific ground. The dissent finds no material violation and adopts no independent harmlessness ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a second duplicative prejudice inquiry cannot rescue a conviction after material suppression has already undermined confidence in its verdict. Bagley makes the requisite effect part of the constitutional violation itself. The Court has found that effect through the combined disclosure counterfactual, not merely identified an error whose effect remains unexamined. Brecht retains Chapman for its specified preserved protected-silence claim, and O’Neal extends that treatment to its specified personal-intent category; neither decision supplies a general habeas rule displacing Brady’s own materiality requirement. Nor does this decision replace Bagley with Chapman. The State’s surviving evidence has already been considered, and it does not restore confidence after the lawful combined uses are taken into account. Ordinary habeas gates and independently presented claims retain their own rules. For this established violation, the appropriate relief is a conditional new-trial opportunity, not an additional weaker review of the same prejudice or a declaration of innocence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bagley: treats materiality as a defining element of the suppression violation, not an initial screen before a second duplicative prejudice test.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993, and O’Neal v. McAninch, February 21, 1995: preserve their actual category-specific holdings and distinguish them from claim-defining Brady materiality.

#### Precedent Treatment

Brady and Bagley govern collective materiality and the trial prosecution’s responsibility. Williams remains confined to its distinct grand-jury setting. Schlup remains a gateway rule. Brecht and O’Neal retain their category-specific holdings; this decision neither adopts a general habeas Kotteakos rule nor extends Chapman universally. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Courts must assess the established favorable suppression collectively, including legitimate effects on preparation, corroboration, impeachment and examination of the investigation. Prosecutors bear case-specific responsibility for investigating-police information. A completed material Brady violation carries no second duplicative harmlessness hurdle. The holdings neither require open-file discovery nor treat every nondisclosure as structural error, and they do not excuse defense lack of diligence about known evidence.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. The three accept cumulative reasonable-probability review and consider the police-held material. They find that the supported additional uses do not sufficiently weaken the surviving identifications, physical evidence and adverse credibility findings to undermine confidence in this verdict. They treat the jury’s knowledge of a framing theory as relevant but not conclusive, and Beanie’s absence as a limitation on some uses rather than a bar to every investigative use. Their disagreement concerns the combined application: they reject the majority’s assessment that the new corroboration and provenance challenges materially change the evidentiary picture. They do not demand proof of innocence, impose a materiality threshold on each item, or hold that mere legal sufficiency defeats Brady. Having found no material violation, they would affirm denial of this claim without adopting a second harmlessness ground. Their application is noncontrolling.

Justice Stevens joins the Court’s application of Bagley without withdrawing his earlier request-sensitive criticism of that standard. The Court does not reject that criticism or adopt a new restrictive pretrial-disclosure theory; no additional writing is filed here.

#### Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for the district court to issue a conditional writ. The district court fixes a reasonable period for Louisiana to retry Kyles; failure to retry within that period requires release under the writ. No immediate unconditional release or acquittal is ordered, and the Court does not predict the result of any new trial.

### Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co., No. 93-1631. — 1995-04-19

Authority: [Record](../records/Rubin_v_Coors_Brewing_Co_merits_1995-04-19.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### The challenged scheme does not materially advance its asserted health interest

**Controlling proposition:** The Government may not suppress truthful, nonmisleading numerical alcohol-content statements on lawful malt-beverage labels unless the restriction directly and materially advances a substantial interest. Preventing strength competition and its health harms is substantial, but the challenged scheme fails that requirement on this record; predictive judgment is permissible, while abstract concern and unexplained inconsistencies do not establish the necessary connection.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stevens agrees that the scheme fails in practice but supplies a distinct judgment ground rather than a framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this numerical-label ban has not been shown materially to reduce the strength competition invoked to defend it. Central Hudson requires a real connection between the restriction and the substantial interest; Edenfield requires justification for the actual restraint. The operating scheme permits alcohol information for other beverages, permits malt-beverage advertising where no corresponding state prohibition applies, and yields to state-required label disclosure. Those features leave comparable incentives operating while suppressing useful factual labels. The Court does not demand identical rules for all beverages, controlled studies in every case, or regulation of all sources at once. Edge permits a demonstrated incremental accommodation, while Discovery Network and Lanphere require the chosen distinction to address the asserted harm. The Government’s predictive argument and concern that existing preferences reflect the ban deserve consideration. They do not explain how this scheme materially works despite its inconsistencies. The holding concerns accurate, nonmisleading percentages, not deceptive claims or direct control of alcohol strength.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies its substantial-interest and direct-advancement requirements.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the requirement of responsive justification for the actual commercial-speech restriction.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, and Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, April 18, 1995: apply the requirement that the selected speech restriction address the demonstrated harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., June 25, 1993: distinguishes its supported state-based scheme; its holding creates no categorical vice-speech exemption.

### The ban independently lacks reasonable proportional fit

**Controlling proposition:** Independently of the failure of material advancement, the categorical suppression of accurate numerical beer-label information lacks a reasonable fit to the asserted strength-competition harm in light of its unexplained breadth and workable measures directed more closely at that harm. Reasonable fit requires proportionality, not the least restrictive imaginable means, and the Court prescribes no particular substitute regulation.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight direct joiners adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stevens’s distinct concurrence is not counted as a Fox-framework vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the label prohibition is disproportionate to the harm offered to justify it. Fox requires a reasonable fit, not perfection or the least restrictive conceivable choice. Here direct regulation of alcohol strength, focused controls on promotion emphasizing high strength, and a restriction directed to the allegedly affected market segment provide concrete comparisons with a ban on every accurate percentage. The Government answers that national strength limits could displace state choices and that a malt-liquor limitation might miss competition elsewhere. Those objections do not explain suppressing useful comparison information across the challenged category, including information that may assist selection of weaker products. Discovery Network and Lanphere require an account of that actual burden; Ibanez likewise requires a justification responsive to the selected restriction. The existing record and litigated district-court ground permit this alternative holding although the court of appeals stopped at advancement. The Court orders no tax, strength ceiling, national label design or replacement program, and leaves properly justified deceptive-claim regulation available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable proportional fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network and Lanphere: apply their distinct fit inquiry to the burden and supported alternatives here.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the need for justification responsive to the actual truthful-information burden.

### The asserted need to assist state policy supplies no alternative justification on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the Government’s state-assistance rationale is properly considered, its unsubstantiated assertion that federal suppression of accurate label information is needed to facilitate state alcohol policy does not justify this restriction. Legitimate accommodation of differing state policies remains possible when its need and operation are supported; federalism and the Twenty-first Amendment do not alone replace First Amendment scrutiny.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight Justices directly adopt this rejection of the asserted alternative justification. The Court decides no independent forfeiture issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government has not established the particular state-policy justification advanced for federal label suppression. Edge recognizes that an effective accommodation of differing state policies may satisfy commercial-speech review. It does not treat an invocation of state authority as proof of the need for a federal information ban. Here the Government has not demonstrated why States cannot operate their own alcohol policies without this suppression of truthful percentages. The statutory state-law provisions and the agency’s differing administration of labeling and advertising describe the scheme; they do not themselves prove its necessity or effect. The Court assumes the rationale may be considered and therefore need not decide Coors’s preservation objection. Nor does it deny the States’ authority to address alcohol harms or hold that federal assistance can never be legitimate. On this record the asserted assistance supplies neither a sufficient independent justification nor an answer to the demonstrated defects in advancement and proportional fit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edge Broadcasting: preserves the legitimacy of supported state-policy accommodation while distinguishing the missing demonstrated mechanism here.
- Central Hudson: requires the asserted justification to support the actual speech restriction rather than stand as an unexamined label.

#### Precedent Treatment

Central Hudson and Fox remain the commercial-speech framework. Discovery Network, Edenfield, Ibanez and Lanphere supply the actual requirements of a harm-related classification, responsive evidence, and reasonable fit. Edge preserves supported incremental and state-based regulation at its controlling scope. No precedent is overruled; no categorical vice exception, strict-scrutiny rule for all commercial speech, or least-restrictive-means requirement is adopted.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged truthful numerical malt-beverage label ban fails both material advancement and, independently, reasonable proportional fit. The unsupported state-assistance rationale does not cure either defect. The advertising judgment, distinct strength-description and design rules, state enactments and other applications of §205 remain outside the relief. Lawful direct regulation and properly justified measures against deception remain available without a judicially prescribed replacement program.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment. Accurate, unadorned information about a lawful product cannot be suppressed here simply because consumers may use it to choose a stronger beverage. His Edge dissent identifies the objection to keeping consumers uninformed, while Discovery Network requires the selected speech category to relate to an actual harm. He agrees that this scheme fails to advance the stated objective, but does not suggest that greater success at withholding truthful knowledge would cure the underlying objection. His Lanphere joinder in a bounded Central Hudson application did not abandon that distinct ground. He does not deny the Government’s authority to regulate alcohol’s harms directly, forbid deceptive claims, or require properly justified useful disclosures. He declines to join the Court’s commercial-speech framework, its acceptance of the information-suppression interest as framed, or its Fox alternative holding. His truthful-information rationale is noncontrolling; the eight-Justice Court opinion controls without combining separate grounds.

#### Procedure After Action

The injunction against the challenged truthful numerical label restriction remains in force. No further merits remand is required. The Government may pursue lawful, appropriately justified alternatives, but the Court commands no particular replacement. The unappealed advertising judgment and separate descriptive-strength provisions are undisturbed.

### Stone v. Immigration and Naturalization Service, No. 93-1199. — 1995-04-19

Authority: [Record](../records/Stone_v_Immigration_and_Naturalization_Service_merits_1995-04-19.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### Reconsideration does not postpone the original-order filing period under this INA scheme

**Controlling proposition:** Under former 8 U.S.C. §1105a, a motion to reopen or reconsider does not postpone the finality or filing period of the original deportation order for judicial review; a petition directed to that order must satisfy its own statutory period. Separately reviewable reconsideration dispositions have their own review period, and subsection (a)(6) requires consolidation when both reviews are properly pending; this rule does not make every reconsideration refusal reviewable.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg: five direct votes for the same statutory ground. The four dissenters adopt a different construction, not a narrower version of this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress preserved separate review periods for the original deportation order and a later reconsideration disposition. Locomotive Engineers supplies the ordinary rule that timely administrative reconsideration postpones finality, but former §1105a incorporates that procedure subject to its own modifications. The added consolidation command treats the two review objects as independently capable of reaching the reviewing court. The shortened original-order period confirms that structure. Consolidation could also do some work under the dissent’s reading when judicial review precedes reconsideration; the Court does not deny that possibility. It finds the amended scheme’s separate-review construction more persuasive. Ibarra governs timely criminal reconsideration, and Darby bars extra exhaustion requirements in its APA setting; neither prevents Congress from specifying a different review arrangement here. Marvin Stone’s March 1993 petition therefore cannot obtain review of the July 1991 order. The Court decides neither the immigration merits nor a new challenge to the reconsideration refusal, and it derives no jurisdictional rule merely from the absence of an administrative stay.

**Precedent treatment:**

- ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S. 270: distinguishes its ordinary administrative finality rule because the amended INA supplies a specific review modification; its separate treatment of original orders and reopening refusals remains intact.
- United States v. Ibarra, October 15, 1991: preserves its timely criminal-reconsideration rule and distinguishes this particular statutory review arrangement.
- Darby v. Cisneros, June 21, 1993: preserves its limits on extra APA exhaustion requirements; it does not decide the filing finality of an INA order.

#### Precedent Treatment

Locomotive Engineers remains the ordinary administrative reconsideration rule, subject here to the INA’s specific modification. Ibarra retains its criminal-appeal holding, and Darby retains its APA exhaustion holding. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The original deportation order and a separately reviewable reconsideration disposition carry independent filing periods under former §1105a. Optional reconsideration is not a prerequisite to judicial review. Finality for filing, agency enforceability, exhaustion, and a judicial or administrative stay remain distinct. No general equitable-tolling rule, blanket bar to reconsideration review, or immigration merits rule is decided.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Breyer, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O’Connor and Souter, dissents. Congress expressly incorporated the ordinary administrative-review procedure, and Locomotive Engineers treats a timely reconsideration motion filed before judicial review as postponing reviewable finality. Ibarra supports that treatment of an operative order still under timely reconsideration; Darby cautions against adding an unprovided procedural forfeiture. The dissent reads mandatory consolidation as coordination when an original judicial petition is already pending and review of reconsideration is later sought. It therefore gives subsection (a)(6) work without treating it as withdrawal of the incorporated pre-review rule. On that construction, the March 25 petition was timely after the February 3 denial. The four would restore review of the original order within the statutory channel. They would not revive an expired right through a late motion, grant an automatic administrative stay, impose optional reconsideration as exhaustion, decide an already pending court’s jurisdiction by agency choice, or award immigration relief. This construction and remedy are noncontrolling.

#### Procedure After Action

The original-order timeliness dismissal remains in force. The separate reconsideration-denial component is unchanged. No new remand, stay, deportability determination, or suspension-of-deportation award issues from this decision.

### California Department of Corrections v. Morales, No. 93-1462. — 1995-04-25

Authority: [Record](../records/California_Department_of_Corrections_v_Morales_merits_1995-04-25.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### The disposition has no single controlling rationale

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment granting relief on the ex post facto hearing-frequency claim is reversed, 5–4. The habeas relief resting on that judgment must be vacated on remand; the amended schedule and ordinary state review govern Morales's parole process. This disposition neither orders release nor approves every state-law suitability or postponement decision.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas support the judgment. O’Connor's plurality opinion, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, has four votes for its individualized-postponement ground. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on his sufficient-risk ground, including retained authority to advance a hearing. No opinion supplies five votes for a single constitutional rationale, and neither rationale is established as a controlling logical subset of the other under Marks.

**Explanation of authority and limits:** The Court reverses the hearing-frequency judgment, but the five Justices supporting reversal do not adopt one rule for future parole changes. The plurality distinguishes Cavanaugh through the annual default, defined class, separately reasoned interval prediction, initial review, and three-year maximum. It sustains this exercise without an interim-reopening premise. The Chief Justice instead asks whether the change creates a sufficient risk of increased punishment and relies in part on his construction that the Board retains authority to advance the hearing. That authority is not an established ordinary reopening procedure. A finding satisfying the plurality's scheduling inquiry does not necessarily answer the Chief Justice's risk inquiry on a different record; his inquiry also is not confined to the plurality's particular statutory structure. Marks does not permit joining those distinct reasons by adding their votes. Cavanaugh's binding opportunity rule therefore remains in force at its stated scope. The judgment resolves this claim, while neither a general sufficient-risk formula nor a categorical exemption for individualized postponement becomes the Court's law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993: remains controlling on its unqualified withdrawal of alternate mandatory opportunities; the divided explanations of this reserved individualized setting establish no majority amendment of its rule or its rejection of an actual-release proof requirement.
- Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188 (1977): supplies the inquiry into a fractured judgment's narrower ground; neither explanation here is shown to be a logical subset of the other, so the votes are not combined into a new test.
- Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981), and Miller v. Florida, 482 U.S. 423 (1987): retain their protection against binding adverse changes in punishment, without a vested release right or an altered statutory maximum as a prerequisite.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990): retains the punishment-related boundary rather than a prohibition of every retrospective procedural disadvantage.

The Court does not decide the rejected false-information and sentence claims, later amendments, a hypothetical release date, parole suitability, or the validity of an unadmitted state-law challenge. It establishes no right to release, damages, or generally enforceable interim hearing.

#### Precedent Treatment

Cavanaugh's rule governing the binding withdrawal of alternate mandatory consideration opportunities remains in force, including its rejection of a requirement to prove that the lost hearing would have produced release. Weaver's mandatory-benefit protection, Miller's binding-operation inquiry, and Collins's punishment boundary remain undisturbed. Jackson supplies California's separate-reasons construction and initial review, with permission for overlapping factual grounds; its hypothetical advancement discussion proves no ordinary reopening system. Under Marks, the distinct reversal explanations are not combined into a new constitutional test. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The judgment removes Morales's federal hearing-frequency relief on the admitted claim. It creates no single controlling rationale for other individualized parole postponements and no general sufficient-risk formula. The plurality's statutory distinction and the Chief Justice's risk analysis retain their stated noncontrolling status. Cavanaugh remains controlling within its existing scope. No later statute, release entitlement, general interim-hearing right, or blanket procedural exemption is decided.

#### Separate Writings

Justice O’Connor, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, supports reversal in a plurality opinion. Cavanaugh protects a binding release-consideration opportunity even though release remains discretionary; unchanged eligibility or sentencing limits therefore do not answer Morales's claim. The present statute, however, retains annual review unless an actual hearing produces a distinct prediction about the intervening years. It applies only after more than one life-taking conviction, requires stated grounds, and caps the interval at three years. In re Jackson requires separately directed postponement reasons and allows review of that determination; facts supporting denial and postponement may overlap. Morales's Board separately identified his offense, prior murder, criminal history, and needs for prolonged observation, evaluation, or treatment. The plurality treats those interval-directed findings as a material distinction from automatic deletion of every alternate hearing. It acknowledges that the forecast can become outdated, and it does not equate initial review with later reconsideration. Its ground needs no established reopening practice, release-probability statistic, or finding that earlier release was legally impossible. Nominal findings, blanket postponement, and materially different operation remain outside its conclusion. Its constitutional rationale has four votes and is noncontrolling.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment. He applies the Ex Post Facto Clause to a sufficient risk of increased punishment and finds only a speculative and attenuated possibility here. The limited class, required individualized findings, stated reasons, and maximum interval constrain delay; the amendment changes scheduling rather than the substantive suitability standard or sentence. He construes the permissive maximum interval and the Board's continuing scheduling authority as retaining power to advance a hearing, including expedited consideration. In re Jackson's discussion of a possible advanced hearing supports the permissibility of that construction, but does not prove an established practice or an enforceable ordinary reopening process. The Chief Justice adopts no mandatory filing trigger, timetable, or duty to grant interim review. His reliance is on retained authority, together with the statutory constraints, and he does not treat unchanged eligibility or discretion alone as dispositive. A law materially reducing eligibility, removing meaningful review, or predictably extending confinement on a demonstrated record may present a different case. He would vacate the hearing-frequency habeas relief while leaving the amended process and ordinary state review applicable. His sufficient-risk rationale and construction concerning advancing a hearing have no additional joins and are noncontrolling.

Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, dissents. Cavanaugh makes the loss of a binding ordinary consideration opportunity constitutionally material without requiring proof that release would have followed. California's prediction and separately reviewable reasons constrain the initial decision, but do not preserve the later occasion for an eligible prisoner to present progress or changed circumstances. The dissent acknowledges the annual default, restricted class, distinct interval prediction, and three-year cap. Its objection concerns their operation once postponement is imposed, not factual overlap between suitability and postponement reasons. Morales became eligible during the interval; the nineteen-year matrix did not make earlier release legally impossible. No established ordinary interim system replaces the lost hearing. The possibility of discretionary advancement does not establish such a system. The dissent would affirm the hearing-frequency relief and require consideration under the offense-date annual rule, without directing parole or awarding damages. It neither invalidates every individualized scheduling change nor establishes a numerical risk or burden-shifting formula. These four Justices' application of Cavanaugh is noncontrolling.

#### Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit for proceedings consistent with reversal, including vacatur of the habeas relief requiring offense-date annual hearings. Morales's sentence and parole process remain subject to the amended schedule and ordinary state review. The Court prescribes neither a release date nor a new suitability hearing date, and it does not adjudicate an unadmitted state-law challenge. No new Supreme Court scheduling condition or retained application is established.

### United States v. Williams, No. 94-395. — 1995-04-25

Authority: [Record](../records/United_States_v_Williams_merits_1995-04-25.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### Express refund consent includes this lien-backed collection claim

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1346(a)(1) permits a refund suit by a person who, although not personally liable for the assessment against another, paid under the coercive force of a federal tax lien to free property claimed as her own and alleges that the Government wrongfully collected that payment. Section 7422(a)'s duly filed administrative claim and §6511's applicable timing requirements remain mandatory; §6511's taxpayer terminology does not implicitly exclude this timely collection claim from the express waiver.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined on this ground by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Breyer: seven direct votes for the stated plaintiff category and jurisdiction-only disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted refund waiver reaches Williams's claim about money collected from her through liens asserted against her property. Section 1346(a)(1) expressly covers wrongful collection as well as erroneous assessment. Nordic Village requires clear monetary consent; this statute supplies it, rather than leaving the remedy to general jurisdiction or fairness. Dalm requires reading the waiver with the administrative-claim and timing provisions, and Williams duly presented a timely claim. Those provisions do not silently remove this collection claim merely because the assessment named someone else. The Government's integrated reading gives the timing provision a claimant restriction that the express waiver does not impose here. Stahmann supports distinguishing a payment to free one's goods from an unsolicited payment for another, while its different tax incidence and collector-action setting prevent treating it as a construction of these statutes. The holding permits adjudication of the alleged wrongful collection. It does not find the liens invalid, fix an amount, excuse an untimely claim, or authorize strangers' and volunteers' suits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., February 25, 1992: applies its demand for unequivocal monetary consent and distinguishes its text permitting nonmonetary readings; the present refund waiver expressly authorizes monetary recovery.
- United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (1990): applies its connected claim-and-timing requirements, while distinguishing its untimely action from this timely nonassessed claimant.
- United States v. California, April 26, 1993: preserves its refusal to create a common-law refund remedy; this action rests on the express congressional remedy it left open.
- Stahmann v. Vidal, 305 U.S. 61 (1938): applies its distinction between collection from one's property and a volunteered payment, while retaining its different statutory incidence and collector-action limits.

Section 6511(a) allows, for a tax requiring a return, a claim within three years of filing the return or two years of payment, whichever expires later; if no return was filed, the period is two years from payment. Its separate stamp-tax clause allows three years from payment. No lateness is alleged here. Section 7422(b) permits suit whether or not payment was under protest or duress. The coercive lien and property connection identify the facts and limited plaintiff category decided; they do not create a universal protest or duress prerequisite or decide every other claimant's eligibility.

### Williams also falls within taxpayer for these refund provisions

**Controlling proposition:** Independently, a person whose claimed property and own payment are subjected to the lien-backed tax collection presented here is a person subject to an internal-revenue tax within §7701(a)(14) for the connected refund provisions, even though she is not personally liable for the assessment. This construction allows her timely administrative refund claim; it neither makes every payer a taxpayer for all Code purposes nor adjudicates the debt or lien's validity.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's separately identified alternative holding, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, and Breyer: five direct votes. Stone-Zsela and Scalia do not join or decide this independent definitional ground. Kennedy and Thomas reject it. The alternative controls through its own five-Justice coalition, without combining distinct opinions under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds, as an independent ground, that the connected taxpayer provisions include Williams in the limited collection setting before it. Section 7701(a)(14) speaks of a person subject to an internal-revenue tax, and the asserted federal liens subjected property she claimed and her own payment to collection. Personal assessment and subjection to that collection are not identical. The Government correctly insists, under Dalm, that §7422's duly filed claim requirement and §6511 be read together; the Court does so without treating the refund machinery as available only to the assessed debtor. Nordic Village's clarity requirement remains satisfied by the express monetary waiver read with this limited definition. The definitions govern title 26 unless otherwise distinctly expressed or manifestly incompatible with statutory intent; this construction concerns these connected refund provisions rather than an unexamined transfer of every Code definition into title 28. The Court does not decide that any payment creates taxpayer status everywhere, that the underlying assessment may be challenged on every ground, or that Williams has proved a refund entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dalm: applies the integrated treatment of waiver, claim, and timing provisions without converting its late-claim holding into a decision about this plaintiff category.
- Nordic Village: applies clear-consent analysis to the actual refund text and its connected definition; no general jurisdictional or equitable label supplies consent.

### Distinct third-party mechanisms do not foreclose this refund action

**Controlling proposition:** The cited quiet-title, wrongful-levy, and substitute-fund provisions do not make those mechanisms exclusive of this §1346(a)(1) collection claim or require their exhaustion before her duly claimed refund suit. Their own triggers and restrictions remain in force: a lien is not a levy, discharge into a substitute fund is permissive and requires an agreement, and the fund remedies presuppose the corresponding fund.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined on this construction by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Breyer: seven direct votes. This ground answers the asserted statutory obstacle; remedial hardship does not independently waive immunity.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government's other cited remedies do not withdraw the express refund route for this claim. Section 2410 permits quiet-title proceedings against a claimed federal lien; it does not automatically become a refund action after payment. Section 7426(a)(1) requires a levy, and none occurred here. Section 6325(b)(3) permits the Secretary to discharge sold property upon an agreement holding proceeds as a fund with the same lien priority. It does not command an agreement or prove that Williams obtained one. Sections 7426(a)(3) and (b)(4) address rights and recovery in an actual substitute fund. Section 7426(c) conclusively presumes the assessment valid in proceedings under that section; this distinct restriction is neither erased nor silently transferred to §1346. Dalm preserves the refund claim prerequisite, but supplies no duty to exhaust different proceedings. California forbids creating a common-law remedy; the present suit uses enacted consent. The Court need not resolve disputes about advice, knowledge, or administrative willingness to recognize the statutes' distinct operation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dalm: preserves the statutory refund-claim requirement without adding an exhaustion duty for quiet-title, levy, or substitute-fund proceedings.
- United States v. California: preserves the distinction between an express remedy and one newly created by a court; the practical limitations of other remedies corroborate statutory construction but do not create consent.

The holding is jurisdictional. Lien validity and priority, purchaser status and consideration, the effect and scope of any binding Government concession, wrongfulness of collection, payment components, and recoverable amount remain for further proceedings as necessary. No interest, fees, damages, or unrestricted challenge to Rabin's underlying assessment is awarded or authorized by this decision.

#### Precedent Treatment

Nordic Village's unequivocal-consent rule remains intact and is satisfied by the express refund text at the stated scope. Dalm's administrative-claim and timing conditions remain binding; its late-claim holding did not decide this timely nonassessed payer. United States v. California retains its bar against manufacturing a common-law refund right and is distinguished by the express remedy here. Stahmann supports treating payment to free one's own goods as different from a volunteered payment, while its tax-incidence and collector-action setting remain material limits. No precedent is overruled, and no general equitable waiver is created.

#### Law After Decision

The express refund waiver permits the stated lien-backed collection claim after satisfaction of the administrative-claim and timing rules. Independently, the five-Justice taxpayer-definition holding includes this collection relationship within the connected refund provisions. The third-party remedies retain their separate statutory triggers and do not foreclose this suit or add an exhaustion step. Jurisdiction does not establish wrongful collection or entitlement to any money. Volunteers, strangers, universal taxpayer status, and an unrestricted challenge to another person's assessment remain outside the holdings.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Scalia concurs, joining the Court's express-waiver and connected timing analysis and its treatment of the distinct third-party remedies. Nordic Village requires clarity in the scope as well as the existence of a monetary waiver. Here the express wrongful-collection language supplies that consent, and the remote timing provision cannot plausibly impose the significant implicit claimant exclusion urged by the Government. He withholds the independently sufficient conclusion that Williams is a taxpayer under §§6511 and 7701 because that alternative is unnecessary to his disposition. He expresses no contrary view on that definition. Ordinary administrative-claim and timing requirements remain effective, and he joins no equitable exception to sovereign immunity.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the Court's primary ground and judgment. The collection was directed against Williams through a lien on property she claimed, and her payment therefore gives her a concrete collection injury even though another person was assessed. The levy, quiet-title, and substitute-fund mechanisms did not provide an adequate route in these circumstances: no levy occurred, quiet title did not furnish a realistic postpayment recovery, and a discretionary discharge requiring an agreement was not an assured substitute for refund review. He treats that remedial setting as confirmation of the enacted waiver's application, not as an equitable enlargement of consent. His ground remains confined to a compelled payment connected with the claimant's property, leaving volunteers and strangers outside the holding. He does not adopt the separate taxpayer-definition alternative. The refund suit proceeds subject to the administrative claim, merits, and proof requirements, without a present monetary award. His additional explanation does not create a general adequacy-of-remedy test.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice Thomas, dissents. Dalm requires the broad refund language to be read with the duly filed claim requirement, the timing provision's taxpayer language, and §7701's definition. In their view those connected provisions do not unequivocally authorize a person concededly not liable for the tax to invoke the taxpayer refund action. Wrongful collection and personal liability are distinct, but collection against claimed property does not itself make that person the taxpayer Congress identified. Nordic Village requires clear consent for the requested remedy and its scope; inconvenience cannot supply it. The specially drawn third-party statutes reinforce that reading without proving that any alternative actually was available to Williams or had to be exhausted. The dissent does not call a lien a levy, treat a permitted substitute-fund agreement as mandatory, or decide lien validity. Stahmann involved a statute intended to burden the producers and an action against the collector; it does not settle this different waiver. The two would reverse the jurisdictional judgment and restore dismissal of this refund action, leaving other lawfully authorized remedies and the merits unadjudicated. Their claimant construction is noncontrolling.

#### Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand remain in force. The district court may adjudicate the refund action under the ordinary claim, merits, and proof requirements, resolving lien validity and priority, any binding concession, wrongfulness, and amount as necessary. The Court orders no immediate payment, interest, fees, or damages and predicts no merits result.

### New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co.; Pataki v. Travelers Insurance Co.; Hospital Association of New York State v. Travelers Insurance Co., Nos. 93-1408, 93-1414 and 93-1415. — 1995-04-26

Authority: [Record](../records/New_York_State_Conference_of_Blue_Cross_Blue_Shield_Plans_v_Travelers_Insurance_Co_merits_1995-04-26.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### The reviewed 13% hospital differential is not preempted

**Controlling proposition:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the reviewed 13% hospital differential merely because its price effect may influence a covered plan’s choice of insurer or coverage arrangement. In these commercial-insurance applications the measure neither uses ERISA status or covered-plan benefits as its legal benchmark nor commands plan benefits, eligibility, uniform administration or an alternative enforcement remedy; the Court reserves economic effects sufficiently acute to operate as a substantive coverage command or effectively restrict insurer choice.

**Authority:** Justice Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined on this proposition by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes. The direct self-funded application is outside this merits holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the price influence shown here does not establish the relation to an ERISA plan that §514(a) requires. Shaw supplies connection or reference as the inquiry, while recognizing that some effects are too remote. Mackey permits ordinary legal processes despite their costs to plans. This differential operates through hospital-payment categories that exist whether customers obtain coverage through an ERISA plan or otherwise. It does not use a covered plan’s benefits as the mandatory legal measure, as Board of Trade did, or alter plan reimbursement rights as FMC did. The contrary argument properly observes that the charge is selective and designed to affect choices. But intended economic influence alone does not transform a market price into a plan command. ERISA’s objectives and the presumption against displacement of traditional state health-cost regulation guide that reading, without promising plans uniform market costs or exempting every state health law. Acute effects amounting to substantive compulsion could present a different case. The present record does not establish them; insurance saving and direct self-funded application remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85: applies the connection-or-reference inquiry and its remote-effects boundary without making every cost consequence preemptive.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825: applies the distinction between singling out ERISA plans and ordinary legal effects, including costs, without making general applicability an automatic defense.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguishes its mandatory covered-plan benefit benchmark, which these reviewed payment categories do not contain; that holding remains intact.
- FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52: distinguishes its alteration of plan subrogation and benefit calculation; its separate saving/deemer architecture remains undisturbed.

### The additional commercial-insurer 11% charge is not preempted

**Controlling proposition:** The reviewed additional 11% commercial-insurer charge likewise is not preempted under §514(a): its revenue destination and additional price differential do not establish a required plan benefit, insurer choice or administrative arrangement on this record. This application adopts neither a numerical safe harbor for combined charges nor immunity for financial pressure that actually operates as a substantive coverage command or effectively restricts insurer choice.

**Authority:** Souter’s Court opinion and the same nine named Justices directly adopt this distinct application. It is not a holding that the additional 11% applies to every payor or that the reproduced period fixes liability under a later extension.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that payment of the additional commercial-insurer component into a state pool does not change the relevant plan relationship. The 13% differential remains with hospitals; subdivision 14-e directs this additional component and its investment income to the general fund. That distinction matters to the statute’s operation, but neither revenue destination defines ERISA benefits or their administration. Shaw requires a sufficient connection, and Mackey shows why added costs alone do not meet that requirement. Board of Trade involved a mandatory measure supplied by an existing covered plan, which is absent here. Respondents emphasize that the additional 11% increases the combined incentive to choose another insurer. The Court considers that argument without finding that premiums fell, plans switched, or every future differential would be harmless. The supplied record shows influence on market choices, not an effective substantive command. Insurance saving is therefore unnecessary. The complete extension law, live prospective relief and any reimbursement implementation remain for proceedings on the actual law and record below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw: applies its relation boundary to the additional charge’s actual operation, rather than treating legislative purpose or percentage alone as conclusive.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between ordinary added economic costs and the relation ERISA preempts; it supplies no quantitative exemption.
- Board of Trade: preserves its covered-plan-content benchmark and distinguishes this additional payor charge, whose amount does not use plan benefits as the legal measure.

### The conditional HMO assessment is not preempted

**Controlling proposition:** The reviewed HMO assessment of up to 9%, subject to its actual Medicaid participation, enrollment, exemption, reduction and payment conditions, is not preempted by §514(a) on this record. It affects HMO prices and participation choices without a demonstrated command to an ERISA plan’s benefit structure, eligibility, insurer choice or uniform administration; its conditions receive no categorical immunity from preemption if a different application establishes such a connection or effective compulsion.

**Authority:** Souter’s Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes for this separately analyzed assessment. The decision supplies no insurance-saving holding for the distinct 9% measure.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the HMO assessment requires its own analysis rather than treating every surcharge as the same hospital rate. It is paid directly to a pool, excludes Medicaid and regional-pilot discharges, and permits reductions or elimination through the distinct participation and enrollment conditions described above. The good-faith fee-for-service route yields only a 75% reduction. Those rules can influence an HMO’s Medicaid participation and costs, but the record does not show that they prescribe a covered plan’s benefits, eligibility or claims administration. Shaw and Mackey require attention to the actual plan connection; FMC’s compulsory alteration of plan rights cannot be inferred from every provider or payor obligation. The contrary argument stresses the participation conditions’ organizational pressure. That pressure remains relevant, but is not shown here to impose substantive plan coverage or effectively restrict insurer choice. The Court adopts no numerical coercion threshold and no finding of inevitable enrollment. Because §514(a) does not preempt this reviewed application, the separate saving-preservation question need not be resolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw: applies connection-or-reference analysis separately to the HMO measure and preserves the possibility that more acute indirect effects could suffice.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between actual administrative commands and economic consequences without dismissing duties merely because imposed through generally applicable law.
- FMC: distinguishes compulsory changes to plan reimbursement rights from the HMO participation and payment conditions shown here, retaining the insured/self-funded distinction where saving and deemer issues must be reached.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No direct self-funded merits ruling, new insurance-saving test, numerical safe harbor, uniform-cost guarantee, FEHBA ruling, stop-loss-letter ruling, Tax Injunction Act holding, or amended-law rate command is adopted. These are record-specific applications of the relation boundary, not categorical immunity for health regulation, price regulation, generally applicable statutes or indirect burdens.

### The reserved direct-plan claim remains live after limited vacatur

**Controlling proposition:** Insofar as the reviewed judgment or associated ERISA hospital-surcharge injunction adjudicates or protects a direct self-funded application left unresolved here, that portion is vacated and remanded for consideration under the announced law and any properly presented independent ground. Vacatur removes the prior adjudication’s force as a continuing final entitlement to protection without rejecting the claim, guaranteeing interim relief, disturbing independently surviving FEHBA or stop-loss relief, or imposing a payment or escrow command.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes for this component-specific procedural disposition under 28 U.S.C. §2106. No direct-plan relation, saving or deemer merits conclusion commands a vote because those questions are not decided.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that reservation of this application must preserve the claim without silently sustaining the prior final adjudication. Section 2106 permits reversal, vacatur and appropriate further proceedings for matters lawfully under review; the Court uses that authority to separate decided applications from the unresolved one. X-Citement Video preserves both available defenses of a favorable judgment and a lawful remand option. It does not make an available defense an established injunction entitlement. FMC requires respect for distinct insurer and self-funded inquiries, but does not decide this direct-payor application for the Court. Respondents may therefore pursue the reserved ground below. Treating the reserved ground?s availability as sufficient to preserve final protection assumes success on a ground the Court leaves open; categorical merits reversal would make the opposite error. The limited vacatur adopts neither assumption. Its application-defined scope does not invent a freestanding direct-plan decree. Independent FEHBA and stop-loss grounds remain, and the lower courts must assess any supported interim or fund-administration request without a new Supreme Court payment, escrow or timing command.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: applies its distinction between an available judgment-defending alternative and a required immediate decision, preserving a lawful remand without treating the alternative as a proved entitlement to relief.
- FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52: preserves its distinct insured/self-funded statutory inquiries, without deciding the reserved hospital-payor application by analogy alone.

This vacatur is expressly chosen for the reviewed unresolved component. It establishes no universal rule that a remand preserves an injunction, that a reversal automatically dissolves every order, or that reservation requires interim protection.

#### Precedent Treatment

Shaw and Mackey retain their connection-or-reference framework and limits on remote effects. Board of Trade’s covered-plan benchmark is distinguished, not narrowed; a separate exempt benefit arrangement still does not erase reference to a covered plan. FMC’s subrogation holding and insured/self-funded saving/deemer distinction remain intact. John Hancock’s component-specific statutory qualifications and Fabe’s separate McCarran-Ferguson holding remain undisturbed; neither supplies an unneeded insurance-saving decision here. Wolens’s distinct airline contract rule is not transplanted into ERISA. No precedent is overruled. X-Citement Video preserves alternative defenses and lawful remand without converting an undecided ground into an established injunction entitlement. Section 2106 authorizes the express limited vacatur; the Court creates no general mandate-timing or automatic-injunction rule.

#### Law After Decision

The three reviewed commercial-insurance and HMO measures survive the asserted ERISA relation challenge because the shown price and participation influences do not impose plan terms or administration and do not establish equivalent acute compulsion. The decision clarifies the boundary left open for ordinary indirect economic effects in Board of Trade. Laws mandating plan benefits, specifically referring to ERISA plans, interfering with uniform administration or creating alternative enforcement remedies remain outside this holding. ### United States v. Lopez, No. 93-1260. — 1995-04-26

Authority: [Record](../records/United_States_v_Lopez_merits_1995-04-26.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### The original possession offense lacks the required commerce connection

**Controlling proposition:** The original §922(q)(1)(A) school-zone possession prohibition cannot sustain this prosecution on the chain from local firearm possession through crime costs, impaired education and reduced productivity to the national economy. Its stand-alone regulated class and enacted elements supply no adequate commercial connection, and that generalized downstream-effects theory cannot supply one; the holding does not categorically exclude all noneconomic intrastate conduct from federal regulation.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas: five direct votes for this bounded ground and affirmance. The Chief Justice’s additional six-route framework has no additional joins and is not the Court’s exhaustive test. No Marks synthesis is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government’s asserted chain does not establish the necessary connection between this offense and commerce. Wickard permits aggregation in its economic-class setting; Perez likewise sustains regulation of an economic class despite the small effect of an individual instance. Neither makes every local possession offense commercial because its eventual social costs may influence national productivity. Allied-Bruce involved actual multistate commercial operations and expressly left the constitutional boundary open. The reported paid delivery and the handgun’s manufacture outside Texas do not narrow the class Congress enacted or add a commerce condition to this charge. The contrary argument properly emphasizes broad congressional authority and judicial respect for rational legislative judgments. But aggregation cannot itself supply a connection whose asserted logic would reach virtually every serious local problem. The conclusion requires no universal formal-findings prerequisite and does not revive immunity for traditionally local governmental functions. Broad commercial regulation, including appropriate inclusion of intrastate conduct necessary to a valid interstate regime, remains available. The separate discharge offense and materially different enactments remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111: preserves aggregation at its economic-class scope; the school-possession/productivity chain does not extend that holding to this stand-alone offense.
- Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146: preserves Congress’s regulation of an economic class without requiring each instance to have a substantial effect; its regulated commercial setting remains material.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: distinguishes actual interstate commercial operation from remote-object history; its statutory breadth and reserved constitutional ceiling remain intact.
- Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Assn., 452 U.S. 264: preserves broad review of congressional commercial regulation without treating deference as a grant of general police power or imposing a universal formal-findings requirement.
- New York v. United States, June 19, 1992: distinguishes compelled state administration from direct regulation of private persons; neither its anti-compulsion rule nor the traditional local subject alone decides this case.
- Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority, 469 U.S. 528: remains undisturbed in its generally applicable regulation of state activity; traditional-governmental-function immunity is not revived.

### The possession text cannot be saved by adding a new commerce element

**Controlling proposition:** The Court will not insert an unexpressed commerce element into original §922(q)(1)(A) to sustain this charge. Because the constitutional ground requires affirmance of dismissal under the enacted prohibition, the Court does not decide the independent indictment defect that would arise if a permissible construction made a nexus an element.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas: five direct votes for the no-insertion disposition and the precise reservation. No Justice’s conditional willingness to consider the indictment issue is counted as a separate holding deciding it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that interpretation cannot substitute a different offense for the one enacted. Bass construes ambiguous firearms language in light of federalism; Gregory likewise requires attention to statutory clarity where the usual federal balance is displaced. Those decisions operate through available readings, not a judicial power to add any condition that might avoid a constitutional problem. X-Citement Video concerned the reach of an existing knowledge term and therefore does not authorize insertion of a new commerce term here. This provision identifies possession, culpability and location, subject to its stated exemptions, but includes no nexus phrase to construe. The trial stipulation about manufacture outside Texas cannot become that missing statutory condition. Lopez also renews the lower court’s Stirone and Russell objection that proof cannot replace a necessary grand-jury allegation. That conditional question would require its own decision if an added-element construction were adopted. Because the Court rejects that construction and affirms on the commerce-power ground, it leaves the indictment alternative and the sentencing objections unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336: preserves federalism-sensitive construction of ambiguous firearms language, without authorizing addition of an element absent from this unambiguous provision.
- Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452: preserves its clarity principle within permissible statutory interpretation; it supplies no power to rewrite this offense.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: distinguishes interpretation of an enacted knowledge term from adding a new jurisdictional condition; its separate rules for defending a favorable judgment remain intact.
- Stirone v. United States, 361 U.S. 212: supplies the grand-jury allegation boundary invoked against substituting trial proof for a necessary indictment allegation; the renewed objection remains conditional and is not decided after affirmance on another sufficient ground.
- Russell v. United States, 369 U.S. 749: supplies the grand-jury allegation boundary invoked against curing omission of a necessary offense allegation through later proof; that conditional indictment objection is left unresolved because the commerce ground is sufficient.

#### Precedent Treatment

Wickard and Perez remain available at their economic-class scope; neither is overruled. Allied-Bruce retains its actual-commerce construction and its express constitutional reservation. New York and Garcia retain their distinct limits concerning state compulsion and generally applicable regulation. Bass and Gregory permit construction, while X-Citement Video interprets an existing statutory term; none authorizes insertion of the missing commerce element. Stirone and Russell are not newly applied to invalidate this indictment because the conditional nexus question is not reached.

#### Law After Decision

Effective April 26, 1995, the original school-zone possession offense cannot be supported by the offered generalized crime-cost, education and productivity chain. The controlling ground is the five-Justice bounded commerce-connection holding, not a categorical prohibition on regulating noneconomic intrastate conduct and not the Chief Justice’s entire six-route framework. No general formal-findings requirement is adopted. Broad national commercial regulation remains intact. The decision announces no Second Amendment rule, decides no firearm’s protected status, and changes no standard under the Elections Clause or Reconstruction Amendments. No amended possession statute or separate discharge prosecution is adjudicated.

#### Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court’s limited holdings and concurs. Federal commerce authority is broad but enumerated. His complete account permits regulation of: actual interstate transactions; channels of commerce; instrumentalities, persons and things in interstate commerce; economic classes substantially affecting an actual interstate market; intrastate conduct whose inclusion is necessary to prevent evasion or material impairment of a valid interstate regime; and materially established interstate externalities that individual States cannot internalize. Each route requires its relevant connection to be established. A jurisdictional phrase must perform genuine constitutional work in the individual case. No other Justice joins this complete formulation.

He rejects the chain from guns near schools through violence or fear, impaired education, reduced productivity and the national economy because it supplies no judicially enforceable stopping point. National importance and generalized downstream social costs do not establish the missing enumerated-power connection. The defect is not the word noneconomic: a valid interstate regime or genuinely demonstrated interstate spillovers can support regulation of intrastate conduct under his account. Wickard and Perez remain at their actual economic-class scope. He would not insert a missing element, adjudicate an amended law, or foreclose a materially different enactment supported by its own constitutional authority. Commerce authority and individual rights are distinct inquiries; ordinary state police authority does not settle compliance with an independently applicable right. His concurrence establishes no Second Amendment classification and preserves the Elections Clause and Reconstruction Amendments’ own texts and standards. The additional framework is noncontrolling.

Justice Breyer, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, dissents. Congress could rationally find that the class of school gun possession substantially affects national commerce through disrupted education, workforce preparation and travel. Wickard and Perez require attention to the class rather than treating one defendant’s effect as independently substantial. The dissent would retain judicial review, a substantial class relationship and reasonable means, while deferring to the legislative judgment presented here. It rejects a universal findings prerequisite and does not equate every imaginable consequence with a substantial commercial effect. In its view, the Court discounts the practical national connection by treating the local and noncommercial features of possession as insufficiently connected to the class-wide effects. Safe schools’ desirability alone and the gun’s remote interstate manufacture are not its grounds. The dissent reads the original offense without a new nexus element; on that reading the indictment omits no required commerce allegation. It would reverse the constitutional dismissal and remand for the unresolved supervised-release and special-assessment objections, not approve the entire sentence. Its application is noncontrolling.

#### Procedure After Action

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal and dismissal mandate remain operative, as does the district court’s ensuing dismissal. The Supreme Court merits proceeding ends without a new prosecution, sentence, trial, or sentencing remand. The Court leaves the alternative indictment theory and sentencing objections unadjudicated because the constitutional ground is sufficient. It issues no order concerning a different statute or the separate discharge prohibition.

### United States v. Harris, No. 94-297. — 1995-04-27

Authority: [Record](../records/United_States_v_Harris_merits_1995-04-27.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### The vehicle-taking economic class supplies the commerce connection

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may apply original §2119 to the armed forcible acquisition of the vehicle proved here as regulation of the economic vehicle-taking class within the interstate motor-vehicle theft-prevention scheme. The statute's separately enacted requirement that the particular vehicle have been transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce must be proved; neither remote movement alone nor generalized crime, insurance, or productivity costs independently supplies this holding's constitutional ground.

**Authority:** Justice O’Connor's opinion of the Court, joined on this ground by Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: eight direct votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment on a distinct instrumentality ground and does not join the economic-class rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the regulated taking has a commercial connection missing from the stand-alone possession offense in Lopez. Congress addresses forcible acquisition of the vehicle itself, a form of theft within the interstate vehicle market. Wickard and Perez permit regulation of an economic class without requiring each instance to produce a substantial interstate effect. Lopez preserves that authority while rejecting a chain through general crime costs, education, and productivity. The defendants' asserted retaliatory motive does not change the enacted acquisition class or create a profit-intent exception. The Buick's Michigan manufacture and Arkansas and Mississippi titling satisfy the existing vehicle-history condition; the Court inserts no new element and finds no intended interstate resale. Allied-Bruce cautions against treating remote object history as constitutionally conclusive. That caution is respected because the taking class, rather than manufacture alone, supplies the selected connection. The holding neither federalizes every property crime nor decides an independent instrumentality theory. Both the carjacking predicate and the dependent firearm count survive this commerce challenge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: applies its retained economic-class boundary and distinguishes its inadequate stand-alone possession/downstream-effects chain; no missing element is supplied.
- Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942): applies aggregation within a regulated economic class rather than demanding a substantial effect from each individual taking.
- Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146 (1971): applies Congress's authority over the economic class despite an individual instance's local character.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: preserves its distinction between actual commercial operation and remote object history; prior manufacture alone is not the constitutional ground here.

### Knowing participation and intentional assistance give the original offense definite content

**Controlling proposition:** Original §2119 requires knowing armed participation in taking or attempting to take the covered vehicle from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation; aiding-and-abetting liability requires intentional assistance in that taking, not mere presence, association, or awareness of another's plan. Read with that ordinary criminal fault, the statute gives fair notice of the charged conduct and defeats the presented lack-of-fault and vagueness challenges without adding a profit motive, interstate-resale intention, knowledge of federal law, or the later intent-to-kill-or-seriously-injure element.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer, directly adopts the complete construction and its bounded application. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the knowing-participation and fair-notice propositions and rejection of a general legal-knowledge requirement; no additional Stone join is attributed to the separately stated intentional-assistance formulation. His separate conclusions concerning knowledge of the vehicle's interstate history and permanent deprivation have no additional joins and are not holdings of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory silence does not make this serious forcible-taking offense a crime of unwitting involvement. Staples preserves ordinary factual culpability, and X-Citement Video requires attention to the whole statutory setting rather than automatic strict liability. The taking and force terms identify the wrongful conduct; participation in that taking must be knowing, and assistance must be intentional. Shabani's rejection of guilt by association supplies a consistent boundary, without converting aiding and abetting into conspiracy. Ratzlaf's known-illegality rule rested on distinct willfulness language and does not add a universal legal-knowledge element here. A claimed retaliatory motive does not negate participation in the taking as a matter of law. The lower record treats Harris's claimed ignorance as a defense, not an accepted finding. No supplied original charge or preservation record establishes an omitted-fault instruction. The Court rejects the presented statute and notice challenge without approving unknown instructions, finding harmless error, or ordering a new trial. Knowledge of interstate history and deprivation duration receive no majority resolution.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Staples v. United States, May 23, 1994: applies contextual factual culpability despite silence while retaining its distinct regulated-characteristic holding.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: applies contextual criminal-fault construction without transplanting its depiction elements or deciding every jurisdictional-knowledge question.
- United States v. Shabani, November 1, 1994: preserves the distinction between knowing participation and mere association; its conspiracy holding does not itself define this separate offense.

### Section 924(c) expressly adds a consecutive term for this armed carjacking

**Controlling proposition:** The offense-date §924(c) authorizes its additional, consecutive punishment for the original §2119 carjacking predicate even though firearm possession is integral to that predicate's definition. The Government must still establish firearm use or carrying during and in relation to a qualifying federally prosecutable predicate; this cumulative-authorization holding does not equate possession with every §924(c) condition or determine universal element overlap.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined on this statutory holding by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes. The holding sustains the reviewed forty-one-month carjacking terms and additional sixty-month firearm terms, not an unreviewed weapon enhancement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted punishment directions encompass this pairing. Section 924(c) reaches any qualifying federal crime of violence, includes predicates with their own deadly-weapon enhancement, adds punishment to the predicate, and expressly forbids concurrent service. The defendants distinguish an offense defined by firearm possession from an otherwise complete offense carrying an enhancement. But the parenthetical's inclusion is nonexclusive; it does not narrow the general command to the examples it names. Original §2119's later enactment and silence therefore supply no exception for armed carjacking. Ordinary construction resolves this question before residual lenity, which Ratzlaf preserves for genuine remaining ambiguity. Smith keeps firearm use or carrying and relation to the predicate distinct; the Court does not deem incidental possession sufficient. Deal addresses a different question about additional convictions in one prosecution and does not independently decide this statutory pair. The existing five-year firearm terms stand against the presented challenge. No different weapon classification, higher sentence, merger, or concurrent conversion is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ratzlaf: preserves residual lenity after ordinary construction; the inclusive, additional, and consecutive directions leave no residual ambiguity for this pairing.
- Smith v. United States, June 1, 1993: preserves the separate firearm-use and relation requirements; a predicate's possession element does not automatically establish all §924(c) elements.
- Deal v. United States, May 17, 1993: distinguishes its conviction-sequence question, which does not itself decide cumulative punishment under these two statutes.

### Hunter permits the clearly authorized punishment in this single prosecution

**Controlling proposition:** Applying Missouri v. Hunter, the clearly authorized §2119 and §924(c) convictions and consecutive punishments imposed in this single prosecution do not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause even assuming overlap under Blockburger. This holding applies the governing same-trial precedent to the clear statutory direction and neither decides universal element overlap nor changes the rules for successive prosecutions.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this bounded application. Stevens joins the application of controlling Hunter while preserving his earlier objection to legislative multiplication of convictions; his reservation does not subtract a vote from this limited holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the clear cumulative direction resolves the double-jeopardy objection under Hunter. Blockburger supplies a method for discerning whether two offenses carry distinct legislative punishment, and Whalen applies that construction principle when contrary authorization is absent. Hunter establishes the governing consequence when the legislature instead clearly authorizes both punishments at one trial. Section 924(c)'s additional and consecutive commands provide that authorization here. The Court therefore need not decide whether every possible original carjacking violation satisfies every condition of the firearm offense. The defendants' overlap argument cannot displace the clear direction in this same-trial setting. Dixon preserves Grady's distinct successive-prosecution rule; neither a common episode nor the present judgment silently changes it. This decision makes no independent claim that multiple convictions lack stigma or collateral consequences and does not endorse every possible multiplication of charges. It applies the existing precedent to this pairing. Both convictions and their supplied consecutive terms remain in force without resentencing.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Missouri v. Hunter, 459 U.S. 359 (1983): applies its governing same-trial rule to the clear cumulative authorization without reconsidering its constitutional foundation.
- Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932): applies its construction role rather than treating possible overlap as an absolute bar to this expressly authorized same-trial punishment.
- Whalen v. United States, 445 U.S. 684 (1980): preserves the presumption against cumulative punishment absent contrary legislative intent and distinguishes the clear direction here.
- United States v. Dixon, June 28, 1993: preserves its successive-prosecution holdings, including the continued operation of Grady.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 (1990): remains governing law for its essential-conduct successive-prosecution setting; this single prosecution does not invoke or displace that rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides no independent instruction, indictment, preservation, harmlessness, sufficiency, or intradistrict-venue question. It determines neither knowledge of the vehicle's interstate history nor an independent permanent-deprivation requirement by a majority. No later carjacking intent amendment, later firearm tier, new short-barrel classification, serious-injury or death enhancement, or different charging pair is adjudicated.

### United States v. Pinson, No. 94-164; Eighth Circuit No. 93-2851. — 1995-05-08

Authority: [Record](../records/United_States_v_Pinson_merits_1995-05-08.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### I. The targeted examination of this closed home's heat patterns is a search

**Holding and operative rule:** A targeted technological examination of a closed home constitutes a Fourth Amendment search when, as here, its reported heat-pattern information reveals otherwise unobservable information concerning concealed domestic operation. External reception and the absence of an image identifying a person or object do not defeat protection; investigative purpose or use of equipment alone is insufficient, and the Court adopts no general rule governing every sensor or exterior emission.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. This directly controlling application concerns the covered dwelling and demonstrated differential-heat output. Stone-Zsela also states a broader formulation in his separate writing; the other six do not join that formulation merely by joining this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Fourth Amendment protects the privacy of a home against an examination that obtains otherwise concealed domestic information. Katz makes physical penetration unnecessary; Karo protects information about a home's interior that ordinary observation from outside cannot disclose. Police used the instrument to examine heat patterns at a covered third-floor window, roof, and skylight. Although it received surface radiation and did not identify an interior person or object, the resulting pattern conveyed information concerning concealed domestic operation. Treating its physical medium as dispositive would deny the protection Karo recognizes. Hicks likewise rejects an exception for supposedly unimportant concealed information. Riley's naked-eye observation through actual openings does not establish ordinary exposure here. Greenwood's purposeful disposal of garbage does not surrender all information conveyed by escaping heat, and Place's contraband-only sniff differs from information also produced by lawful activity. The Court decides this examination, without assuming greater device capabilities, public availability, particular flight conditions, or a general threshold for other sensing methods.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; privacy protection does not require physical penetration of the home.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied to the reported concealed domestic information; its protection does not depend on the information arriving as an ordinary visual image.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321: applied by analogy to the additional examination of concealed information and its rejection of an unimportant-information exemption; no physical manipulation is found here.
- Florida v. Riley, 488 U.S. 445: distinguished as naked-eye observation through actual openings; its opinions supply none of this record's missing altitude or public-flight facts.
- California v. Greenwood, 486 U.S. 35: distinguished; purposeful placement of garbage for collection does not establish surrender of domestic information conveyed by heat.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696: distinguished; the narrow contraband-information character of the sniff is absent from this heat examination.
- Dow Chemical Co. v. United States, 476 U.S. 227: distinguished; the outdoor industrial photographs do not authorize this examination of a closed dwelling.
- Minnesota v. Dickerson, decided June 7, 1993: consistent with the distinction between information obtained through authorized observation and additional unauthorized probing; no tactile search is presented.

### II. No warrant exception justifies this scan on the established record

**Holding and operative rule:** This search of protected home information required a warrant unless a recognized exception applied. The July 25 scan had no warrant, and no consent, exigency, or other recognized exception is established; the subsequent warrant for physical entry does not retroactively authorize the scan.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: six Justices. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. Kennedy and Breyer do not reach justification because they find no search.

**Controlling explanation:** Once the examination is recognized as a search of protected domestic information, the ordinary warrant requirement applies. Karo preserves recognized exceptions, including genuinely exigent circumstances; it does not authorize warrantless home surveillance merely because the receiving instrument remains outside. Nothing in this record establishes consent, exigency, or another exception for the July 25 scan. The Government's exterior-measurement theory addresses whether a search occurred and supplies no separate justification after that question is resolved. Nor does the limited detail of the output establish an exception for searches considered less revealing. The July 30 warrant authorized a later physical entry; its issuance cannot supply advance judicial authority for an earlier completed search. This conclusion concerns the scan's justification. It neither determines the validity of that later warrant nor requires exclusion of the evidence found during its execution. Those distinct questions remain subject to the record and procedural limits governing the remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; protected surveillance is subject to the warrant requirement and its established exceptions.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied; the warrant presumption for concealed home information retains recognized exceptions, including true exigency, none established here.

### III. Correct the preserved search ruling without reopening unpresented warrant questions

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth Circuit's judgment is vacated insofar as its rejection of the preserved suppression claim and affirmance of the conviction rest on the categorical no-search ground, and the case is remanded for lawful disposition of that claim under the corrected search rule. Pinson's failure to challenge affidavit sufficiency on appeal remains operative: the mandate directs no new excision-and-probable-cause hearing, revives no waived or forfeited argument automatically, and permits further warrant, suppression, or reliance questions only to the extent properly available under the existing record and ordinary preservation and remedial law.

**Authority:** Part III of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. The mandate controls directly. The remedial authorities below delimit unresolved possibilities; none supplies an independent finding admitting or excluding the evidence. Kennedy and Breyer prefer affirmance on their no-search ground; their acceptance of these procedural limits if the search holding governs does not add votes for vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** The circuit rejected the preserved suppression claim because it considered the scan no search. That premise cannot sustain the challenged conviction affirmance. But its footnote expressly records that Pinson did not challenge affidavit sufficiency on appeal, and the circuit did not decide that question. Correcting the search rule does not erase this limit. Karo's separate treatment of an untainted warrant establishes a possible legal distinction, not a finding about Pinson's affidavit. Olano distinguishes intentional waiver from forfeiture and imposes additional conditions before forfeited error may be corrected. A preserved search question does not establish those conditions for another argument. Leon requires supported objective reliance, subject to its exclusions, rather than automatic validation by a later magistrate. Evans concerns qualifying court-employee clerical error and reasonable reliance; deliberate police scanning is not that error. The lower court must determine what remaining issues are lawfully available. This Court orders neither suppression nor a particular ultimate conviction consequence and does not reopen the separate sentencing question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part IV: retained as the distinct possibility of sufficient untainted warrant information; no determination of Pinson's affidavit or entitlement to a new hearing follows.
- United States v. Olano, decided April 26, 1993: retained; intentional waiver differs from forfeiture. If Rule 52(b) is actually invoked, error, plainness, ordinarily outcome-affecting prejudice, and the separate discretionary inquiry concerning fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings must be satisfied as applicable. This mandate does not establish any predicate or revive an argument automatically.
- United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897: left available only if procedurally open and factually supported. Objective reliance affords no protection for knowing or reckless deception of the magistrate, abandonment of judicial neutrality, an affidavit so lacking probable-cause indicia that reliance is wholly unreasonable, or facial warrant deficiency precluding reasonable reliance. The inquiry includes information-obtaining and supplying officers and assumes execution within the warrant's scope; no final reliance finding is made.
- Arizona v. Evans, decided March 1, 1995: confined to qualifying non-law-enforcement court-employee clerical error plus objectively reasonable reliance. Reserved police or mixed institutional errors supply no technology exception for this deliberate scan, and no final exclusion ruling follows here.

#### Law After Decision

Lower courts must treat this targeted closed-home thermal examination as a search on its demonstrated informational content. Seven Justices support that bounded rule; six additionally hold that no warrant exception is established for this scan. The rule does not depend on an invented capacity to see through walls or a finding about public availability, altitude, or flight frequency. It creates no general rule for every sensor, every emission, or every technological measurement. The absence of a warrant exception does not itself decide exclusion or the validity of a later warrant. The seven-Justice mandate preserves the appellate nonchallenge to affidavit sufficiency and leaves only lawfully available proceedings open. The Chief's broader formulation and the dissenters' limited no-search theory are separate positions, not additional controlling holdings.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The Chief joins Parts I and III: this examination is a search, and the challenged judgment must be vacated with the stated preservation-sensitive remand. He would state the home-information rule more generally: law-enforcement use of sense-enhancing technology from outside a home to obtain information about activity within that ordinary public observation could not otherwise obtain constitutes a Fourth Amendment search. His reasoning does not turn on whether the instrument receives reflected light, sound, heat, or another physical signal. The protection of the interior cannot end at the physical surface of its walls. Treating every measurable escaping emission as abandoned would permit increasingly capable instruments to obtain domestic information while preserving only formal protection against entry. The officers deliberately obtained heat-pattern information connected with concealed activity that their unaided senses could not discern; the absence of a conventional image does not change that conclusion.

The Chief does not classify ordinary visual observation from a lawful vantage, smoke or other conditions perceptible to unaided members of the public, utility records obtained through lawful process, or every technological measurement near private property as a search. His general rule retains its trigger: deliberate technological acquisition of otherwise unavailable information about activity inside a home. He expresses no view on excised-affidavit sufficiency, procedural availability of that issue, ultimate suppression, or Leon's application. His remand does not direct a new probable-cause determination. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. No other Justice joins his broader formulation.

**Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Breyer.** These Justices would affirm rejection of the search claim and the challenged conviction affirmance because the demonstrated output was comparative exterior warmth. Karo disclosed the presence of a particular object inside a home; this account does not show identification of a person, object, or exact interior activity. The causal inference officers drew from surface conditions does not, in their view, establish acquisition of the concealed fact Karo protected. They recognize that Katz and Karo make neither physical penetration nor the receiver's location dispositive. Their disagreement concerns the content obtained on this record, not immunity for all outside observation. Riley's exposed conditions and Dow's limited external depiction illustrate that distinction without establishing missing public-flight facts or converting a home into industrial premises.

They adopt no general heat-abandonment rule, contraband-only analogy, public-availability test, or technology good-faith defense. More revealing surveillance remains open. They need no alternative holding approving affidavit sufficiency or objective reliance. If the Court's search rule governs, they accept that any further proceedings must respect the same preservation limits; that conditional procedural agreement does not change their votes to affirm. They do not independently revisit the lesser-harms sentence.
