#### Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1014.
**Decided:** June 24, 1992.

For the live-controversy holding in *Lee v. Weisman*, June 24, 1992, No. 90-1014, see Federal Courts — Prospective equitable relief.

##### Whether officials may organize clergy-led worship at public secondary-school graduation

**Holding:** Public secondary-school officials violate the Establishment Clause when they select clergy, request invocations and benedictions, guide the prayers' religious content and incorporate that worship into the official graduation program. Neither nonsectarian language nor formally optional attendance cures that sponsorship; no separate showing of punishment or quantified social pressure is required to invalidate this practice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient sponsorship ground. Kennedy does not join Part II.

**Current force:** Remains controlling for the identified school-organized graduation worship, independently of a separate coercion showing.

**Material application:** The principal selected the rabbi, requested prayer, supplied civic-prayer guidance and placed the exercise in the program. A desire for solemnity does not authorize the State to undertake religious presentation; the record does not establish that officials wrote or edited a complete prayer script.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding does not prohibit genuinely private prayer or private forum speech, replace the general Establishment Clause framework, or resolve adult legislative prayer, funding, denominational aid or universal accommodation questions. Lemon's purpose and entanglement prongs are not separately decided; broader views in Souter's concurrence are not additional Court holdings.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the First Circuit and preserve the prospective injunction against officials' authorizing or encouraging the challenged clergy-led graduation prayer practice. The injunction reaches the official program, not private prayer or genuinely private forum speech.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, are applied: nonsectarian language, voluntary attendance and permission to abstain do not cure official school worship. Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, is applied through its prohibition on a primary effect advancing religion, without a replacement test. Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783, is distinguished as legislative prayer in a different setting; Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, protects private religious equal-access activity rather than official sponsorship.

**Independent alternative holding:** School-directed clergy prayer at a public secondary-school graduation independently violates the Establishment Clause when school control and the ceremony's importance place objecting students under substantial pressure to participate, appear to participate or forgo the occasion. Formally optional attendance and the opportunity to stand or remain silent do not remove that pressure here; mere offense alone is insufficient.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Kennedy's concurrence in part and in the judgment, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices expressly adopt this independently sufficient ground. It controls through direct majority support, not Marks. Stone does not join this writing.

**Current force of alternative holding:** Remains independently controlling for the stated school-directed secondary-school graduation practice and substantial participation pressure.

**Limits, remedy, transition, or precedent treatment:** The absence of a disciplinary penalty does not eliminate the injury; a student need not protest publicly or miss the singular educational ceremony to avoid apparent participation. The ground does not equate disagreement or ordinary courtesy with coercion and leaves mature adult audiences and other accommodations open. It independently sustains the same limited prospective injunction. Engel and Schempp apply to this indirect religious pressure; Marsh is distinguished by the adult legislative setting and different freedom to leave. Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668, supplies the minimum prohibition on coerced religious support or participation without extending its display holding. County of Allegheny v. ACLU, 492 U.S. 573, is not reopened as to displays or every accommodation dispute.

#### Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District, 508 U.S. 384 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-2024.
**Decided:** June 7, 1993.

##### A religious perspective cannot be excluded from an admitted subject

**Holding:** When a public school makes an after-hours forum available for private discussion of family life and child-rearing, it may not reject that otherwise eligible discussion solely because the viewpoint is religious. This prohibition applies even assuming a nonpublic forum; it does not require an unlimited public forum or invalidate neutral restrictions on subjects and activities outside the actual forum.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s speech portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association, 460 U.S.37: viewpoint neutrality within a nonpublic forum applied.
- Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, 473 U.S.788: permissible forum limits distinguished from forbidden viewpoint exclusion.
- Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S.263: religious perspective does not by itself remove otherwise eligible private speech from equal-access protection.

##### Equal private access does not establish religion here

**Holding:** Allowing this voluntary, publicly accessible, after-hours film presentation without school sponsorship does not violate the Establishment Clause. Equal access serves a secular forum purpose and neither primarily advances religion as governmental activity nor creates excessive entanglement; this holding authorizes access to private speech, not an official devotional program.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s establishment portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter (6 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The separate Sunday-worship application and the facial validity of every religious-use restriction are outside review. No unlimited public forum, school sponsorship, compulsory worship or public devotional financing is required.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand for nondiscriminatory treatment and declaratory or injunctive relief as necessary; genuinely neutral scheduling, safety and subject limits remain available.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Widmar, 454 U.S.263: neutral access and private attribution applied.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S.602: purpose, primary effect and entanglement applied, not replaced.
- Lee v. Weisman, June 24, 1992: the official-worship and pressure holdings distinguished and preserved.

#### [Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, 512 U.S. 687](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1993/records/Board_of_Education_of_Kiryas_Joel_Village_School_District_v_Grumet_merits_1994-06-27.md)

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 93-517, 93-527 and 93-539.

**Decided:** 1994-06-27.

##### The State may not select a denomination as the recipient of general governmental authority

**Holding:** The Establishment Clause forbids a State deliberately to allocate general civil authority to a community selected as a religious community, even when the immediate recipients are elected public lay officials rather than clergy. Chapter 748 violates that rule because its design and context use denominational membership as the organizing basis of the special school district; religious homogeneity or coincidence with an existing village line alone would not establish the violation.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II introduction and II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg: five Justices. This is an independently sufficient ground. O'Connor does not join that portion, and Kennedy's separate judgment rationale is not counted as a join.

**Material application:** Chapter 748 grants all powers of a union-free school district through a board of five to nine members elected by village voters for terms not exceeding five years. It contains no special-education-only limitation, sunset, religious voter or office qualification, or rabbinical appointment. The secular public program and professional staffing do not narrow that general statutory grant.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Larkin v. Grendel's Den, Inc., 459 U.S. 116: applies its civil-authority boundary by structural analogy to deliberate denominational allocation through a lay public board; it does not treat this board as a church or clergy veto.
- Larson v. Valente, 456 U.S. 228: applies denominational neutrality to the State's choice of recipients of general public authority; no general disability on religious communities follows.
- McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618: preserves equal political citizenship and distinguishes religious officeholders from governmental allocation by denomination.

**Independent alternative holding:** Chapter 748 independently violates religious neutrality by conferring an anomalously particular benefit of general school-government power on this selected denominational community without neutral terms or dependable assurance of equal treatment for comparable communities. The defect follows from this enactment's design and context, not a rule that every special act is invalid, every general statute is neutral, or an unproved finding that another religious community has already been refused.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Souter's opinion, Part II-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg: six Justices. This is an independently sufficient alternative ground; Kennedy expressly declines it.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Larson v. Valente: applies the prohibition on denominational preference to the particular governmental benefit and its allocation.
- Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos, 483 U.S. 327: distinguishes its general nonprofit religious-employer accommodation from a special denominational franchise; its permission to accommodate remains effective.

##### Accommodation preserves educational access but does not cure this allocation

**Holding:** A State may accommodate religiously grounded needs beyond what free exercise independently requires, but accommodation does not authorize this denominational allocation of general governmental power. Affirmance leaves the children's statutory special-education rights and lawful neutral local public services intact, without commanding a particular placement, validating the adequacy of earlier services or prescribing a replacement district.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II-C and the implementing limits in Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg: six Justices. This holding rejects the accommodation defense and defines the remedy's limits; Kennedy reaches the judgment on his separate ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos: preserves permissible accommodation beyond free-exercise compulsion, with its denominational-neutrality and application limits.
- Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306: preserves lawful accommodation of private religious needs without authorizing this governmental franchise.
- Wolman v. Walter, 433 U.S. 229: preserves the possibility of properly administered neutral-site services; no particular replacement is approved.
- Board of Education v. Wieder, 72 N.Y.2d 174, 527 N.E.2d 767: respects the state court's recognition of local educational discretion without turning permission into a compulsory separate school.
- Aguilar v. Felton, 473 U.S. 402, and School District of Grand Rapids v. Ball, 473 U.S. 373: neither is overruled, modified or used to order restoration of the terminated annex program.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602: remains governing law; this bounded neutrality decision neither replaces its framework nor imposes a new universal analytical sequence.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No controlling rationale is synthesized under Marks from Kennedy's different religious-boundary theory or other separate writings. The Court does not replace Lemon, overrule Aguilar or Ball, create an aid-merits holding in Zobrest, revise Shaw, decide the state constitutional issue, or find religious voter qualifications, religious instruction, mandatory assimilation, a denied request by another faith, permanent religious demographics or a free-exercise entitlement to a separate district. It neither selects individual placements nor requires elimination of culturally responsive secular instruction.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Federal invalidation of Chapter 748 is affirmed, 7–2, in all three dockets. Implementation must preserve statutory educational duties and lawful neutral services. The Court prescribes no replacement district, individual placement, transfer date, damages or repayment, and does not invalidate the village, close private schools, restore the discontinued annex program or resolve the reserved state constitutional question.

#### Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills School District, 509 U.S. 1 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-94.
**Decided:** June 18, 1993.

##### Antecedent legal grounds must be considered on this remand

**Holding:** The live reimbursement claim should return for adversarial consideration of its statutory and regulatory basis and any independently dispositive funding limits before this Court decides establishment. The lower courts must expressly decide whether to entertain the district’s forfeited defenses, considering the family’s opportunity to respond and prejudice; avoidance does not automatically revive abandoned grounds.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, Antecedent legal grounds must be considered on this remand portion; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Adickes, 398 U.S.144 — ordinary preservation rule acknowledged, not bypassed silently.
- Singleton, 428 U.S.106 — discretion exercised for this antecedent-law remand.
- Ashwander, 297 U.S.288 — constitutional-necessity principle applied without an automatic forfeiture exception.
- Burlington, 471 U.S.359 — statutory reimbursement context retained; no automatic award.

##### Lack of a duty and an affirmative prohibition are different

**Holding:** On remand, absence of a federal mandate to supply the interpreter cannot by itself establish that local provision or reimbursement is prohibited. The courts must determine the actual operation of IDEA, 34 C.F.R.§76.532(a)(1) and any properly entertained state-law defense, distinguishing a bar on religious worship, instruction or proselytization funding from a regulation implementing only the constitutional limit.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, Lack of a duty and an affirmative prohibition are different portion; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No Establishment Clause merits, reimbursement entitlement or revived Free Exercise claim is decided. A regulation implementing only the constitutional limit may not avoid the constitutional question; absence of a federal duty does not establish a prohibition on local provision.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate and remand for adversarial consideration of reimbursement authority and independent limits, including whether to entertain forfeited defenses and resulting prejudice; no award or permanent funding bar.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Burlington, 471 U.S.359 — reimbursement authority distinguished from an automatic entitlement.
- Mueller, 463 U.S.388; Witters, 474 U.S.481; Meek, 421 U.S.349; Wolman, 433 U.S.229; Ball, 473 U.S.373 — no merits treatment by the Court; competing aid precedents remain available.

#### Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1014.
**Decided:** June 24, 1992.

For the Establishment Clause grounds in *Lee v. Weisman*, June 24, 1992, No. 90-1014, see First Amendment — Official school prayer.

##### Whether continued exposure at a forthcoming graduation preserves a live claim for prospective relief

**Holding:** A student who remains in the same public-school system and faces its challenged official prayer policy at her forthcoming high-school graduation has a live claim for prospective protection although her earlier graduation is over. Deborah Weisman's continued exposure permits review and preservation of the injunction without deciding her father's taxpayer standing.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices adopt the threshold proposition.

**Current force:** Remains controlling as stated for the student's continuing prospective exposure.

**Material application:** Deborah remains enrolled at Classical High School, and the stipulated record makes clergy-led prayer at her later graduation likely. Her relationship to that forthcoming policy application is concrete; formal optional attendance does not remove it.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court decides no taxpayer-standing rule and creates no general advisory-jurisdiction exception for completed school controversies that might recur.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Preserve review and the injunction against the challenged official practice. The injunction's substantive scope follows the First Amendment holding.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, supply the substantive school-prayer interest without supplying a new taxpayer-standing rule.

## Religion and public schools

### Official graduation worship and independent religious pressure

**Governing question:** Clergy-led prayer organized by public secondary-school officials for the official graduation program.

**Current rule:** Officials violate the Establishment Clause by selecting clergy, requesting invocations and benedictions, guiding their religious content and incorporating the worship into the official ceremony. Nonsectarian wording and optional attendance do not cure that sponsorship; no separate punishment or quantified-pressure showing is required for this ground. Independently, school-directed clergy prayer is forbidden when the school's control and the occasion's importance place objecting adolescents under substantial pressure to participate, appear to participate or forgo the ceremony. Optional attendance and standing or remaining silent do not necessarily remove that pressure; mere offense is insufficient.

**Authority by component:**

- Independently sufficient sponsorship ground: [Lee v. Weisman, “Officially organized graduation worship is forbidden”](https://github.com/Alex-Oss222/scotus-career/blob/main/terms/OT1991/records/Lee_v_Weisman_merits_1992-06-24.md), June 24, 1992; Blackmun's Part II joined by Stone, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter.
- Independently sufficient pressure ground: *Lee*, “The school-directed ceremony also imposes impermissible religious pressure”; Kennedy's concurrence joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter. Each rule has five direct supporters; neither depends on Marks or on treating Stone as joining Kennedy.

**Present operation effective:** *Lee*, June 24, 1992, for the identified secondary-school graduation practice.

**Exceptions and limits:** The decision applies existing school-worship and Lemon principles at this recurring graduation setting without creating a replacement establishment test. Private prayer and genuinely private equal-access speech remain distinct. Adult legislative prayer, general accommodation, funding and denominational aid are not decided.

### Religious viewpoints in an after-hours school forum

**Governing question:** What instructions govern religious viewpoints in an after-hours school forum?

**Current rule:** When a public school admits private family-life and child-rearing discussion to an after-hours forum, it cannot exclude an otherwise eligible presentation solely for its religious viewpoint, even assuming a nonpublic forum. Neutral restrictions outside the forum's actual subjects remain possible. Equal access to voluntary, publicly accessible after-hours films without school sponsorship has a secular forum purpose, does not primarily advance religion as governmental activity and causes no excessive entanglement. That establishment holding concerns private speech, not an official devotional program; it supplies no replacement for the inherited establishment framework or universal ruling on worship restrictions.

### Religious targeting, comparable secular conduct and strict scrutiny

**Governing question:** What instructions govern religious targeting, comparable secular conduct and strict scrutiny?

**Current rule:** A law is nonneutral when its text, definitions, exemptions and operation identify religious conduct for distinctive burdens even without naming the religion; adverse impact or the words ritual or sacrifice alone do not establish that trigger. Substantially leaving comparable secular conduct unregulated despite its threat to the asserted interests defeats general applicability at least to that extent. Nonneutral or nongeneral restrictions require compelling interests and narrow tailoring. Appreciable comparable harm left unregulated despite feasible controls defeats the selective compelling-interest showing, and available neutral narrower controls and underinclusion can independently defeat tailoring. Smith is not replaced by a general exemption regime; all boundaries of general applicability and exemption from genuinely general anti-cruelty laws remain open. The enactment-history plurality supplies no additional controlling test.

### Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, Nos. 93-517, 93-527 and 93-539

Merits decision, June 27, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 30, 1994; decided June 27, 1994. On writs of certiorari to the New York Court of Appeals, 81 N.Y.2d 518, 618 N.E.2d 94 (1993). The consolidated cases concern whether Chapter 748's creation of a school district coterminous with Kiryas Joel violates the Establishment Clause despite its secular special-education purpose. The petition in No. 93-517 also requests replacement of Lemon and its primary-effect approach; the Court resolves the district's validity on sufficient narrower grounds. 

The trial court granted summary judgment under federal and state constitutional grounds; the Appellate Division affirmed on primary effect. The Court of Appeals affirmed under the federal Establishment Clause and expressly reserved state constitutional law. Citizen-taxpayer respondents proceeded under New York State Finance Law §123 after the association and officers were denied standing in those capacities. The adversely affected districts seek review of the concrete judgment; no new federal taxpayer-standing exception is announced.

The village incorporated in 1977 under general law. Its residents shared the Satmar faith, but its incorporation and their voluntary association are not the challenged acts. Public special services began at a religious-school annex in 1984 and ended after Aguilar and Ball in 1985. Parents reported panic, fear and trauma at outside placements and withdrew most children; that account is not a finding that interaction violated religious doctrine. Wieder in 1988 recognized authority to arrange a local public program, without requiring it. Chapter 748 followed in 1989 and grants all union-free-district powers. The present public program provides secular special education, employs outside professionals, uses coeducational classrooms without religious symbols and serves pupils from neighboring districts. These circumstances do not reduce the board's general power over schools, teachers, textbooks, discipline and property taxation. The act supplies no religious voter or office qualification, rabbinical appointment, special-education-only limitation or sunset.

Chapter 748 provides:

> Section 1. The territory of the village of Kiryas Joel in the town of Monroe, Orange county, on the date when this act shall take effect, shall be and hereby is constituted a separate school district, and shall be known as the Kiryas Joel village school district and shall have and enjoy all the powers and duties of a union free school district under the provisions of the education law.
>
> § 2. Such district shall be under the control of a board of education, which shall be composed of from five to nine members elected by the qualified voters of the village of Kiryas Joel, said members to serve for terms not exceeding five years.
>
> § 3. This act shall take effect on the first day of July next succeeding the date on which it shall have become a law.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participate at argument and decision. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

The New York Court of Appeals judgment invalidating Chapter 748 under the Establishment Clause is affirmed in all three dockets, 7–2.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Federal invalidation of Chapter 748 in Nos. 93-517, 93-527 and 93-539 | Affirmed, 7–2, in each docket | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Scalia, Thomas | The judgment invalidating the special school-district act stands; lawful secular special-education arrangements and statutory rights remain available |

The remedy reaches Chapter 748's creation of the particular district. It does not invalidate the village, close private religious schools, erase IDEA entitlements, require integration into a specified classroom, establish a new district or set an automatic transfer date. Implementation remains with the appropriate state authorities and courts within constitutional and statutory limits. Scalia and Thomas would reverse the federal ground and leave the expressly reserved state constitutional question for further state proceedings, not declare the act immune from state law.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The State may not select a denomination as the recipient of general governmental authority

**Controlling proposition:** The Establishment Clause forbids a State deliberately to allocate general civil authority to a community selected as a religious community, even when the immediate recipients are elected public lay officials rather than clergy. Chapter 748 violates that rule because its design and context use denominational membership as the organizing basis of the special school district; religious homogeneity or coincidence with an existing village line alone would not establish the violation.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II introduction and II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Ginsburg: five Justices. This is an independently sufficient ground. O'Connor does not join that portion, and Kennedy's separate judgment rationale is not counted as a join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional defect lies in the State's allocation of authority, not in a finding that the public school teaches religion. Larkin prohibits a transfer of discretionary governmental power to religious institutions. Chapter 748 does not reproduce its church veto: lay voters elect a public board, and ordinary public-school law applies. The structural concern nevertheless reaches a deliberate legislative choice of a denomination as the organizing recipient of general school-government power. Larson supplies the requirement of denominational neutrality, while McDaniel protects religious citizens from political disqualification. Those principles coexist. The village's lawful incorporation, secular instruction, outside professional staff and service to children from neighboring districts are substantial considerations, but do not cure this particular allocation. Nor do the children's educational needs require a permanent grant of general district powers on that basis. The Court leaves ordinary geographic government and neutral local services available. It invalidates neither voluntary religious association nor a district merely because its residents share a faith.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Larkin v. Grendel's Den, Inc., 459 U.S. 116: applies its civil-authority boundary by structural analogy to deliberate denominational allocation through a lay public board; it does not treat this board as a church or clergy veto.
- Larson v. Valente, 456 U.S. 228: applies denominational neutrality to the State's choice of recipients of general public authority; no general disability on religious communities follows.
- McDaniel v. Paty, 435 U.S. 618: preserves equal political citizenship and distinguishes religious officeholders from governmental allocation by denomination.

##### The particular denominational grant lacks a neutral basis for equal treatment

**Controlling proposition:** Chapter 748 independently violates religious neutrality by conferring an anomalously particular benefit of general school-government power on this selected denominational community without neutral terms or dependable assurance of equal treatment for comparable communities. The defect follows from this enactment's design and context, not a rule that every special act is invalid, every general statute is neutral, or an unproved finding that another religious community has already been refused.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg: six Justices. This is an independently sufficient alternative ground; Kennedy expressly declines it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this exceptional allocation supplies no adequate neutral account of which similarly situated communities may receive the same governmental benefit. Larson requires denominational neutrality, and Amos distinguishes a generally available religious accommodation from preference for a particular denomination. The problem is not simply that the Legislature addressed one difficulty first. Its chosen structure grants broad continuing authority to the identified religious community without a neutral assurance governing comparable claims. A later legislature need not consider or decide another request, and legislative inaction may leave no merits decision whose reasons can be reviewed. That structural concern does not require inventing a prior refusal to another faith. The lawful general incorporation of the village illustrates a different basis for civic organization. Secular instruction and genuine need remain relevant, but cannot alone explain the denominational selection of governmental authority. Neutral criteria or otherwise equal arrangements may permit accommodation; the Court commands no particular statute and does not assume that a formally general law could never conceal the same preference.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Larson v. Valente: applies the prohibition on denominational preference to the particular governmental benefit and its allocation.
- Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos, 483 U.S. 327: distinguishes its general nonprofit religious-employer accommodation from a special denominational franchise; its permission to accommodate remains effective.

##### Accommodation preserves educational access but does not cure this allocation

**Controlling proposition:** A State may accommodate religiously grounded needs beyond what free exercise independently requires, but accommodation does not authorize this denominational allocation of general governmental power. Affirmance leaves the children's statutory special-education rights and lawful neutral local public services intact, without commanding a particular placement, validating the adequacy of earlier services or prescribing a replacement district.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion, Part II-C and the implementing limits in Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg: six Justices. This holding rejects the accommodation defense and defines the remedy's limits; Kennedy reaches the judgment on his separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that protecting these children's access to secular education is a legitimate objective. Amos permits accommodation beyond constitutionally compelled exemptions, and Zorach distinguishes permissible attention to religious needs from governmental sponsorship. But a legitimate service objective does not cure an impermissible allocation of civil power. The current secular curriculum, professional staffing and participation of children from other districts do not narrow the statute's grant of all union-free-district powers. The State remains able to provide appropriate services through neutral arrangements. Wieder recognized discretion to arrange a local public program without creating this district; it did not require such a program or establish that outside attendance violated religious doctrine. Wolman identifies the possibility of properly administered services at neutral sites, not the adequacy of any particular proposed placement here. The Court does not decide whether earlier services met each child's needs, close private religious schools, invalidate incorporation or extinguish IDEA eligibility. Aguilar, Ball and Lemon need not be revised to decide this special act.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos: preserves permissible accommodation beyond free-exercise compulsion, with its denominational-neutrality and application limits.
- Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306: preserves lawful accommodation of private religious needs without authorizing this governmental franchise.
- Wolman v. Walter, 433 U.S. 229: preserves the possibility of properly administered neutral-site services; no particular replacement is approved.
- Board of Education v. Wieder, 72 N.Y.2d 174, 527 N.E.2d 767: respects the state court's recognition of local educational discretion without turning permission into a compulsory separate school.
- Aguilar v. Felton, 473 U.S. 402, and School District of Grand Rapids v. Ball, 473 U.S. 373: neither is overruled, modified or used to order restoration of the terminated annex program.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602: remains governing law; this bounded neutrality decision neither replaces its framework nor imposes a new universal analytical sequence.

##### Authority, limits and questions not reached

The two independently sufficient invalidation grounds have direct majorities. Kennedy's religious-boundary rationale and the principal opinion's equal-treatment rationale are not combined under Marks: Kennedy would reject government religious gerrymanders even if all faiths received equivalent ones, while the principal opinion identifies a defect in this particular unequal allocation. Agreement in judgment does not establish a logical subset or a complete opinion join. Stevens's additional state-supported religious-separation rationale has only Stevens, Blackmun and Ginsburg.

The Court does not overrule Lemon, Aguilar or Ball; adopt a comprehensive replacement establishment test; create an aid-merits holding in Zobrest; revise Shaw's racial equal-protection holding; find a religious qualification for voters or officers; or adjudicate the reserved state constitutional question. A petition requested replacement of Lemon, but the present decision rests on the sufficient narrower grounds. No finding of religious instruction, mandatory assimilation, denial to another faith, permanent religious demographics or free-exercise entitlement to a separate district is made. The judgment does not prescribe an individual service placement or require elimination of culturally responsive secular instruction.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments govern. Larkin's structural boundary applies to the actual denominational allocation while its church-veto facts remain distinct. Larson's neutrality rule and McDaniel's equal political citizenship are preserved together. Amos, Zorach and Wolman leave lawful accommodation available. Lemon, Aguilar and Ball are not overruled. Zobrest decided no Establishment Clause merits, and Carter's statutory reimbursement rule supplies no exception to denominational neutrality. Shaw's rejection of the particular design-only racial territorial claim remains intact; this decision rests on an independently identified establishment injury. Lee's school-worship and pressure holdings, Lamb's Chapel's private-access holding and Lukumi's actual neutrality rules retain their separate scopes. No comprehensive new establishment test is adopted.

Effective June 27, 1994, Chapter 748's deliberate denominational allocation of general civil authority is invalid under a five-Justice holding. Its particular unequal governmental benefit independently fails under a six-Justice holding. Legitimate accommodation does not cure those defects, and secular special-education rights remain. Religious homogeneity alone is no constitutional disqualification, and neither the form of special legislation nor a formally general law conclusively decides neutrality. Kennedy's independent boundary theory and Stevens's additional separation theory remain separate positions. The Court changes no general incorporation rule, racial districting standard, statutory education entitlement or school-aid precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun concurs, joined by no other Justice. He regards Lemon's effect and entanglement principles as generally sound, and sees the Court's analysis as consistent with them. His position is affirmative adherence, not merely a conclusion that reconsideration is unnecessary. The decision's particular neutrality and allocation rules do not establish his concurrence as an additional opinion of the Court. He also joins Stevens's separate writing.

Stevens concurs, joined by Blackmun and Ginsburg. In his view, the State's affirmative organization of this religious separation supports attachment to the group's faith, beyond merely distributing a secular educational service on equal terms. He distinguishes private religious education, voluntary off-site release time and exemption from a general governmental burden. Current secular teaching, outside staff and lay public governance are accepted facts; his objection does not depend on relabeling the curriculum religious. The proposed concern for tolerance is a constitutional assessment of the State's chosen arrangement, not an empirical finding that a particular child retained a faith because of the district and not an order for compulsory assimilation or a particular integrated placement. The writing supplies a three-Justice additional rationale, not another holding.

O'Connor concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by no other Justice. She joins the particular-benefit, equal-treatment and accommodation analysis, but not the complete Larkin delegation analogy. Ordinarily rights and benefits should not depend on a person's faith, while a genuine religious burden may be accommodated without selecting a favored sect. She recognizes that this community may presently have a distinctive need and that special legislation is not inherently unlawful. The structural difficulty is the lack of dependable neutral terms for comparable groups: a future legislature may simply decline to address their requests, without a decision explaining why. A generally available district arrangement or neutral eligibility administered through reviewable decisions could present a different case; she commands neither. Religious residents remain entitled to political participation and public services. She favors more precise, context-sensitive establishment rules over forcing every category through one rigid Lemon formula, without rejecting every valid insight of decisions applying it. She would reconsider Aguilar in a proper case so that neutral special education might be provided at sectarian premises on equal terms. That prospective view changes neither Aguilar nor Ball here and draws no aid-merits support from Zobrest, which resolved antecedent questions.

Kennedy concurs in the judgment, joined by no other Justice. The decisive objection is the State's own deliberate use of religion to draw the boundaries of a political unit. A geographic description does not cure purposeful religious line drawing; offering equivalent religious gerrymanders to every faith would not cure it either. Private association, neutral incorporation and lines derived from geography or history remain different, and residents do not lose self-government merely because they share a faith. He acknowledges the secular school and preexisting lawful village but regards the Legislature's purposeful adoption of the religious community's line for this new unit as the State's own forbidden criterion. He expressly rejects the principal opinion's lack-of-assured-future-equal-treatment ground. A State may respond to a unique burden without arranging every future accommodation in advance, and a later unequal treatment claim can be considered when it arises, although extension or invalidation may pose remedial choices. Religious practical distress need not violate a specific tenet to support permissible accommodation; it creates no entitlement to this district. Larson and Larkin's religious-allocation boundary and Lukumi's objective neutrality principles support his separate ground. Shaw supplies no racial-design holding to import. He declines unnecessary Lemon reliance and regards Aguilar and Ball as possible subjects for later reconsideration; neither changes now.

Stone-Zsela concurs, joined by no other Justice. The statute creates an elected public board, not a rabbinical veto, so Larkin supplies a structural analogy rather than an identical factual rule. Secular instruction and real educational need count, but the decisive inquiry is whether religious membership actually organized this special allocation of general public authority. The act's design and context establish that defect here; demographic homogeneity alone would not. One-off legislation can be lawful, and a formally general statute can still be engineered for a denomination. Actual eligibility, governmental reasons, powers conveyed and access for comparable communities reveal which is occurring. Shaw rejected racial territorial design alone as the particular equal-protection deprivation asserted within a common electorate. This case rests on a distinct Establishment Clause injury: the State's use of denomination to organize governmental authority. It neither imports the rejected racial-design theory nor requires excluded voters to prove an election loss. Religious residents retain equal civic standing, and children retain equal access to lawful secular disability services. A neutral local program may meet those needs without this allocation. Wieder permits such an arrangement without commanding a placement, while Carter creates no exception to denominational neutrality. He joins affirmance with implementation left to the responsible state authorities and courts, and sees no need to revise Lemon, Aguilar or Ball.

Scalia dissents, joined by Thomas throughout. Chapter 748 creates an ordinary public institution with elected lay officials, secular instruction and general public duties, not a religious institution's veto. Larkin therefore does not establish that the State delegated to a church, and McDaniel protects religious citizens from civic disability. The lawful village line and distinctive educational and cultural problem explain the district without an established religious office qualification or devotional program. Even when accommodation is religiously motivated, Amos, Walz and Zorach allow responses beyond what free exercise commands. He accepts the prohibition on denominational favoritism but rejects the inference that known homogeneity and special legislation establish it here. Absence of guaranteed future accommodations is not present discrimination; courts can address a supported later unequal-treatment claim, including the existing benefit if necessary. He also rejects Kennedy's application because the Legislature used the existing lawful geographic community to provide secular education. The dissent favors abandoning Lemon's unitary method for fidelity to longstanding accommodation traditions and would overrule Aguilar and Ball at an appropriate opportunity. These are noncontrolling positions, not an agreed comprehensive replacement test or a present overruling. Zobrest supplies no aid-merits precedent, and surviving accommodation authorities carry the limited public-school argument. Reversal would remove the federal ground while leaving the reserved state constitutional claim for the state courts. The dissent approves no clergy veto, religious voting qualification, governmental worship or unlimited exemption.

### Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District, No. 91-2024

Merits decision, 1993-06-07

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-02-24; decided 1993-06-07. Review of the Second Circuit, 959 F.2d 381 (1992), affirming summary judgment for the school district.

Question: Whether the district could exclude a religious perspective on family and child-rearing from an otherwise admitted after-hours community subject, and whether the Establishment Clause required that exclusion.

The church sought after-hours access to school facilities for a film series addressing family life and child-rearing from a Christian perspective. The district permitted social, civic and recreational uses but twice rejected this application under its religious-use prohibition. The requested meetings were open to the public and lacked school sponsorship. An earlier denial of Sunday worship services is not challenged here. The Court does not treat every kind of worship as within the forum or decide the broadest possible forum classification.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0 in the judgment.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A religious perspective cannot be excluded from an admitted subject

**Controlling proposition:** When a public school makes an after-hours forum available for private discussion of family life and child-rearing, it may not reject that otherwise eligible discussion solely because the viewpoint is religious. This prohibition applies even assuming a nonpublic forum; it does not require an unlimited public forum or invalidate neutral restrictions on subjects and activities outside the actual forum.

**Authority:** White’s speech portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the district’s stated reason for denial is viewpoint discrimination. Perry and Cornelius permit a government to define a limited forum but require neutrality among perspectives on a subject it admits. Family life and child-rearing were eligible topics, and the films addressed those subjects from a religious perspective. Describing religion itself as an excluded subject cannot change that relationship. Nor does exclusion of every religious denomination cure discrimination between religious and nonreligious views. The record does not establish that the application was denied for anticipated disorder or some independent neutral eligibility rule. The district’s later alternatives therefore cannot sustain the stated denial. The Court need not decide whether all of the property is a public forum or whether every religious activity must be permitted there. The earlier Sunday-service application is outside this case. On remand, genuinely neutral scheduling, safety and subject-matter rules remain available, but the same viewpoint distinction does not.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association, 460 U.S.37: viewpoint neutrality within a nonpublic forum applied.
- Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, 473 U.S.788: permissible forum limits distinguished from forbidden viewpoint exclusion.
- Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S.263: religious perspective does not by itself remove otherwise eligible private speech from equal-access protection.

##### Equal private access does not establish religion here

**Controlling proposition:** Allowing this voluntary, publicly accessible, after-hours film presentation without school sponsorship does not violate the Establishment Clause. Equal access serves a secular forum purpose and neither primarily advances religion as governmental activity nor creates excessive entanglement; this holding authorizes access to private speech, not an official devotional program.

**Authority:** White’s establishment portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter (6 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the district cannot justify viewpoint discrimination by invoking an establishment problem that equal access does not create. Widmar explains why a government’s neutral provision of space does not make every private speaker’s message its own. The proposed films would be shown after school, attendance would be voluntary, and the meetings would be open to the public rather than presented as school instruction. Under Lemon, the forum serves a secular community-use purpose; allowing a religious perspective on an admitted subject does not produce governmental advancement of religion or excessive entanglement. The Lee decision addresses official graduation worship and religious pressure within that school event, conditions absent here. The contrary argument confuses public ownership of the room with school sponsorship of the message. Nothing in this decision orders religious instruction, public financing of devotion or compulsory participation. It also leaves outside review the separate denial of Sunday worship services and other activities outside the forum’s lawful scope.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Widmar, 454 U.S.263: neutral access and private attribution applied.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S.602: purpose, primary effect and entanglement applied, not replaced.
- Lee v. Weisman, June 24, 1992: the official-worship and pressure holdings distinguished and preserved.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: forum viewpoint neutrality, Widmar and the governing establishment framework governed. After this decision: religious family-life discussion cannot be excluded as a viewpoint within this admitted subject, and equal access here creates no establishment. Seven join the speech portion, six the Lemon-based application. Separate writings supply no replacement establishment test; neither the whole religious-use rule nor every possible worship restriction is facially adjudicated.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy concurs in part and in the judgment. He accepts the viewpoint holding and the absence of establishment where the school merely permits nonsponsored private use. He declines to endorse the Lemon/endorsement formulation, which is unnecessary to his conclusion; his narrower ground is noncontrolling as a separate writing. Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs in the judgment. They regard equal access as a permissible neutral accommodation and agree that the district cannot discriminate against a religious perspective. They reject reliance on Lemon and distinguish government establishment of a sect from neutral permission for religious expression. Neither writing supplies five votes to replace the governing test. Souter joins the Court without a separate opinion.

The school may retain genuinely neutral rules for comparable applicants. No damages award, public financing of devotional instruction or admission of the unchallenged Sunday-service application is ordered.

Supreme Court review is complete; the lower courts implement the express remand and appropriate declaratory or injunctive relief. No later school action is assumed. The distinct public-payment question in Zobrest remains undecided.

### Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp., No. 93-1525. — 1995-02-21

### The Court may consider this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal

**Controlling proposition:** The Court elects to consider Amtrak's governmental-entity status despite Lebron's earlier express disavowal of that argument because it supplies a legal basis for the same preserved First Amendment claim, falls within the governmental-actor question presented, and can be resolved on the established institutional record without a new factual theory or additional findings. Yee separates a new supporting argument from a distinct claim and independently enforces the question presented. The appellate reliance on Amtrak's private status makes that premise relevant without establishing a rule that any passing discussion preserves every argument. No actual adversarial prejudice or broader concession is found, and the Court imposes no burden on the respondent to prove prejudice.

**Precedent treatment:**

### Special federal creation and permanent majority appointment make Amtrak governmental

**Controlling proposition:** When the Federal Government creates a corporation by special law to advance governmental objectives and permanently retains authority to appoint a majority of its governing directors, the corporation is governmental for First Amendment purposes notwithstanding a statutory declaration that it is not a governmental agency. Amtrak satisfies those combined conditions; ordinary operating discretion in its corporate officers does not require a separate external federal command for each challenged act.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's constitutional-status portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight Justices directly support the same permanent-majority-appointment rule and its application; Justice O'Connor's conditional view of a question she declines to reach is not a join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress cannot settle constitutional responsibility simply by choosing a corporate label. Amtrak was specially created to pursue national transportation objectives, and the statutory arrangement permanently retains governmental authority over selection of its governing majority. The Secretary's position, the five other direct Presidential selections, and the preferred shareholder's governmental selections must not be mistaken for nine direct Presidential appointments. The different routes nevertheless establish the required enduring majority authority; the corporate president's selection within the institution does not defeat it. Congress's nonagency classification keeps its statutory operation, but cannot place an institution created and governed in this manner beyond the Constitution. San Francisco Arts & Athletics distinguishes a federally chartered and assisted private corporation; charter, subsidy, or regulation alone is not the ground here. Nor does delegation of everyday decisions to corporate officers make this institution private. An external federal order to reject Lebron's work is therefore unnecessary. The Court decides governmental status, leaving the advertising policy's constitutionality and the suitable remedy for later adjudication.

**Precedent treatment:**

- San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Inc. v. United States Olympic Committee, 483 U.S. 522 (1987): distinguishes federal charter and assistance to a private institution from special creation coupled with permanent governmental majority-appointment authority.
- Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co., 500 U.S. 614 (1991), and Georgia v. McCollum, 505 U.S. 42 (1992): preserve attention to actual governmental legal machinery in their jury-selection settings; neither makes billboard advertising an exclusively governmental function or supplies a general rule for all publicly benefited corporations.
- Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 419 U.S. 345 (1974), and Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991 (1982): distinguish attribution of a private entity's particular act from identifying an institution that is itself governmental under the combined rule.
- Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., decided November 14, 1994: keeps the distinct inquiry into an entity's state sovereign immunity separate; absence of immunity does not imply constitutional privacy.
- American National Red Cross v. S. G., 505 U.S. 247 (1992): preserves its express charter-jurisdiction rule, which supplies no constitutional actor classification.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Federal charter, subsidy, regulation, governmental property, public benefit, a single appointment, temporary investment, or merely the current ownership of shares is not independently sufficient under this rule. Permanent authority to appoint the governing majority, governmental objectives, and special governmental creation must be present together. The Court decides no universal agency status, sovereign immunity, Treasury obligation, federal tort-liability rule, or charter jurisdiction. The 1994 recodification from former 45 U.S.C. §541 to 49 U.S.C. §24301(a) does not itself adjudicate constitutional status. ### McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission, No. 93-986 (current petitioner: executor of Margaret McIntyre's estate). — 1995-04-19

Authority: [Record](../records/McIntyre_v_Ohio_Elections_Commission_merits_1995-04-19.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### Compelled identification on these political leaflets requires exacting scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-defined requirement that an individual identify herself on ordinary political issue leaflets burdens core political expression, including the choice to speak anonymously. The State must satisfy exacting scrutiny by showing narrow tailoring to an overriding state interest; viewpoint neutrality or the election setting alone does not lower that standard.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the author's choice to omit her identity is protected as part of political expression. Talley protects anonymous handbills and recognizes the place of anonymous publication in public discussion. Ohio's requirement selects publications by their electoral subject and conditions their circulation on identification. It is therefore a content-based burden on the individual's communication, not simply a neutral rule for administering a ballot. Burson preserves demanding scrutiny for campaign-specific restrictions even though its different polling-place judgment had no shared validity rationale. Ohio invokes the modest size of the identification obligation and its election-related purpose. Neither answers whether the State may impose the burden on this ordinary issue leaflet. The Court applies exacting scrutiny requiring narrow tailoring to an overriding interest. It does not decide that anonymity is always benign or require proof that this speaker faced retaliation. Candidate finance reports, organizational attribution, coordinated spending, and fraudulent impersonation present distinct questions left outside this holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection of anonymous handbills to this individual political leaflet; the Court does not establish immunity for fraudulent communications.
- Burson v. Freeman, May 26, 1992: applies the independently majority-supported scrutiny distinction; its fractured polling-place validity judgment does not settle this leaflet requirement.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780: distinguishes the election-administration setting; it does not reduce scrutiny for every election-related speech restriction.

### The asserted voter-information interest does not justify this identification demand

**Holding and operative rule:** A general interest in helping voters evaluate a source does not justify requiring this ordinary individual to place her name and residence or business address on her independently produced, truthful political leaflet. The Court leaves candidate finance, campaign committees, organizational spending, and other materially distinct disclosure settings for separate analysis.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that voters' interest in source information does not justify this direct condition on an ordinary citizen's political pamphlet. Talley protects the decision to publish without personal attribution; an author need not establish that identity has no possible informational value. Ohio relies on Buckley's campaign-finance disclosure and Bellotti's discussion of identifying corporate advertising. Those sources concern different mechanisms and interests. A report identifying financial participation in a campaign is not the same requirement as putting a private individual's name and address on every issue leaflet she distributes. Corporate spending likewise supplies no automatic rule for this record, which establishes neither a campaign organization nor coordinated financing. The Court does not hold that source information is useless or that all disclosure is forbidden. It holds that the general informational rationale is inadequate for the burden imposed here. Ohio's committee alternatives and personal-correspondence exception do not transform Margaret McIntyre's independently produced leaflets into those different statutory categories or justify this sanction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies protection for the ordinary author's choice of anonymous publication.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: distinguishes campaign-finance reporting and its specific interests; those disclosure holdings remain undisturbed.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: distinguishes corporate-advertising source information; its observation does not resolve this private individual's leaflet.

### Fraud prevention does not justify this application to truthful independent advocacy

**Holding and operative rule:** Although preventing electoral fraud is a weighty interest, Ohio may not impose this categorical identification requirement on truthful, independently produced individual issue leaflets without the necessary narrow fit to that interest. The enforcement order and $100 fine must be set aside; properly tailored fraud, impersonation, and materially different disclosure rules remain available.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment on his distinct anonymous-political-pamphlet ground; Justice Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ohio's fraud-prevention interest does not supply the missing fit. Talley protects anonymous expression without preventing laws directed at false or fraudulent communications. Ohio's identification requirement reaches ordinary truthful advocacy regardless of any finding of deception, false attribution, or corrupt financing. The record contains no adjudicated fraud in McIntyre's leaflet. The State argues that identification helps investigators find wrongdoers and deters unlawful claims before they affect an election. That practical benefit does not explain why every individual issue pamphlet must carry a name and address when existing laws address fraud and false attribution more directly. The personal-correspondence exception and permission to exempt impractical small items do not cure this application to printed leaflets; no implementing exemption rule resolves the fine presented. The Court neither assumes that anonymity guarantees honest speech nor invalidates all disclosure provisions. It sets aside this sanction, preserves properly tailored enforcement against actual abuse, and leaves organizational and financial-reporting requirements outside the judgment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60: applies anonymous-publication protection while distinguishing measures directed at fraud.
- Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1: leaves candidate-finance disclosure and corruption-related interests intact rather than treating them as dispositive of this ordinary leaflet.
- First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765: leaves materially different corporate source-disclosure questions outside the individual sanction decided here.

#### Precedent Treatment

Talley's anonymous-handbill protection applies to this individual's political issue leaflet. Burson's majority-supported scrutiny distinction remains separate from its fractured validity judgment concerning polling-place environs. Anderson's election-administration framework does not decide this direct speech burden. Buckley's campaign-finance disclosure holdings and Bellotti's corporate-advertising discussion concern materially different interests and mechanisms and remain undisturbed. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Effective April 19, 1995, the State may not enforce this name-and-residence-or-business-address requirement against the truthful, independently produced individual political leaflets at issue. Seven Justices directly adopt exacting scrutiny and the inadequate information and fraud-prevention justifications; Thomas supplies a separate eighth judgment vote. No Marks synthesis is needed. The decision leaves candidate and committee disclosures, coordinated expenditures, large financial contributions, organizational spending, fraudulent impersonation, and appropriately tailored fraud regulation outside the adjudicated application. It does not create an automatic personal-correspondence or small-item exemption, decide an unproduced exemption rule, or adjudicate subsection (B).

### Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston; No. 94-749 — 1995-06-19

Authority: [Record](../records/Hurley_v_Irish_American_Gay_Lesbian_and_Bisexual_Group_of_Boston_merits_1995-06-19.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### Private parade composition and compelled inclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices adopt the as-applied private-expression holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the right to speak includes control over the composition of a private expressive presentation. Barnette prohibits compulsory affirmation, and Tornillo protects editorial choice against compelled inclusion. Turner preserves the importance of a speaker’s selection within a particular medium; its separate broadcast context is not mechanically imported here. A parade presents expression through the groups and messages its organizers choose, even if those selections are permissive or varied. GLIB’s bannered unit has its own protected message, but that protection creates no entitlement to insert it into another private speaker’s presentation. Use of public streets under a neutral permit does not convert this parade into a governmental forum. Roberts and PruneYard concern access without the same compelled alteration of expression. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations, with credibility deference, rejects the conclusion that absence of a single theme eliminates protection. The Court invalidates only this compelled expressive inclusion, without finding every customer identity a message or requiring proof that spectators endorse each contingent.

**Precedent treatment:**

- West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette: applies protection against compelled expression.
- Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo: applies editorial selection protection.
- Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC: applies existing medium-specific editorial protection, without importing unrelated broadcast results.
- Roberts v. United States Jaycees; PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins: distinguish access not materially altering the private expressive composition.
- Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union; Fiske v. Kansas: apply independent review of intertwined First Amendment characterizations while retaining credibility deference.

The respondents’ state-action/public-forum theory against the City was dismissed and not cross-appealed; it is not revived here. The importance of message misattribution in another setting remains open. No general antidiscrimination exemption, facial invalidation or judgment about the correct political message is announced.

#### Precedent Treatment

The treatment accompanying the holding controls. Ordinary nondiscrimination applications remain distinct and enforceable.

#### Law After Decision

Private parade composition receives protection against compelled insertion of an identified expressive unit. Other expressive-association disputes require a comparably material expressive burden; ordinary customer identity alone is not a message.
