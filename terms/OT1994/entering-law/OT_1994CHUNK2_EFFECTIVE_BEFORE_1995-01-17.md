## Tome_v_United_States_merits_1995-01-10.md — 1995-01-10

### Event

Tome v. United States, No. 93-6892. Argued October 5, 1994; decided January 10, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 3 F.3d 342 (1993), which affirmed an aggravated-sexual-abuse conviction. The question is whether original Rule 801(d)(1)(B) permits substantive admission of prior consistent statements made after the alleged motive to fabricate arose. A.T.'s accounts to six witnesses followed the asserted custody motive; motive and abuse remain allegations rather than fresh factual findings. Render form: full. Basis: the writings distinguish the controlling temporal rule, interpretive limits, and alternative-admission remand.

### Holdings

### Substantive prior consistent statements require the premotive relationship

**Controlling proposition:** Under original Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(B), a prior statement offered substantively to rebut an express or implied charge of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive must have been made before the particular charged fabrication, influence, or motive arose. The declarant must testify and be subject to cross-examination concerning the statement, and the statement must be consistent with that testimony and offered for that specified rebuttal purpose; general relevance or repetition alone does not satisfy this nonhearsay rule.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, and Ginsburg. Six Justices adopt the same rule and explanation; no aggregation of separate rationales is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Rule carries forward the premotive requirement associated with its specific rebuttal terms. Salerno requires satisfaction of the chosen evidentiary provision's actual conditions; general fairness cannot replace them. Daubert displaces common-law requirements incompatible with enacted rules but permits compatible inherited meaning to illuminate their terms. Here the words describing rebuttal of recent fabrication or improper motive invoke the distinction between answering the source of an accusation and merely repeating an account. The defense asserted that A.T. accused her father to remain with her mother. The challenged accounts followed that asserted custody motive and therefore do not qualify under this subparagraph. A later statement may sometimes possess indirect probative force, as the Government argues, but relevance alone does not establish this particular condition for substantive admission. The Court decides neither that A.T. fabricated her account nor that the statements are inadmissible for every purpose. Other exceptions and properly limited nonhearsay use retain their separate requirements.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, 505 U.S. 317 (1992): applies its requirement that the selected hearsay provision's conditions be satisfied; its similar-motive and forfeiture holdings remain distinct.
- Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993): applies its distinction between incompatible common-law additions and compatible interpretive guidance; no expert-evidence factor is transferred to this Rule.

### Alternative admissibility and ordinary error review remain for remand

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of admission under Rule 801(d)(1)(B) leaves the Court of Appeals to decide other preserved grounds of admission and, if the statements are not admissible on such a ground, the consequences under ordinary harmless-error rules. For preserved nonconstitutional evidentiary error, the Government must establish whole-record fair assurance that the error did not substantially sway the judgment; grave doubt defeats harmlessness, and neither immediate retrial nor acquittal follows solely from the Rule-construction error.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, and Ginsburg. Six Justices adopt these remedial limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that identifying the wrong admission rule does not complete appellate review of the conviction. Williamson separates an exception's proper construction from the admissibility of particular evidence and from the relief an established error requires. The Court of Appeals left the medical-diagnosis and residual-exception grounds undecided; those grounds remain available only within their own terms and ordinary preservation limits. The reviewing court may not assume their satisfaction from the present holding. If no lawful ground sustains admission, Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos require evaluation of the whole record under the Government's harmlessness burden. The rejection of the temporal limitation below did not supply that evaluation. Chapman would apply only if a constitutional trial error were actually reached and found; this decision finds none. The Court also does not revive an unpreserved aggregate Rule 403 objection or reopen every separate appellate issue. Remand permits the necessary bounded inquiry while leaving both guilt and the ultimate need for a new trial undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Williamson v. United States, 512 U.S. 594 (1994): applies its separation of admissibility, preservation, and judgment consequences.
- Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946): applies its whole-record substantial-influence standard to preserved nonconstitutional error.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): preserves its beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard only for constitutional trial error actually reached and found.
- United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993): distinguishes forfeited error from this preserved construction question; no new preservation finding is made.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Rule 803(4) requires statements for medical diagnosis or treatment describing history, symptoms, pain, sensations, or their inception or general cause insofar as reasonably pertinent to diagnosis or treatment. Then-Rule 803(24) applies to a statement not specifically covered by any of the foregoing exceptions and requires equivalent circumstantial guarantees of trustworthiness, a material fact, greater probative value on the offered point than other evidence obtainable through reasonable efforts, service of the Rules' general purposes and the interests of justice, and advance notice affording a fair opportunity to prepare, including the intended statement's particulars and the declarant's name and address. The Court finds no such condition satisfied or unsatisfied. Rehabilitation-only use, other properly limited nonhearsay purposes, and factbound questions outside the admitted temporal issue remain undecided.

### Precedent Treatment

Salerno and Daubert supply the enacted-rule and compatible-background principles. Williamson and Kotteakos govern the separate remedial inquiry; Chapman and Olano retain their distinct constitutional-error and forfeiture scopes. No earlier decision is overruled.

### Law After Decision

Effective January 10, 1995, original Rule 801(d)(1)(B) has the stated premotive condition for this substantive use. The Court adds no general exclusion of postmotive statements and changes no other hearsay exception, rehabilitation rule, preservation rule, or harmless-error burden.

### Separate Writings

Justice Scalia joins the Court's text-and-context construction and explains that Advisory Committee Notes can persuade as expert commentary without obtaining special authority from their authors' role as draftsmen. He does not adopt a rule that committee expectations themselves establish an omitted condition. The Court's explanation rests on enacted language and compatible inherited usage, so his concurrence does not subtract from the six votes for either holding.

Justice Breyer, joined by Justices O'Connor and Thomas, would affirm. The Rule requires genuine rebuttal, but its words do not expressly make chronology conclusive in every case. A statement's circumstances can weaken an accusation of fabrication even when an asserted motive existed earlier. The Tenth Circuit relied on the initial disclosure's spontaneity and its assessment of the alleged custody motive, rather than on consistency alone. Breyer's approach retains the declarant's testimony and cross-examination, a true rebuttal purpose, and Rules 402 and 403. His method of examining the actual purpose and inferential force of a statement, reflected in United States v. Moore, does not equate every repeated account with admissible evidence. He regards compatible common-law guidance as useful but insufficient to impose an invariant condition the enacted text does not expressly state. The dissent finds no separate exception satisfied and offers no independent harmlessness determination; its disagreement is the construction supporting the judgment below.

### Procedure After Action

The mandate reverses the Tenth Circuit judgment and returns the case to that court to determine whether another preserved ground admits the statements and, if not, to apply ordinary harmless-error rules. Review here is complete; appellate proceedings continue. A new trial follows only if error requiring that relief is established.
