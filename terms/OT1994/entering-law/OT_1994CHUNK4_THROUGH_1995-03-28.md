### Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co., No. 93-1783. — 1995-03-21

Authority: [Record](../records/Director_Office_of_Workers_Compensation_Programs_v_Newport_News_Shipbuilding_and_Dry_Dock_Co_merits_1995-03-21.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### The Director's general administrative interest does not confer independent review

**Holding and operative rule:** The Director's general interest in correct administration of the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act does not by itself make the Director adversely affected or aggrieved under 33 U.S.C. §921(c) by this employee's unappealed benefits award. The refusal to entertain that challenge is affirmed; no rule of categorical governmental incapacity is adopted, and the separate Special Fund disposition remains undisturbed.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Breyer: eight direct rationale votes. Justice Ginsburg concurs in the judgment and agrees with the case-specific statutory result, but does not join this explanation. No fractured-opinion synthesis is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that administrative responsibility does not itself satisfy the condition Congress placed on judicial review. Section 921(c) requires a person adversely affected or aggrieved; §921(b)(3)'s administrative party-in-interest route and §939's claimant-assistance duties do not transfer Harcum's appellate entitlement to the Director. Germain requires respect for the particular statutory review route, and Gardner requires fidelity to enacted conditions rather than an administrator's preferred policy. The Director identifies no impairment of a specific entrusted function here. Harcum did not appeal, and disagreement with his award does not establish an independent adverse interest. Possible employer insolvency presents speculative Fund exposure that the requested benefits change would not reduce. Perini's treatment of respondent participation does not decide who may initiate review. Greenwich Collieries governs persuasion in covered adjudications, not this appellate entitlement. Uniform administration is important, but accepting that interest alone would erase the statutory condition. The Court reserves express governmental review authority, genuine financial or independently statutory injury, and the Director's respondent role; it decides neither compensation nor Fund liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249: applies respect for the requirements of the particular statutory route; its bankruptcy appellate holding remains unchanged.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to enacted conditions rather than administrative additions; its benefits-causation holding is not expanded.
- Director, OWCP v. Perini North River Associates, 459 U.S. 297: distinguishes respondent participation from a Director-initiated appeal; the respondent issue remains reserved.
- Director, OWCP v. Greenwich Collieries, June 20, 1994: preserves its persuasion-burden rules and treats them as distinct from statutory appellate entitlement.
- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992: leaves its Article III holdings unchanged; the statutory disposition makes a new constitutional rule unnecessary.

#### Precedent Treatment

The Court applies Germain's respect for statutory review conditions and Gardner's fidelity to enacted limits. Perini does not resolve a right to initiate appellate review. Greenwich Collieries remains the rule for persuasion in covered adjudication, and Lujan's Article III holdings remain unchanged. No precedent is overruled, and no general restriction on Congress's capacity to authorize governmental litigation is announced.

#### Law After Decision

Effective March 21, 1995, a general interest in correct administration does not alone satisfy the Director's §921(c) aggrievement requirement for this claimant's unappealed award. This adds the bounded statutory application; it does not decide all governmental aggrievement, respondent status, disability merits, or Fund liability. Congress's ability to confer an express review right and claims based on genuine financial or independently statutory injury remain open.

#### Separate Writings

Justice Ginsburg concurs in the judgment. She agrees that the existing review provision does not authorize this independent challenge on the general administrative interest asserted. She does not infer that Congress deliberately intended every disparity produced by that provision. Congress may supply governmental review authority; statutory agreement here does not require endorsement of a broader account of legislative purpose. She does not decide Harcum's benefits, the Special Fund merits, or the Director's respondent role.

#### Procedure After Action

### Anderson v. Edwards, No. 93-1883. — 1995-03-22

Authority: [Record](../records/Anderson_v_Edwards_merits_1995-03-22.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### A lawful common-caretaker unit does not itself impute unavailable income

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal AFDC law permits California to apply its benefit schedule to the otherwise eligible applicants and recipients lawfully grouped under the challenged common-caretaker rule; a grant smaller than the sum of separate grants does not itself establish forbidden assumed support. Federal membership, exclusion, availability, disregard, and equitable-treatment requirements remain operative, and neither household residence nor a unit label makes an outsider's unavailable resources countable.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that defining the aided unit and deciding what income is available are distinct inquiries. King, Lewis, and Van Lare protect against fictitious support from persons outside the relevant legally protected relationships; they do not establish an invariable separate-unit entitlement for nonsibling children. California measures the needs of applicants grouped through the common caretaker and applies its unit-size schedule. Verna Edwards herself receives no aid, so the Court does not sustain the rule by assuming her membership or contribution. The recipients correctly insist that a label cannot evade the availability regulations. Those regulations retain their full force, including the substitute-parent prohibition, protection against reductions solely from a nonresponsible person's presence, and actual or qualified legal availability requirements. But a difference from hypothetical separate grants is not itself a finding of outside income. Dandridge preserves bounded state choice over benefit levels without repealing these safeguards. The Court rejects the categorical challenge and leaves concrete forbidden attributions for their actual facts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- King v. Smith, 392 U.S. 309: distinguishes the invalid substitute-parent assumption while preserving its protection against fictitious support.
- Lewis v. Martin, 397 U.S. 552: preserves the actual-support limitations; it does not prescribe a universal nonsibling unit boundary.
- Van Lare v. Hurley, 421 U.S. 338: distinguishes reduction attributed to an unaided nonresponsible person's presence from measurement of this lawful aided unit; its outside-support protection remains.
- Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471: applies bounded state discretion over benefit levels without dispensing with federal statutory or regulatory conditions.

### Mandatory family inclusion does not exhaust lawful unit composition

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 602(a)(38)'s qualified mandatory inclusion of co-resident parents and qualifying siblings does not make those relationships the exclusive permissible assistance unit. Optional nonparent caretakers or nonsiblings may decline aid only when mandatory inclusion does not require membership, and §605 creates no duty to transfer non-AFDC income between nonsiblings or other unit members.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's required inclusions are not an unwritten prohibition on every other lawful grouping. Section 602(a)(38) directs inclusion of the co-resident parent and qualifying sibling, subject to its stated exceptions, dependency conditions, and Title II proviso. Gilliard addresses that congressional family rule and associated child-support treatment; it does not establish the asserted ceiling on state unit definition. The contrary argument gives the mandatory list exclusive force its terms do not supply. Nor is the answer that every individual can freely withdraw: an optional nonparent caretaker or nonsibling can decline only outside mandatory inclusion, and the cited sibling example may require both children to decline together. Income legally considered in a valid unit need not be transferred separately between every recipient, but unavailable resources do not become countable through that observation. Section 605 concerns proper use of the AFDC grant, not a mutual private support duty or non-AFDC transfer obligation. Restricted awards, payee arrangements, and claimed unlawful opt-out barriers remain fact-dependent questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bowen v. Gilliard, 483 U.S. 587: applies the distinction between Congress's required family inclusion and an asserted exclusive unit definition; its child-support holding remains limited to its own statutory setting.
- King, Lewis, and Van Lare: retain their unavailable-outside-support protections notwithstanding lawful membership and grant computation.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to enacted conditions without inserting an additional exclusivity command.

### The classwide equitable-treatment objection fails on its merits

**Holding and operative rule:** Applying the same unit-size schedule to eligible applicants lawfully grouped under the common-caretaker rule does not violate §§233.10(a)(1) and 233.20(a)(1)(i) merely because consolidation reduces aggregate aid or an unaided caretaker's needs are absent from a grant. Classifications must remain reasonable, statewide, objective, equitable in light of the Act, and consistent with eligibility requirements; the Court assumes without deciding that the cited provisions are enforceable under §1983.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that equitable treatment does not require summing the grants of hypothetical separate units. Both regulations have independent force: classifications may not arbitrarily exclude persons or conflict with the Act's provisions and purposes, and need and assistance must be determined objectively and equitably. The Court accepts the district court's hardship finding and the argument that reduced aid may discourage relatives from caring for children. Section 601's family-support purposes nevertheless do not prescribe separate-unit totals as the only lawful measure. A common schedule for the same number of aided members supplies an objective comparison. When the caretaker does not claim aid, that grant does not include the caretaker's own needs; this distinction alone does not establish arbitrary treatment of poor relatives. Dandridge preserves bounded benefit-level choices, while King, Lewis, and Van Lare continue to prohibit fictitious support. Administrative convenience cannot excuse an inconsistent eligibility restriction. The Court resolves this preserved classwide merits ground, reserving specific inequitable administration and making no §1983 enforceability ruling.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471: applies bounded benefit-level discretion, not a replacement of the regulations' equity commands with constitutional rational-basis review.
- King, Lewis, and Van Lare: preserve the substantive availability limits within the equitable scheme.
- Suter v. Artist M.: its enforcement-channel treatment does not decide this substantive AFDC construction; enforceability of the cited provisions is assumed without decision here.

### The outsider-proration option does not require fragmentation of this lawful unit

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 612's optional reasonable proration of shelter, utilities, and similar needs when household members are not claiming aid together does not require dividing this otherwise lawful assistance unit. A unit-size schedule is not itself an election of that option; when a State elects proration, the statutory SSI qualification and §233.20(a)(5)'s separate SSI, landlord-tenant, both-standards, and plan requirements remain binding.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's proration option concerns a different relationship from the aided unit sustained here. Section 612 addresses a claimant sharing a household with people not claiming aid together. Its detailed protections matter when that option is used; they do not command fragmentation of every lawful unit so the State can recombine it through proration. The recipients' strongest objection is that a State could evade those safeguards merely by relabeling separate claims. The answer is the bounded membership ruling, not an unlimited labeling power: federal inclusions and exclusions, availability, and equitable treatment first constrain formation of the unit. Once those requirements permit this grouping, applying its size-based schedule does not itself invoke outsider proration. Section 233.20(a)(5) remains independently operative, including its landlord-tenant bar and conditional requirement to prorate both standards reasonably under a specified plan. No particular percentage, SSI household, or landlord-tenant application is approved. This construction preserves both unit formation and the distinct optional proration mechanism.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bowen v. Gilliard, 483 U.S. 587: retains the mandatory-family-grouping rule without making a household of nonclaimants identical to an aided unit.
- Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471: preserves lawful benefit-level choices, subject to the independently operative proration conditions.

### Independent construction supplies the result without a binding-guidance holding

**Holding and operative rule:** The statutes and binding regulations independently permit this bounded common-caretaker rule and defeat the preserved classwide objections. ACF-AT-94-6 may confirm that reading at its actual scope, but guidance does not create authority, override a regulation, or resolve an objection it does not address; the maximum deference owed to the guidance is left undecided.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes at the stated bounded scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the governing texts supply the necessary authority. Gardner requires attention to the conditions actually enacted; NationsBank concerns an actual statutory delegation and does not establish the force of every administrative instrument. Guernsey likewise requires separate examination of the regulations and the issue the guidance addresses. The 1994 transmittal distinguishes unit membership from available income and illustrates both consolidated and separate units. In either example an outsider's unavailable resources remain excluded; where state policy retains separate units, sharing cannot be presumed across them. That account confirms the Court's construction. It cannot replace analysis of §233.10's Act-purpose requirement, which the transmittal does not separately examine, and the Court has decided that ground itself. The reproduced 1986 instructions are an excerpt, not proof of an entire document. The Court neither accepts an administrative trump over availability protections nor decides a broader deference question unnecessary to the judgment. No finding of actual grant misuse follows from the guidance's remedial discussion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies independent fidelity to the enacted conditions.
- NationsBank of North Carolina, N.A. v. Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co., January 18, 1995: distinguishes its particular delegation and agency choice; no general guidance deference rule is imported.
- Shalala v. Guernsey Memorial Hospital, March 6, 1995: applies separation of distinct regulatory questions and the actual instrument's function; no AFDC regulation is displaced.

#### Precedent Treatment

King, Lewis, and Van Lare remain controlling against fictitious outside support and are distinguished from lawful unit formation. Gilliard retains its mandatory-family-inclusion and related child-support scope without establishing an exclusive unit definition. Dandridge retains bounded benefit-level discretion, subject to all federal requirements. Beaton's contrary categorical equation of common-caretaker grouping with forbidden assumed income cannot govern this rule; its unaddressed equity and proration questions are decided independently here. Gardner, NationsBank, and Guernsey retain their actual statutory and regulatory boundaries. Suter supplies no substantive AFDC-unit rule or necessary enforceability disposition. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Effective March 22, 1995, the challenged common-caretaker grouping survives the classwide availability, exclusive-inclusion, equitable-treatment, and exclusive-proration objections. The equity claim is resolved, not waived or left for initial remand consideration. Actual unlawful income attribution, violations of mandatory membership/exclusion/disregard rules, properly presented opt-out barriers, and specific inequitable administration remain subject to their own factual and legal requirements. The Court decides no later welfare-reform statute, constitutional entitlement to a grant amount, general §1983 enforceability question, or maximum guidance-deference rule. All controlling propositions have nine direct opinion joins; no Marks inquiry is needed.

### Operative statutory and regulatory qualifications

Section 602(a)(38), except as otherwise provided in the same part, requires inclusion of a dependent child's parent and a brother or sister satisfying §606(a)(1)–(2) or §607(a) when living in the same home. Their income or available income enters the determination under paragraphs (7) and (8), notwithstanding §405(j) for Title II benefits. The reproduced 1986 instructions cover otherwise eligible natural/adoptive parents, dependent blood or adoptive siblings within the State's age limit, and a married stepparent subject to a generally applicable state duty equal to a natural parent's support duty. A stepbrother or stepsister is not automatically included merely because the stepparent is. In incapacity cases, and under a State's unemployment-of-the-principal-earner program, both parents are included if otherwise eligible.

Those instructions retain exclusions for SSI recipients, persons failing citizenship/alienage requirements, aliens ineligible because of sponsor income/resources or agency or organizational sponsorship, and lump-sum-income ineligibility. Required overlapping co-resident memberships consolidate. A family need not apply for a grandchild in the minor-mother example, but applying triggers the linked mandatory grouping. A nonparent caretaker or nonsibling can decline aid only when not mandatorily included; a required sibling cannot withdraw alone while leaving the required sibling grouping intact, and the supplied example may require both siblings to decline. The reproduced instructions distinguish Title II payments to a representative payee in the household from payments to an outside payee, with the latter counted only to the extent made available for the beneficiary. No hypothetical restricted award or payee dispute is adjudicated as a named family's fact.

Section 233.90(a)(1) determines deprivation through death, continued absence, incapacity, or a covered State-plan unemployment case by reference to a natural/adoptive parent or the qualifying married stepparent with the generally applicable equal support duty. Its second sentence independently forbids using a substitute parent's, man-in-the-house's, or other undescribed person's presence to find ineligibility or assume income. The Court does not reduce that regulation to parental deprivation alone.

Section 233.20(a)(2)(viii) forbids reducing a need item solely because a non-legally-responsible individual is present and forbids assumed contributions, subject separately to §233.20(a)(3)(xiv)'s stepparent-income provision, §233.20(a)(5)'s authorized proration, and §233.51's sponsored-alien regime. These are distinct qualified provisions, not permission to presume every resident's contribution. For a co-resident married stepparent without the generally applicable equal support duty, paragraph (a)(3)(xiv) counts income after deducting the first $90 of gross earnings; the State need-standard allowance for the stepparent and qualifying co-resident tax dependents whose needs are not otherwise included, excluding sanctioned or required-but-noncooperating persons; amounts actually paid to qualifying dependents outside the home; and outside-household alimony or child support. If the stepparent is in the unit, paragraph (a)(11)(i)–(ii)'s disregards apply instead. Sponsored-alien deemed income does not count toward unsponsored family members' need unless actually available. No individual stepparent or sponsorship calculation is made.

Section 233.20(a)(3)(ii)(D) considers income after applicable disregards, subject to paragraph (a)(3)(xiii), and resources available for current use. Consistently with other provisions of the chapter, actual availability suffices; the alternative requires both a legal interest in a liquidated sum and legal ability to make it available for support and maintenance. No sum unavailable to every unit member becomes available merely through grouping. The separate gross-income limit denies aid for a month when non-assistance income exceeds 185 percent of the State need standard, including special needs, for the same family composition, without the specified paragraph (a)(11)(i), (ii), and (viii) earned-income and tax-credit disregards except as paragraph (a)(3)(xix) permits. That permission allows a State-plan full-time-dependent-student earned-income disregard for no more than six months per calendar year; JTPA income disregarded under that paragraph, paragraph (a)(3)(xvii), or both has a combined six-month limit. The Court recalculates no family's eligibility.

Section 233.10(a)(1) requires reasonable program classifications and stated eligibility conditions, prohibits arbitrary or unreasonable exclusions and inequitable treatment in light of the Act's provisions and purposes, and requires every statutory eligibility condition. Narrower coverage is permitted only where the Act or its legislative history authorizes it. Administrative-efficiency or independent state-welfare conditions remain permissible only when consistent with the Act's provisions and purposes. The plan must distinguish groups inside and outside federal financial participation, apply conditions consistently and equitably statewide, preserve the opportunity to apply and obtain an eligibility determination, and use methods consistent with assisting eligible persons to qualify. Its separate title XVI uniformity condition is not converted into an additional AFDC holding.

Section 233.20(a)(1)(i) independently requires objective and equitable determination of need and assistance and consideration of all types of income in the same way except where federal statute specifically authorizes otherwise. The income-uniformity exception does not erase the separate equity requirement or decide whose income belongs to a unit. These two equity provisions are applied on their merits, without a ruling whether they confer an enforceable §1983 right.

Section 612 permits reasonable proration of shelter, utilities, and similar needs when individuals share a household without claiming aid together; an SSI recipient subject to the statutory one-third reduction is excluded from that calculation. Section 233.20(a)(5) separately prohibits proration with respect to such an SSI recipient and where a bona fide landlord-tenant relationship exists. If a State elects this option, it must prorate both need and payment standards reasonably; the plan must identify allowances, procedure, and circumstances, including which other persons count as sharing the household. Election is permitted, not compelled. None of these conditions authorizes a new proration percentage here.

Section 605 concerns proper use of the AFDC grant for its beneficiaries. It creates no duty to transfer a child's or caretaker's non-AFDC income and changes no general state-law support obligation. Counseling and guidance, advice about protective payments or guardianship, and authorized penalties remain qualified permitted responses rather than mandatory cumulative remedies. No caretaker misuse is found. The disposition rests on lawful membership and actual income rules, not an assumed transfer duty.

The 1994 transmittal permits statewide unit policies within these limits. Its consolidated-grandmother-and-five-children example excludes a surviving male friend's unavailable funds; its separate-unit example forbids presumed sharing across those separate units. The instrument discusses §233.20 equity but does not separately decide §233.10's Act-purpose language. The Court supplies that analysis independently. The 1986 transmittal is known here only through its reproduced excerpt.

### Swanner v. Anchorage Equal Rights Commission, No. 94-124. — 1995-03-27

Authority: [Record](../records/Swanner_v_Anchorage_Equal_Rights_Commission_merits_1995-03-27.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### RFRA requires a distinct inquiry into this application of the housing laws

**Holding and operative rule:** For prospective enforcement of the marital-status housing prohibitions against Swanner, Swanner must establish a substantial burden on his religious exercise; if he does, the government must produce evidence and persuade that applying the burden to him furthers a compelling governmental interest by the least restrictive means. The adverse federal prospective-enforcement determination is vacated for that inquiry because commercial participation and a general prohibition of discrimination do not by themselves complete it; no exemption or ultimate statutory justification is decided.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas: five direct rationale and judgment votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer dissent from the vacatur. Their agreement that RFRA governs does not turn their distinct application into Court law.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that RFRA requires separate attention to burden and justification in this application. Clearwater requires that inquiry for future enforcement of preexisting law, with the claimant proving substantial burden and the government bearing production and persuasion on compelling interest and least restrictive means. The Alaska court recognized the harm of discriminatory denials, but its reliance on voluntary commercial participation and its conclusion that prohibition is the most effective tool do not complete the statutory inquiry. Lee gives substantial weight to burdens an exemption would impose on others; Roberts recognizes the injury of exclusion from protected opportunities. Those concerns must be evaluated here, not replaced by an assumption that commercial activity forfeits religious protection. Conversely, availability of other housing does not erase the independent transactional and dignitary injury. The remand permits that injury to support justification while requiring assessment of the actual burden, any legally comparable exemption, and less restrictive means. No constitutional ruling or automatic landlord exemption follows from the need for this statutory examination.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Church of Scientology Flag Service Organization, Inc. v. City of Clearwater, December 5, 1994: applies its prospective, claimant-specific RFRA inquiry and distinct burdens to the present housing enforcement; its reservations concerning RFRA's constitutionality and retrospective monetary liability remain.
- United States v. Lee, 455 U.S. 252: preserves its recognition that exemptions can impose religious limitations on third parties; commercial activity is not treated as categorical forfeiture under RFRA.
- Roberts v. United States Jaycees, 468 U.S. 609: recognizes the weight of protected access and injury from exclusion; it does not automatically decide justification under every antidiscrimination law.
- Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872: its constitutional neutral-law rule does not replace the enacted RFRA inquiry; its constitutional holding remains unchanged.
- Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, June 11, 1993: preserves objective neutrality, comparable secular harm, and strict-scrutiny rules without deciding the constitutional claim here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No final substantial-burden or justification finding is made. The Anchorage ordinance's distinct individual-home exception applies when the renter would share common living areas with the owner, lessor, manager, agent, or other person; no finding places Swanner within it, and its legal comparability cannot be assumed either way. The Court neither invalidates the housing provisions nor ranks all marital-status protections. It does not decide religious teaching or advocacy, religious-mission housing, intimate owner-occupied arrangements, RFRA's constitutionality, or retrospective monetary liability. Alaska statutory construction, the state constitutional ruling, and separate notice/delegation due-process rulings are outside reconsideration. The federal constitutional exemption claim is reserved.

#### Precedent Treatment

Clearwater supplies the prospective RFRA inquiry and its separate burdens. Lee and Roberts retain their third-party and protected-access significance without deciding every religious accommodation. Smith and Lukumi retain their constitutional rules; RFRA provides a distinct statutory inquiry, and this decision makes no constitutional change.

#### Law After Decision

Effective March 27, 1995, the prospective housing-enforcement claim requires RFRA adjudication directed to the burden on this claimant and the government's justification of this application. Commercial participation is not forfeiture, antidiscrimination labeling is not automatic justification, and alternate housing is not an automatic answer to direct injury. This is a bounded application/remand rule. It does not establish that the government will fail or that Swanner must receive an exemption. The Court preserves ordinary standing and appropriate-relief limits and the statute's protection of belief and preservation of the Establishment Clause.

#### Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents, joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer. They would affirm rejection of the federal exemption for these commercial refusals. Assuming without deciding a substantial burden, they conclude that the government has justified this application: each identified applicant was denied an otherwise offered housing opportunity on the prohibited ground, and an exemption permitting that denial would remove the applicant's protection in the transaction. The interests are protecting each covered person from that denial and preserving equal access to the covered rental market without making applicants absorb individual landlords' religious objections. Referral or notice would leave the same unequal treatment by the landlord who offered the unit. No less restrictive accommodation preserving that protection has been shown on this record; this conclusion does not shift RFRA's production and persuasion burden from the government.

The dissent rejects a rule that entry into commerce forfeits religious protection. The Commission regulates the terms of offered commercial housing, not belief, worship, teaching, or public expression. Lee supports attention to the religious restrictions an exemption imposes on others, and Roberts supports the direct access and dignitary interests. The dissent distinguishes an application-specific justification from a conclusion that the statutory label alone makes every prohibition compelling. It reserves an accommodation preserving the applicant's full protection without material transferred cost or lost access.

The dissent also rejects the federal constitutional claim independently under Smith and Lukumi: the admitted provisions do not target religion, make religious motivation an element, or allow comparable secular commercial refusals while forbidding religious ones. The individual-home exception for shared common living areas addresses a different intimate setting on this record; it does not establish a comparable exception for Swanner's offered commercial tenancies. This view does not decide regulation of religious teaching or advocacy, housing operated as part of a religious institution's mission, owner-occupied arrangements implicating intimacy or privacy, or a scheme granting comparable secular exemptions while withholding a religious one. These conclusions support affirmance for the dissenters only; neither their RFRA application nor their constitutional ruling is a holding of the Court.

#### Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Supreme Court of Alaska for proceedings consistent with the statutory holding concerning prospective enforcement. The claimant must establish substantial burden; the government must then establish compelling interest and least restrictive means for this application. The court may consider direct transactional and dignitary harms as well as legally material alternatives and exemptions. No new evidentiary hearing is commanded irrespective of what the proper inquiry requires. No provision is invalidated, automatic landlord exemption ordered, or retrospective amount or liability adjudicated; the distinct state-law and due-process determinations are outside this mandate.

### Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co., No. 93-1577. — 1995-03-28

Authority: [Record](../records/Qualitex_Co_v_Jacobson_Products_Co_merits_1995-03-28.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### Color may identify source under the Lanham Act

**Holding and operative rule:** A color is eligible for trademark protection when it has acquired source-identifying significance and is nonfunctional, subject to the ordinary Lanham Act requirements, including likelihood of confusion where required. Color alone is not categorically excluded, but neither bare color without secondary meaning nor ownership of color in the abstract is protected.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a color can perform the source-identifying function the Lanham Act protects. Sections 1052 and 1127 describe marks by that function and contain no categorical exclusion for color. Gardner requires applying the enacted conditions, not adding a policy restriction absent from the text. Two Pesos protects qualifying trade dress and keeps distinctiveness, functionality, and confusion separate; it does not establish that bare color is inherently distinctive. Here the district court found that Qualitex's green-gold press-pad color had acquired secondary meaning and that Jacobson's copying was likely to confuse purchasers. Those findings are not replaced with new findings by this Court. Shade comparisons can be addressed through ordinary proof of source significance and confusion. The possible depletion of useful colors requires attention to functionality and competitive need, not rejection of every color mark. The Ninth Circuit's contrary category rule and cancellation direction therefore cannot stand. The independently sustained unfair-competition judgment remains intact, and ordinary statutory requirements govern further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: applies the separation of qualifying source identification, nonfunctionality, and confusion; its inherent-distinctiveness rule for restaurant trade dress is not extended to bare color.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to statutory terms without an invented category exclusion; it supplies no separate trademark proof rule.

### Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features

**Holding and operative rule:** Acquired source significance does not make a functional color protectable: a feature essential to the product's use or purpose or affecting its cost or quality remains outside trademark protection, and protection cannot foreclose color features shown to be competitively necessary. The record's absence of a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade defeats a categorical functionality objection to its eligibility; no general rule about every aesthetic feature is decided.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes on this operative limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that trademark law protects source identification without granting control of useful product features. Inwood identifies functionality when a feature is essential to use or purpose or affects cost or quality. That boundary remains independent of secondary meaning: consumers' recognition does not justify excluding competitors from features they need for reasons unrelated to source identification. A color may sometimes perform such a function, and the availability of alternative colors must be considered in the actual product setting. The lower findings distinguish the usefulness of color generally in concealing stains from a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade. They identify no improvement in use, quality, or longevity attributable to the shade and describe numerous available tones. The Court relies on those findings without creating a new market definition or evidentiary presumption. The possibility that another color or market presents different competitive needs therefore does not support a universal color bar. It remains a reason to apply functionality in each proper case, not to dispense with that requirement here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844, 850 n.10: applies the essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality functionality boundary to the asserted color feature; functionality remains independent of source significance.
- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: preserves the separate nonfunctionality requirement; neither its holding nor this decision confers rights in useful designs or generic subject matter.

#### Precedent Treatment

Two Pesos retains its protection of qualifying trade dress and its separate requirements; its restaurant-trade-dress rule does not establish inherent distinctiveness of bare color. Inwood's functionality boundary remains controlling and is applied to the claimed color feature. Gardner supports applying the enacted statutory conditions. No general aesthetic-functionality test or new evidentiary presumption is adopted.

#### Law After Decision

Effective March 28, 1995, the Lanham Act permits protection of source-identifying, nonfunctional color subject to ordinary statutory requirements. The categorical color exclusion is rejected; functionality, acquired source significance for bare color, and confusion where required remain distinct. Protection does not extend to color in the abstract, functional color, or color lacking secondary meaning. This decision does not resolve every color-market competition question or grant rights in useful or generic product features.
