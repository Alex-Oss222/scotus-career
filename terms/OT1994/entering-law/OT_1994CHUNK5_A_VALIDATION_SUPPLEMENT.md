### Schlup v. Delo; No. 93-7901. — 1995-01-23

Authority: [Record](../records/Schlup_v_Delo_merits_1995-01-23.md#public-projection); public substance is copied in the [generated chunk handoff](../render-inputs/OT_1994CHUNK2.md).

### Crime innocence invokes a demanding probability gateway

**Controlling proposition:** A prisoner seeking to overcome otherwise applicable successive or abusive habeas barriers to independent constitutional claims through innocence of the offense must present reliable new evidence and show, considering the probative record as a whole, that it is more likely than not that no reasonable, properly instructed juror would find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. This procedural gateway does not itself establish innocence, prove any constitutional violation or shift the ultimate burden on the underlying claims; Sawyer's clear-and-convincing rule remains confined to its distinct death-eligibility setting.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, gateway and all-evidence portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six of nine participants directly adopt this rule and explanation. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support a stricter crime-innocence quantum and would affirm.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the probability inquiry concerns innocence of the crime, not whether a concededly guilty person qualified for a death sentence. Carrier supplies the exceptional route where constitutional error probably convicted an innocent person; Sawyer preserves its different eligibility inquiry without deciding this crime question. McCleskey retains the innocence exception while enforcing finality through barriers to repetitive and abusive claims. The prisoner's burden remains demanding because the question concerns reasonable jurors considering the augmented evidentiary record, not the habeas judge's personal doubt. Kuhlmann's plurality discussion supports examination of all probative evidence, but its formulations are not converted into a separate majority holding. Jackson's trial-record sufficiency question cannot replace that task: evidence sufficient when credited at trial may have a different effect when reliable new evidence is considered with it. The State's request for one uniformly higher burden disregards the different question and the gateway's limited function. Passing this threshold permits further consideration within the governing claim rules; it does not award a writ or adjudicate innocence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478: applies and specifies its probability-based crime-innocence exception without changing cause and prejudice.
- Sawyer v. Whitley, 505 U.S. 333: preserves the clear-and-convincing death-eligibility gateway and resolves the crime-innocence question that decision left open; it is not overruled or given a categorical impeachment exclusion.
- McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467: preserves successive/abusive-claim barriers and the exceptional innocence route, without treating potential constitutional merit as sufficient to pass it.
- Kuhlmann v. Wilson, 477 U.S. 436: uses the relevant plurality discussion of the whole probative record as persuasive guidance, preserving its actual authority limit.
- Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307: distinguishes trial-record legal sufficiency from the likely effect of an augmented record on reasonable jurors; its sufficiency rule remains intact.

**Operative evidentiary qualifications:** The court considers all relevant probative evidence, including evidence excluded at trial or becoming available afterward; trial-admissibility rules do not categorically delimit the gateway record, and even improperly admitted trial evidence may have probative force subject to reliability. The court must assess reliability and likely credibility, not accept every affidavit as true or treat the original verdict as a conclusive answer. Timing, delay and inconsistency may matter; no categorical rule excludes impeachment evidence or discredits a witness solely because the witness is a prisoner. A mere possibility of acquittal, a juror who might disagree, ordinary constitutional-merits prejudice or the reviewing judge's personal reasonable doubt is insufficient. The reasonable juror fairly considers the evidence and follows the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt instruction.

### This legal error requires a bounded remand despite the statutory refusal permission

**Controlling proposition:** The courts' use of the rejected clear-and-convincing crime gateway and reliance on the original jury's credibility function require vacatur and renewed assessment on this record; §2244(b)'s permission to refuse a qualifying subsequent petition does not itself supply an adequate independent ground to sustain that legally mistaken refusal. The Court remands through the Eighth Circuit to the District Court for application of the probability rule and a reasoned determination of necessary factual development, without deciding whether every successful innocence showing compels review, choosing a general appellate standard, or ordering a hearing, writ, retrial, release or stay automatically.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, statutory-refusal and remand rationale, joined by Justices O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five directly adopt this bounded rationale. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela supplies a sixth vote for vacatur and the same gateway-assessment remand on his stated ground, without joining the separate §2244(b) analysis. No aggregation of different rationales is needed for the five-Justice holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that discretion to refuse renewed review must be exercised under the proper governing law. McCleskey explains both the statutory permission and its qualifications; neither the label second petition nor the filing of new affidavits decides the result. The earlier federal consideration of guilt-phase ineffective assistance supplies a real legal-merits proceeding, and the lower court's additional discussion of that claim remains relevant. It does not establish an independently sufficient ground disposing of every claim despite today's legal correction. The new accounts concern the meaning of the existing videotape, identification and the interval between attack and response. The lower analysis used a stricter burden and treated credibility too categorically as the original jury's task. Those features prevent accepting it as the required probability assessment. Even assuming the ultimate refusal is discretionary, reconsideration is required here; the Court need not settle the broader discretion question. Keeney and Townsend preserve necessary factual development of properly available claims, but their predicates are not established merely by this remand. The District Court must decide what further development this gateway assessment needs.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McCleskey, 499 U.S. 467: applies the distinction between conditional consideration of new, nonabusive grounds and legally constrained refusal of successive or abusive grounds; neither permission nor exception becomes an unconditional command.
- Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, 504 U.S. 1, and Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293: preserve necessary factual development within their properly presented, available-claim setting and the deliberate-bypass qualification; ordinary counsel negligence alone does not forfeit a required hearing there, but no successive/abusive gateway is eliminated.
- Carrier, 477 U.S. 478: requires use of the selected crime-innocence rule before the present refusal can be sustained on its innocence assessment.

**Governing statutory terms:** Section 2244(b) provides:

> (b) When after an evidentiary hearing on the merits of a material factual issue, or after a hearing on the merits of an issue of law, a person in custody pursuant to the judgment of a State court has been denied by a court of the United States or a justice or judge of the United States release from custody or other remedy on an application for a writ of habeas corpus, a subsequent application for a writ of habeas corpus in behalf of such person need not be entertained by a court of the United States or a justice or judge of the United States unless the application alleges and is predicated on a factual or other ground not adjudicated on the hearing of the earlier application for the writ, and unless the court, justice, or judge is satisfied that the applicant has not on the earlier application deliberately withheld the newly asserted ground or otherwise abused the writ.

The prior-hearing alternatives are a factual evidentiary merits hearing or a legal merits hearing, each followed by a federal denial of release or other habeas remedy to a person held under a state judgment. Both unless conditions must be preserved: the later application must allege and rest on an unadjudicated factual or other ground, and the court must be satisfied that the newly asserted ground was not deliberately withheld or otherwise an abuse. New evidence is not automatically a new ground; absence of deliberate withholding does not eliminate every other kind of abuse. McCleskey requires consideration when both conditions are met and other barriers, including nonexhaustion and procedural default, are absent. The State must plead abuse with clarity and particularity by identifying prior writ history and newly raised claims and alleging abuse before the prisoner's burden arises. Cause and prejudice and the exceptional innocence route remain separate; cause and prejudice is not pressed in this review. Subsection (a)'s separate wording is not inserted into subsection (b), and no new subsection (c) predicate is established.

**Application, reservations and remedy:** The videotape was trial evidence. Green's asserted prompt call, Faherty's movement account and other later identification accounts must be assessed with the existing record; the Court finds no witness truthful or false and does not find the gateway satisfied. Proper claim classification, exhaustion, default, abuse, retroactivity and a genuinely sufficient independent merits ground retain their separate operation. Remand is not mandatory constitutional-merits review. Any necessary live testimony is for a reasoned determination under the applicable rules, not an automatic consequence of affidavits or vacatur. The merits of the suppression and ineffective-assistance claims, a freestanding innocence entitlement and any ultimate guilt determination remain undecided.

Carrier supplies the crime-innocence route, while Sawyer retains its distinct clear-and-convincing eligibility rule. McCleskey's statutory qualifications, abuse pleading, cause/prejudice framework and innocence exception remain. Kuhlmann's relevant discussion retains plurality force, and Jackson's trial-record sufficiency rule remains separate. Keeney and Townsend govern necessary factual development within their actual available-claim conditions. Herrera retains only its record-specific insufficiency holding, Delo v. Blair supplies no precedent from equal division, Brecht remains a preserved-error merits decision, and McFarland supplies no automatic gateway or stay. No prior controlling holding is overruled.

Effective January 23, 1995, reliable new evidence of crime innocence is assessed with the whole probative record under the more-likely-than-not/no-reasonable-juror gateway. The prisoner retains that demanding burden and must separately establish any underlying constitutional claim. Section 2244(b)'s qualified permission, lawful independent barriers and necessary factual-development rules remain; the Court decides neither a universal mandatory-review rule after a successful showing nor a general appellate standard for refusal. The judgment corrects the legal inquiry and creates no freestanding innocence remedy.

### O'Neal v. McAninch, No. 93-7407 — 1995-02-21

Authority: [Record](../records/ONeal_v_McAninch_merits_1995-02-21.md#public-projection).

### Chapman governs the preserved personal-intent instructional claim

**Holding and operative rule:** For a preserved constitutional trial violation, properly available on federal habeas, in which the combined jury instructions and prosecutor's statement permit conviction without proof of the defendant's own intent required for the offense, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the whole record, that the error did not contribute to the verdict. Kotteakos's substantial-and-injurious-effect and grave-doubt framework does not govern this claim; the standard for other reserved categories is not decided.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct joins at this level of generality. Part III's proposed extension to ordinary preserved constitutional trial errors generally has only O'Connor, Stone-Zsela and Souter and does not control.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt for this preserved constitutional instructional claim. Chapman supplies the inquiry whether the violation contributed to the actual verdict, rather than whether sufficient untainted evidence could support another verdict. Brecht retained that rule for preserved Doyle error on habeas; it left other categories open. The alleged permission to convict without finding O'Neal's own required intent concerns the jury's constitutional task as directly as the protected-silence error addressed there. No feature of this claim justifies reducing the State's showing merely because review is collateral. Finality and respect for state judgments remain substantial interests, but independent habeas requirements continue to protect them, and Chapman permits harmless convictions to stand. Section 2254(a)'s custody requirement does not expressly reverse the harmlessness burden after a qualifying violation. The Court resolves this instructional category, without deciding every other constitutional collateral claim, finding the assumed violation, or treating it as structural error.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: extended from preserved Doyle error to the preserved personal-intent instructional/argument category; its original rule survives, while other reserved categories remain unresolved by the Court.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied with the State's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden and actual-verdict contribution inquiry intact.
- Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946): not extended to replace Chapman for this constitutional claim; its existing operation for genuinely nonconstitutional trial error remains unchanged.
- In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970): applied as the constitutional requirement that the prosecution prove the elements of the charged offense; no new personal-intent element is imposed on offenses that do not require it.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana, June 1, 1993: preserved as the distinct rule for a defect vitiating the jury's governing reasonable-doubt standard; the assumed error here is not declared structural.

### The proper standard requires renewed review, not an automatic writ

**Holding and operative rule:** The Sixth Circuit's substantial-influence assessment with a prisoner prejudice burden does not supply the required Chapman determination; its challenged judgment must be vacated for review of the combined claim under the correct rule. If an available constitutional violation is established and the State fails to prove beyond a reasonable doubt on the existing record that it did not contribute to the verdict, the writ shall issue for the affected conviction or convictions unless the State retries O'Neal within a reasonable time; no immediate writ or present retrial deadline is imposed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct joins in the remedy and its conditions. Stevens and Kennedy add votes for vacatur and further proceedings on a different harmlessness ground, making the judgment 7–2 without enlarging the Chapman coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a new application of the proper standard is necessary, but does not award relief merely because the court of appeals used the wrong rule. The instructions-only ruling did not finally resolve the assumed combined effect of the charge and the prosecutor's misstatement. Nor did a finding of no substantial injurious influence under a prisoner burden establish the stronger assurance Chapman requires from the State. The evidence of principal participation remains part of the whole-record inquiry; the Court does not reject that evidence or decide its weight. Brecht supplies the distinction between correcting the review rule and ordering a writ after the State fails the required showing. The receiving courts may resolve the constitutional claim or, on an express assumption of error, determine harmlessness under Chapman. A writ requires an established violation that warrants relief. Ordinary habeas gates and the connection between the violation and each affected conviction remain; neither an unrecorded state of grave doubt nor the terms of the earlier conditional order are presumed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: its whole-record remand and conditional-relief distinction are extended to this instructional claim, without automatic reinstatement of a prior writ.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applied to the existing trial record; a weaker substantial-influence finding is not treated as the required finding beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Estelle v. McGuire, December 4, 1991, and Victor v. Nebraska / Sandoval v. California, March 22, 1994: their whole-charge reasonable-likelihood inquiry remains the rule for identifying an ambiguous instruction's constitutional defect; the Court does not decide that antecedent application here.
- Schlup v. Delo, January 23, 1995: its gateway for otherwise barred claims remains independent; neither passage through that gateway nor an innocence showing is invented for this claim.

Brecht's preserved-Doyle Chapman holding remains in force and now extends to the preserved personal-intent instructional/argument category presented here. Chapman retains its burden and proof standard. Kotteakos continues within its existing genuinely nonconstitutional trial-error domain; this decision creates no habeas remedy for ordinary state-law error. Winship's element-proof requirement and Estelle/Victor's identification of instructional error remain distinct from harmlessness. Sullivan's structural rule and Schlup's separate gateway remain unchanged. No precedent is overruled.

Effective February 21, 1995, Chapman governs this preserved personal-intent instructional/argument category on federal habeas as well as the preserved Doyle category already governed by Brecht. The State must establish noncontribution to the verdict beyond a reasonable doubt on the whole record. Kotteakos grave doubt is not an alternative dispositive test for this claim.

The Court has not adopted a general Chapman rule for every other category of preserved constitutional trial error. O'Connor, Stone-Zsela and Souter support that further extension in Part III; Ginsburg and Breyer reserve it. That three-Justice position is not current law. Nor does the Stevens–Kennedy concurrence establish a constitutional-habeas Kotteakos rule. The controlling propositions rest on five direct joins; no Marks construction or aggregation of different standards is necessary or adopted.

Proof of the constitutional violation, preservation, exhaustion, other independent habeas gates, structural-error rules and any prejudice element defining a different constitutional claim remain separate. The decision neither shifts every habeas burden to the State nor determines actual harmlessness or the ultimate entitlement to a writ.

### Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, No. 94-38. — 1995-04-18

Authority: [Record](../records/Lanphere_and_Urbaniak_v_Colorado_merits_1995-04-18.md#public-projection).

#### Holdings

### The intended-speech condition requires commercial-speech review

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State otherwise makes covered records available, a rule denying access and prohibiting use because the requester intends truthful, lawful commercial solicitation is subject to the governing commercial-speech standard. The absence of a general entitlement to every government record does not remove this speech-selected condition from First Amendment review; a qualified right to particular court records is left undecided.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justices O'Connor and Scalia dissent from the application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged line concerns expression as well as access. Colorado prohibits using the covered records and their information to solicit business for pecuniary gain and conditions inspection on a promise against direct solicitation. Identical record requests thus receive different treatment because of the protected communication the requester plans to make. Central Hudson governs truthful, nonmisleading commercial expression concerning lawful activity. Shapero establishes that targeted legal-service mail falls within that protection, while Discovery Network requires justification for a selected commercial category. Colorado's custody of the records does not erase these requirements. Its strongest argument distinguishes access to information from later communication of information already acquired, but the challenged enactment regulates both access and use within an existing disclosure regime. The Court need not decide whether experience and logic independently guarantee access to these particular court materials. Nor does this holding compel disclosure of confidential files, create records, or forbid neutral access conditions. It requires review of the speech condition actually imposed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies commercial-speech protection to truthful solicitation about lawful services.
- Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association, 486 U.S. 466: applies protection for truthful targeted mail while distinguishing the separate source-of-information question.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: applies review of a speech-selected commercial category notwithstanding governmental control of the facility involved.
- Houchins v. KQED, Inc., 438 U.S. 1: does not establish an absolute governmental privilege to attach this speech restriction to otherwise available records; no general access holding is added.
- Nixon v. Warner Communications, Inc., 435 U.S. 589: does not establish an absolute governmental privilege to attach this speech restriction to otherwise available records; no general access holding is added.
- Press-Enterprise Co. v. Superior Court, 478 U.S. 1: preserves the separate experience-and-logic inquiry for qualified access; its application to these records is not decided.

### Colorado has not demonstrated material advancement in this application

**Holding and operative rule:** For a restriction on truthful, lawful commercial solicitation, the State must demonstrate real harms and material advancement of its substantial interests in the relevant setting. Colorado's generalized privacy, abuse-prevention, and public-confidence justifications do not establish that this categorical use-and-access restriction materially advances those interests as applied to these plaintiffs' proposed direct mail.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justices O'Connor and Scalia dissent from the application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantial interests do not dispense with proof of the connection between those interests and the chosen burden. Edenfield requires real harms and material advancement in the particular professional setting; it does not invariably demand empirical studies. Privacy for persons merely accused of offenses, prevention of solicitation abuse, and confidence in justice are substantial concerns. But Colorado has not shown why denying these otherwise available names and addresses to these speakers materially protects those interests. The lower court inferred meaningful privacy simply from the plaintiffs' persistence in seeking access. That inference does not establish the effect of the selected restriction. The conceded availability of the information through the El Paso County News further bears on that explanation, without creating a rule that every privacy measure must cover every source. Shapero distinguishes targeted mail from coercive personal confrontation. No finding of unlawful or misleading proposed mail answers that distinction here. Supported controls on actual deception, harassment, or protected information remain available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the real-harm and material-advancement burden without requiring studies in every case.
- Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association, 486 U.S. 466: applies the distinction between targeted mail and coercive personal solicitation; the Court does not adjudicate unexamined letter-specific content.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: preserves the need for a connection between the commercial selection and the harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the requirement of justification responsive to truthful professional expression; actual deception remains regulable.

### The categorical prohibition lacks the required reasonable fit

**Holding and operative rule:** The restriction must reasonably fit the State's substantial interests and may not burden more truthful, lawful commercial speech than that fit permits; the State need not employ the least restrictive means. This categorical prohibition and access assurance lack the required fit as applied, because Colorado has not justified selecting every proposed direct solicitation over neutral access protections or measures addressing actual abuses.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: seven direct rationale votes. Justices O'Connor and Scalia dissent from the application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the wholesale speech criterion is broader than Colorado has justified. Fox requires a reasonable, proportionate fit rather than the least restrictive conceivable measure. Discovery Network requires the chosen category to correspond to the asserted harm. Colorado distinguishes the point of official disclosure from the later mailing and argues that withholding information imposes only an indirect burden. That distinction deserves consideration, but it cannot establish fit by itself when the statute also prohibits the information's solicitation use and selects otherwise identical requests by intended expression. The record does not explain why neutral redaction, delayed release, opt-out protections, retrieval fees, or direct regulation of deceptive, coercive, and harassing solicitation are inadequate to support a more fitted response. The Court does not mandate any particular replacement system or find that every alternative is required in every case. It rejects this categorical criterion on this record. Confidential records and independently justified neutral limits remain subject to their own rules.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees of State University of New York v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993: applies the relationship between the selected commercial category and the harm; alternatives are relevant without requiring regulation of all sources simultaneously.
- Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association, 486 U.S. 466: preserves appropriately directed safeguards against misleading or abusive mail instead of an unsupported blanket barrier.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: retains protection for materially different professional settings and supported regulation of deception or coercion.

#### Precedent Treatment

Central Hudson and Fox remain the governing framework. Discovery Network, Edenfield, and Ibanez supply the selected-category, material-advancement, and truthful-professional-speech principles applied here. Shapero protects targeted mail without alone determining access to government information. Houchins and Nixon do not turn the absence of a general access entitlement into an absolute privilege to burden speech; the Court leaves the separate qualified-access inquiry under Press-Enterprise undecided. No precedent is overruled, and no strict-scrutiny or commercial/political-speech equality rule is adopted.

#### Law After Decision

Effective April 18, 1995, the challenged solicitation-use prohibition and access assurance cannot sustain withholding otherwise available information in this application under Central Hudson and Fox. Material advancement and reasonable fit remain distinct requirements; each deficient showing supports the judgment. The ruling does not require disclosure of confidential records, resolve access to every court filing or proceeding, command creation of a list, or invalidate independently justified neutral access rules. Regulation of false, deceptive, coercive, or harassing solicitation remains available. Seven Justices directly join the controlling explanation; no Marks inquiry is needed.

### Schlup v. Delo; No. 93-7901. — 1995-01-23

Authority: [Record](../records/Schlup_v_Delo_merits_1995-01-23.md#public-projection); public substance is copied in the [generated chunk handoff](../render-inputs/OT_1994CHUNK2.md).

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court's probability gateway and all-evidence rule and concurs in vacatur and remand. Finality is weighty, but the procedural route remains available where reliable new evidence indicates that constitutional error may have convicted an innocent person. Sawyer's death-eligibility threshold answers a different question. The lower courts must evaluate the videotape, timing and eyewitness accounts together with the trial record; passage through the gateway authorizes consideration of otherwise barred constitutional claims within their governing rules, not an innocence judgment or automatic release.

He adds guidance for the reliability inquiry. The habeas court should explain the new evidence's reliability, any independent corroboration, why the evidence was unavailable at trial, whether delay impairs credibility, whether state misconduct or ineffective counsel explains its absence, and exactly how it changes the evidentiary case the jury heard. These are nonexclusive considerations relevant to reliability and probability, not additional elements, a checklist or a new diligence requirement. He does not decide the witnesses' truthfulness or shift the underlying claims' ultimate burden. He does not join the Court's separate statutory-refusal analysis or announce a general rule governing residual refusal discretion.

Justice Kennedy dissents. Justice Thomas joins his opinion in full; Justice Scalia joins its stricter-gateway rule and application, but not its alternative discussion of the probability formulation. Kennedy would require clear and convincing evidence, on the probative record as a whole, that no reasonable juror would find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. He proposes extending Sawyer's quantum to crime innocence; he does not claim that Sawyer already decided that question. McCleskey's treatment of abusive litigation and the exceptional nature of renewed review support the more rigorous showing in his view. The continuing identifications, uncertain timing and delayed or inconsistent accounts leave the submitted evidence insufficient under that quantum. This is not a finding that every new witness lied or that any inculpatory trial evidence invariably defeats an innocence claim.

Kennedy and Thomas additionally question the Court's predictive formulation if a probability rule is used. They would adapt Jackson's reasonable-juror approach to the enlarged evidentiary record, with limited factual inquiry where the submissions indicate that the necessary innocence showing may be made. They do not substitute the unchanged trial-record-only sufficiency test, order a hearing automatically, or establish an independent failure under a correctly applied Carrier rule. Their proposed stricter rule and application would sustain the judgment; their alternative discussion does not control.

Justice Scalia also dissents in an opinion joined by Justice Thomas. Section 2244(b), in their reading, preserves real authority to decline successive or abusive review, subject to its two qualifications and review for abuse. The earlier federal legal-merits adjudication satisfies a hearing alternative despite the lack of the now-requested factual hearing. They rely on the accepted repeat/abuse posture, the actual consideration and refusal below, and the evidentiary submission to find no abuse; they also regard the stricter gateway as correct. They do not convert permission into mandatory dismissal, classify every present claim as previously adjudicated, or find an unexamined ground deliberately withheld.

Salinger and Sanders support consideration of prior litigation within their respective settings, while McCleskey preserves the statute's conditions and judicial limits on discretion. Wong Doo shows that an adequate independent discretionary ground can sometimes preserve a judgment despite a legal error. Scalia and Thomas do not use that proposition to declare these affidavits necessarily insufficient under a correctly applied probability rule. Their statutory rationale is additional to the stricter-standard objection, but its application here shares their conclusion that the lower court used a legally proper quantum. They recognize that misconception of law or arbitrary reasons can constitute abuse and announce no unconditional affirmance should the proper gateway later be satisfied. These views do not control the remand.

### O'Neal v. McAninch, No. 93-7407 — 1995-02-21

Authority: [Record](../records/ONeal_v_McAninch_merits_1995-02-21.md#public-projection).

**O'Connor, joined by Stone-Zsela and Souter, Part III:** These three Justices would extend Chapman beyond the category necessary to decide this case, to ordinary preserved constitutional trial errors generally when properly before a federal habeas court and susceptible to harmless-error review. They read Brecht's reason for retaining the State's burden as arising from the constitutional character of an established trial violation and the risk of its contribution to the verdict, not a feature peculiar to protected silence. In their view, distinguishing ordinary trial-error categories would add complexity without identifying a different basis for allocating that risk. Rose v. Clark and Yates v. Evatt supply continuity from prior collateral applications of Chapman, although neither is misdescribed as having already settled the later choice between standards. Independent habeas gates protect finality, and the State may preserve a conviction by proving harmlessness. Their proposed extension leaves structural error, procedural default, exhaustion and prejudice defining a distinct constitutional claim untouched. Ginsburg and Breyer withhold a join from this generalization while joining the specific instructional rule and mandate. This broader position has three votes and does not settle Brecht's remaining reservations.

**Stevens, joined by Kennedy, concurring in the judgment:** They would vacate because the lower court's prisoner-burden formulation does not preserve the grave-doubt consequence of the Kotteakos rule they would adopt for this non-Doyle category. Collateral review may use a substantial-influence threshold while still requiring the reviewing court to assess the whole record and the State to explain why an established trial violation did not substantially influence the verdict. Kotteakos supplies both that actual-verdict inquiry and the consequence of genuine grave doubt; mere evidence sufficient to convict is not enough. They would respect Brecht's binding preserved-Doyle holding without extending Chapman to this instructional claim. They do not find the appellate judges actually doubtful, declare a constitutional violation, or order a present writ. Their remand would require renewed Kotteakos review, with any ultimate relief confined to convictions affected by a proved violation that cannot be treated as harmless under their rule. They support vacatur and further proceedings but oppose the Court's Chapman direction. Their alternative standard has two adherents and does not govern the remand.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting:** They would affirm within the accepted legal question. In their view, section 2254(a)'s requirement of custody in violation of federal law supports requiring a prisoner in this non-Doyle trial-error category to establish substantial injurious influence before obtaining collateral relief. They accept that the statute does not expressly state a tie-breaking rule and that Kotteakos and Chapman allocate uncertainty differently in their own settings. Their proposed distinction concerns the collateral remedy after criminal review has ended. Brecht's preserved-Doyle holding remains binding, but they would not extend it to the category it reserved. A prisoner unable to establish the requisite substantial injury—including one whose showing leaves that matter evenly balanced—would not obtain relief under their rule. They rely on the court of appeals' existing rule and no-influence conclusion without making a new whole-trial factual finding. Their position does not demand innocence in every habeas case, recast constitutional error as merely state-law error, or classify a Sullivan defect as ordinary trial error. This petitioner-prejudice rule has two votes and is not controlling.

### Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, No. 94-38. — 1995-04-18

Authority: [Record](../records/Lanphere_and_Urbaniak_v_Colorado_merits_1995-04-18.md#public-projection).

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs while joining the Court's opinion. Protection for speaking with lawfully obtained information does not create a general First Amendment right to obtain every state criminal-justice record or require government to create or disclose every source. Ordinary access begins with enacted law and distinct historical access doctrines. The judicial-proceeding cases require their own history and structural inquiry; a qualified right to particular court filings or proceedings is not resolved here. Colorado's existing access regime nevertheless cannot select protected intended speech for the unjustified burden the Court rejects.

Justice O'Connor, joined by Justice Scalia, dissents. Official dissemination of accused persons' identifying information for targeted pecuniary solicitation presents a privacy and potential-abuse concern different from a ban on communicating information already acquired. Fox permits reasonable fit without the least restrictive means, and the State need not eliminate every outside source before protecting information at its own disclosure point. Their position accepts First Amendment review, acknowledges both statutory commands, and would sustain this application. It establishes no general power to suppress independently acquired truthful information, no immunity from scrutiny for governmental benefits, and no ruling that all criminal records lack qualified access protection. These are the complete published separate positions of April 18, 1995; each is noncontrolling. The dissents' acceptance of First Amendment review does not make them joins in the Court's advancement or fit conclusions.
