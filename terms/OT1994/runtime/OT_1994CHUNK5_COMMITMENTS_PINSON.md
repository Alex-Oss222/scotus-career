## 58. United States v. Pinson, assigned No. 94-164

### Components and record boundaries

The preserved question concerns the warrantless July 25, 1991, approximately 1:20 a.m. **helicopter-mounted** FLIR scan of this home. Patterson's testimony describes comparison of radiated surface heat, not absolute temperature measurement, seeing through walls, or identification of people or objects. The affidavit reports excess heat at a covered third-floor window, roof, and skylight. The July 30 physical search was separately conducted under a warrant. No altitude, regular public-flight finding, general public FLIR availability, detailed disturbance evidence, image, or full affidavit is supplied.

The legal search question is preserved. Footnote 2 says that Pinson **did not challenge affidavit sufficiency on appeal**, and the circuit decided no such issue. That neither waives his preserved search argument nor establishes that the untainted material suffices. Search coverage, justification for warrantless action, exclusion, warrant validity, and the conviction's ultimate fate remain separate. The affirmed lesser-harms sentencing issue is outside the supplied question; no medical factual finding or authority to disregard the statutory minimum is added.

The operative current rules are “Possessory seizures and the claimant's own Fourth Amendment interest” (Padilla, May 3, 1993, preserving Soldal); “Contraband recognized through a lawful protective patdown” (Dickerson, June 7, 1993); and “Forfeited error and discretionary correction under Rule 52(b)” (Olano, April 26, 1993). Evans, March 1, 1995, distinguishes violation and exclusion and establishes only its qualifying court-employee-error/objective-reliance rule. None supplies a thermal-imaging exception. Pinson asserts his own home's privacy, not a coconspirator's interest.

### Competing lawful paths

**Search path.** A targeted instrumental examination that converts otherwise unobservable heat patterns at a closed residence into information about concealed domestic activity invades the home's reasonable privacy even though the instrument receives emissions outside and does not image objects through walls. Karo III protects electronically obtained information about the concealed interior without requiring physical entry or revelation of conversations. Hicks rejects a threshold based on the personal importance of what the additional search happens to reveal. Here the covered window and the equipment's differential heat information supply the concrete connection to concealed activity; police investigative purpose alone would not suffice. On this record, no consent, exigency, or other recognized warrant exception is established. The rule does not decide every enhanced sense, every exterior observation, public use of technology, or a numerical resolution or temperature threshold.

The strongest objection is that Karo ascertained an object's continuing presence inside, whereas this device measured only exterior temperatures and left officers to infer their cause. The search-path response is that inference does not remove the protected informational object where the investigative examination of otherwise imperceptible home heat patterns supplies evidence of concealed domestic operation. Nor is this Place's contraband-only information: lawful activity produces heat too. Greenwood's deliberate placement of garbage for collection does not establish surrender of domestic information simply because heat escapes. Riley's naked-eye view of a partially open greenhouse and Dow's outdoor industrial photographs do not decide this closed-home scan, and their flight or equipment facts cannot be borrowed.

**No-search path.** The demonstrated examination reveals only comparative temperature at exterior surfaces, rather than the presence of an object or person inside; inference from that exposed physical condition is not itself acquisition of a concealed domestic fact. Karo's interior-location information is distinguished, while Riley and Dow show that improved observation does not invariably become a search. This ground must be confined to what this device actually disclosed. It cannot rest on a categorical right to fly, general abandonment of every emission, a supposed contraband-only capacity, or an asserted absence of all technological threats to home privacy.

The strongest objection is that separating exterior heat from its interior source can make the home's privacy depend on the mechanics of transmission—the problem Katz and Karo rejected. The no-search response is that the line rests on the content actually obtained, not simply the receiver's location: external relative warmth permits multiple causal explanations and does not itself disclose a concealed object, person, or particular intimate activity. More revealing surveillance may require a warrant. Both paths remain legally supportable; neither follows mechanically from the word “surface.”
