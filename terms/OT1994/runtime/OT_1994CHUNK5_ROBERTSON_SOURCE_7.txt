Respondent’s central contention (Br. 10-11) is that the 
phrase “affecting commerce” does not invoke Congress’s 
full power under the Commerce Clause, because it does 
not trigger application of a “class of activities” analysis. 
According to respondent (Br. 8-11), only when Congress 
itself specifies the class of activities that a statute 
regulates may courts consider the aggregate effect on 
commerce of the class’s activities. When Congress does 
not explicitly invoke the “class of activities” doctrine, 
the statute requires that the activities in each case, 
considered in isolation, be “substantial.” Respondent 
thus proposes that the “affecting commerce” rubric be 
reduced from its current position as invoking all of 
Congress’s Commerce Clause authority to a middle 


(1)

ground of uncertain scope “lying somewhere in a no- 
man’s land between ‘in commerce’ and [a class-of-acti- 
vities analysis].” Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dob- 
son, No. 93-1001 (Jan. 18, 1995). ¢.ip op. 9. That result 
would have the effect of “unnecessarily complicating the 
law and breeding litigation,” ibid., and is not supported 
by this Court’s cases. 

1. As this Court recently reaffirmed, the phrase “af- 
fecting commerce,” which Congress used to describe the 
jurisdictional reach of RICO, is a term of art that sig- 
nals Congress’s “intent to exercise its Commerce 
Clause powers to t! 2 full.” Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. 
v. Dobson, slip op. 7 (citing Russell v. United States, 471 
U.S. 858, 859 (1985)). Congress used that phrase in 
RICO with full awareness that it was invoking “the 
fullest jurisdictional breadth constitutionally permis- 
sible under the Commerce Clause.” H.R. Rep. No. 1549, 
91st Cong., 2d Sess. 70 (1970) (quoting NLRB v. Reliance 
Fuel Oil Corp., 371 U S. 224, 226 (1963) (per curiam) 
(emphasis deleted)). See U.S. Br. 13-16. Contrary to 
respondent’s contention (Br. 8-10), the class-of-activities 
analysis applies whenever Congress chooses to use its 
full constitutional authority; that analysis is simply a 
way to determine whether a particular activity affects 
commerce when measured by the full authority of 
Congress. 

Respondent proposes (Br. 14-17) the Sherman Act, 15 
U.S.C. 1, as a “model” for the jurisdictional inquiry 
under RICO. This Court has repeatedly emphasized, 
however, that “Congress, in passing the Sherman Act, 
left no area of its constitutional power unoccupied; it 
‘exercised all the power it possessed.’” United States v. 
Frankfort Distilleries, Inc., 324 U.S. 293, 298 (1945); see 
also Summit Health, Ltd. v. Pinhas, 500 U.S. 322, 328- 
329 & n.10 (1991). And the “line of business” approach 


3 


that this Court has endorsed in Sherman Act cases 
necessarily looks beyond the activities of the parties 
immediately before the Court. See, e.g., Summit Health, 
Ltd. v. Pinhas, 500 U.S. at 332 (competitive significance 
of conduct “must be measured, not just by a parti- 
cularized evaluation of [plaintiff's] own practice, but 
rather, by a general evaluation of the impact of the 
restraint on other participants and potential participants 
in the market from which he has been excluded”); Mc- 
Lain v. Real Estate Board of New Orleans, 444 U.S. 232, 
242-243 (1980) (plaintiff need not prove that the chal- 
lenged illegal practice, by itself, affected commerce). 
Indeed, respondent concedes that the Court’s analysis in 
Summit Health, supra, “could be read to endorse a class- 
of-activities approach” in assessing the jurisdictional 
reach of the Sherman Act. Br. 16 n.13. To avoid that 
suggestion, respondent is compelled to argue that the ap- 
proach of the Sherman Act cases—the “model” he 
proposes—should not be followed in interpreting RICO, 
because that approach “is uniquely appropriate to 
antitrust law.” Ibid. 

Contrary to respondent’s argument that “the in- 
dividual activities of the [RICO] enterprise” must be 
viewed in isolation (Br. 5), this Court’s cases have 
applied the class-of-activities analysis to other statutes 
that (like RICO and the Sherman Act) involve “general 
prohibitions” (Br. 10). In Polish National Alliance v. 
NLRB, 322 U.S. 643 (1944), for example, the Court held 
that certain practices were proscribed by the National 
Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. 152(7), because they 
“tend[ed] to lead to a labor dispute burdening or 
obstructing commerce.” 322 U.S. at 648. In so holding, 
the Court first noted that when Congress legislates with 
respect to activities that “affect” or “obstruct” com- 
merce, it manifests its purpose to bring those activities 


4 


“within the full sweep of its constitutional authority.” 
322 U.S. at 647. The Court then explained that while 
“Congress * * * left it to the Board to ascertain 
whether proscribed practices would in particular situa- 
tions adversely affect commerce when judged by the full 
reach of the constitutional power,” that question “is not 
to be determined by confining judgment to the quanti- 
tative effect of the activities immediately before the 
Board. Appropriate for judgment is the fact that the 
immediate situation is representative of many others 
throughout the country, the total incidence of which if 
left unchecked may well become far-reaching in its harm 
to commerce.” /d. at 648. The Court made the same 
point (and quoted from Polish National Alliance at 
length) in NLRB v. Reliance Fuel Oil Corp., 371 U.S. at 
226-227, the case that was cited in the House Report that 
accompanied RICO to illustrate the constitutional sig- 
nificance of the phrase “affecting commerce.” H.R. Rep. 
No. 1549, supra, at 70. 

More recently, the Court applied the same “class-of- 
activities” analysis in Russell v. United States, 471 U.S. 
858 (1985). The defendant in that case attempted to set 
fire to a two-unit apartment building. He was convicted 
under 18 U.S.C. 844(i), which prohibits the arson or 
attempted arson of any building “used in interstate or 
foreign commerce or in any activity affecting interstate 
or foreign commerce.” That statute, like RICO, re- 
quires that a nexus with commerce be proven in each 
prosecution. This Court nonetheless unanimously af- 
firmed the conviction, rejecting the defendant’s claim 
that the building he attempted to burn did not “affect([] 
interstate or foreign commerce.” The Court explained 
that the phrase “affecting commerce” expresses Con- 
gress’s intent “to exercise its full power under the 
Commerce Clause,” 471 U.S. at 859 & n.4, and noted that

Finally, respondent relies (Br. 10-11) on the Court’s 
statement in Gulf Oil Corp. v. Copp Paving Co., 419

persons and activities that affect commerce.” The differ- 
ence in the inquiry, however, does not assist respondent. 
When Congress defines the regulated class in the 
statute, it dispenses with the need for any proof of a nex-

e.g., Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146 (1971), cited by 
Gulf Oil Corp. v. Copp Paving Co., 419 U.S. at 197 n.12. 
That is not so when Congress uses the term of art 
“affecting commerce,” but the nexus to commerce in a 
particular case may still be established by any 
permissible means of invoking Congress’s commerce 
power, including the class-of-activities analysis. See, 
e.g., United States v. Staszcuk, 517 F.2d at 59-60 (while 
courts may not “disregard the statutory language which 
requires the prosecutor to prove some connection with 


interstate commerce in every case,” jurisdiction “is
