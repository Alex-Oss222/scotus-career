## 46. Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado — April 18, 1995

**Question and separated components:** The particular claims concern criminal-court records used to obtain names and addresses for truthful direct-mail legal and treatment services. Section 24-72-305.5 both prohibits use of the covered records and information to solicit business for pecuniary gain and requires an applicant's assurance of no direct solicitation before inspection. A decision cannot treat the first command as nonexistent. Whether all or a subset of criminal-justice records carries a qualified access right is distinct from the validity of the speech-based condition on otherwise available records. No new statewide disclosure regime or entitlement to every personal datum is necessary to decide this application.

**Two paths:** Reversal holds the actual distinction unjustified under Central Hudson and Fox, even assuming no freestanding right to all requested information. The government preserves access for other purposes while imposing a speech-use restriction; privacy and overreaching are legitimate concerns but the record must explain how this selection materially addresses those concerns and fits the truthful mail activity at issue. Its strongest counterargument is that a State can retain meaningful control over disclosure of sensitive accused-person information without preventing every subsequent source of solicitation. Affirmance treats the restraint on the State's own provision and resulting use of the information as a reasonably fitted privacy measure, materially different from Shapero's outright communication ban. Its strongest counterargument is that the law is not simply refusal to compile or sell a list: it prohibits use, selects commercial speech, leaves other dissemination including the identified newspaper available, and rests on general suppositions of abuse.

No money award, automatic delivery of every file, or immunity for misleading solicitation is ordered. ## 47. Celotex Corp. v. Edwards — April 19, 1995

**Three separate questions:** The automatic stay under §362; the scope of the particular §105 injunction; and the duty to seek relief from or direct review of an injunction issued within the bankruptcy court's subject-matter competence. Section 105 is not a subject-matter jurisdiction grant. Section 1334(b)'s related-proceeding jurisdiction need not be exclusive jurisdiction over property. The matured Northbrook bond obligation is distinct from Celotex's underlying debt. None of these distinctions alone decides the other two questions.

**Paths and strongest counterarguments:** The reversal path treats the order's reference to proceedings involving a debtor, including bonded appeals, as reaching the collection effort in light of the issuing court's explicit published interpretation. The insurance collateral and surety relationship create at least a substantial, nonfrivolous related-proceeding jurisdiction question, so objections to this application should be presented to the issuing court and its reviewing courts. United Mine Workers and Walker support orderly direct challenge while preserving their jurisdictional qualifications. The strongest objection is that an injunction cannot expand itself by interpretation beyond the actual text or attach unrelated third-party assets to an estate on generalized management concerns. The affirmance path adopts the narrow order-scope ground: enforcement of the matured independent surety obligation is outside this debtor-directed order, so allowing it is not disobedience. Its strongest objection is that the issuing court has expressly ruled otherwise and §1334(b) reaches more than estate property; an outside court's limiting construction may become collateral overruling in substance.

Stevens and Ginsburg would affirm because the particular order does not reach the matured third-party undertaking, without reaching the lower majority's broader bankruptcy-power rationale. No refund, new bond amount, permanent release, or ultimate entitlement to injunction relief is invented.

## 48. McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission — April 19, 1995

**Question and scope:** The challenged application requires a private individual's name and residence or business address on her printed leaflets opposing a school-tax issue. The source text describes the relevant category of campaign publications and expenditures and the exception for personal correspondence not machine-reproduced for general distribution. The Ohio opinion quotes the statute only in relevant part, with an ellipsis; this handoff does not claim an independently complete statutory quotation. No omitted provision is adjudicated. There is no adjudicated fraud, candidate committee, or funded organization in McIntyre's activity.

**Alternative paths:** Reversal applies Talley's protection of anonymous leaflets and exacting scrutiny of a compelled identification burden on core political expression. A generally useful attribution rule is not sufficiently tailored to impose this requirement on this individual's truthful issue leaflet merely to facilitate fraud investigation and inform voters. Its strongest counterargument is that identifying the source is a small, viewpoint-neutral requirement that can deter false campaign claims before effective remedies are possible. Affirmance accepts that limited attribution condition for election communications, relying on the fraud-enforcement and source-information interests recognized in Buckley and Bellotti and distinguishing Talley's general all-handbill ordinance. Its strongest counterargument is that Bellotti discussed corporate advertising and Buckley concerned distinct financial disclosures, whereas this statute directly conditions ordinary individual issue advocacy and the State has not established why its separate fraud laws are insufficient here.
