## Event, posture, and record

Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co., No. 93-1631. Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 2 F.3d 355 (August 23, 1993). The question is whether the federal ban on truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels satisfies the First Amendment. The separately upheld, unappealed advertising restriction is outside the judgment under review.

Render form: full. The entry preserves independently sufficient advancement and fit grounds, a distinct judgment-only concurrence, and the actual controlling treatment of Edge and Lanphere in an explicit topology.

BATF denied Coors’s proposed labels and advertisements in 1987. After the first appeal and remand for factual development, the district court upheld the advertising restriction but enjoined the labeling restriction on advancement and fit grounds. Coors did not appeal the advertising ruling. The Tenth Circuit affirmed relief on advancement without reaching fit. The existing trial record and presented arguments permit resolution of the independently litigated fit ground without a new factual remand.

Section 205(e)(2) prohibits malt-beverage alcohol-content statements unless state law requires them. Its wine clause requires alcohol-content statements only above fourteen percent by volume; it does not prohibit lower-strength wine disclosure. Section 205(f)(2), addressing advertising, contains an alcohol-information exception for malt beverages and wines, without the labeling clause’s state-required-disclosure exception. A later interstate malt-beverage paragraph expressly addresses both labeling and advertising and conditions their application on similar state requirements for comparable intrastate transactions. The agency administers the label ban except where state law affirmatively requires disclosure, but administers the advertising ban where a corresponding state advertising prohibition exists. This is the operating construction, not a holding that the later paragraph mentions only advertising or a new statutory challenge to that construction.

Under the challenged §7.26 regime, state-required statements follow the manner specified by the State; absent such a specification, they use percentage by weight or volume rather than proof, maximums or minimums. Low or reduced alcohol requires less than 2.5 percent alcohol by volume. Non-alcoholic requires the directly conjoined, readily legible, contrasting-background statement that the product contains less than 0.5 percent alcohol by volume. Alcohol-free requires no alcohol. These distinct permissions do not provide all useful numerical comparison information. Sections 7.29(f) and (g), concerning strength descriptions and numerical or design suggestions of strength, are outside this adjudication. Separate outdoor-advertising and publisher/radio provisions are not conflated with the state-law clauses or independently adjudicated. The April 1993 interim suspension of regulatory enforcement complied with the injunction; it was not statutory repeal or a concession of invalidity.

The Government invokes strength competition, possible shifts in consumer preferences, and assistance to state alcohol policy. Coors contests the effectiveness and breadth of suppressing accurate percentages while other channels and beverages receive different treatment. No independently verified fifty-state tally or future strength war is found. The Court assumes the state-assistance rationale may be considered and rejects its unsupported justification on the merits; it does not decide forfeiture.

## Controlling holdings

### The challenged scheme does not materially advance its asserted health interest

**Controlling proposition:** The Government may not suppress truthful, nonmisleading numerical alcohol-content statements on lawful malt-beverage labels unless the restriction directly and materially advances a substantial interest. Preventing strength competition and its health harms is substantial, but the challenged scheme fails that requirement on this record; predictive judgment is permissible, while abstract concern and unexplained inconsistencies do not establish the necessary connection.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stevens agrees that the scheme fails in practice but supplies a distinct judgment ground rather than a framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this numerical-label ban has not been shown materially to reduce the strength competition invoked to defend it. Central Hudson requires a real connection between the restriction and the substantial interest; Edenfield requires justification for the actual restraint. The operating scheme permits alcohol information for other beverages, permits malt-beverage advertising where no corresponding state prohibition applies, and yields to state-required label disclosure. Those features leave comparable incentives operating while suppressing useful factual labels. The Court does not demand identical rules for all beverages, controlled studies in every case, or regulation of all sources at once. Edge permits a demonstrated incremental accommodation, while Discovery Network and Lanphere require the chosen distinction to address the asserted harm. The Government’s predictive argument and concern that existing preferences reflect the ban deserve consideration. They do not explain how this scheme materially works despite its inconsistencies. The holding concerns accurate, nonmisleading percentages, not deceptive claims or direct control of alcohol strength.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies its substantial-interest and direct-advancement requirements.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the requirement of responsive justification for the actual commercial-speech restriction.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, and Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, April 18, 1995: apply the requirement that the selected speech restriction address the demonstrated harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., June 25, 1993: distinguishes its supported state-based scheme; its holding creates no categorical vice-speech exemption.

### The ban independently lacks reasonable proportional fit

**Controlling proposition:** Independently of the failure of material advancement, the categorical suppression of accurate numerical beer-label information lacks a reasonable fit to the asserted strength-competition harm in light of its unexplained breadth and workable measures directed more closely at that harm. Reasonable fit requires proportionality, not the least restrictive imaginable means, and the Court prescribes no particular substitute regulation.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight direct joiners adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stevens’s distinct concurrence is not counted as a Fox-framework vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the label prohibition is disproportionate to the harm offered to justify it. Fox requires a reasonable fit, not perfection or the least restrictive conceivable choice. Here direct regulation of alcohol strength, focused controls on promotion emphasizing high strength, and a restriction directed to the allegedly affected market segment provide concrete comparisons with a ban on every accurate percentage. The Government answers that national strength limits could displace state choices and that a malt-liquor limitation might miss competition elsewhere. Those objections do not explain suppressing useful comparison information across the challenged category, including information that may assist selection of weaker products. Discovery Network and Lanphere require an account of that actual burden; Ibanez likewise requires a justification responsive to the selected restriction. The existing record and litigated district-court ground permit this alternative holding although the court of appeals stopped at advancement. The Court orders no tax, strength ceiling, national label design or replacement program, and leaves properly justified deceptive-claim regulation available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable proportional fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network and Lanphere: apply their distinct fit inquiry to the burden and supported alternatives here.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the need for justification responsive to the actual truthful-information burden.

### The asserted need to assist state policy supplies no alternative justification on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the Government’s state-assistance rationale is properly considered, its unsubstantiated assertion that federal suppression of accurate label information is needed to facilitate state alcohol policy does not justify this restriction. Legitimate accommodation of differing state policies remains possible when its need and operation are supported; federalism and the Twenty-first Amendment do not alone replace First Amendment scrutiny.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight Justices directly adopt this rejection of the asserted alternative justification. The Court decides no independent forfeiture issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government has not established the particular state-policy justification advanced for federal label suppression. Edge recognizes that an effective accommodation of differing state policies may satisfy commercial-speech review. It does not treat an invocation of state authority as proof of the need for a federal information ban. Here the Government has not demonstrated why States cannot operate their own alcohol policies without this suppression of truthful percentages. The statutory state-law provisions and the agency’s differing administration of labeling and advertising describe the scheme; they do not themselves prove its necessity or effect. The Court assumes the rationale may be considered and therefore need not decide Coors’s preservation objection. Nor does it deny the States’ authority to address alcohol harms or hold that federal assistance can never be legitimate. On this record the asserted assistance supplies neither a sufficient independent justification nor an answer to the demonstrated defects in advancement and proportional fit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edge Broadcasting: preserves the legitimacy of supported state-policy accommodation while distinguishing the missing demonstrated mechanism here.
- Central Hudson: requires the asserted justification to support the actual speech restriction rather than stand as an unexamined label.

## Event

Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co., No. 93-1631. Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 2 F.3d 355 (August 23, 1993). The question is whether the federal ban on truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels satisfies the First Amendment. The separately upheld, unappealed advertising restriction is outside the judgment under review.

Render form: full. The entry preserves independently sufficient advancement and fit grounds, a distinct judgment-only concurrence, and the actual controlling treatment of Edge and Lanphere in an explicit topology.

Section 205(e)(2) prohibits malt-beverage alcohol-content statements unless state law requires them. Its wine clause requires alcohol-content statements only above fourteen percent by volume; it does not prohibit lower-strength wine disclosure. Section 205(f)(2), addressing advertising, contains an alcohol-information exception for malt beverages and wines, without the labeling clause’s state-required-disclosure exception. A later interstate malt-beverage paragraph expressly addresses both labeling and advertising and conditions their application on similar state requirements for comparable intrastate transactions. The agency administers the label ban except where state law affirmatively requires disclosure, but administers the advertising ban where a corresponding state advertising prohibition exists. This is the operating construction, not a holding that the later paragraph mentions only advertising or a new statutory challenge to that construction.

Under the challenged §7.26 regime, state-required statements follow the manner specified by the State; absent such a specification, they use percentage by weight or volume rather than proof, maximums or minimums. Low or reduced alcohol requires less than 2.5 percent alcohol by volume. Non-alcoholic requires the directly conjoined, readily legible, contrasting-background statement that the product contains less than 0.5 percent alcohol by volume. Alcohol-free requires no alcohol. These distinct permissions do not provide all useful numerical comparison information. Sections 7.29(f) and (g), concerning strength descriptions and numerical or design suggestions of strength, are outside this adjudication. Separate outdoor-advertising and publisher/radio provisions are not conflated with the state-law clauses or independently adjudicated. The April 1993 interim suspension of regulatory enforcement complied with the injunction; it was not statutory repeal or a concession of invalidity.

The Government invokes strength competition, possible shifts in consumer preferences, and assistance to state alcohol policy. Coors contests the effectiveness and breadth of suppressing accurate percentages while other channels and beverages receive different treatment. No independently verified fifty-state tally or future strength war is found. The Court assumes the state-assistance rationale may be considered and rejects its unsupported justification on the merits; it does not decide forfeiture.

## Holdings

### The challenged scheme does not materially advance its asserted health interest

**Controlling proposition:** The Government may not suppress truthful, nonmisleading numerical alcohol-content statements on lawful malt-beverage labels unless the restriction directly and materially advances a substantial interest. Preventing strength competition and its health harms is substantial, but the challenged scheme fails that requirement on this record; predictive judgment is permissible, while abstract concern and unexplained inconsistencies do not establish the necessary connection.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stevens agrees that the scheme fails in practice but supplies a distinct judgment ground rather than a framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this numerical-label ban has not been shown materially to reduce the strength competition invoked to defend it. Central Hudson requires a real connection between the restriction and the substantial interest; Edenfield requires justification for the actual restraint. The operating scheme permits alcohol information for other beverages, permits malt-beverage advertising where no corresponding state prohibition applies, and yields to state-required label disclosure. Those features leave comparable incentives operating while suppressing useful factual labels. The Court does not demand identical rules for all beverages, controlled studies in every case, or regulation of all sources at once. Edge permits a demonstrated incremental accommodation, while Discovery Network and Lanphere require the chosen distinction to address the asserted harm. The Government’s predictive argument and concern that existing preferences reflect the ban deserve consideration. They do not explain how this scheme materially works despite its inconsistencies. The holding concerns accurate, nonmisleading percentages, not deceptive claims or direct control of alcohol strength.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies its substantial-interest and direct-advancement requirements.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the requirement of responsive justification for the actual commercial-speech restriction.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, and Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, April 18, 1995: apply the requirement that the selected speech restriction address the demonstrated harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., June 25, 1993: distinguishes its supported state-based scheme; its holding creates no categorical vice-speech exemption.

### The ban independently lacks reasonable proportional fit

**Controlling proposition:** Independently of the failure of material advancement, the categorical suppression of accurate numerical beer-label information lacks a reasonable fit to the asserted strength-competition harm in light of its unexplained breadth and workable measures directed more closely at that harm. Reasonable fit requires proportionality, not the least restrictive imaginable means, and the Court prescribes no particular substitute regulation.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight direct joiners adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stevens’s distinct concurrence is not counted as a Fox-framework vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the label prohibition is disproportionate to the harm offered to justify it. Fox requires a reasonable fit, not perfection or the least restrictive conceivable choice. Here direct regulation of alcohol strength, focused controls on promotion emphasizing high strength, and a restriction directed to the allegedly affected market segment provide concrete comparisons with a ban on every accurate percentage. The Government answers that national strength limits could displace state choices and that a malt-liquor limitation might miss competition elsewhere. Those objections do not explain suppressing useful comparison information across the challenged category, including information that may assist selection of weaker products. Discovery Network and Lanphere require an account of that actual burden; Ibanez likewise requires a justification responsive to the selected restriction. The existing record and litigated district-court ground permit this alternative holding although the court of appeals stopped at advancement. The Court orders no tax, strength ceiling, national label design or replacement program, and leaves properly justified deceptive-claim regulation available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable proportional fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network and Lanphere: apply their distinct fit inquiry to the burden and supported alternatives here.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the need for justification responsive to the actual truthful-information burden.

### The asserted need to assist state policy supplies no alternative justification on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the Government’s state-assistance rationale is properly considered, its unsubstantiated assertion that federal suppression of accurate label information is needed to facilitate state alcohol policy does not justify this restriction. Legitimate accommodation of differing state policies remains possible when its need and operation are supported; federalism and the Twenty-first Amendment do not alone replace First Amendment scrutiny.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight Justices directly adopt this rejection of the asserted alternative justification. The Court decides no independent forfeiture issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government has not established the particular state-policy justification advanced for federal label suppression. Edge recognizes that an effective accommodation of differing state policies may satisfy commercial-speech review. It does not treat an invocation of state authority as proof of the need for a federal information ban. Here the Government has not demonstrated why States cannot operate their own alcohol policies without this suppression of truthful percentages. The statutory state-law provisions and the agency’s differing administration of labeling and advertising describe the scheme; they do not themselves prove its necessity or effect. The Court assumes the rationale may be considered and therefore need not decide Coors’s preservation objection. Nor does it deny the States’ authority to address alcohol harms or hold that federal assistance can never be legitimate. On this record the asserted assistance supplies neither a sufficient independent justification nor an answer to the demonstrated defects in advancement and proportional fit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edge Broadcasting: preserves the legitimacy of supported state-policy accommodation while distinguishing the missing demonstrated mechanism here.
- Central Hudson: requires the asserted justification to support the actual speech restriction rather than stand as an unexamined label.

## Precedent Treatment

Central Hudson and Fox remain the commercial-speech framework. Discovery Network, Edenfield, Ibanez and Lanphere supply the actual requirements of a harm-related classification, responsive evidence, and reasonable fit. Edge preserves supported incremental and state-based regulation at its controlling scope. No precedent is overruled; no categorical vice exception, strict-scrutiny rule for all commercial speech, or least-restrictive-means requirement is adopted.

## Law After Decision

The challenged truthful numerical malt-beverage label ban fails both material advancement and, independently, reasonable proportional fit. The unsupported state-assistance rationale does not cure either defect. The advertising judgment, distinct strength-description and design rules, state enactments and other applications of §205 remain outside the relief. Lawful direct regulation and properly justified measures against deception remain available without a judicially prescribed replacement program.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment. Accurate, unadorned information about a lawful product cannot be suppressed here simply because consumers may use it to choose a stronger beverage. His Edge dissent identifies the objection to keeping consumers uninformed, while Discovery Network requires the selected speech category to relate to an actual harm. He agrees that this scheme fails to advance the stated objective, but does not suggest that greater success at withholding truthful knowledge would cure the underlying objection. His Lanphere joinder in a bounded Central Hudson application did not abandon that distinct ground. He does not deny the Government’s authority to regulate alcohol’s harms directly, forbid deceptive claims, or require properly justified useful disclosures. He declines to join the Court’s commercial-speech framework, its acceptance of the information-suppression interest as framed, or its Fox alternative holding. His truthful-information rationale is noncontrolling; the eight-Justice Court opinion controls without combining separate grounds.

## Procedure After Action

The injunction against the challenged truthful numerical label restriction remains in force. No further merits remand is required. The Government may pursue lawful, appropriately justified alternatives, but the Court commands no particular replacement. The unappealed advertising judgment and separate descriptive-strength provisions are undisturbed.
