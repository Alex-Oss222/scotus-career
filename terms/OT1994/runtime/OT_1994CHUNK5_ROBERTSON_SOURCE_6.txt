CONSTITUTIONAL AND STATUTORY 
PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


1. The Commerce Clause of the Constitution, Article 
I, Section 8, Clause 3, provides: “The Congress shall 
have Power . . . to regulate Commerce with foreign Na- 
tions, and among the several States, and with the Indian 
Tribes.” 


2. The pertinent provisions of the Racketeer Influenced 
and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO), 18 U.S.C. 
§§ 1961 et seq., are set forth in an appendix to this brief. 


STATEMENT 


1. On November 15, 1983, respondent Juan Paul 
Robertson, stole a Cadillac containing $980,000 and two 
kilograms of cocaine. In 1984, respondent and his wife 
purchased a home near Phoenix for $270,000 in cash. 
Pet. App. 3a; Tr. 61-76, 371-72. They also made ad- 
ditional large cash purchases totalling approximately 
$700,000. Pet. App. 3a; Tr. 383-405. 


In 1985, respondent and Richard Horchler agreed as 
equal partners to buy mining claims in Alaska. Horchler 
negotiated the purchase of the claims for $125,000. He 
and a crew went to Alaska to begin work to make the 
claims operational. There, he purchased heavy equipment, 
including a bulldozer and frontloader, bought hand tools, 
and made arrangements to buy a generator. Respondent 
gave Horchler $85,000 for initial purchases and for sal- 
aries. Tom Black, a friend of respondent, purchased a 
small truck in Los Angeles and drove to Alaska with 
some of his tools to assist in setting up the mine. While 
in Alaska, Horchler and respondent agreed to name the 
mining operation “Double Eagle.” The “Double Eagle” 
mining partnership lasted from June 20, 1985 until July 
31, 1985, when it was dissolved. Thereafter, the mining 
operation became “Robertson Mining Company.” A small 
quantity of gold was mined and sold in 1985. Tr. 825- 
36, 608-12, 909, 915, 1004-15, 2067. 


3 


In 1986, Tim Robertson, respondent’s brother, and 
Boyce Clock drove the Cadillac to Alaska so that they 
could work with respondent and others at the mine. The 
Cadillac was wrecked after they arrived, and was inoper- 
able at the mine. Respondent obtained his pilot’s license 
in 1986, bought a plane, and used it to fly from Alaska. 
Tr. 757-58, 1720, 1904-05. 


According to respondent’s ex-wife, in 1986, she and 
respondent received a $300,000 loan secured by the house 
in Phoenix. Respondent allocated a portion of this loan 
for mine expenses. Approximately $200,000 of gold was 
sold by the mine. All of the purchasers were Alaskan 
refiners. Respondent kept about $30,000 of nuggets. Jd. 
at 453-60, 782-83, 1906-07. In 1987, the mine was not 
profitable; and that year respondent gave the mine to his 
wife as part of their dissolution. Jd. at 1985-86. 


2. On October 17, 1991, respondent was indicted on 
eight narcotics and racketeering counts. A jury found 
respondent guilty on four narcotics counts and one count 
of investing racketeering proceeds in an enterprise, the 
activities of which affect interstate commerce, in violation 
of 18 U.S.C. 1962(a). Respondent was sentenced to con- 
current terms of twenty years imprisonment on each of 
the five counts. 


3. The court of appeals reversed respondent’s RICO 
conviction and affirmed the narcotics convictions. Pet. 
App. 7a-10a. In a unanimous decision, the panel held 
that RICO’s interstate nexus requirement was not satis- 
fied because “the Government failed to establish that the 
RICO enterprise had anything more than an incidental 
effect on interstate commerce.” Jd. at 9a. The court 
noted that “none of the gold obtained from the mine was 
sold outside Alaska.” Jd. The court further found that 
“(t]he mine was a relatively small and entirely local op- 
eration.” Jd. The court rejected the Government’s argu- 


4 


ment that respondent's use of an airplane and the Cadillac 
were sufficient to establish the requisite impact on inter- 
State commerce. /d. at 10a. Evidence that some start-up 
funds originated in Arizona was similarly disregarded as 
not an activity of the enterprise. “There was no evidence 
introduced to show that the activities of the enterprise 
itself affected interstate commerce.” Jd. at 9a. 


Judge Reinhardt, writing a special concurrence, ex- 
pressed concern over the overcharging of respondent by 
the prosecution. “Rather than simply prosecuting the de- 
fendant on the basis of the overwhelming evidence of his 
illicit drug activity, the prosecutors . . . decided to turn 
the defendant’s ownership of a gold mine into the center- 
piece of his prosecution.” Jd. at 28a-29a. Judge Rein- 
hardt stated that the “practice of overcharging a defend- 
ant involves an abuse of the prosecutor’s generally unre- 
viewable discretion.” Jd. at 29a-30a. He further noted 
that RICO “seems particularly susceptible to prosecutorial 
abuse, given that its scope is broad and somewhat ill- 
defined, and its penalties are severe.” Id. at 32a. 


The Government's petition for rehearing was denied and 
its suggestion for rehearing en banc was rejected without 
dissent on April 26, 1994. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Respondent was charged and convicted under 18 U.S.C. 
§ 1962(a) which makes unlawful the investment of funds 
derived from a pattern of racketeering activity in an enter- 
prise whose activities “affect[] interstate or foreign com- 
merce.” The activities of respondent’s gold mine did not 
“affect” interstate commerce within the meaning of the 
Statute. 


A. Under this Court’s precedents, congressional power 
to regulate activities affecting interstate commerce extends 
only to those activities which individually or when ag- 
gregated as a class have a “substantial effect” upon inter- 
state commerce. If Congress sets out to regulate a par- 


—— = = <> 


5 


ticular industry or “class of activity,” courts review Con- 
gress’s determination that the cumulative effect of the 
activities of the industry or class has a substantial effect 
on interstate commerce. Neither the language of the 
RICO statute nor its legislative history demonstrates a 
congressional purpose to regulate a “class of activities.” 
Rather, 18 U.S.C. § 1962(a), like the Sherman Act, is 
a general prchibition. Accordingly, under RICO the fact- 
finder must analyze the individual activities of the enter- 
prise in question to determine whether they nave a sub- 
stantial effect on interstate commerce. 


B. Applying the “substantial effect” standard and con- 
ducting a particularized inquiry compel the conclusion that 
respondent’s gold mining enterprise had an insufficient 
nexus with interstate commerce under 18 U.S.C. § 1962 
(a). First, the sales of gold from the mine were only 
approximately $200,000 over a three-year period, and all 
the sales were made to refiners within Alaska. Second, 
the investment of monies in the mine and equipment in 
order to establish and sustain the operation is not an “ac- 
tivit{y] of [the mine] which affects[] interstate commerce.” 
Third, the use of the telephones and mails is a con- 
comitant but is not an activity of the mine as contem- 
plated by the RICO statute. If such use were a sufficient 
“effect” on commerce, then federal laws like RICO and 
the Sherman Act would cover every imaginable form of 
economic activity. 


C. The “substantial effect” standard and a rigorous 
particularized inquiry serve to preserve the delicate bal- 
ance between state and federal power and to maintain 
the separation of powers. To apply a lesser standard 
would, in the case of RICO, result in an unwarranted 
federalization and proliferation of criminal and civil cases. 
In general, because other statutes, such as the Sherman 
Act, also base jurisdiction on effects upon commerce, it 
would result in a further overburdening of the federal 
courts.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE ACTIVITIES OF RESPONDENT'S GOLD MINE 
DID NOT HAVE A SUBSTANTIAL EFFECT ON 
INTERSTATE COMMERCE AND ARE THERE- 
FORE BEYOND THE REACH OF RICO. 


Congress “can exercise only the powers granted to it” 
by the Constitution. McCulloch vy. Maryland, 17 U.S. 
(4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819). “[U]nlike the reserved po- 
lice powers of the States, which are plenary unless chal- 
lenged as violating some specific provision of the Con- 
stitution, the connection with interstate commerce is itself 
a jurisdictional prerequisite for any substantive legisla- 
tion by Congress under the Commerce Clause.” Hodel v. 
Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Ass'n, 452 US. 
264, 311 (1981) (Rehnquist, J., concurring). Under the 
Commerce Clause, Congress can regulate (1) the chan- 
nels of interstate commerce; (2) the instrumentalities of 
interstate commerce; and (3) activities affecting com- 
merce. Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146, 150 (1971). 


In the instant case, jurisdiction is asserted based upon 
the third category—activities affecting commerce. The 
Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act 
(RICO), 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961 et seq., prohibits the invest- 
ment of racketeering proceeds into an “enterprise . . . 
the activities of which affect[] interstate or foreign com- 
merce.” Jd. § 1962(a). The issue here is the requisite 
“effect” on interstate commerce necessary to fall within 
the reach of RICO. 


A. Under this Court’s precedents, Congress’s power to 
regulate activities affecting interstate commerce 
extends only to those activities which individually 
or as a class have a substantial effect on interstate 
commerce. 


Petitioner urges the Court to hold that “perceptible and 
identifiable” effects upon interstate commerce are sufficient 
to confer jurisdiction under the Commerce Clause. Pet. 
Br. 25; see infra at Part I, A-5. This Court, however, 
has long held that the Commerce Clause grants Congress 


7 


the power to regulate activities “affecting commerce” only 
when they have a substantial effect on interstate com- 
merce. In NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 
U.S. 1 (1937), the Court held that Congress could regu- 
late labor relations at any manufacturing plant which 
had interstate sales. See id. at 40-42. Jones & Laughlin 
was issued less than one year after the Court struck down 
the application of the same statute to striking coal miners 
because mining was “manufacturing” not “commerce.” 
See Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 302 (1936). 
The Jones & Laughlin Court adopted a more expansive 
definition of “commerce,” which emphasized the inter- 
connected nature of the national economy, declaring that 
a work stoppage “would have a most serious effect upon 
interstate commerce.” Jones & Laughlin, 301 U.S. at 41 
(emphasis added). The requirement of a “serious effect” 
was necessary to counter the concern, as expressed by 
Chief Justice Hughes, that 


[t]he authority of the federal government may not be 
pushed to such an extreme as to destroy the distinc- 
tion, which the commerce clause itself establishes, 
between commerce “among the several States” and 
the internal concerns of a state. That distinction be- 
tween what is national and what is local in the ac- 
tivities of commerce is vital to the maintenance of our 
federal system. 


Id. at 30. 


In United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100 (1940), this 
Court stated, “But it does not follow that Congress may 
not by appropriate legislation regulate intrastate activities 
where they have a substantial effect on interstate com- 
merce.” Id. at 119 (citing Santa Cruz Fruit Packing Co. 
Vv. NLRB, 303 U.S. 453, 466 (1938)) (emphasis added). 
In United States v. Wrightwood Dairy Co., 315 U.S. 110 
(1942), Chief Justice Stone wrote that the commerce 
power “extends to those intrastate activities which in a 
substantial way interfere with or obstruct the exercise of 


8 


the granted power.” /d. at 119 (emphasis added). Simi- 
larly, in Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 ( 1942), in an 
oft-quoted passage, the Court stated: 


even if appellee’s activity be local and though it may 
not be regarded as commerce, it may still, whatever 
its nature, be reached by Congress if it exerts a sub- 
stantial economic effect on interstate commerce, and 
this irrespective of whether such effect is what might 
at some earlier time have been defined as “direct” or 
“indirect.” 


Id. at 125 (emphasis added); see also McLain v. Real 
Estate Bd. of New Orleans, Inc., 444 U.S. 232, 244 
(1980); Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States, 
379 U.S. 241, 258 (1964); Katzenbach v. McClung, 379 
U.S. 294, 302 (1964); Perez, 402 U.S. at 151-52. The 
necessity of a “substantial effect” on interstate commerce 
was directly addressed by Justice Harlan who, writing for 
the Court in Maryland v. Wirtz, 392 U.S. 183 (1968), 
declared: “Neither here nor in Wickard has the Court 
declared that Congress may use a relatively trivial impact 
on commerce as an excuse for broad general regulation 
of state or private activities.” Jd. at 196 n.27: see also 
Hodel, 452 U.S. at 309 (Rehnquist, J., concurring); 
Russell v. United States, 471 U.S. 858, 859 n.4 (1985) 
(referring to “all activity substantially affecting interstate 
commerce”) (emphasis added) (citations omitted). 


1. Most regulations of activities “affecting com- 
merce” are “class of activities” statutes that do 
not require a particularized inquiry into a party’s 
effect on interstate commerce. 


Congress has exercised its power to regulate activities 
“affecting commerce” in two distinct ways. Typically, 
Congress has enacted statutes regulating an entire class 
of activities that in the aggregate has a substantial effect 
on interstate commerce. In some instances, however, 
Congress enacts a general prohibition, like the Sherman 


9 


Act. In such general prohibitions, Congress writes a 
jurisdictional requirement into the text of the statute. 


In 1942, this Court held that the production of a single 
wheat farmer was within the scope of federal regulation 
because “taken together with that of many others simi- 
larly situated” it had “a substantial influence on price 
and market conditions.” Wickard, 317 U.S. at 128. Con- 
gress has relied on the “cumulative effect” principle of 
Wickard as its constitutional justification for the enact- 
ment of civil rights legislation;* regulatory measures af- 
fecting the sale of foods and additives;* a registration 
law for drug producers; * and criminal statutes. 


For example, in Title II of the Consumer Credit Pro- 
tection Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 891 et seq., Congress made 
explicit findings that extortionate credit transactions affect 
interstate commerce.‘ In interpreting the Act, this Court 
held that—based on legislative findings that loan-sharking 
in general substantially affected interstate commerce— 
Congress could make criminal any purely intrastate loan- 
sharking activity: 


Where the class of activities is regulated and that 
class is within the reach of federal power, the courts 
have no power “to excise as trivial, individual in- 
stances” of the class. 


Perez, 402 U.S. at 154 (quoting Wirtz, 392 U.S. at 193).

The judicial inquiry is relatively superficial when Con- 
gress has identified the activity or industry to be regulated, 
and findings (explicit or implicit) demonstrate a substan- 
tial effect on interstate commerce. “The court must defer 
to a congressional finding that a regulated activity affects 
interstate commerce, if there is any rational basis for such 
a finding.” Hodel, 452 U.S. at 276. 


Thus, in cases involving a “class of activities” statute, 
a court need find only that the party is a member of a 
class whose activities Congress could rationally have 
found have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. 
When interpreting such statutes, the “substantial effect” 
on interstate commerce is relatively easy to show because 
an entire industry or market will almost always have a 
substantial effect on interstate commerce. 


2. General prohibitions require a particularized in- 
quiry into the nexus between a party’s activity 
and interstate commerce. 


In contrast, the judicial inquiry is more rigorous and 
involves a particularized determination of fact when cases 
are brought under statutes containing general prohibitions, 
like the Sherman Act and RICO. Congress cannot pos- 
sibly predetermine all the types of trade or enterprises in- 
tended to be covered by RICO or the Sherman Act. In 
such statutes, Congress has included a nexus element re- 
quiring proof that a particular activity or enterprise suf- 
ficiently affects interstate commerce. See 15 U.S.C. § 1; 
18 U.S.C. § 1962. Congress leaves the nexus determina- 
tion to be made by the courts on a case-by-case basis. As 
stated by this Court in Gulf Oil Corp. v. Copp Paving 
Co., 419 U.S. 186 (1974): 


The jurisdictional inquiry under general prohibitions 
. .+ like § 1 of the Sherman Act, turning as it does 
on the circumstances presented in each case and re- 
quiring a particularized judicial determination, dif-

A. The “substantial effects” test serves to preserve 
the delicate balance between state and federal 
power. 


Since the adoption of the Constitution, a constant ten- 
sion has existed between the need for a strong national 
government on the one hand and the interests of the 
States, their citizens, and decentralized government on the 
other hand. This tension is reflected in the Court’s juris- 
prudence during over 200 years of interpreting the Com- 
merce Clause. With the transformation from an agrarian 
to a highly sophisticated and technological economy, the 
Commerce Clause has been used increasingly as a basis 
for federal legislation, including, of course, the enactment 
of criminal statutes. Nevertheless, 


the interpenetrations of modern society have not 
wiped out State lines. It is not for us to make 
inroads upon our federal system either by indifference 
to its meintenance or excessive regard for the unify- 
ing forces of modern technology. 


Polish Nat'l Alliance of the United States of N. Am. v.

The “substantial effect” standard serves to preserve the 
delicate balance between state and federal power and to 
maintain the separation of powers. Maintaining some 
limit on the scope of Congress’s regulatory power is nec- 
essary to prevent federalization of all imaginable activity. 
If mere use of the telephones and the mails brings an 
activity within Congress’s reach, federal regulation also 
could cover babysitting, and jaywalking could be a fed- 
eral crime. 


Similarly, in the absence of a finding that Congress 
intended to regulate a class of activities, a rigorous judi- 
cial inquiry regarding jurisdiction should be required. 
Some deference to congressional determinations is due, 
but when Congress leaves the jurisdictional inquiry to the 
fact-finder that determination should be individualized. 
This is particularly true when a defendant’s liberty is at 


stake.

18 U.S.C. § 1962. Prohibited activities 


(a) It shall be unlawful for any person who has received 
any income derived, directly or indirectly, from a pattern 
of racketeering activity or through collection of an unlaw- 
ful debt in which such person has participated as a prin- 
cipal within the meaning of section 2, title 18, United 
States Code, to use or invest, directly or indirectly, any 
part of such income, or the proceeds of such income, in 
acquisition of any interest in, or the establishment or 
operation of, any enterprise which is engaged in, or the 
activities of which affect, interstate or foreign commerce. 
A purchase of securities on the open market for purposes 
of investment, and without the intention of controlling or 
participating in the control of the issuer, or of assisting 
another to do so, shall not be unlawful under this sub- 
section if the securities of the issuer held by the purchaser, 
the members of his immediate family, and his or their 
accomplices in any pattern or racketeering activity or the 
collection of an unlawful debt after such purchase do not 
amount in the aggregate to one percent of the ovtstanding 
securities of any one class, and do not confer, either in 
law or in fact, the power to elect one or more directors 
of the issuer. 


(b) It shall be unlawful for any person through a pattern 
of racketeering activity or through collection of an un- 
lawful debt to acquire or maintain, directly or indirectly,

gaged in, or the activities of which affect, interstate or 
foreign commerce. 


(c) It shall be unlawful for any person employed by or 
associated with any enterprise engaged in, or the activities 
of which affect, interstate or foreign commerce, to con- 
duct or participate, directly or indirectly, in the conduct 
of such enterprise’s affairs through a pattern of racketeer- 
ing activity or collection of unlawful debt.
