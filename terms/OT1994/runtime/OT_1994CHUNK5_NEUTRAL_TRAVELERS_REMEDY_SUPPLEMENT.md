## Original injunction: February 3, 1993

The complete district opinion and order, 813 F. Supp. 996, appears at printed A63–A90 of `OT_1994CHUNK5_TRAVELERS_LOWER_ORDERS_SOURCE.txt`, reproduced from the [93-1414 Archive appendix](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0497). Its operative conclusion at A89–A90 has three distinct parts:

1. **ERISA surcharge relief:** Because it found all three surcharges preempted, the district court enjoined defendants from enforcing them “against any commercial insurers or HMOs in connection with their coverage of any ERISA plans.” The operative sentence itself does not separately say that every direct self-funded payor enjoys a freestanding injunction. The opinion discusses self-insured plans, including at A65 and A79, but those discussions must not replace the actual command's wording.
2. **FEHBA relief:** The court separately enjoined enforcement of the 11% and 13% surcharges against insurers participating in the FEHBA program. Only Mutual of Omaha raised the FEHBA challenge, and only as to those two surcharges; the opinion expressly did not decide FEHBA preemption of the 9% charge (A84 n.18). The later appellate analysis concerns experience-rated plans and expressly reserves community-rated plans (A14–A15 n.2 of the amended opinion).
3. **Actuarial Letter relief:** The court enjoined enforcement of paragraphs 1, 2, 3, and 5 against commercial insurers providing stop-loss coverage to self-funded ERISA plans. This is separate relief involving insurance for excess losses; it is not a direct-plan hospital-surcharge injunction. The district court rejected the challenge to paragraphs 4, 6, and 7 (A87–A90).

## Stay and escrow: February 9, 1993

The complete district stay opinion and order appears at A93–A98. The motion arose under then-Rule 62(c). Its conclusion states that the February 3 order is stayed as to the **13% surcharge**, but **not** as to the **9% or 11% surcharges**. Nevertheless, plaintiffs and any other parties subject to the 9% or 11% charges were ordered to pay those funds into an **interest-bearing escrow account pending resolution of defendants' appeal** (A94, A98).

The 13% stay was based on hospitals' dependence on that revenue and the comparative monetary harm to plaintiffs. The court distinguished the 9% and 11% charges, which had been paid into escrow rather than collected by the State; it rejected a stay that would permit State collection (A96–A97). Thus neither “all charges were unenforceable” nor “all surcharges were stayed” describes this order. The stay's text does not supply a separate FEHBA carveout from its 13% direction. The merits basis and the interim implementation remain different questions.

## Appellate judgment and supported last-known status

The original Second Circuit opinion issued October 25, 1993 (A35–A62). The January 12, 1994 rehearing order grants rehearing, requires no further briefing or argument, and announces an amended opinion to follow (A91–A92). The complete amended opinion is dated January 14, 1994 (A1–A34). Its conclusion affirms in part, reverses in part, and directs the district court to enter judgment consistent with the opinion (A34).

The amended opinion sustains ERISA preemption of the three surcharges; independently sustains FEHBA preemption of 13% and 11% charges in the experience-rated setting before it; affirms Actuarial Letter preemption as to paragraphs 1, 2, 3, and 5; and reverses the district court's rejection of paragraph 4 preemption. Paragraphs 6 and 7 were not challenged on that appeal (A33 n.7). Unlike the original opinion's reservation of FEHBA (A58 n.7), the amended opinion actually resolves that ground (A13–A17). The amended opinion governs the judgment described here.

## Bounded disposition consequences; no choice made

The verified materials permit a reviewing court, **if its merits holding warrants that action**, to reverse the reviewed ERISA surcharge judgment to the extent decided, require conforming treatment of the associated injunction, and remand any reserved direct-self-funded application or deemer question for resolution. That can be specified while leaving the independent FEHBA and Actuarial Letter dispositions undisturbed. It need not assert that a particular direct-plan interim injunction remained effective immediately before the decision, nor promise that such protection continues after it.

If direct-plan applications are reserved rather than adjudicated, the disposition must make their status explicit: a merits reversal on the decided insurer/HMO applications does not itself decide the reserved applications; any vacatur of their existing judgment or relief must itself be stated, confined to that component, and justified. “Remand” alone must not silently vacate a judgment or silently preserve an injunction. The materials permit that bounded choice to be made; they do not select it. Interim protection, escrow administration, reimbursement, exact payment obligations, and application of statutory extensions may be left to the lower courts under the resulting mandate and applicable law, without computing or ordering unverified sums here.

The Blues reply requests reversal of the ERISA surcharge decision (p.20); HANYS requests reversal and alternatively remand for necessary deemer resolution (pp.3 n.2, 14). No complete source read in this pass requests a specified partial vacatur or guarantees continuing direct-plan interim protection. No separate general appellate-mandate authority was newly validated in this bounded source pass; the quoted lower directions establish what these courts ordered, not a universal rule about mandate timing. A disposition requiring a new affirmative interim command or a particular automatic mandate effect would need its own eligible authority and factual basis.

The missing later implementation documents do not prevent an ordinary, expressly bounded disposition and remand. They limit claims about an exact current payment or protection regime. The existing packet's statutory-extension and monetary-remedy limits remain applicable.
