## 63. Day v. Holahan +

<!-- BEGIN_SECTION I -->

### SECTION II — STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

#### Position and scope

**Approval Status:** USER-SUPPLIED POSITION. The user supplies the following disposition and full reasoning for all three challenged arrangements, with the linked reporting rule separately analyzed. This is Stone’s preparation instruction, not a completed Court judgment, vote, opinion or holding.

**Build:** First Amendment and campaign finance.

**Scope:** Responsive financing and its special reporting provision; the $100 annual political-committee contribution ceiling; and MCCL’s as-applied nonprofit exemption. Preserve the stated boundaries for ordinary disclosure, valid contribution regulation, voluntary public financing and ordinary business-corporation treasury spending.

**Same-term premise control:** Any McIntyre rule governs only after an actual earlier effective simulated decision. The supplied reasoning does not predetermine that decision or another Justice’s position.

**Stone’s disposition.** Affirm the judgment as to the three challenged arrangements, but on narrower grounds. Invalidate Minnesota’s particular responsive-financing trigger and the special reporting provision tied to that trigger; invalidate the $100 annual contribution ceiling applicable to political committees and funds; and preserve as-applied relief for Minnesota Citizens Concerned for Life from the State’s rigid nonprofit eligibility rule. The judgment does not invalidate public campaign financing, ordinary campaign disclosure, reasonable limits on contributions to political committees, or restrictions on general-treasury election spending by ordinary business corporations.

#### Governing principle

Campaign-finance rules must be analyzed according to the relationship they regulate and the burden they actually impose. A contribution to a candidate, a contribution to an intermediary political committee, an expenditure made independently of a candidate, a public subsidy, a disclosure obligation, and the use of a corporation’s general treasury are not constitutionally interchangeable merely because each involves money.

Government may protect against corruption and its appearance, prevent circumvention of otherwise valid contribution limits, require disclosure serving genuine informational or enforcement functions, and establish voluntary systems of public financing. *Buckley* sustains contribution regulation, disclosure, and public financing while giving substantially greater protection to independent expenditures. *California Medical* further establishes that contributions to political committees may be regulated when unrestricted transfers would provide a means of circumventing candidate-contribution limits.

Those interests do not give government authority to suppress political advocacy merely because one speaker possesses greater financial resources or because lawmakers prefer a different distribution of political influence. Regulation must address an identified feature of the electoral process, not the quantity or comparative effectiveness of lawful political expression.

#### I. Responsive public financing

Public financing of political speech is not equivalent to suppressing private political speech. The government does not ordinarily burden Speaker A merely because it finances additional speech by Speaker B. *Buckley* itself sustained public financing as a permissible means of facilitating political participation.

That principle does not make every responsive-financing mechanism constitutional. When the government makes the amount of an independent expenditure the automatic measure of a financial response benefiting the opposed candidate, courts should examine the actual operation of the mechanism rather than apply a categorical rule in either direction.

The relevant questions are whether participation in the public-financing system remains genuinely voluntary; whether the independent speaker remains legally free to spend; whether the governmental response is bounded rather than indefinitely escalating; whether the State has identified a legitimate programmatic objective other than equalizing political influence; and whether the record demonstrates a substantial relationship between the trigger and that objective.

Minnesota’s arrangement fails that inquiry on the record presented. An independent expenditure exceeding $100 automatically increased the affected candidate’s spending authority and, for an eligible candidate, produced an additional public subsidy equal to one-half of the independent expenditure. Yet participation in Minnesota’s public-financing system was already extraordinarily high before the challenged mechanism existed: approximately 96 percent in 1990 and 97 percent of legislative candidates in 1992. The record therefore did not substantiate the claimed need for this mechanism to preserve candidate participation. The statutory timing provisions also created serious implementation problems because entitlement to some subsidies depended on electoral results that could not be established within the prescribed payment period.

The defect is therefore not that public funds answer private speech. It is that this particular automatic and potentially escalating response was insufficiently connected to the programmatic justification offered for it.

**Administrable rule.** A responsive public-financing provision is not unconstitutional merely because independent political spending activates it. Courts examine the actual legal burden on the independent speaker, the voluntariness and boundaries of the public-financing arrangement, and the demonstrated relationship between the trigger and an identified program purpose. Equalization of competing speakers’ political influence is not itself a sufficient justification.

This holding leaves legislatures substantial room to finance political participation, including systems that adjust public benefits as electoral circumstances change. It does not authorize government to use public financing primarily as a device for neutralizing disfavored independent advocacy.

#### II. The linked reporting obligation

The special reporting requirement must be separated from campaign disclosure generally.

Disclosure ordinarily serves independent constitutional purposes. *Buckley* recognized informational, anticorruption, and enforcement interests supporting campaign-finance disclosure while preserving protection where disclosure creates sufficiently serious associational burdens. Nothing in this judgment disturbs Minnesota’s ordinary reporting requirements for independent expenditures.

The challenged special reporting provision is different. Minnesota already required reporting of campaign expenditures through other provisions, while subdivision 6b existed specifically to provide the information necessary to operate the responsive-financing trigger. The Eighth Circuit found that the special provision was directed to implementing that mechanism and was not independently necessary once the trigger fell.

**Administrable rule.** Disclosure is evaluated according to the informational, anticorruption, source-identification, or enforcement function it independently performs. Invalidating a substantive campaign-finance mechanism does not invalidate ordinary disclosure concerning the same speech. But an additional reporting obligation whose material function is solely to administer an invalid mechanism cannot survive merely under the general label of disclosure.

The special reporting obligation therefore falls with the responsive-financing arrangement. Minnesota’s ordinary independent-expenditure disclosure rules remain unaffected.

#### III. The $100 political-committee contribution limit

The Court should reject any categorical proposition that contributions to a political committee present no corruption or circumvention concern simply because the committee itself exercises no governmental power.

*California Medical* establishes otherwise. Contributions to an intermediary political committee may be limited when the committee can serve as a route around valid restrictions on contributions to candidates. The constitutional inquiry follows the function of the money, not merely the formal identity of its immediate recipient.

Minnesota nevertheless goes substantially further. Its $100 annual ceiling applies broadly to contributions received by political committees and political funds without adequately distinguishing committees that channel resources toward candidates from organizations principally engaged in independent advocacy. The amount is also exceptionally restrictive in practical operation. The undisputed record indicated that between one-quarter and one-third of MCCL-CSPC’s contributions in the most recent election cycle exceeded $100.

The Constitution does not establish a fixed minimum dollar amount for political contributions, nor does *Buckley’s* historical $1,000 figure become a permanent constitutional safe harbor. The question is functional: whether the State has drawn its limitation closely enough to an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism while leaving individuals a meaningful opportunity to associate and pool resources for political activity.

Minnesota has not shown why an across-the-board $100 annual ceiling is necessary to prevent circumvention or improper candidate dependence. A State may regulate transfers through committees where the structure creates a realistic route around candidate-contribution limits, but it may not invoke “special-interest money” at a high level of generality and impose a restriction so low that ordinary collective political activity is materially impaired.

**Administrable rule.** For contributions to political committees, courts should identify the committee’s legally permitted relationship with candidates, the route by which unrestricted contributions could produce corruption or circumvent candidate limits, the practical magnitude of the challenged ceiling, and whether the limit leaves meaningful collective political association available. No single dollar figure is constitutionally required.

The $100 ceiling therefore cannot stand. The decision leaves open materially higher limits and more carefully designed restrictions directed to candidate contributions, coordination, conduit arrangements, or other demonstrated circumvention mechanisms.

#### IV. Independent expenditures by the nonprofit organization

The nonprofit issue requires a different inquiry again.

*MCFL* did not transform three descriptive facts about one organization into immutable constitutional elements. Its analysis focused on why the organization differed from an ordinary business corporation: its resources reflected political support rather than commercial accumulation, it lacked shareholders or other equity owners whose economic interests could be used to finance political messages, and it was not materially functioning as a conduit for business-corporation or union treasury funds.

At the same time, *Austin* remains controlling law. Government may distinguish an ordinary corporation using treasury wealth accumulated through the advantages of the corporate economic form from a voluntary political association, while permitting political spending through a segregated political fund. Nothing in today’s decision holds that corporate identity is constitutionally irrelevant or that every corporation possesses an unrestricted right to make general-treasury independent expenditures.

Minnesota may therefore use the characteristics identified in *MCFL* as an administratively convenient safe harbor. It may not, however, treat every factual feature of *MCFL* as an absolute condition when an organization outside the literal wording presents the same constitutionally relevant characteristics.

MCCL’s incidental business activity generated minimal income associated with its political operations, and the State did not dispute that its corporate contributions were insignificant. Minnesota nevertheless denied the exemption because MCCL conducted some business activity and lacked a formal policy prohibiting significant corporate contributions. The Eighth Circuit correctly recognized that those formal departures did not establish the concerns that justified regulation of ordinary corporate treasury spending.

**Administrable rule.** A legislature may establish objective conditions providing an automatic nonprofit safe harbor. An organization falling outside that safe harbor may obtain as-applied protection by showing that it functions materially as a voluntary political association rather than as a vehicle for deploying economically accumulated or conduit corporate wealth. Relevant considerations are whether the organization has equity owners or economic claimants, whether substantial commercial activity generates its political resources, whether business corporations or unions materially finance its treasury, and whether the resources used for political advocacy substantially reflect voluntary political support.

Minor incidental revenue or the absence of a particular formal corporate policy does not alone determine constitutional status. Conversely, incorporation as a nonprofit does not immunize an organization materially financed or controlled by commercial interests.

On the undisputed record, MCCL falls on the protected side of that distinction. Section 211B.15 therefore cannot constitutionally be applied to prohibit its independent expenditures on the ground that it fails Minnesota’s literal statutory checklist.

#### Boundary of the decision

The controlling principle is not equality of political spending. It is preservation of a political process in which government may regulate identifiable corruption, circumvention, compelled economic support, source opacity, and other demonstrated mechanisms without assuming authority to decide how much political influence lawful speakers should possess.

Government may regulate the pathways by which money becomes candidate dependence or evades lawful limits. It may identify the sources of electoral spending. It may finance additional participation. It may distinguish voluntary political resources from economically accumulated corporate treasury resources under the governing precedents. But when regulation materially limits independent advocacy or collective political association, the government must identify the mechanism that creates the legitimate regulatory interest and fit its rule to that mechanism.

**Judgment:** The judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed. The responsive-financing provision and its specially linked reporting requirement remain unenforceable; the $100 political-committee contribution ceiling remains invalid; and MCCL is entitled to as-applied protection from the challenged nonprofit restriction. The Court does not disturb ordinary disclosure requirements, reasonable political-committee contribution limits addressing corruption or circumvention, voluntary public financing, or the continued application of *Austin* outside the *MCFL*-type nonprofit context.

## 67. Kelley v. Board of Trustees of the University of Illinois +

<!-- BEGIN_SECTION I -->

### SECTION II — STONE POSITION SUPPLEMENT

#### Position and scope

**Approval Status:** USER-SUPPLIED POSITION. The supplied disposition, reasoning and express alternatives are recorded as preparation instructions. No Court judgment, vote, rationale or coalition is created by this supplement.

**Build:** Equal opportunity and remedies.

#### Case-specific reasoning

Title IX permits institutions to address unequal athletic opportunity in an overall program. It does not require maintaining every existing men's team, nor does proportionality become a universal command to cut men. The actual budget and participation circumstances matter. Sex-conscious restructuring receives the established important-objective/substantial-relationship inquiry. It does not depend on the separate analysis governing governmental racial treatment and does not resolve whether any imaginable team elimination would be lawful.

#### Disposition, application and preserved limits

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** AFFIRM the judgment of the Seventh Circuit.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The judgment for the University remains in force. No separate remedial instruction is required.

#### Issue 1: Title IX participation opportunities

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory claim and rejects the premise that Title IX guarantees continuation of a particular men’s team.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Title IX protects equal participation opportunity, not a vested right to the continued existence of a particular varsity team. For selection of sports and participation opportunities, the institution is evaluated across the athletic program rather than through sport-for-sport symmetry. Substantial proportionality may be relevant to compliance, but it is not a quota and is not the exclusive route. Title IX does not itself require elimination of men’s teams, and a university may add, eliminate, or restructure teams for legitimate fiscal and programmatic reasons so long as its participation opportunities satisfy the statute.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Title IX does not entitle athletes to preservation of a particular intercollegiate team. Participation opportunities are evaluated across the athletic program rather than by requiring a men’s and women’s counterpart in every sport, and proportionality is neither a rigid quota nor the exclusive means of compliance.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The loss of men’s swimming is a concrete injury to its athletes, but the statute does not convert that injury alone into sex discrimination. The University administered a broader program in which women remained materially underrepresented in participation opportunities. Retaining women’s swimming while eliminating men’s swimming did not itself establish that the program denied men equal athletic opportunity. The plaintiffs identify no statutory rule requiring sport-for-sport symmetry or continuation of every existing men’s team.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** This holding concerns participation opportunities and selection of sports. It does not allow inequality in facilities, coaching, equipment, scheduling, scholarships, or another separately regulated component to be offset by participation elsewhere. Nor does it approve a quota, make proportionality exclusive, or require an institution to reduce men’s opportunities.

#### Issue 2: Equal protection and sex-conscious athletic restructuring

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional claim because a public university’s express sex-conscious treatment is state action.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Correcting a demonstrated inequality in athletic participation opportunity is an important governmental objective. Compliance with Title IX confirms the University’s lawful remedial responsibility but is not an independent constitutional justification. The University must show a substantial relationship between the sex-conscious choice and the documented inequality. Mere numerical imbalance is insufficient by itself. The record must show a meaningful disparity, a genuine fiscal or programmatic restructuring, and a real connection between preserving the underrepresented sex’s opportunities and correcting or avoiding continuation of that inequality.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A public university may consider sex when restructuring a financially constrained athletic program to avoid perpetuating a demonstrated inequality in participation opportunities, but only when the sex-conscious choice is substantially related to that remedial objective. Numerical disparity alone is insufficient, and the classification may not rest on stereotype, pretext, or a measure materially broader than the demonstrated remedial need.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Women were approximately 44 percent of the undergraduate population but only 23.4 percent of intercollegiate athletes. The University faced a substantial athletic-department deficit and eliminated several programs rather than adopting a general rule that men were ineligible for athletics. Preserving women’s swimming while women remained markedly underrepresented was substantially related to avoiding further contraction of their already unequal participation opportunity. The decision did not rest on an assumption that men or women were unsuited to swimming and did not use exact numerical parity as an automatic command.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The principle is symmetrical and applies to whichever sex is demonstrably underrepresented. This case does not decide classifications based on sexual orientation, gender identity, transgender status, or an individual’s eligibility for a sex-designated team.

- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The lower courts shall determine, without treating omission from the rental agreement as categorically dispositive, whether Wellons possessed the vehicle with the renter’s permission and without theft or fraud; whether he owned or lawfully controlled the searched luggage; whether the vehicle was lawfully seized or impounded; and whether the dog sniff, automobile exception, inventory-search doctrine, consent, or another warrant exception supports admission of the evidence. #### Issue 1: Privacy in the rental vehicle

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the categorical legal rule and remands its fact-dependent application.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Omission from a private rental contract is relevant because it bears on lawful possession and the owner’s allocation of control, but it is not a categorical constitutional forfeiture. A driver who possesses the car with the renter’s permission, exercises control, and is neither a thief nor a fraudulent possessor may have a legitimate expectation of privacy despite violating a contractual driver restriction.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A driver’s absence from a rental agreement does not by itself defeat a Fourth Amendment expectation of privacy when the driver possesses and controls the vehicle with the authorized renter’s permission and without theft, fraud, or comparable wrongful possession.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The Fourth Circuit converted one private contractual term into a universal constitutional rule. The Amendment asks about the actual relationship to the place searched. Permission from the renter, possession of the keys, practical control, the ability to exclude strangers, and the absence of criminal acquisition all bear on that relationship. The rental company’s retained contractual rights remain relevant, especially if it revoked possession or requested lawful repossession before the search, but the record must be assessed rather than ended by a name missing from a form.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not hold that a thief, carjacker, person using fraud to obtain the vehicle, or driver continuing after effective repossession has a protected expectation. Nor does renter permission automatically establish privacy when the full circumstances show no lawful or socially recognized control.

#### Issue 2: Privacy in personal luggage within the vehicle

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the distinct container question and rejects the Fourth Circuit’s derivative categorical rule.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Privacy in personal luggage depends on ownership, possession, control, and the steps taken to preserve it as private. Even if Wellons ultimately lacked a protected interest in the vehicle, that conclusion would not automatically extinguish his independent interest in a closed personal bag.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A person’s lack of a legitimate expectation of privacy in a vehicle does not by itself eliminate a legitimate expectation of privacy in that person’s closed luggage found within the vehicle.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The lower court treated the car as swallowing every container inside it. Fourth Amendment interests do not merge that way. The government may search a container under the automobile exception when probable cause lawfully reaches it, or under another valid exception, but the person may still challenge the legal authority for that search based on the person’s own privacy in the container.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not decide ownership of each bag, whether Wellons disclaimed an item, whether probable cause extended to all containers, or whether a standardized inventory independently authorized opening them.

#### Issue 3: Warrant exceptions and remedy

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone does not resolve factual and doctrinal applications the categorical ruling prevented the lower courts from addressing.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Once Wellons’s possible privacy interests are recognized, the government remains free to establish lawful impoundment, probable cause, consent, inventory authority, or another exception. The proper disposition is vacatur and remand, not automatic suppression.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** When a lower court denies a Fourth Amendment claim solely for lack of a protected privacy interest under an erroneous categorical rule, the case should be remanded for determination of any preserved warrant exception and the appropriate suppression consequence.
