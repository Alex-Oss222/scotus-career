## 2. Posture and the components requiring action

Kansas alleges that Colorado's post-Compact groundwater development and operation of the Winter Water Storage Program (WWSP) and Trinidad Project materially depleted waters protected by the Compact. Colorado disputes proof, asserts defenses, and brings Lake McKinney and Kansas well counterclaims. The United States intervened concerning federal reservoir operations. Trial before Special Master Arthur Littleworth occupied 141 days from September 1990 through December 1992. A January 2, 1990 order separated remedy proceedings while allowing remedial/economic evidence necessary to liability, defenses, and counterclaims. The present report therefore does not supply a final remedial decree.

The Master's six substantive recommendations are: find a violation from post-Compact Colorado well pumping; find Kansas failed to prove a WWSP violation; confirm the earlier partial summary judgment rejecting a separate WWSP prior-approval requirement; dismiss the Trinidad claim; dismiss the Lake McKinney counterclaim; and dismiss the Kansas well counterclaim. If a violation is found, he recommends further proceedings concerning remedy. Report pp. 336–337; Volume III contains the earlier interlocutory analyses tendered for confirmation.

Seven exceptions require distinct treatment: Colorado's four concern laches, pre-Compact pumping, benefits of the 1980 Operating Plan, and the standard of proof; Kansas's three concern Trinidad Operating Principles, WWSP depletion, and usable-water methodology. The prior-approval WWSP ruling and the two counterclaim recommendations are unexcepted components, but still require identified grounds and disposition. The absence of an exception does not make every premise of those analyses a reusable rule.

## 3. Compact text and qualifications that must remain separate

The agreement was signed December 14, 1948; Congress consented May 31, 1949. Article I apportions waters and John Martin conservation benefits; it does not establish a fixed division of every flow into a Colorado 60 percent and Kansas 40 percent share.

Article III-B defines the covered waters as waters originating in the Arkansas's natural drainage basin, including tributaries, upstream of the Stateline, **excluding imports from other basins**. Article IV-A limits the Compact to that definition. Article IV-B leaves outside its concern New Mexico's or its citizens' rights to use Trinchera Creek or other Purgatoire tributaries in New Mexico. Article III-C identifies official Stateline gaging flows; VI-B counts Frontier Canal and similarly situated canal flows across the line and gives Kansas exclusive administration of Frontier's Colorado headworks for Kansas irrigation. These qualifications cannot be replaced by an assumption that every molecule flowing in the channel is apportioned on the same basis.

The operative Article IV-D states:

> This Compact is not intended to impede or prevent future beneficial development of the Arkansas River basin in Colorado and Kansas by Federal or State agencies, by private enterprise, or by combinations thereof, which may involve construction of dams, reservoir, and other works for the purposes of water utilization and control, as well as the improved or prolonged functioning of existing works: Provided, that the waters of the Arkansas River, as defined in Article III, shall not be materially depleted in usable quantity or availability for use to the water users in Colorado and Kansas under this Compact by such future development or construction.

Article IV-C separately recognizes Corps operation for authorized purposes: flood-control releases may occur at times and rates necessary or advisable without regard to either State's ditch capacities or requirements; maintenance may require conservation-pool releases or prohibit storage until completed; flood operations may temporarily use conservation storage. These are actual textual qualifications, not a general immunity for all federal or state reservoir operations.

Article V's ordinary John Martin regime has separate triggers:

- **Winter, November 1–March 31 (A):** entering water is stored up to available conservation capacity; Colorado may demand river-flow releases up to 100 c.f.s., used without avoidable waste.
- **Summer, April 1–October 31 (B):** the same storage rule applies except during the decreed-priority operations in F and G. Colorado may demand river-flow equivalents up to 500 c.f.s.; Kansas may demand the portion between 500 and 750 c.f.s. irrespective of Colorado's demand.
- **Stored-water releases (C):** either or both States may demand releases during summer. The ordinary separate limits are Colorado 750 and Kansas 500 c.f.s., combined 1,250; the Administration may authorize increases for extraordinary conditions. When conservation storage falls below 20,000 acre-feet, the limits are 600, 400, and 1,000 respectively.
- **Upstream calls (D):** A–C releases impose no upstream Colorado call except during the F–G decreed-priority regime.
- **Use, delivery, accounting (E):** simultaneous river-flow and stored-water releases are possible; water must be applied promptly to beneficial use **unless the Administration authorizes downstream storage**. Colorado releases are measured near the dam; Kansas's entitlement is satisfied by an equivalent in Stateline flow. The Administration determines appropriate travel-time allowances. There is no accumulation of credits or debits. Operations follow Article VIII procedures.
- **Exhaustion and priority (F–G):** an Administration finding that conservation storage will or may be exhausted within fourteen days triggers notice specifying a commencement day within that interval. Changed conditions can justify cancellation or modification. Colorado then administers District 67 and upstream decrees by relative priorities as before John Martin, until an Administration finding and notice that conservation water is again available. Except as these and other Compact provisions control, upstream users are unaffected by District 67 decrees while conservation water is available. Outside priority operation, District 67 follows filed distribution agreements, or its relative decrees if none. During priority operation Kansas has no share of river flow entering John Martin, but Colorado-origin water actually crossing the Stateline is apportioned to Kansas.
- **Transfers and enlarged ditch rights (H):** if usable quantity and availability in District 67 and Kansas would thereby be materially depleted or adversely affected, District 67 decreed rights may not be transferred to another Colorado district or upstream diversion/use, and District 67 or Stateline-to-Garden-City ditch diversion rights may not exceed present totals, without Administration findings that no such depletion or adverse effect will result. Notice follows the applicable State's timing and manner for legal proceedings. This distinct provision cannot simply be transposed into a guarantee for every post-Compact increase in pumping.

Article VI-A preserves Kansas jurisdiction over its originating waters and waters crossing into Kansas. Its protection of Colorado administration, distribution, diversion, and beneficial use begins **“Except as otherwise provided”**; state decrees do not answer the interstate issue merely by existing.

Article VIII-B authorizes bylaws, rules, and regulations **consistent with the Compact**, procedures, and implementation functions; procedures involving John Martin operation require the District Engineer's approval. VIII-D gives each State one vote, requires unanimity for Administration action, and allows arbitration after a divided vote **only upon a subsequent unanimous referral**. It is not an automatic tie breaker.

VIII-G requires cooperative collection and exchange of relevant facts, data from state officials on request, and designation of necessary gages. It permits required ditch measuring devices, at users' expense, if the Administration deems them necessary. This is not an enacted universal metering or preservation obligation for every well. VIII-H says violations or prejudicial actions brought to the Administration's attention **shall** be promptly investigated; when deemed advisable after investigation, the Administration **may** report findings and recommendations to the state official for appropriate action. The United States brief's obligatory-report paraphrase at p. 7 is not the text. VIII-I makes Administration factual findings prima facie evidence, expressly **not conclusive**.

Article IX-A and consent Act §2 reserve federal sovereignty, rights, and jurisdiction over the area and waters. The Chief of Engineers is **authorized** to operate John Martin conservation features conformably with the Compact, subject to exceptions the Chief and the Administration **jointly approve**. Neither “authorized” nor the joint-exception mechanism may be omitted. IX-B permits modification or termination by unanimous action of the States while preserving rights already established or recognized.

## 4. Colorado exception: proof and the role of the Master

**Source path:** Report pp. 65–70, 228–337; Colorado pp. 91–99; United States pp. 32–36; actual Nebraska and Daubert entries in the supplement; Colorado v. New Mexico, 467 U.S. 310, 315–323.

The Master uses preponderance because Kansas seeks enforcement of an existing apportionment, rather than a new equitable division. He treats enforcement of a Compact as analogous to enforcement of a decree. Colorado and the United States answer that sovereign status and the risk of an erroneous interstate finding can require clear and convincing proof of disputed facts even where no new apportionment is requested. The United States expressly distinguishes the absence of a separate new-injury requirement from the quantum needed to establish contested violation facts.

Actual Nebraska (1993) holds that enforcement of an existing decree does not require the showing of new injury needed for a new allocation. That distinction is material, but does not itself decide every contested-fact burden under this Compact. Nor does the 1987 Texas description of a compact as a contract necessarily settle the quantum. Conversely, the clear-and-convincing analysis in the 1984 Colorado case concerns a proposed diversion from a fully appropriated stream; its allocation of uncertainty to the proponent of change does not decide Compact enforcement by analogy alone.

One legally available path resolves the quantum expressly after comparing those functions. Another reserves the general question if the pumping evidence satisfies either standard and WWSP fails even preponderance. The latter requires independent, component-specific evaluation of the record; it cannot be accomplished by restating the Master's recommendation. The Master identifies substantial pumping depletion under materially different expert methods, while treating WWSP's much smaller signal as uncertain. Any selected path must distinguish burden of production, ultimate persuasion, and the required degree of conviction.

The Court bears independent responsibility for the result while respecting the Master's findings and opportunity to hear technical testimony. Colorado v. New Mexico describes respect and a tacit presumption of correctness; it does not turn the Master into a lower court whose every finding is mechanically protected by Rule 52. Stevens's separate writing in that case supplies a stronger practical caution against replacing credibility and trial expertise with a cold-record reconstruction. The present packet identifies both positions without deciding a present Justice's commitment.

## 5. Colorado exception: laches and temporal relief

**Source path:** Report pp. 147–170; Colorado pp. 24–63; Illinois v. Kentucky, 500 U.S. 380, 388; Texas v. New Mexico, 482 U.S. 124, 128–133.

The Master's rejection is not a categorical holding that laches can never operate between States. He accepts that inexcusable delay can eventually make relief inconsistent with fundamental justice, then finds neither the required delay nor causal prejudice established here. Colorado's exception seeks protection against monetary liability for periods before Kansas initiated the Administration process in March 1985; it expressly does not seek to extinguish prospective compliance. Earlier, differently framed defenses in the report must not expand the exception into a complete bar.

Colorado identifies Kansas's knowledge of increasing wells, 1950s and later USGS reports, Administration discussions, and communications involving Corrigan and DeKeyser as inquiry notice. It argues that delayed litigation lost pumping records and testimony and allowed substantial development and growing historical liability. Kansas distinguishes knowledge of wells and declining gross flows from knowledge of **material depletion in usable quantity or availability**, given drought, changing river uses, John Martin operation, and Colorado's emerging regulation. The Master accepts that distinction and considers early reliance on Colorado administration reasonable. Colorado disputes the evidentiary support for that inference and says the later modeling difficulty arose from the delay itself.

Prejudice requires causal attention. Evidence that old data do not exist does not alone prove timely notice would have caused their collection or preservation. The death of a witness matters to what relevant evidence is actually lost, rather than simply to elapsed time. Farmers' reliance on Colorado law and economics differs from reliance on Kansas silence, though Colorado's asserted burden of accumulated state liability must also be addressed. No party's need for technical reconstruction eliminates these factual disputes.

Illinois says laches is generally inapplicable against a State in the course of rejecting a boundary claim on the proof of prescription/acquiescence. It does not decide an absolute Compact rule. Texas rejects good-faith uncertainty as a complete defense to past Compact performance and recognizes remedial flexibility; it likewise does not establish that every equitable consideration is irrelevant to remedy. The Court can reject this exception on the factual showing without announcing an absolute rule. A partial temporal restriction instead requires its legal source, triggering notice, causal prejudice, and exact affected relief. No automatic limitations period or final damages cutoff has been established. Equities concerning the eventual form of relief remain distinct from this exception.

## 6. Colorado exception: protected pre-Compact pumping

**Source path:** Report Volume II pp. 182–219; Colorado pp. 64–84; Kansas's arguments and exhibits as reproduced in the report and Kansas appendix.

Both sides recognize protected pre-Compact use; disagreement concerns its definition and amount. Kansas proposed approximately 11,000 acre-feet from wet 1948. Colorado proposed a varying entitlement associated with later decrees, averaging 49,275 acre-feet, and an alternative reconstruction of about 36,837 acre-feet for dry 1940. The Master selects 15,000 acre-feet, the highest use during the negotiating years shown by the accepted nearer-period estimates. That is a baseline in the liability comparison, not a newly decreed absolute annual pumping cap or a final damages figure.

The legal choice is between protecting established actual use under IV-D and permitting subsequent exercise of previously appropriated rights as quantified in later state decrees. Colorado stresses preexisting appropriation, normal climatic variability, Article VI administration, treatment of ditch rights, and state practice. The Master relies on VI's express qualification and IV-D's inclusion of improved or prolonged existing works: retrospective state adjudication cannot itself enlarge the interstate entitlement. Rejecting Colorado's decreed-quantity rule does not prove Kansas's single wet-year figure; the two steps must remain separate.

The factual challenge also stands independently. Multiple USGS publications relied on the same Hurr underlying estimates; five publications are not five independent measurements. The precise original calculation materials were unavailable, and Major's confidence in agency work did not replace personal reconstruction. Colorado's power-use and acreage alternative, however, had coverage and nonelectric-pumping difficulties, and disputed assumptions about old pumping efficiency and later equipment. The report considered surficial wells, small wells, and climate adjustments rather than declaring them legally irrelevant. The opposing paths require weighing these specific evidentiary limits. Neither institutional publication nor the existence of a later decree mechanically supplies the missing historical volume.

For aggregate post-Compact pumping, the Master considered power billing ledgers, nonelectric proportions, pump efficiency, and corrections rather than simply accepting Kansas's original demand. The exact depletion comparison then requires river modeling and usable-water analysis. Pumping volume cannot be equated directly with Stateline loss.

## 7. Colorado exception: the 1980 Operating Plan

**Source path:** Report pp. 171–181, 258–260; Report Appendix App. 107–117; Colorado pp. 85–91.

The Plan changes the practical release regime through separate conservation accounts, normally 60 percent Colorado and 40 percent Kansas. Those percentages concern designated conservation storage; they do not divide all river water or imported supplies. It includes other-water accounting, a 35 percent deduction addressing transit losses with priority treatment of deficits and a 1,700 acre-foot Kansas transit reserve, and provisions allocating excess amounts 11 percent to Kansas and 24 percent to Colorado. The Master's shorthand that Colorado receives 24/35 of surplus is not the complete rule. Evaporation, spills, and account conditions matter. Annual notice by February 1 can terminate the Plan March 31, with existing water governed through November 1. Section VI preserves interested parties' ability to object and represent their interests in Administration or court controversies.

Colorado does **not** except to rejection of a complete settlement or bar. It seeks credit for asserted benefits or avoided transit losses, including losses connected with wells, estimating a partial benefit rather than complete compensation. The Master treats the Plan as a separately bargained exchange: Colorado surrendered winter river-flow demands, Kansas surrendered its summer river-flow entitlement, and both obtained more useful timing. He finds no agreement to discharge the well claim or purchase a general offset against later liability. Colorado answers that practical additional water reduces actual injury regardless of whether the parties intended a release.

Two distinct questions must therefore remain separate. A free-standing legal credit for independently exchanged Plan benefits differs from correctly including actual Plan operation in hydrologic comparisons. Denial of the first does not authorize a hypothetical river model to erase the Plan from years when it operated. The report expressly recognizes that point and discusses changed model output. Likewise, imported water is excluded from the apportioned source, but actual imported return flows can reduce actual loss. Crediting those returns does not create an entitlement to future imports. No general bank of legal credits contrary to Article V-E(5), and no final numerical offset, follows automatically.

## 8. Kansas exception: Trinidad Operating Principles

**Source path:** Report pp. 373–435; Kansas pp. 6–20 and appendix; United States pp. 16–21 and n. 8.

The Trinidad Project was authorized in 1958; the Administration unanimously approved Operating Principles in 1967. Kansas argues that their mandatory language, approval history, protective purpose, and the Administration's implementation authority make their violation independently enforceable as a Compact violation. Its factual targets include carrying the Model right's remaining 20,000 acre-foot storage into a 39,000 acre-foot joint-use pool and winter storage for additional ditches. The Master's dismissal analysis assumes the complained-of inconsistency with the Principles for purposes of the motion; that assumption must not be reported as an adjudicated violation of every Principle.

The Master reads the Principles as cooperative project arrangements intended to protect IV-D, not an amendment or formally adopted rule creating an independent Compact command. VIII-B permits consistent implementation but does not confer unlimited power to enlarge the Compact. The United States supports that distinction. Kansas's strongest answer is that consistent administration need not be a formal amendment, particularly where the States and federal operators accepted specific protections; the legal significance of unanimous adoption cannot be dismissed merely because the document is called “Principles.” The Court would need to identify which authority made which undertaking enforceable and at what level.

On the Master's IV-D analysis, the appropriate comparison is actual operation **with and without the project**, not actual operation against a hypothetical project operating perfectly under the Principles. Kansas's historical Bureau studies and 1979–1984 shortage analysis are relevant but address different assumptions and time scales. Predicted average project benefits can answer a depletion claim only insofar as they are actually available and usable when needed; deviation from a study assumption does not itself prove aggregate Compact injury. Colorado's November 6, 1988 direction stopping the disputed practices pending amendment or court action is not a concession of historical Compact depletion or perpetual immunity from a later claim.

A path sustaining dismissal on the presented Compact theory must preserve the Master's express reservation of any independent contractual or third-party-beneficiary claim, Report p. 408 n. 6, and the unresolved broader question of an action compelling performance of Administration duties, p. 429. It cannot hold that no undertaking in the Principles is enforceable by anyone in any forum. A contrary path accepting independent Compact enforceability still must specify the provision, legally operative adoption, breach, and appropriate further proceedings. The present liability-stage report supplies no final Trinidad damages award.

## 9. Kansas exception: WWSP material depletion

**Source path:** Report pp. 228–263, 306–337; Kansas pp. 20–35 and appendix; United States pp. 22–27. This is distinct from the unexcepted prior-approval issue below.

The WWSP generally stores water from November 15 through March 15 that participating ditches would otherwise divert in winter, making it available later; the tested program began in 1976, with the interruption and participation differences described in the report. A state water decree does not itself establish Compact compliance. The Master finds that Kansas failed to prove material depletion from this program, even by preponderance. He expressly does **not** find that the WWSP causes no depletion.

Kansas relies on revised H-I model comparisons, usability evidence, and conditional results obtained using Colorado proposals. It emphasizes that a model can measure the difference between scenarios more accurately than it predicts absolute flows because common errors cancel. Thus an absolute flow error larger than the claimed effect is not, by itself, a valid refutation of every comparative model. Kansas also challenges the treatment of calculated accretions, particularly gains assigned to flood periods when Kansas could not use them, and seeks either recognition of the claim or further consideration of that issue.

The Master's criticism is more specific. Two hypothetical runs need not share the same errors, and winter evaporation and storage assumptions can alter those errors asymmetrically. He identifies substantial monthly and annual errors, storage overprediction, modeled spills around 255,000 acre-feet against measured spills around 87,000, and approximately 31,000 of 37,000 acre-feet of modeled accretions concentrated in May–June 1985. Timing errors might move gains from a usable period into a flood period, or the gains might actually be unavailable; neither alternative can be assumed. The report also discusses evaporation methods and the lack of calibration for particular changes. These concerns support a fact-specific sufficiency inquiry, not an automatic rule rejecting hydrologic models whenever an effect is smaller than absolute calibration error.

Kansas's approximately 53,000 total and 40,000 usable acre-foot assertions, and an approximately 27,000 figure associated with a conditional HCI computation, are contested estimates. A figure elicited under disputed assumptions is not an independent concession that all underlying assumptions or usability conclusions are correct. Conversely, a model's sufficient reliability for the much larger aggregate pumping effect does not establish its power to resolve this smaller program effect. Daubert admissibility and ultimate persuasive sufficiency remain different questions; the report assumes or accepts admissibility and principally addresses weight.

A path accepting Kansas's exception must explain which comparison is reliable, how actual Plan operation and imported returns enter, how timing and accretions are treated, and whether further factfinding is required. A path rejecting it must address correlated-error cancellation rather than relying exclusively on raw flow-error size. The burden may make an unresolved uncertainty dispositive; control of data alone does not automatically reverse ultimate persuasion or establish spoliation. Article VIII's precise information provisions, rather than an invented universal metering duty, govern any claimed duty. No final WWSP remedial amount is available.

## 10. Kansas exception: measuring usability

**Source path:** Report pp. 291–305; Kansas pp. 35–45 and appendix; United States pp. 27–31.

Article IV-D protects usable quantity and availability, not an undifferentiated annual total. The Master favors the Durbin approach with Larson's modifications over Spronk's more finely timed analysis on this record. Larson uses historical average irrigation usability of 72 percent in summer and 25 percent in winter, plus a 9.9 percent recharge component based on the pre-Compact 1925–1948 period excluding the 1942 flood. Those differ from Durbin's original 78 percent, 24 percent, and 15 percent figures. The underlying method includes monthly and seasonal limits: summer 40,000 and 140,000 acre-feet, winter 7,500 and 40,000. These features must not disappear from a statement that the Master merely applies one percentage to all depletion.

Spronk reconstructs daily full-use conditions near Garden City and applies the resulting portion of usable days to monthly modeled depletion. He considers streamflow thresholds, rain, natural flows, calls and transit, and recharge. The power-plant allowance raises the threshold to 20 c.f.s. for **1970–1973**; otherwise the discussed threshold is 5 c.f.s. The Master regards the reconstructed gage as probably adequate for its relatively coarse purpose. His central objections are the attempted daily precision combined with uncertain monthly model output and attribution of recharge associated with increased Kansas pumping to Colorado. A supposed inherently defective gage is not the full or principal explanation.

Kansas answers that aggregate averages obscure the years of greatest shortage, when pumping and need were greatest, and that daily evidence can reveal usable losses otherwise hidden. Its cumulative bar chart is cumulative, not a series of independent annual amounts. The opposing path asks whether the apparently finer method creates unsupported accuracy or charges Colorado for Kansas's later development. The Master recognizes that a daily approach can be valid in theory; he does not announce a permanent prohibition or universal preference for seasonal averages.

The ultimate record question is how much additional water, absent the challenged Colorado use, Kansas could beneficially have used under the Compact, including supported recharge and actual timing. The dismissal of Colorado's independent well counterclaim does not resolve the separate effect of Kansas pumping on this calculation. Any acceptance, modification, or remand must identify that distinction. The report's aggregate usable estimates are liability evidence; neither a permanent mandatory formula nor final compensable acre-feet follows without further proceedings.

## 11. Unexcepted components

**WWSP prior approval — Report pp. 338–372.** The 1951 Administration resolution recommends obtaining Administration and affected-user approval before additional upstream conservation storage. The Master reads it as policy recommended to the Governors, rather than an amendment or a source of a general veto. The Compact's consistent-rule authority and federal reservation do not alone create that veto. The 1944 Flood Control Act provides for state views and recommendations, not the asserted approval power; the 1962 Fryingpan-Arkansas authorization does not enact the claimed condition. The Master excludes affidavits insofar as they offer legal conclusions or unsupported hearsay, and need not reach alternative laches, estoppel, or supersession theories. This narrow partial summary judgment leaves the IV-D actual-depletion obligation in force. His refusal to stay for Administration proceedings rests on reasonable efforts, conflicting state reports, and the unanimity impasse; it is not an absolute rule dispensing with every future administrative step.

**Lake McKinney — Report pp. 436–446.** Article V-E(2)'s prompt-beneficial-use rule and its Administration-authorized downstream-storage exception still matter after the 1980 account system. The Master finds the approximately 3,600 acre-foot, twelve-foot pool necessary for short retention between the inlet and outlet. Observed levels do not establish the water's origin, duration, or unauthorized retention of John Martin releases; the cited 1967 episode is not sufficiently traced. Dismissal for failure of this proof does not authorize all downstream storage or erase the exception's authorization requirement. There is no need to announce a broader burden rule if Colorado's proof fails the less demanding test.

**Kansas well counterclaim — Report pp. 447–459.** Colorado asserts injury through extra John Martin calls and transit-loss/account effects. The Master finds no demonstrated reduction of Colorado's protected supply: after the 1980 Plan Kansas draws on its own 40 percent conservation account; earlier evidence does not establish additional calls attributable to Kansas pumping, and asserted lost surplus is unquantified. The underlying Kansas groundwater and river systems must not be conflated with all Colorado-origin covered waters. This is dismissal of the counterclaim on the presented injury proof, not a general license for Kansas to violate the Compact. Kansas pumping's effect on usable-water estimation remains a separate question.
