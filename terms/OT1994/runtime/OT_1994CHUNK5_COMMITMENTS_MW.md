## 52. California Department of Corrections v. Morales, No. 93-1462

### Statutory rule, record, and entering law

The offense-date rule required: “The board shall hear each case annually thereafter.” The challenged 1981 version of California Penal Code §3041.5(b)(2) provided:

> The board shall hear each case annually thereafter, except the board may schedule the next hearing no later than three years after any hearing at which parole is denied if the prisoner has been convicted, in the same or different proceedings, of more than one offense which involves the taking of a life, and the board finds that it is not reasonable to expect that parole would be granted at a hearing during the following years and states the bases for the finding.

Annual consideration remains the statutory default. The exception permits, rather than requires, postponement. It requires both the multiple-life-taking-offense condition and an individualized prediction addressed to the intervening years, with stated bases. The next hearing must occur no later than three years after the denial; a shorter interval remains permitted. The statute does not itself establish ordinary interim reconsideration on changed circumstances. The later general two-year and later five-year regimes are not this enactment; their conditions cannot be imported into it.

Morales's 1980 offense preceded the amendment. His record includes two murder convictions, a failed parole release, the grave circumstances of the later offense, institutional-programming concerns, and psychiatric assessments. At the initial 1989 hearing the Board denied parole and separately deferred the next hearing for three years. Its postponement statement referred to the particularly cruel offense and the need for longer observation, the prior first-degree murder, a criminal history warranting longer assessment, and psychiatric evaluations requiring further observation, evaluation, or treatment. The statement is evidence of the Board's prediction, not a finding that release before the next hearing was legally impossible. His earliest eligibility date was August 2, 1990. The nineteen-year matrix figure is a guideline from which the regulations permit a justified departure, not a binding minimum foreclosing an earlier date. The claim is loss of consideration, not an established entitlement to release.

In re Jackson, 39 Cal.3d 464 (1985), supplies authoritative state construction of the distinct postponement finding. Reasons must be separately directed to continuing unsuitability during the interval; the facts supporting them may overlap those supporting current unsuitability. Jackson invalidated the inadequate combined statement before it; it did not adjudicate Morales's separately stated reasons. Its own case involved the 1982 two-year interval. Its discussion and footnote addressing the 1981 multiple-offender provision are relevant without conflating the two enactments. Jackson's ordinary administrative/habeas review concerns the initial postponement decision. Its hypothetical discussion of changed circumstances does not establish an enforceable later reopening mechanism. Its federal ex post facto discussion must be evaluated under the subsequent controlling authorities, including Cavanaugh. Its reported aggregate denial rates do not measure this statutory class or Morales's likelihood of release. Its procedural termination after Jackson's death does not become an order for a live hearing in this case.

Actual Cavanaugh v. Roller (November 30, 1993) holds that a retrospective, unqualified replacement of mandatory annual consideration with a two-year interval increases punishment where ordinary parole depends on consideration and no retained ordinary interim review is established. A limited medical route conditioned on life expectancy of one year or less did not replace ordinary review. Neither unchanged initial eligibility and maximum punishment nor release discretion independently answers the loss of a binding meaningful opportunity. The prisoner need not prove an omitted hearing would have produced release. Cavanaugh reserved individualized postponement supported by findings, meaningful interim review, and other materially different mechanisms. Those reservations are not a holding that any one label automatically validates a later law, nor a command that every reserved mechanism must be present together.

Weaver protects the operation of mandatory good-conduct credits despite the absence of a vested release date; more demanding discretionary credits did not replace that lost entitlement. Miller concerns the increased effect of binding sentencing guidelines despite the same statutory maximum, including the removal of findings and review previously necessary for the longer sentence. Collins rejects a free-standing test of any procedural disadvantage, but does not immunize a procedural change that increases punishment in operation. These rules and Cavanaugh, not a bare vested-right theory or Jackson's earlier federal conclusion alone, govern.

The State's reply describes review for merit of inmates' requests for earlier hearings. The validated argument record did not identify a governing rule, and the initial administrative appeal does not establish later reconsideration. No commitment below depends on an established ordinary interim mechanism. The unresolved hearing day (July 15 or July 25, 1989), counterfactual release date, and possible application of later amendments are not supplied by inference. The year and three-year postponement suffice for the admitted claim. There is no basis in this packet to dismiss this admitted merits matter on an invented later mootness event.

## 53. United States v. Williams, No. 94-395

### Statutory components, record, and entering law

The lower judgment, 24 F.3d 1143 (9th Cir. 1994), reverses the district court's dismissal for lack of refund jurisdiction and remands. It is not a judgment awarding Williams a refund. Williams paid to remove federal tax liens asserted against the house she claimed as her property so a sale could close; the taxes had been assessed against her former husband, Rabin, and she was not personally liable for them. She filed an administrative refund claim within the applicable time and received a denial. The asserted coercion concerns the lien's effect on the sale and threatened consequences of failing to close. No levy is established. No substitute-fund agreement is established.

The relevant provisions must be read together and kept distinct:

- **28 U.S.C. §1346(a)(1)** grants concurrent district-court/Court of Federal Claims jurisdiction over any civil action against the United States to recover an internal-revenue tax alleged erroneously or illegally assessed or collected, a penalty allegedly collected without authority, or any sum alleged excessive or in any manner wrongfully collected under the internal-revenue laws. It is an express monetary refund waiver; the issue is its scope.
- **26 U.S.C. §7422(a)** requires a refund claim duly filed according to law and applicable regulations before suit. **Section 7422(b)** permits suit whether or not payment was under protest or duress. Duress is therefore not a universal added statutory prerequisite.
- **Section 6511(a)** prescribes the claim period: for a tax for which the taxpayer must file a return, three years from the return or two years from payment, whichever is later; if no return was filed, two years from payment. The separate stamp-tax clause provides its own three-year period. The contested question is whether “by the taxpayer” restricts claimant identity as the Government argues, not whether Williams missed a deadline.
- **Section 7701(a)(14)** defines taxpayer as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” within the Code's definition rule and its qualification for a distinct expression or manifest incompatibility with statutory intent. Whether that language includes a person subjected to collection through liens on claimed property, or only the legally liable taxpayer for this purpose, is disputed. A payment alone is not a complete answer.
- **Section 2410(a)(1)** permits specified quiet-title litigation over a federal lien. It does not automatically supply a postpayment refund remedy.
- **Section 7426(a)(1)** provides a wrongful-levy action for a person other than the assessed taxpayer with the required interest; this case establishes a lien and payment, not a levy.
- **Section 6325(b)(3)** permits the Secretary to discharge property when a sale agreement provides that proceeds are held as a substitute fund subject to the same lien priority. The Secretary “may” discharge under that mechanism; agreement and a fund are not established here. Sections **7426(a)(3) and (b)(4)** provide the corresponding action and monetary relief concerning an actual substitute fund. They are not a presently existing fund or a guaranteed discharge on these facts. Section **7426(c)** presumes the assessment valid for that statutory action; the same presumption cannot be silently appended to a §1346 action.

The Government's integrated reading of §§6511, 7422, and 7701 is substantial: if only the legally liable taxpayer can file the duly filed claim required for suit, §1346's broad subject-matter wording does not authorize this plaintiff. Its specialized-remedy argument also points to different limits and the conclusive-assessment provision. The Government does not identify an express exclusivity clause barring every other third-party remedy; its position is that no other waiver authorizes this claim. A court rejecting that reading must explain the connected provisions, not decide on sympathy for a remedy. Conversely, choosing that reading does not require a finding that Williams actually could obtain a substitute fund or had sufficient time for a useful quiet-title suit.

Actual Nordic Village (February 25, 1992) requires unequivocal consent for an affirmative monetary remedy. Plausible nonmonetary readings of the old Bankruptcy Code §106(c) precluded such a waiver; general jurisdiction, legislative history, or property labels did not fill the gap. It does not answer the claimant scope of this express tax-refund waiver. Actual United States v. California (April 26, 1993) declined to create a common-law refund right from federal reimbursement of state taxes and refused to revive the contractor's expired derivative claim. It reserved express congressional remedies. Neither precedent supplies a presumption that every Government interpretation, however disconnected from the statutory scheme, defeats an express waiver.

Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (1990), requires reading §1346 with the duly filed claim and §6511 timing requirements; equitable recoupment does not independently create jurisdiction over an untimely refund suit. It did not decide this timely nonassessed-payer question. Its explanation of overpayment includes money exceeding what is properly due and does not depend on a later discovery date. Stevens's dissent supplies his own reasoning about the broad wrongfully-collected language and the pay-first structure, but does not repeal Dalm's binding claim requirement.

Stahmann, 305 U.S. 61 (1938), allowed producers who paid the tax assessed against a ginner to pursue recovery after paying to free their cotton. Its reasoning rejects treating those payments as volunteers' payments simply because assessment ran against another person. The Bankhead Act's intended incidence on producers and the collector-action posture distinguish it from this statutory waiver case. It supports the nonvolunteer analogy; it does not by itself decide §1346/§6511 plaintiff scope, dispense with an administrative claim, or decide the underlying tax's validity.

The merits remain materially disputed. The appellate account of a Government concession, the joint-appendix hearing's descriptions of erroneous collection, and the parties' assertions do not establish an uncontested binding concession of every lien issue; the Government's reply expressly resists that treatment. Williams's purchaser status, adequate consideration, lien validity and priority, precise payment components, and any binding concession must be addressed as necessary below. The differing acquisition dates and amounts in the materials are not silently harmonized. No commitment sets a recovery amount or authorizes Williams to contest every aspect of Rabin's underlying assessment.

**Stevens.** His Dalm dissent directly supports taking the broad wrongfully-collected language and the pay-first structure seriously, while his California join preserves the distinction between a congressional remedy and a newly invented common-law one. Here the claim is timely and invokes express monetary consent. The strongest opposing route is Dalm's requirement to read §1346 with §§6511 and 7422, treating their taxpayer language as a claimant limitation. He accepts the integrated reading but rejects its proposed result: a person from whom the Government obtains the payment by asserting liens on property she claims is her own has a refund claim concerning that collection, and §6511's timing language does not silently remove the waiver for that claim. This does not rest on the nonbinding portion of his Dalm dissent or excuse late claims. Stahmann strengthens the conclusion that payment to free property is not an unsolicited gift, with its distinct statutory incidence acknowledged. He would refuse a supposed holding that all lien issues were conceded or that anyone may pay another's tax and demand a refund. His join requires separating jurisdiction from liability, preserving §7422(b)'s absence of a universal duress condition, and leaving the amount and merits to further proceedings.

**O'Connor.** Her Dalm and Nordic Village joins require an enacted waiver read with its conditions; her California opinion rejects judicial creation of a refund right while leaving express remedies available. Those principles permit this suit because §1346 expressly addresses wrongful collection, not only erroneous assessment, and Williams presents her own payment extracted through asserted liens against claimed property. The strongest barrier is that she is not Rabin's taxpayer and §6511 says a claim is filed “by the taxpayer.” For these connected refund provisions, a person subjected to collection in this manner can make the timely claim concerning the amount obtained from her; absence of personal liability for the assessment does not place that collection beyond the explicit waiver. She would not define every payer as a taxpayer for every Code purpose. The separate third-party mechanisms have real prerequisites: no levy here and no agreed substitute fund. Their existence supplies no unstated exhaustion or exclusive-remedy command. Her support does not depend on whether Williams was told of those alternatives. She would refuse reasoning that relaxes sovereign immunity for hardship alone, treats the Secretary's permission as an obligation, or finds the liens invalid on the jurisdiction record. Affirmance authorizes adjudication, not payment.

**Scalia.** His authorship of the actual Nordic Village opinion and his Dalm join make the connected text decisive. This waiver explicitly permits an action for a sum allegedly wrongfully collected under the revenue laws; unlike old §106(c), its monetary character cannot be avoided by a plausible nonmonetary reading. The strongest contrary construction treats §6511's “by the taxpayer” as a categorical restriction to the personally liable debtor. He reads that provision in the refund scheme as setting the conditions and time for refund claims, not overriding §1346's express collection language for a person made to pay through a lien asserted against her own claimed property. Section 7701 says “subject to” a tax, not “assessed with” it; for these refund provisions he accepts that the actual subjection of claimed property to tax collection supplies the relation, without defining every payer as a taxpayer throughout the Code. This narrow category does not depend on legislative history or a general remedial presumption. No clause supplied makes §7426 exclusive of this action, and its levy/fund conditions cannot be assumed away. Stahmann's treatment of the payments as nonvoluntary is a useful analogy, but its incidence and collector posture prevent making it the waiver. His join requires that the statutory text do the work and that §7422(b) not be replaced by a universal coercion requirement. He would refuse an opinion based merely on fairness, an invented concession, or a claim that he abandons Nordic Village whenever a remedy would be useful. He affirms only jurisdiction, reserving volunteered payments and merits defenses.

**Kennedy.** His Dalm opinion supplies the closest personal starting point: §1346's broad words must be read with the statutory conditions for a duly filed refund claim. His Nordic Village and California joins reinforce Congress's control over the remedy. The strongest affirmance route is the distinction between assessment and collection, coupled with Williams's own payment to remove a lien and the absence of a levy or guaranteed substitute-fund route.
