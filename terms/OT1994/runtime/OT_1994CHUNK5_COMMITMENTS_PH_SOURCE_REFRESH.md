## What the additional sources establish

**Petitioner's brief, printed pp.14–19, especially p.15 n.5.** The brief identifies contempt, obstruction, perjury, false-declaration law, and Rule 11 as means of addressing litigation misconduct. It expressly acknowledges that overlap alone does not preclude prosecution under §1001. Its separate construction argument is that surrounding rules bear on whether Congress included this conduct in the first place. Footnote 5 quotes the Manual through Deffenbaugh as recommending against §1001 prosecution of false statements submitted in federal court proceedings and recommending §§1503 and 1621 instead. That is party-supported evidence of a stated prosecution policy, not an adjudicated statutory rule. The full original Manual provision was not independently recovered in this refresh.

**Government's brief, printed pp.19–21, especially p.20 n.9.** The Government acknowledges the recommendation but characterizes it as guidance for false-affidavit cases. It explains it by reference to the Manual's recognition that several circuits construe judicial coverage more narrowly than Bramblett suggests, and states that prosecution policy is not binding statutory construction. These qualifications matter: the Government does not concede court exclusion, a comprehensive ban on every judicial §1001 prosecution, or the unlawfulness of this prosecution. The two briefs support the common bounded fact of guidance favoring other charges in the described court-statement setting; their differing formulations do not establish identical scope for unsworn pleadings. No exact scope broader than the shared evidence is found.

**Petitioner's brief, printed pp.23–27, read with pp.17–19.** Its strongest point concerns the effect of possible prosecution on legitimate adversarial work: parties and counsel must plead under time constraints, investigate disputed facts, comply with contestable discovery demands, and require opponents to prove their claims. Criminal exposure can alter that conduct even when the eventual defense succeeds. The brief acknowledges that vigorous advocacy does not include knowing falsification. It therefore offers an institutional chilling concern, alongside its disputed analogy between a civil denial and a criminal not-guilty plea. Predicted warnings, referrals, and defensive lawyering are arguments, not proved empirical findings. The contention that subsequent production of documents establishes an innocent misunderstanding does not undo the jury's falsity finding.

**Government's complete response, printed pp.21–34.** It distinguishes formal nonacquiescence and legal argument from knowing factual falsehoods, identifies a disclosure duty as necessary to a concealment charge, and stresses the actual findings here. It argues that good-faith pleading rules do not authorize knowingly false denials, that parallel crimes can apply to unsworn statements, that comparable §1001 protections apply in agency adjudications, and that the proposed administrative/adjudicative line has generated inconsistent results. Its reference to Mezzanatto is checked against the actual January 18 decision: that case rejects categorical invalidation of the specified consensual impeachment waiver while preserving supported individualized challenges and reserved broader uses. It creates no universal finding of prosecutorial good faith and does not decide the institutional consequences of criminalizing litigation statements.

The exact filings, reviewed again in petitioner's pp.2–4, keep the counts distinct. Count V includes the affirmative account of records delivered to the former trustee and the assertion that no subsequent request had been made. Counts VI and VII deny specific location allegations as untrue. They are not converted into fact-free notices of nonacquiescence simply because they appear in an answer. The supplied record treats them as knowing factual falsehoods. This supplement adds no alternative acquittal based on truth, lack of materiality, or innocent mistake.

## Shared legal comparisons after the source refresh

**Precedent and special justification remain separate from first-instance construction.** Bramblett addressed §6 and the statutory history; it cannot be rejected on the factually incorrect assertion that it overlooked them. The exclusion path says its treatment was unsound and identifies what subsequent experience adds: judicial attempts to confine the broad construction, uncertain function boundaries, and the acknowledged enforcement guidance. The retention path says those developments reflect contestable lower-court exceptions, not failure of Bramblett's basic all-branch rule. The disagreement is substantive. Statutory stare decisis has force even when no private contract or property arrangement is identified, and criminal statutes are not categorically exempt from it.

**Reliance has more than one subject.** The recommendation weakens an inference that the Executive has built an indispensable, uniformly exercised enforcement program on using §1001 for litigation statements. It does not eliminate prosecutorial reliance everywhere, establish how often the advice was followed, or prove that Hubbard knew it. Lawful reliance of advocates on procedural rules differs from reliance on permission to lie; those rules themselves require honesty. Neither side gets congressional ratification from silence. The source limits prevent an empirical conclusion that practice has been uniform in either direction.

**Institutional chill is not answered simply by condemning lies.** Knowledge, falsity, and disclosure-duty limits reduce the prospect of convicting lawful advocacy. They do not make the costs of an allegation, investigation, or disputed classification disappear. Conversely, possible chilling cannot by itself defeat clear criminal coverage: perjury, obstruction, and contempt also affect litigation incentives. The legal inquiry is whether the proposed scope is supported by the enacted terms, the relevant prior construction, and their coherent operation—not whether false pleadings are socially desirable. The Government's executive-agency adjudication example must be answered on the entity definition or a defensible statutory distinction; a general claim that all adjudication must be exempt would reach beyond the present paths.

**The manual supplies no standalone defense.** All eight decline a ground that would vacate the convictions merely because charging allegedly conflicted with guidance. This record establishes no enforceable assurance to Hubbard, estoppel, selective-prosecution claim, or prejudicial breach of a procedural entitlement. The manual's descriptive significance does not transform it into law.

**Lenity cannot silently bypass Bramblett.** Those using residual lenity must first state the treatment of the precedent that otherwise supplies the broader construction, and then identify genuine uncertainty remaining after the ordinary tools. Neither competing briefs, circuit disagreement, nor a cautious prosecution policy automatically establishes ambiguity. The operative Ratzlaf and Granderson holdings supply a confined residual rule, not a universal least-punishment command. A decision rejecting one proposed judicial-function exception also does not settle whether a court is an included entity.
