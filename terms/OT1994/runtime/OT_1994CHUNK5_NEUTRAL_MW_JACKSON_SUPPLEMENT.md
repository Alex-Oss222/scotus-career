## Source and complete read

*In re Lawrence A. Jackson*, 39 Cal.3d 464, 703 P.2d 100, Crim. No. 24381 (California Supreme Court, August 8, 1985), was read in full from `tmp/ot1994_chunk5_prior/In_re_Jackson.txt`, including all thirteen footnotes. Primary source: `https://static.case.law/cal-3d/39/html/0464-01.html`; the retrieved HTML is preserved alongside the text. Chief Justice Bird wrote; Mosk, Kaus, Broussard, Reynoso, Grodin, and Lucas concurred.

Jackson directly examined the 1982 amendment authorizing a next hearing within two years, upon the Board's finding that parole could not reasonably be expected at a hearing during the following year and its statement of the bases. Footnote 4 separately identifies the 1981 multiple-life-taking-offenses provision at issue in Morales. Jackson's phrase “two-year postponement” for that provision means postponing the otherwise annual hearing for two additional years, producing a maximum three-year interval after the denial. Its “one-year postponement” under the 1982 provision produces a maximum two-year interval. The two enactments and intervals must remain distinct.

## What this source adds to the neutral framing

### 1. A separately reasoned postponement is a state-law requirement, with an express allowance for overlapping facts

Jackson's Part IV, pages 477–480, authoritatively construes the statement-of-reasons requirement. It considers the 1981 and 1982 amendments' addition of postponement reasons alongside the previously required reasons for refusing to set a parole date. It requires postponement reasons to be **separately stated and specifically directed to that question**. Present unsuitability predicts a public-safety risk; postponement additionally predicts that this risk is likely to continue during the intervening period. Related decisions are not identical decisions.

The court expressly rejects a requirement that the facts supporting the two determinations must be completely different. The same facts can support both; the record must identify why they justify postponement and reflect recognition of the separate additional choice. Thus the released packet's reference to Morales's objection that the grounds overlap must not become a legal rule that duplication itself defeats postponement. The proper issue is whether the separately stated reasons address the additional predictive question, not whether they repeat a fact mentioned in the suitability analysis.

Jackson found the Board's explanation in that case deficient. The Board combined the two decisions in a single statement, recited the legal criteria, and supplied three reasons without showing that it had separately considered postponement. The underlying reasons were a brutal and senseless offense, two prior assaults, and psychiatric evaluations unsupportive of release. That ruling establishes the importance of the distinct explanation; it does not adjudicate the sufficiency of Morales's later, separately stated postponement explanation.

The source describes an administrative directive containing suggested postponement reasons, some paralleling suitability factors, and recommends conformity to its holding. The directive's own full text has not been supplied or independently read. Its description in Jackson is not an additional regulation or evidence that every later Board decision complied.

### 2. The source supports review of the initial decision, not a proved process for reopening on changed circumstances

Jackson sought habeas after exhausting administrative remedies. Part IV explains that reasons let an inmate decide whether to pursue administrative or judicial review and permit meaningful review of the postponement. This is concrete support for a mechanism to challenge the legality of the initial postponement, including whether its explanation satisfies the statute.

Page 475 separately discusses counsel's hypothetical examples of changed medical condition or later cooperation with law enforcement. The court calls those examples extraordinary and hypothetical on its record, and says it is conceivable that the Board could advance the hearing and order immediate release. It does **not** find that a general reopening process exists, identify a rule requiring it, establish a filing or decision timetable, or demonstrate how changed circumstances would produce an ordinary interim suitability hearing.

This narrows, but does not eliminate, the released packet's source limit. It would now be inaccurate to say the sources supply no judicial review of an initial postponement. It remains accurate that a meaningful, generally available route to obtain later reconsideration on new circumstances is unproved. The State's Morales reply assertion about reviewing inmate communications remains an assertion; Jackson does not convert it into an adjudicated practice. A theory that depends on interim reconsideration must still identify its trigger, legal availability, decision duty, timing, and supporting authority, or reserve that issue for further proceedings.

### 3. Jackson's federal reasoning is more qualified than a categorical procedural exception

Part III upheld the 1982 amendment under the state and federal Ex Post Facto Clauses. It treated the change as procedural in its operation because suitability and release criteria remained unchanged, consideration continued at least biennially, reasons and hearing protections constrained postponement, and the effect on actual early release appeared slight. It recognized that the postponement eliminated the possibility of setting a parole date during the intervening period. It did not deny that a hearing can matter to punishment.

The opinion expressly rejected the absence of a vested parole right as a sufficient answer. Footnote 12 says that not every retrospective encroachment on annual review passes as merely procedural; the importance of the opportunity and the facts of each case matter. Jackson also distinguished the loss of the only practical parole opportunity in a short maximum sentence from a reasoned postponement during an indeterminate life sentence. This supplies the State's strongest particularized explanation, not a rule immunizing all parole administration from the Clause.

Jackson's own federal analysis is a state court's view. It predates *Collins*, *Miller*, and the actual controlling *Cavanaugh v. Roller*. Its broader “substantial right” vocabulary and its treatment of the discretionary character of release must therefore be evaluated under that later controlling law. Jackson cannot displace Cavanaugh's holding that the binding loss of an ordinary mandatory opportunity can increase punishment without proof that release would have occurred. Conversely, Cavanaugh expressly reserved individualized postponements supported by findings; Jackson supplies relevant state-law detail for examining that reserved distinction. Nothing in this supplement selects the answer.

### 4. The annual-hearing baseline was expressly assumed on a reasoned legal ground

Jackson committed his offense in September 1961, before a statutory annual-hearing guarantee. The court did not uphold postponement simply because no annual right existed in 1961. It held that the 1977 legislation expressly conferred its benefits on existing inmates and analyzed his claim **as if annual review had existed when he offended**, discussing the Legislature's affirmative extension of those benefits. Thus a distinction based solely on the older offense date would misdescribe Jackson's reasoning.

Morales's asserted annual-hearing baseline was already in force when he committed his 1980 offense. That remains a factual and statutory distinction, but it does not by itself dispose of Jackson's reasoning because the court deliberately conducted its analysis on the annual-review baseline. The federal validity or general reach of Jackson's treatment of later-conferred benefits is not an additional question that this supplement proposes to decide.

### 5. The empirical and release-date evidence has express limits

Jackson quotes two legislative committee analyses for the approximately 90 percent initial and 85 percent subsequent unsuitability rates. These are source-supported figures reported and relied upon by the state court, not an independently reviewed dataset or measurements of the three-year multiple-offender provision. They do not determine the probability that Morales's postponed hearing would affect his custody.

The court also referred to recent petitions by other indeterminate-sentence inmates who had obtained suitability findings but had release dates three and one-half to nineteen years later. It expressly acknowledged that these cases were not a statistically representative sample. It recognized that the record lacked evidence showing how often suitability yields release soon afterward. A description that calls this an established general lag or a finding about Morales's actual release date would exceed the source.

Footnote 10 states that, under the regulations it cites, a suitable inmate's total life term is calculated from a base term, enhancements or other crimes, and appropriate preprison credit. If time served exceeds that total, release is upon time already served; otherwise postconviction credit is awarded and a date is set. This establishes that the opinion did not regard a long delay after every suitability finding as legally inevitable. These regulations concern the setting described in Jackson. They do not by themselves resolve Morales's disputed matrix and credit calculation or establish that he was entitled to immediate release.

The opinion describes a statutory right to counsel at the DSL suitability hearing for life prisoners. Footnote 9 distinguishes a separate hearing applying more favorable old-law criteria under *Stanworth*, for which the cited *DeMond* decision found no statutory counsel entitlement. The distinction must remain if counsel is used as a safeguard: one hearing's entitlement does not automatically govern every different parole proceeding.

### 6. The actual state disposition was abatement

Jackson died while the appeal was pending. Footnote 3 explains that the California Supreme Court nevertheless addressed the recurring questions of public importance through its state-law discretion. The opinion announced its conclusions on the ex post facto issue and the reasons requirement, but its operative disposition declared all proceedings permanently abated and directed the superior court to enter that order. It did not ultimately order another hearing for Jackson or issue a live individualized remand to the Board.

This procedural setting does not erase the California court's announced statutory construction. It does require an accurate description of the disposition and prevents treating the case as proof that interim relief was actually implemented for this inmate. No implication about the present federal controversy's justiciability follows from a different inmate's death or the California court's authority to address recurrent state questions.

## Effect on the competing paths already identified

An analysis distinguishing Cavanaugh now has stronger primary support for California's separate, reviewable predictive-decision requirement. It must still explain why that requirement meaningfully changes the loss of the later ordinary opportunity; initial review cannot silently become interim reconsideration.

An analysis applying Cavanaugh must address the actual separate-reasons protection and Jackson's permitted factual overlap. It cannot dismiss the statute merely because unsuitability and postponement reasons share facts, nor say California authorized postponement without a distinct explanation. It may still examine whether a prediction made at the earlier hearing substitutes for consideration of later developments when ordinary interim review remains unestablished.

A limited factual or legal remand remains a possible path if an essential theory depends on actual interim practice. Jackson specifies what is already known about initial-decision review and what remains missing; it does not mandate a remand or cure that evidentiary gap.
