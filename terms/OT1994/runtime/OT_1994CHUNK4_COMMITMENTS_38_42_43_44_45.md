## 38. Anderson v. Edwards — March 22, 1995

**Disposition:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer reverse the categorical regulatory ground sustaining the injunction and remand for any genuinely preserved independent ground. They reject the equation of grouping eligible applicants into an assistance unit with attribution of unavailable income from an outside, legally nonresponsible person. They do not find that every caretaker resource is available, determine an individual award, or dismiss every possible equitable-treatment/proration objection.

**Operative rule:** Federal AFDC restrictions against assumed income from a substitute parent or other nonresponsible individual do not, merely by themselves, prohibit a State from treating eligible dependent children and caretaker applicants in one household as a single assistance unit for its benefit schedule. The State must still observe actual federal income-availability and mandatory-inclusion provisions; a unit label does not authorize counting resources that the governing law forbids it to count. Section 602(a)(38)'s mandatory family inclusions do not themselves establish that every other grouping is forbidden. A lower holding resting only on the contrary categorical inference cannot stand.

**Two paths and strongest objections:** The recipients' strongest affirmance path reads the regulatory prohibition on reducing a grant solely because of a nonresponsible individual's presence as extending to the lower benefit generated by this grouping. The objection to reversal is thus substantive: the State must not accomplish forbidden attribution through accounting terminology. The reversal path distinguishes a needs schedule reflecting shared household costs from an assumption that a third person's income supports the child. Its strongest answer is that the challenged rule covers members receiving assistance and does not itself establish the forbidden external attribution; King, Lewis, and Van Lare protect the actual availability boundary but do not fix every assistance unit. Dandridge recognizes state benefit design within federal limits, not permission to ignore those limits. The State's path succeeds only at that bounded level; any separate operative regulatory limitation remains for adjudication on its own text.

## 42. Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc. — April 18, 1995

**Direct precedent premise:** The unappealed August 13, 1991 dismissal became final before December 19, 1991; the February 11, 1992 motion satisfied the reinstatement timing predicate. Morgan Stanley already decides this exact final-private-judgment application of §27A(b). The statute genuinely changes law; lack of a dictated fraud verdict does not distinguish that holding. Applying precedent produces affirmance of denial of reinstatement, not a new fraud judgment. A contrary disposition requires express reconsideration, not a fictitious factual distinction.

**Alternative paths:** The adherence path protects the completed adjudication under Morgan Stanley while leaving pending cases, direct review, continuing injunctions, government waiver and lawful judicial reopening separate. Its strongest objection is that Congress has restored a narrow prior filing regime without taking fraud adjudication from courts. The reconsideration path follows Stevens's published Morgan Stanley dissent: this remedial change retains judicial application of law and may justify reopening despite completed limitations finality. Its strongest objection is that the same rationale would unsettle the Court's directly applicable precedent only eleven months after decision, with no changed record, legal predicate, or demonstrated intervening unworkability. Constitutional precedents remain revisable, but that alone is not a factual distinction or a conclusion that every dissenter must now acquiesce.

## 43. Shalala v. Whitecotton — April 18, 1995

**Common disposition:** All eight associates reverse the Federal Circuit's entitlement/compensation direction and remand for application of the correct statutory onset rule and consideration of genuinely preserved distinct routes and procedural issues. They neither affirm every medical finding of the special master nor award compensation. The consolidated new-evidence appeal is returned for appropriate consideration with the merits remand; no independent denial or invented mootness judgment is entered on an unexamined submission.

**Common operative rule:** A claimant proceeding on Table onset must prove that the first symptom or manifestation of onset of the Table injury occurred within the applicable postvaccination interval; the first postvaccination recurrence or manifestation of an already present injury does not alone satisfy that requirement. A claimant may instead establish qualifying significant aggravation or an available actual-causation claim under their own requirements. The Government's burden to prove a statutory unrelated cause follows a proper entitlement showing; the exclusion of idiopathic, unexplained, unknown, hypothetical, or undocumentable causes from that defense does not erase the claimant's initial Table burden. Preexisting microcephaly is neither an automatic defense nor a finding of the precise onset, cause, or aggravation of this child's condition.

**Adversarial alternative:** The strongest support for affirmance is the contrast with the residual-seizure provision's express exclusion and the statute's recognition of birth trauma as a government defense. But birth trauma can exist without an earlier manifested Table injury, and the distinct significant-aggravation route has work to do precisely where an injury precedes vaccination. Those provisions therefore do not compel treating the first later symptom as first onset. Conversely, the Government's construction would be overbroad if it treated any prior abnormality as conclusive defeat; the remand explicitly rejects that extension. The three-day DPT interval and preponderance standards remain; §14(b)(3)(B)'s separately specified infection, toxins, trauma or metabolic-disturbance exclusion is not found satisfied, because the Secretary did not assert those causes in the described appeal.

## 44. Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick — April 18, 1995

**Common disposition:** All eight associates affirm the Eleventh Circuit's return of the Myrick and Lindsey common-law claims for further proceedings, while rejecting its categorical suggestion that an express preemption provision always forecloses implied-conflict analysis. The independent Georgia RICO summary judgment remains untouched because the Myricks abandoned that contention. Fruehauf's dismissal is not reopened. No defect, accident causation, consortium entitlement, wrongful-death recovery, or amount of damages is established.

**Common operative ground:** Section 1392(d)'s express requirement depends on a federal safety standard in effect governing the same aspect of performance; no operative federal antilock requirement or preemptive prohibition is established here. Section 1397(k) preserves common-law liability notwithstanding compliance. An express clause and savings clause do not themselves immunize a claim from every actual federal conflict, but the asserted conflict must rest on operative federal law or policy. Judicial invalidation of requirements in Paccar for inadequate support is not, without more, an affirmative federal entitlement to market vehicles free from state liability for lacking antilock equipment. On this record neither express displacement nor the asserted actual conflict warrants summary judgment.

**Two-path test:** Judge Hill's strongest reversal ground treats the continued ability to sell trucks with conventional brakes as a deliberate federal choice that a tort duty would defeat. Its factual/legal premise fails on the supplied provenance: Paccar removed inadequately supported stopping-distance requirements and permitted properly supported new regulation; it did not establish the asserted federal immunity. The strongest objection to affirmance is that federal nonregulation can sometimes reflect a protected policy choice. The common rule accepts that possibility while requiring its demonstration. No Justice makes the unproduced complete Standard 121 revision an affirmative basis of decision. The remaining source limit therefore bars an unsupported conflict finding, not this bounded failure-to-establish-preemption disposition.

## 45. Heintz v. Jenkins — April 18, 1995

**Common disposition:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer affirm the Seventh Circuit's reversal of dismissal and remand for adjudication of the complaint. The Act covers an attorney who regularly engages in covered consumer-debt collection, including collection through litigation; there is no categorical attorney-litigation exclusion. The definition's actual statutory exceptions remain available when established. The alleged regularity, insurance charge, and misleading demand remain allegations, not adjudicated liability.

**Alternative-path assessment:** Exclusion's strongest argument is that provisions regulating communications, threats, or cessation of collection would otherwise interfere with ordinary judicial litigation, so Congress must have meant only nonlitigating collectors. The countervailing text expressly covers indirect regular collection and removed the former attorney exemption; collection lawsuits are ordinary means of debt collection. The lawful narrower response to a genuine anomaly is construction of that particular communication, action, or defense in a case presenting it, not a wholesale occupational exemption. This handoff reaches coverage and return of the complaint only. It makes no finding that the $4,173 financial-protection insurance charge was unauthorized, false, or collected in bad faith.
