## 37. Director, OWCP v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co.

**Docket / event:** 93-1783; merits, March 21, 1995; argued January 9, 1995. Review of the Fourth Circuit's October 29, 1993 decision, 8 F.3d 175.

**Question and component boundary:** Whether the Director may independently petition under 33 U.S.C. §921(c) to challenge the employee-benefit determination on the administrative interest asserted here. The separately decided Special Fund component is outside the admitted review scope. A result on appellate entitlement does not adjudicate Harcum's compensation merits.

**Record and judgment below:** Harcum suffered a disabling work injury. The ALJ classified his disability as permanent partial from May 6, 1988, and allowed Newport News a $101.34 weekly credit for approximately forty weeks of excess temporary-total payments. The Board rejected the ALJ's maximum-medical-improvement rationale but affirmed on the alternative finding that suitable jobs were available from May 6. The Director appealed both this determination and the transfer of employer liability to the §8(f) Special Fund after 104 weeks. Harcum did not independently seek judicial review. The Fourth Circuit refused to reach the disability issue for want of Director aggrievement, but separately reversed and remanded the Fund determination. Preserve the mixed lower judgment; do not describe the entire appeal as dismissed.

**Competing grounds:** The Director's administrative-enforcement interest can be presented as an interest in correct and uniform application of an Act he administers, rather than vicarious representation alone. The contrary position is that disagreement with a benefits construction does not itself make him adversely affected or aggrieved when it neither impairs a delegated administrative function nor threatens the Fund. The lower court itself recognized possible administrative injury where a Board order increases resource expenditure, alters duties, or creates uncertainty in carrying out a specific function; it did not restrict all government aggrievement to money. It found those injuries absent here. The hypothetical insolvency route to the Fund was both speculative and, under the Director's desired benefits adjustment, potentially cost-increasing.

**Law and alternatives:** Section 921(c) authorizes a person adversely affected or aggrieved by a final Board order to petition within sixty days in the circuit where the injury occurred. Section 921(b)(3)'s administrative appeal by a party in interest is distinct. Section 939's administration and claimant-assistance role, §944's Fund responsibility, and the Director's respondent status under *Perini North River Associates*, 459 U.S. 297, require their own treatment. The statutory-entitlement inquiry must not be converted into an assertion that government agencies categorically lack Article III capacity. Conversely, the lower court's constitutional vocabulary is part of its reasoning and cannot be erased. Simulated *Lujan* and *Greenwich Collieries* supply their actual standing and burden rules; neither settles this statutory aggrievement question. Affirmance of the refusal to hear this component, or reversal and remand for review of the preserved benefit issue, are supported paths. Neither permits a new award or alteration of the Fund remand.

**Sources / bounded sufficiency:** [Complete lower opinion](../sources/chunk4/Newport_News_8_F3d_175.txt), especially numbered paragraphs 11–30 and footnotes 1–3; [§921 historical text](../sources/chunk4/LHWCA_1994_921.htm), [§939 historical text](../sources/chunk4/LHWCA_1994_939.htm). Sufficient for the limited entitlement controversy. No merits computation or new factual finding is supported.

## 38. Anderson v. Edwards

**Docket / event:** 93-1883; merits, March 22, 1995; argued January 18, 1995. Review of *Edwards v. Healy*, 12 F.3d 154 (9th Cir. December 1, 1993).

**Question:** Whether federal AFDC law permits California to combine applicants/recipients from two or more nonsibling-dependent-child groupings living with one caretaker relative or a relative married couple into one assistance unit. California seeks reversal of the injunction against its regulations; recipients seek preservation of separate-unit treatment. Unit definition, income availability, mandatory family inclusion, benefit levels, equitable treatment, and the legal force of agency guidance are distinct issues.

**Verified lower record:** California MPP §44-205.31 and successor §82-824.1 combine the covered recipients in one assistance or family-budget unit. The class alleges an improper presumption that caretaker resources are available to children the caretaker has no legal duty to support. The Ninth Circuit affirmed because *Beaton v. Thompson*, 913 F.2d 701 (9th Cir. 1990), invalidated a virtually identical Washington rule. The panel considered itself bound and held California's asserted distinctions, including its reliance on *Bowen v. Gilliard*, insufficient to distinguish that precedent. It did not publish extensive household findings or independently resolve every regulatory contention.

**Neutral issue framing:** California's supported distinction is between defining one unit of applicants sharing costs and attributing a nonapplicant's unavailable resources; the recipients' supported objection is that unit labels cannot defeat a federal restriction against presumed support from persons without support obligations. Whether §602(a)(38)'s mandatory inclusions establish only a minimum or also constrain broader state consolidation is contested, not an already adopted rule. Similarly, whether an economies-of-scale benefit schedule actually implies prohibited income attribution is a legal and record question. No record finding that all caretaker funds are legally available may be inferred from the rule's label.

**Entering law:** *King v. Smith*, 392 U.S. 309; *Lewis v. Martin*, 397 U.S. 552; and *Van Lare v. Hurley*, 421 U.S. 338, address attribution of presumed support. *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. 471, addresses state benefit-level choices within federal limits. *Bowen v. Gilliard*, 483 U.S. 587, concerns Congress's required family inclusion and the associated treatment of child-support payments; it does not by its subject alone decide this nonsibling grouping. Apply the actual §§602(a)(7), (38), and the relevant historical regulations rather than treating these subjects as interchangeable. Current-term *NationsBank* and *Guernsey Memorial Hospital* require attention to the actual statutory delegation, regulation, and administrative instrument; *Suter* does not answer the substantive AFDC question.

**Focused source supplement:** The complete *Beaton* opinion confirms that Washington grouped all needy eligible siblings and nonsiblings living with a single caretaker relative or relative married couple. It invalidated that rule under 45 C.F.R. §§233.90(a)(1), 233.20(a)(2)(viii), and 233.20(a)(3)(ii)(D): the presence of a substitute parent, man in the house, or other individual cannot establish assumed income or ineligibility, and the grant cannot be prorated or reduced solely because of a non-legally-responsible individual's presence. The panel held that an AFDC-recipient adult's income was not materially different for this purpose. Washington argued that it had defined the unit rather than imputed outside income. The district court had additionally relied on equitable-treatment/standards provisions and the conditions on state proration plans; *Beaton* expressly did not reach those grounds or equal protection. Its Washington payment examples are not California record facts. Thus a rejection of its no-deeming rationale does not automatically reject every independently preserved objection.

**Lawful paths / bounded source limitation:** If the federal scheme permits the grouping and the injunction rests on an erroneous categorical no-deeming equation, reverse that ground and preserve any independently supported unresolved application. If grouping necessarily violates an identified federal income or unit limitation, affirm relief limited to that conflict. A blanket determination of every individual resource attribution is unavailable. The short appellate opinion does not support the candidate's page references to pages 158–159 or precise household arithmetic. The 1994 ACF guidance itself has not been produced: do not adopt its alleged content, a guidance-specific deference holding, or a claim that it conclusively rejects all independent objections. That branch-specific limit does not prevent adjudication of the principal regulatory issue on the text and reasoning reproduced in *Beaton*.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower opinion](../sources/chunk4/Anderson_lower_12_F3d_154.txt), all five numbered paragraphs; [complete Beaton opinion](../sources/chunk4/Beaton_913_F2d_701.txt), including quoted regulations and footnotes 3–4. Sufficient for the principal no-deeming dispute and a remedy preserving unresolved grounds. Any resolution that requires unquoted text of an independent regulatory provision must retrieve that text before deciding that ground.

## 39. Swanner v. Anchorage Equal Rights Commission

**Docket / event:** simulation-assigned 94-124; merits, March 27, 1995; scheduled argument January 17, 1995. Final judgment below: 874 P.2d 274 (Alaska May 13, 1994), the replacement opinion on rehearing, not the withdrawn February opinion.

**Question / reach:** Federal Free Exercise Clause and RFRA challenge to applying Alaska's and Anchorage's marital-status housing protections to this landlord. The Alaska Supreme Court's interpretation of its statutes and state constitution is not subject to independent federal revision. Its adverse state-constitutional ruling does not prevent a federal statutory or constitutional claim. The admitted review question does not independently reopen the separate notice/delegation due-process rulings.

**Record:** Swanner, doing business as Whitehall Properties, refused rental or inspection to Bowles, Harper, and Moose solely because each intended to live with an unmarried opposite-sex partner. His sincere Christian belief also rejects opposite-sex nonsexual roommates because of the appearance of immorality. The Alaska court accepted that refusal was religiously motivated and rejected a limitation of religious exercise to rituals or ceremonies. Neither litigant established religious targeting or disputed general neutrality there. The local ordinance expressly excepts an individual home where the tenant would share common living areas with the owner, lessor, manager, agent, or other person. That text must be considered if secular comparability becomes material; no finding that Swanner qualified for that exception is supplied.

**Lower grounds and dissent:** The court held the conduct was marital-status discrimination under settled state law, rejected the federal constitutional claim under *Smith*, and applied Alaska's more protective compelling-interest standard separately. It distinguished access to housing from the independent harm of a discriminatory denial of an otherwise offered transaction. It regarded the latter interest as compelling even if alternate housing existed and emphasized the commercial context, relying on *United States v. Lee*. Footnote 9 assumed RFRA constitutional and applicable, then regarded the same interest and prohibition as sufficient, with no less restrictive alternative. Chief Justice Moore dissented under the state constitution: he found inadequate proof that an exemption threatened housing access, rejected the bare characterization of every discriminatory transaction as necessarily compelling, and distinguished racial/sex discrimination precedents and commercial entry from surrender of religious rights. His positions describe a lower dissent, not a Supreme Court commitment.

**Entering law:** The original [RFRA text](../sources/chunk4/RFRA_107_Stat_1488.txt), §§3, 5–7, applies its substantial-burden threshold and claimant-specific compelling-interest/least-restrictive-means inquiry. Government carries both production and persuasion on justification after the claimant establishes substantial burden. The original definition of religious exercise is exercise under the First Amendment. Section 6 covers preexisting law and implementation; §7 leaves the Establishment Clause untouched. The current-term *Clearwater* holding directly requires application-specific RFRA adjudication of future municipal enforcement and preserves ordinary standing and appropriate-relief limits. It expressly leaves RFRA's constitutionality and retrospective monetary liability open. This source record does not establish a concrete amount or nature of retrospective monetary relief; a decision about that relief cannot be supplied by assumption.

The *Lukumi* standard effective June 11, 1993 requires objective neutrality and treatment of comparable secular harms; it does not replace *Smith* with general constitutional exemptions. *Smith*, 494 U.S. 872, was authored by Scalia and joined by Stevens and Kennedy among currently seated associates; O'Connor's concurrence in the judgment retained a compelling-interest approach. All eight current associates joined *Clearwater*'s statutory RFRA inquiry, so their earlier constitutional positions cannot substitute for applying that statute. *Sherbert*, *Yoder*, *Lee*, *Bob Jones University*, and *Roberts v. United States Jaycees* support competing arguments about burden, application-specific justification, commercial conduct, and protected third-party interests, each at its actual scope.

**Supported alternatives:** Affirm if a qualifying burden is justified under the governing statutory standard (and any independently reached constitutional claim fails); reverse or vacate if that claimant-specific justification fails or requires findings not made below. A limited remand may separate prospective enforcement, any preserved retrospective relief, and the statutory inquiry. No categorical rule that commerce forfeits religious protection, that all antidiscrimination laws automatically satisfy RFRA, or that the mere availability of alternative housing establishes an exemption is supplied by entering law. Constitutionality of RFRA is not a necessary issue merely because the lower court assumed it.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete Alaska opinion and Moore dissent](../sources/chunk4/Swanner_874_P2d_274.txt), pp.276–291, including footnotes 3 and 9; original RFRA; *Clearwater* and *Lukumi* reading slices. Sufficient for the federal exemption/justification controversy and a bounded statutory remand. Missing exact commission remedy prevents inventing monetary or penalty particulars, not consideration of the established merits question.

## 40. Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co.

**Docket / event:** 93-1577; merits, March 28, 1995; argued January 9, 1995. Review of 13 F.3d 1297 (9th Cir. January 3, 1994).

**Question / posture:** Whether color alone can qualify for registration and protection under the Lanham Act. The lower judgment is mixed: trademark-infringement recovery and rejection of Jacobson's cancellation counterclaim were reversed, with directions to enter cancellation judgment; the §43(a) unfair-competition judgment, monetary damages, and injunction remained affirmed. A ruling on registrability must preserve that component separation.

**Findings below:** Qualitex had used green-gold on its SUN GLOW press pads for decades; Jacobson deliberately copied the appearance in its MAGIC GLOW pads. The district court found secondary meaning and likely confusion. A color generally helps conceal staining, but it found no competitive necessity for this particular shade, no improvement in use, quality, or longevity attributable to it, and hundreds or thousands of available tones. The Ninth Circuit sustained the trade-dress findings under clear-error review while rejecting registration of color alone as a legal category. It relied on color depletion, shade-confusion administration, and the availability of protection for overall trade dress or color combined with a design. The contrary line, including *Owens-Corning*, permits registration where normal trademark requirements are met.

**Law and competing routes:** Sections 1052 and 1127 use broad source-identifying language and do not expressly list color as a prohibited category; the issue is whether that text, history, and trademark policy permit color alone to perform the statutory function. Functional features remain outside trademark protection: *Inwood*, 456 U.S. 844, 850 n.10, identifies features essential to use or purpose or affecting cost or quality. Apply any broader competitive-necessity claim as a reasoned legal question, not an assumed finding here. Simulated *Two Pesos* (June 26, 1992) protects inherently distinctive nonfunctional restaurant trade dress without an additional acquired-meaning showing; it does not establish that bare color is inherently distinctive or automatically protected. Registration burdens, acquired distinctiveness, functionality, confusion, and the shape of relief remain separate.

Permit color registration subject to its ordinary predicates and reverse the categorical bar, or sustain a supported categorical construction and affirm the challenged registration disposition. Either path leaves the upheld §43(a) judgment in view and does not decide rights to primary colors, every shade in a market, or useful product features. On this record no new Supreme Court survey finding is needed.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower opinion](../sources/chunk4/Qualitex_13_F3d_1297.txt), numbered paragraphs 1–9, 18–26, 38–50; [§1052](../sources/chunk4/Lanham_1994_1052.htm), [§1127](../sources/chunk4/Lanham_1994_1127.htm); *Two Pesos* reading slice. Sufficient for the category question and bounded remedy.

## 41. Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Jefferson Lines, Inc.

**Later predecision source supplement:** The full petitioner/respondent merits briefs and joint appendix were obtained and independently read after this section's first release. The [scoped neutral supplement](OT_1994CHUNK4_JEFFERSON_SOURCE_SUPPLEMENT.md) supplies the now-verified contest over all four Complete Auto factors, actual bankruptcy findings, stipulated amount and periods, tax-rate transition, statutory qualifications, and requested remedies. Its source additions supersede any inference of an evidentiary gap from the Eighth Circuit's nonreach. They do not change the Eighth Circuit's actual limited holding or select a Supreme Court disposition.

**Docket / event:** 93-1677; merits, April 3, 1995; argued November 28, 1994. Review of 15 F.3d 90 (8th Cir. January 21, 1994), affirming disallowance of the tax claims in bankruptcy.

**Question / facts:** Whether Oklahoma may collect its full sales tax on interstate bus tickets sold in Oklahoma for trips originating there. The lower court describes §1354's text as reaching all tickets sold in Oklahoma regardless of origin or destination; the present admitted application is sales and originating trips in Oklahoma. Do not extend a holding automatically to tickets for wholly out-of-state travel. The tax is formally on the purchaser, collected by the carrier, which owes remittance even if it failed to collect. The lower text reproduces the four-percent tax on gross receipts/proceeds from transportation for hire by common carriers.

**Lower grounds:** The Eighth Circuit held the tax internally consistent: each ticket sale occurs in one State, so identical taxes on that event would not multiply the tax. It nevertheless found external inconsistency because the price reflects transportation outside Oklahoma. It treated *Central Greyhound Lines v. Mealey*, 334 U.S. 653, as controlling in substance despite the difference between a seller's gross-receipts tax and a buyer's sales tax. Having found this apportionment defect, it did not decide the remaining *Complete Auto* factors. A statement that the lower court found internal inconsistency would be false.

**Competing grounds / law:** Oklahoma characterizes the taxable event as a completed local sale supported by local governmental services. Jefferson characterizes the tax base as interstate transportation revenues and argues that labels cannot evade apportionment of the service purchased. *Complete Auto*, 430 U.S. 274, requires substantial nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination, and fair relation to state services. *Goldberg v. Sweet*, 488 U.S. 252, distinguishes internal and external consistency. *Central Greyhound* addresses interstate-carrier gross receipts; the disputed question is its application to this local sales tax. Current *Quill*, *Itel Containers*, and *Associated Industries v. Lohman* preserve their actual tax-specific holdings; no one precedent authorizes every state transportation levy. State-tax doctrine must not be reduced to a matching-tax arithmetic test or to whether the State funded every mile traveled.

**Paths / remedy:** Affirm on the external-apportionment theory; reverse that ground if the local-sale conception is valid and dispose of other preserved grounds only on adequate legal/record support; or remand a genuinely unresolved necessary ground. Reversal of disallowance does not itself justify inventing an assessed dollar amount, a credit regime, or a new mileage formula. Any adopted precedent distinction must identify its effect on *Central Greyhound* rather than silently erase that authority.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower opinion](../sources/chunk4/Jefferson_Lines_15_F3d_90.txt), all numbered paragraphs and statutory footnote; actual tax rules and coalitions in entering-law slice. Sufficient for the stated sales-tax/apportionment controversy. The Supreme Court should not treat the lower court's express nonreach of the other factors as findings against the State.

## 42. Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc.

**Docket / event:** 93-1121; merits, April 18, 1995; argued November 30, 1994. Review of 1 F.3d 1487 (6th Cir. August 3, 1993).

**Facts and posture:** Plaintiffs filed their securities-fraud action on November 25, 1987, concerning 1983 purchases. After *Lampf* and *Beam*, the district court dismissed with prejudice on August 13, 1991. Plaintiffs did not appeal. Section 27A was enacted December 19, 1991; they moved for reinstatement February 11, 1992. The district court accepted that the statutory reinstatement conditions were met but held the command unconstitutional. The Sixth Circuit affirmed. No issue about whether fraud occurred, damages, or ordinary merits defenses was adjudicated.

**Operative reinstatement command:** Section 27A(b), reproduced in the lower opinion, covers a private §10(b) civil action commenced on or before June 19, 1991, which was dismissed as time barred after that date and would have been timely under the laws applicable in the jurisdiction, including retroactivity principles, as they existed June 19, 1991. Such an action shall be reinstated on a plaintiff's motion made within sixty days after December 19, 1991. This dispute concerns a judgment already final without appeal before enactment; pending cases under subsection (a) and dismissals still on direct review are separate.

**Lower grounds and narrower concurrence:** The majority treated compulsory reopening of a completed private judgment as a violation of Article III finality, distinguishing legislation changing pending litigation, prospective injunctions, and ordinary judicial reopening. It distinguished *Sioux Nation*'s waiver by the sovereign and considered related vested-rights concerns. Judge Keith concurred only as to this final unappealed judgment, declining to invalidate the provision as applied to judgments still subject to appellate review. The statutory command does not direct a finding of securities fraud. Conversely, the absence of a dictated merits verdict does not by itself answer the finality objection.

**Controlling simulated law:** *Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co.* (May 23, 1994) already holds that this reinstatement command cannot reopen private §10(b) actions dismissed under *Lampf* where the dismissal became final without appeal before §27A's enactment. Thomas's Court opinion was joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter. The holding expressly accepts a genuine change in substantive law and distinguishes pending cases and prospective judicial relief; do not replace its premise with the lower court's suggestion that Congress changed no underlying law. O'Connor did not participate there. Stevens dissented, joined by Blackmun and Ginsburg. The public dissent regards restoration of earlier limitations expectations as a permissible legislative law change, preserves judicial resolution of fraud and defenses, and uses *Robertson*, *Schor*, *Chase Securities*, and the asserted remedial analogy to *Sioux Nation*. That dissent supplies public continuity, not governing law. Breyer has no vote in that prior adjudication.

**Paths / remedy:** Apply the directly applicable finality rule and affirm denial of reinstatement; or expressly undertake reconsideration of that precedent with a supported stare-decisis analysis. Identical finality facts cannot be relabeled a distinction. A broader ruling on all of subsection (b), subsection (a), due process, or unappealed judgments generally is unnecessary to dispose of this application. The unproved merits remain unproved under either route.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower opinion and Keith concurrence](../sources/chunk4/Plaut_1_F3d_1487.txt); entering-law holding; [complete bounded Morgan Stanley public entry](../entering-law/OT_1994CHUNK4_MORGAN_STANLEY_PUBLIC.md). Sufficient for the final-judgment question and its direct precedent issue. No exact statutory quotation should be attributed to a failed download when it actually comes from the lower opinion.

## 43. Shalala v. Whitecotton

**Docket / event:** 94-372; merits, April 18, 1995; argued February 28, 1995. Review of 17 F.3d 374 (Fed. Cir. February 15, 1994), consolidated appeals 92-5083 and 93-5101.

**Record and procedural components:** Maggie Whitecotton was born April 22, 1975, with a small head circumference. She received her third DPT vaccination August 18, 1975. Her mother reported seizures that evening; the emergency physician did not observe a seizure then, while a physician observed clonic seizures the following day. Later disability and preexisting microcephaly are in the record, but neither independently establishes vaccine causation. The petition was filed August 2, 1990. The special master denied compensation August 16, 1991, and the Claims Court affirmed January 14, 1992. The Federal Circuit also had an appeal from denial of a new-evidence Rule 60(b)(2) motion. It reversed and remanded for determination of compensation, not merely renewed consideration of eligibility.

**Disputed statutory trigger:** The original Table provides the relevant three-day period for a DPT-associated encephalopathy, together with statutory definitions and qualifications. Section 11's petition requirements refer to the first symptom or manifestation of onset, or significant aggravation; §13 requires proof of the entitlement predicates by a preponderance and permits the Government to establish an unrelated cause by a preponderance. Section 13(a)(2)(A) excludes idiopathic, unexplained, unknown, hypothetical, and undocumentable causes from the statutory unrelated-factors category. It does not expressly dispense with the claimant's initial qualifying showing. Section 14(b)(3)(B) excludes encephalopathy from the Table if it is established by a preponderance that the cause is infection, toxins, trauma, or metabolic disturbances. The lower court's footnote 2 states that the Secretary did not argue those identified causes there.

**Strongest lower rationale:** The appellate court read the Table's reference to the first symptom after vaccine administration to allow the first postvaccination manifestation, even if a related condition preceded vaccination. It contrasted the separate residual-seizure qualification's express restriction concerning earlier seizures with the absence of a parallel restriction in the encephalopathy provision. It reasoned that provisions permitting birth trauma as rebuttal evidence would otherwise lose force, and treated unexplained microcephaly as an impermissible idiopathic unrelated cause under *Koston*. These are legal reasons for its reading, not findings that vaccination initiated the disorder.

**Competing construction and alternatives:** The Secretary contends that a claimant must first show the qualifying onset or significant aggravation within the Table period, so a later manifestation of an already existing condition cannot alone satisfy the onset route. That contention does not eliminate the distinct significant-aggravation route, an otherwise available actual-causation claim, or the Government's burden after a proper Table showing. The Court may sustain the lower construction and supported entitlement disposition, or reject that construction and remand for the proper predicate and any preserved alternatives. It must not transform the onset dispute into an unsupported finding that no aggravation occurred or that microcephaly conclusively explains this disability. If the new-evidence appeal becomes moot or is returned for reconsideration, say so; do not silently erase the consolidated procedural component or invent the submission's contents.

**Date-sensitive source transition:** The final rule at 60 Fed. Reg. 7678 became effective March 10, 1995. Section 100.3(c), PDF p.7696, applies the revised Table and qualifications to petitions filed on or after March 10, 1995; earlier petitions use §2114(a) and (b) as in effect on February 8, 1995. This 1990 petition remains governed by that earlier Table. Neither a modern Table nor the new regulatory encephalopathy definition may be retroactively substituted. Admission of that source transition is handled by the operator's sanitized chronology refresh; this packet supplies the verified transition fact without creating an adjudication.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower opinion](../sources/chunk4/Whitecotton_17_F3d_374.txt), including footnote 2 and the consolidated appeals; [official final-rule PDF text](../sources/chunk4/Vaccine_Table_60_FR_7678_pdf_text.txt), especially §100.3(c). Sufficient to adjudicate the disputed onset construction and a remand preserving distinct claims. Any final award on an alternative requiring additional medical findings needs those findings rather than inference.

## 44. Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick

**Docket / event:** 94-286; merits, April 18, 1995; argued February 22, 1995. Review of 13 F.3d 1516 (11th Cir. February 11, 1994).

**Components / facts:** The consolidated litigation includes Benjamin Myrick's injury and Emily Myrick's consortium claims against Freightliner and the Lindsey claims arising from Grace Lindsey's death against Navistar. The alleged defect is the absence of antilock braking equipment on tractor-trailers alleged to have jackknifed across the highway. Defect and causation are allegations, not merits findings. Fruehauf's dismissal and the stipulated treatment of Navistar's predecessor are not live defects to reopen. The district courts entered preemption summary judgments; the Eleventh Circuit reversed the common-law dispositions. The Myricks abandoned their Georgia RICO contention on appeal, and the lower opinion expressly left that statutory summary judgment unaffected (footnotes 3 and 6).

**Statutory distinctions:** The Safety Act's §1392(d) addresses a state safety standard applicable to the same aspect of performance as a federal standard in effect and requires identity with that federal standard. Section 1397(k) states that compliance with a federal safety standard does not exempt a person from common-law liability. The express clause's coverage, the savings clause's scope, and actual conflict with federal law are distinct inquiries. No current law supplied here makes every state claim survive simply because there is a savings clause, or makes every claim disappear because federal regulation exists somewhere in the vehicle's braking system.

**Lower legal dispute:** The majority followed *Taylor* on the absence of express preemption and construed *Cipollone* as foreclosing implied preemption once Congress expressly addressed the subject. Judge Hill dissented: he regarded the federal permission to use air brakes with or without antilock as an affirmative regulatory choice frustrated by state defect liability. The majority also used choice language in recounting the earlier lower precedent. That language is an attributed lower-court legal characterization, not proof that the agency affirmatively adopted a federal policy forbidding tort liability for the omission.

**Regulatory provenance independently checked:** In *Paccar*, 573 F.2d 632 (9th Cir. 1978), the court invalidated the 60-mph stopping-distance requirements because the agency's proof did not adequately establish the reliability and safety of the new systems in use. Paragraph 28 suspended the parts requiring heavier axles and antilock through that invalidation while expressly allowing new standards supported by better evidence. This was judicial invalidation for inadequate support, not an agency rule announcing an affirmative right to sell trucks without antilock. The complete contemporaneous post-*Paccar* Standard 121 revision has not been retrieved; do not claim its full text independently verified or ascribe an unverified preemptive purpose to it. The lower opinions establish the litigated absence of an operative antilock requirement and the parties' rival implications.

**Entering law / paths:** Use the actual simulated *Cipollone*, *Gade*, *Easterwood*, and current-term *Wolens* rules. In particular, *Gade*'s shared narrow holding does not make every compatible state supplement preempted, and it does not reduce all conflict analysis to physical impossibility. *Easterwood* requires attention to what a federal regulation actually covers. The Court may affirm the common-law remand because neither an applicable express prohibition nor an actual federal conflict is established, while correcting an overbroad exclusivity theory; or reverse if a supported operative federal rule actually conflicts with these claims. An affirmative federal-choice rationale requires the source that establishes that choice. Preserve the RICO judgment and decide neither defect, causation, nor damages.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower majority, Hill dissent, and footnotes](../sources/chunk4/Myrick_13_F3d_1516.txt); [complete Paccar opinion](../sources/chunk4/Paccar_573_F2d_632.txt); relevant entering-law provisions. Sufficient for the statutory and demonstrated-conflict question; an unproduced agency policy cannot itself support a conflict finding.

## 45. Heintz v. Jenkins

**Docket / event:** 94-367; merits, April 18, 1995; argued February 21, 1995. Review of 25 F.3d 536 (7th Cir. May 27, 1994).

**Question / facts at dismissal:** Whether the FDCPA covers an attorney who regularly collects consumer debts through litigation. Jenkins's loan authorized insurance against loss or damage to the car. She alleges that the bank instead charged $4,173 for financial-protection insurance covering the bank against default and that its lawyers sought that amount in the collection suit and a settlement letter. Her federal complaint invoked §1692f(1)'s restriction on collecting amounts not authorized by the agreement or permitted by law and §1692e's prohibition on false, deceptive, or misleading representations. Regular debt-collection activity and the challenged charge are allegations accepted at the dismissal stage, not established merits findings. The district court dismissed under an attorney-litigation exemption; the Seventh Circuit reversed and remanded.

**Legal fork:** Section 1692a(6)'s definition reaches the specified debt-collection business or regular collection, directly or indirectly, of debts owed or asserted owed another, subject to its actual exceptions. Congress expressly removed the former attorney exemption in 1986. The statutory-coverage argument relies on that language and deletion. The contrary argument relies on the interaction with ordinary judicial process and the asserted implausibility of subjecting normal litigation communications and demands to provisions drafted for collection practices. The Sixth Circuit's *Green v. Hocking*, 9 F.3d 18, and the lower opinion's treatment of agency commentary describe that opposing approach; neither is current Supreme Court law. Do not characterize the issue as already resolved by a general command never to imply an exemption.

**Components and remedy:** Coverage of regular attorney collectors, application to litigation activity, the elements of §§1692e and §1692f(1), and defenses are separate. If the Act covers the activity, affirm the reinstatement of the complaint and leave actual deception, contractual authorization, regularity, defenses, and damages for the proper stage. If a legally supported exclusion covers it, state its precise scope and the disposition it supports. A statutory-coverage ruling does not establish liability merely because a lawyer lost a collection demand. Exact terms of a particular communication exception or defense must accompany any reliance on it; the verified supplement immediately below identifies such terms and their source. No later pleading amendment is date eligible.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower opinion](../sources/chunk4/Jenkins_25_F3d_536.txt); [1986 amendment](../sources/chunk4/FDCPA_1986_amendment.txt). Sufficient for the regular-attorney/litigation coverage question and a merits remand. A decisive exception or defense beyond the text actually reproduced requires its historical text before use.

**Verified statutory qualification supplement:** The [original official Act](../sources/chunk4/FDCPA_1977_91_Stat_874.txt), 91 Stat. 874–883, has now been read in full alongside the complete 1986 amendment. Section 803(6)(D), codified §1692a(6)(D), excludes a person while serving or attempting to serve legal process on another person in connection with judicial enforcement of a debt; it is an activity-specific exception, not by its terms a complete attorney exclusion. Section 805(c), codified §1692c(c), applies after a consumer's written refusal to pay or written request to cease further communication. It permits further communication to advise that collection efforts are terminated; to notify that the collector or creditor may invoke specified remedies ordinarily invoked by that collector or creditor; or, where applicable, to notify that the collector or creditor intends to invoke a specified remedy. A mailed consumer notice is complete upon receipt. These exceptions are alternatives, and permission does not become an obligation to send a notice.

Section 807(5), codified §1692e(5), prohibits a threat of action that cannot legally be taken or is not intended. Section 813(c), codified §1692k(c), bars liability if the collector proves by a preponderance that the violation was unintentional and resulted from bona fide error notwithstanding maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid that error. It does not automatically excuse every unsuccessful lawsuit or resolve whether a particular legal mistake qualifies. Section 813(e), codified §1692k(e), protects acts or omissions done in good faith conformity with an FTC advisory opinion even if it is later amended, rescinded, or held invalid. A staff commentary is not automatically such an advisory opinion. Section 814(d) withholds trade-regulation and other debt-collection rulemaking authority from the listed enforcement agencies. None of these provisions establishes a defense on this complaint's facts. The original enacted text and the attorney amendment are verified; an exhaustive intervening-amendment review is not claimed, and no post-event amendment enters.

## 46. Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado

**Docket / event:** simulation-assigned 94-38; merits, April 18, 1995; scheduled argument February 21, 1995. Review of 21 F.3d 1508 (10th Cir. April 19, 1994).

**Record / exact challenged structure:** Lawyers seeking clients in DUI, misdemeanor, and traffic matters and the director of a treatment program sought names and addresses from El Paso County Court criminal records for targeted mailings. No finding that their proposed mail was false or unlawful supplies the judgment. Colorado §24-72-305.5 has two operative commands: covered criminal-justice records containing names, addresses, telephone numbers, or other information shall not be used by any person to solicit business for pecuniary gain; the official custodian shall deny inspection unless the applicant signs a statement that the records will not be used for that direct solicitation. The prohibition is not merely a rule about the government's sale of a specially compiled mailing list. The Act's definition of a criminal-justice agency includes courts; the records at issue include official actions. State definitions and access categories are reproduced in the lower opinion. No blanket factual finding that every file or all personal data is publicly inspectable follows.

**Lower majority:** It rejected a general constitutional entitlement to acquire the addresses under *Houchins* and *Nixon* and declined to extend the experience-and-logic access test categorically to these data. Nevertheless, it rejected the State's argument that the absence of an access entitlement ended the First Amendment inquiry. It treated the use restriction as content based and examined the commercial-speech condition under *Central Hudson*. It accepted privacy at the point of governmental disclosure, concerns about solicitation abuse, and confidence in the justice system as sufficient under its tailoring analysis. It distinguished *Shapero*'s regulation of communication from this restriction on governmental supply of information. Other access routes did not, in its view, eliminate the asserted privacy interest.

**Dissent and contrary case:** Judge Aldisert regarded the requested information as public court records within a qualified experience-and-logic access right. Counsel and treatment communications can assist defendants. He emphasized the conceded availability of the same information through the El Paso County News as undermining the restriction's claimed privacy effect and rejected offensiveness or official disapproval as an adequate basis. This is not a finding that all governmental privacy interests are invalid or that every request for a criminal file has constitutional priority.

**Law and available resolutions:** Determine the actual access category and any qualified access right; separately assess whether the speech-based condition is valid even if the State could withhold particular records for a lawful reason. Simulated *Discovery Network*, *Edenfield*, *Ibanez*, and the current speech cases require their own distinctions and evidentiary scope. A government-benefit label alone does not decide an unconstitutional-condition claim. *Central Hudson* does not invariably require the least restrictive means, but the fit between the actual disclosure restriction, the claimed injury, and alternative sources remains material. *Shapero*'s objection to assumptions about truthful targeted mail must be considered alongside the source-of-information distinction. Affirm the summary judgment if the supported access and speech standards permit it; reverse on the relevant access or commercial-speech ground, or remand a necessary unresolved factual question. Do not impose a new statewide records regime or adjudicate unpresented private misuse.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower majority and Aldisert dissent](../sources/chunk4/Lanphere_21_F3d_1508.txt), including statutory quotations, definitions, and footnotes; relevant entering-law cases. Sufficient for the presented access and solicitation-use restriction.

## 47. Celotex Corp. v. Edwards

**Docket / event:** 93-1504; merits, April 19, 1995; argued December 6, 1994. Review of 6 F.3d 312 (5th Cir. November 5, 1993), rehearing denied December 22, 1993.

**Record / procedural sequence:** Edwards obtained a $281,025.80 personal-injury judgment. Northbrook issued a $294,987.88 supersedeas bond; insurance proceeds served as collateral. The Fifth Circuit affirmed September 20, 1990, and issued its mandate October 12, the date Celotex filed its Chapter 11 petition in Florida. On October 17 the bankruptcy court issued a §105 injunction covering specified proceedings involving a debtor, including appeals with bonds. Edwards sought enforcement against Northbrook under Rule 65.1 on May 3, 1991; the district court granted it May 27, 1992. The bankruptcy court's published interpretation, 128 B.R. 478, 484, regarded the injunction as covering these bonds and required bond creditors to seek relief there.

**Three separable issues:** Whether §362 automatically stays collection from the surety; whether the §105 order actually reaches this collection; and whether a court outside the bankruptcy proceeding may permit conduct prohibited by that order rather than require direct review or relief from the issuing court. The bankruptcy court's statutory subject-matter jurisdiction and its authority to issue a particular injunction are related but distinct from collateral obedience. Section 105 does not itself create subject-matter jurisdiction. Section 1334(b)'s related-proceeding jurisdiction is not the same as exclusive jurisdiction over estate property.

**Lower reasons and narrow concurrence:** The majority held that the matured surety obligation was not itself estate property and that §362 did not automatically stay its enforcement after completion of the appeal. It read the injunction's reference to proceedings involving debtors as not facially barring a proceeding against the nondebtor surety, notwithstanding the issuing court's broader reading. It also rejected the bankruptcy court's authority to defeat the bond protection on the jurisdictional/property rationale it considered. Its discussion of collateral and footnote 7 does not establish as fact every alleged consequence for the estate. Judge Jones concurred on the automatic-stay and order-scope grounds and did not reach the broader bankruptcy-power issue. The rehearing opinion defended the outcome as outside the injunction, not as a license to disobey an admittedly applicable valid order.

**Competing argument / paths:** Celotex invokes the estate's practical exposure through the surety relationship and the rule that an injunction issued within jurisdiction must be challenged through orderly direct review. Edwards invokes the independent purpose and maturity of the bond, the order's actual text, and limits on bankruptcy jurisdiction over a nondebtor obligation. *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, and *Walker v. Birmingham*, 388 U.S. 307, supply date-eligible collateral-obedience principles, whose jurisdictional qualifications must not be suppressed. The Court may require use of the bankruptcy court's relief/review route without finally deciding the injunction's ultimate validity; it may instead resolve a genuinely dispositive scope or jurisdiction issue. Either disposition must say what happens to the district enforcement order and leave any reserved direct challenge open. Do not turn a threshold-review holding into a universal automatic stay for sureties or a final merits endorsement of every §105 injunction.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete lower majority, Jones concurrence, and rehearing discussion](../sources/chunk4/Edwards_6_F3d_312.txt); [historical §105](../sources/chunk4/Bankruptcy_1994_105.htm). Sufficient for the identified scope, collateral-review, and remedy controversy on the lower-described order. Any decisive claim about an unquoted clause or quantified estate impact requires the actual order or record evidence.

## 48. McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission

**Docket / event:** 93-986; merits, April 19, 1995; argued October 12, 1994. Review of 67 Ohio St.3d 391 (Ohio September 22, 1993).

**Facts / judgment:** Margaret McIntyre distributed leaflets opposing a school-tax levy on April 27 and 28, 1988. Some carried her name and some did not. Family assistance and leaflets placed on windshields do not establish an organization or candidate committee. After a March 30, 1989 complaint, the Commission imposed a $100 fine on March 30, 1990. The trial court held the statute unconstitutional as applied; the court of appeals reinstated the fine; the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. The case supplies no adjudicated fraud or falsity in her message. Being informed of the identification law bears on notice, not its constitutional validity.

**Statute in context:** Section 3599.09(A), quoted by the lower court in relevant part with an express ellipsis, covers writings and other publications designed to promote a candidate or influence voters in an election and expenditures for such communications, including ballot issues. It requires the name and residence or business address of the responsible person or, for an organization, its chairman, treasurer, or secretary. Its exception is personal correspondence not reproduced by machine for general distribution. It is not a financial-contribution report limited to candidate committees. The quoted terms support the individual-leaflet application. Any wider holding depending on omitted statutory material requires the full historical provision; the ellipsis cannot be represented as a complete statute.

**Lower majority / dissent:** The majority treated identification as a modest election-integrity and voter-information measure, relied on election-regulation decisions and *Bellotti*'s discussion of identifying campaign sources, and distinguished *Talley*'s general ban on anonymous handbills. It considered attribution useful in investigating unlawful campaign communications. Justice Wright's dissent focused on an ordinary citizen's speech about a ballot issue, used *Talley* and *Buckley*'s demanding scrutiny of burdens on political expression, and regarded existing laws against false and fraudulent communications as relevant alternatives. Possible retaliation is a rationale for anonymous speech, not a record finding that McIntyre suffered a specific threat. The lower dispute cannot be compressed to whether the fine was small.

**Questions and lawful scope:** Identify the scrutiny appropriate to compelled identification on an individual's political leaflet and assess the State's actual fraud-enforcement and information interests under that standard. *Anderson v. Celebrezze* addresses election burdens in its context; it does not settle scrutiny of all speech near elections. *Talley*, *Buckley*, and *Bellotti* address different communications and disclosure mechanisms. Simulated *Burson*, *Norman*, *Ladue*, *Turner*, and current *NTEU* retain their factual and doctrinal limits. Affirm if this application is justified under the governing standard, or reverse the fine if it is not. An as-applied resolution can leave organizational identification, candidate financing disclosures, fraudulent communications, and other statutory applications open. Do not invalidate every election disclosure requirement without a reasoned need to reach it.

**Sources / sufficiency:** [Complete official Ohio majority and Wright dissent](../sources/chunk4/McIntyre_67_Ohio_St3d_391.txt), including the challenged provision quoted in relevant part; applicable entering-law cases. Sufficient for the individual's leaflet-identification challenge and the fine's disposition, not an assertion that every part of the statute has been quoted.
