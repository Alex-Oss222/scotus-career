## Chronology and the two orders

The Fifth Circuit's factual account establishes a $281,025.80 April 1989 judgment for Bennie and Joann Edwards, and a $294,987.88 supersedeas bond with Northbrook as surety. Northbrook also insured Celotex and used insurance proceeds remaining payable under a coverage settlement as security. The tort judgment was affirmed September 20, 1990; no rehearing motion or mandate stay was sought, and the mandate issued October 12, 1990, the day Celotex filed its Chapter 11 petition in Florida. Do not replace “same day” with an unsupported time-of-day priority finding.

On October 17, 1990, the bankruptcy judge entered the §105 order. Its paragraph 3, as reproduced in Fifth Circuit footnote 1, states:

> Notwithstanding any exceptions or limitations to the automatic stay contained in §362(b) of the Code, all Entities are hereby jointly and severally stayed, restrained and enjoined from commencing or continuing any judicial, administrative or other proceeding involving any of the Debtors regardless of (a) who initiated the proceeding, (b) whether the matter is on appeal and a supersedeas bond has been posted by the Debtors or (c) the appellant in an appeal is one of the Debtors.

The Fifth Circuit majority and Jones concurrence construed that wording as not reaching a separate proceeding against nondebtor Northbrook. The majority nevertheless acknowledged the bankruptcy court's contrary construction and then addressed power as well. Its rehearing explanation again relied principally on its reading of the order's reach. That is a dispute about the order's scope, not an agreed premise that everyone had deliberately disobeyed an admittedly applicable order.

The bankruptcy court's June 13 opinion describes an intervening **January 10, 1991** order that denied a motion challenging jurisdiction over nondebtor property and lifted the stay in part so pending appeals could proceed, while retaining protection for the bonds. On **May 3, 1991**, before the June omnibus order, the Edwardses moved in the Northern District of Texas under Rule 65.1 to enforce Northbrook's bond. Celotex and Northbrook opposed on stay grounds. The district court did not grant enforcement until **May 27, 1992**, after the omnibus order.

The **June 13, 1991** decretal provisions expressly distinguish three settings:

1. While the relevant appellate process proceeds, the bankruptcy court treats the supersedeas bond as estate property and says §362 stays judgment enforcement against it.
2. Where that court granted relief to complete an appeal and the judgment creditor then prevailed through completion, the creditor is precluded from proceeding against a supersedeas bond without first seeking to vacate the §105 stay in the bankruptcy court.
3. Where the appellate process had already concluded when the petition was filed, the creditor is likewise precluded from proceeding against a bond posted by the debtor without first seeking to vacate the §105 stay there.

The omnibus order denies the bond-release motion except for prior orders allowing completion of appeals, and states: “The Section 105 stay entered by this Court on October 17, 1990, continues in effect.” Its discussion says that, on a successful judgment creditor's motion for relief, **the debtor bears the burden to establish that the §105 stay should continue**. It identifies possible inquiries into avoidance, surety contracts/collateral, and reorganization effects without treating that list as exhaustive. It does not say that every possible avoidance action has already succeeded, that every bond remains estate property forever, or that the stay ends automatically when the appeal ends.

Thus analysis of what was operative at the May 27, 1992 enforcement order must account for both the October order's quoted language and the June order's express coverage and continuation. It cannot stop at the October language as though June 13 came later than the Texas enforcement order. Whether the later order validly binds these creditors and whether its requirements can be challenged collaterally remain legal questions; this chronology does not decide them.

## Subject-matter jurisdiction, adjudicative procedure, and injunction power

Section **1334(b)** provides original **but not exclusive** district-court jurisdiction over civil proceedings “arising under title 11, or arising in or related to cases under title 11.” It is distinct from §1334(a)'s exclusive jurisdiction over the bankruptcy case and from the exclusive estate-property jurisdiction then designated **§1334(d)**. The House 1994 text redesignates the property subsection as (e), but its effective-date note says the 1994 amendments do not apply to title 11 cases commenced before October 22, 1994. Celotex commenced in 1990. Preserve the pre-amendment subsection designation when explaining the statute applicable to this case and the Fifth Circuit's references. The operative §1334(b) wording was not changed by the 1994 amendments shown in the official notes.

Section **157(a)** allows district-court referral of bankruptcy cases and proceedings to bankruptcy judges. Under **§157(b)(1)** a referred core proceeding arising under title 11 or in a title 11 case may be heard and determined by the bankruptcy judge, who may enter appropriate orders and judgments subject to §158 review. The June 13 court classified the bond-property determination as core under §157(b)(2)(A), (G), and (O). That is the lower court's classification, not an independent conclusion adopted by this supplement. Section 157(b)(3) assigns the core/noncore determination to the bankruptcy judge on the judge's own motion or timely party motion and forbids a noncore determination based solely on the possibility that state law affects resolution.

If a proceeding is **not core but otherwise related**, **§157(c)(1)** permits the bankruptcy judge to hear it but requires proposed findings of fact and conclusions of law submitted to the district court. A final order or judgment is entered by the district judge after considering those proposals and reviewing **de novo the matters to which a party timely and specifically objects**. The statute does not say every proposition receives de novo review without an objection. Under the separate **§157(c)(2)** exception, the district court, **with all parties' consent**, may refer a related proceeding for the bankruptcy judge to hear, determine, and enter appropriate orders and judgments, subject to §158 review. No all-parties consent is established in the supplied Celotex record; neither consent nor its absence may be invented.

A finding of §1334(b) relatedness therefore does not, standing alone, dispose of §157 classification, final-order procedure, consent, or the merits of a particular injunction. Conversely, an objection to a bankruptcy judge's final-order authority under §157 does not by its label establish that the district court lacks all related-proceeding subject-matter jurisdiction. Section 105's remedial authority is not an independent grant of subject-matter jurisdiction.

The source also supplies §157(b)(5)'s district-court trial allocation for personal-injury and wrongful-death claims and §157(b)(2)(B)'s exception for liquidating or estimating contingent/unliquidated personal-injury or wrongful-death claims for distribution. Those restrictions should not be confused with deciding whether to preserve the status quo concerning already-entered tort judgments and bonds. The new §157(e) jury-trial provision is a 1994 amendment not applicable to this 1990 bankruptcy under the official transition note and is not used here.

## Available direct relief and review

The June 13 order itself supplies a route to the issuing bankruptcy court to seek relief from the §105 stay, with Celotex bearing the identified continuation burden. A party may challenge jurisdiction, classification/procedure, statutory injunction authority, duration, and particular coverage through that proceeding; no assumption that such a challenge would prevail or fail is supplied.

Section **158(a)** provides district-court review of bankruptcy judges' final judgments, orders, and decrees and, with leave, interlocutory orders and decrees, with appeal taken only to the district court for the judicial district in which the bankruptcy judge serves. For this case that ordinary direct route is the **Middle District of Florida**, followed, when the statutory finality requirements are met, by **Eleventh Circuit** review under **§158(d)**. Section 158(d) gives courts of appeals jurisdiction over final decisions, judgments, orders, and decrees entered under §158(a) and (b); it does not grant an unconditional immediate court-of-appeals appeal from every bankruptcy ruling. A bankruptcy appellate panel path requires the statutory institutional and party-consent predicates; this record establishes no applicable panel and none is inferred. The 1994 publication's expanded panel/election provisions and new §158(a)(2) must not be retroactively applied to the 1990 case. The official amendment note supplies the prior (a) wording and the unchanged district-court/interlocutory and final appellate structure described here.

Section 157(d) separately permits withdrawal of a referral for cause on the district court's own motion or timely party motion and requires withdrawal on a timely motion when resolution requires the specified consideration of both title 11 and other federal laws regulating organizations or activities affecting interstate commerce. This is a different procedure from appealing an injunction and has its own predicates.

Rule 65.1, as quoted in the Fifth Circuit's footnote 2, permits a surety's liability to be enforced on motion without an independent action and supplies the surety's submission to the bond court's jurisdiction. That procedural mechanism does not itself resolve the effect of a separate bankruptcy injunction. Nor does a direct-review route by itself settle whether the challenged order is lawful. A collateral-obedience analysis must preserve that distinction and the jurisdictional qualifications in its controlling entering-law authorities.
