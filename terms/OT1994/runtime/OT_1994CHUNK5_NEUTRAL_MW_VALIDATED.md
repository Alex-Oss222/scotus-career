## 52. California Department of Corrections v. Morales — No. 93-1462

### Identity, route, scope, and relief

The contemplated event is a merits decision on April 25, 1995; argument occurred January 9, 1995. The matter arrives from *Morales v. California Department of Corrections*, 16 F.3d 1001 (9th Cir. February 9, 1994), No. 92-56262, reviewing the Central District of California's denial of a habeas petition. The admitted merits posture is given; this packet does not reconstruct or adjudicate a petition-stage event. The current roster is Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. No particular nonparticipation is established by the permitted materials.

The federal question is whether California may apply its 1981 amendment allowing an individually justified postponement of annual parole reconsideration, for at most three years, to an inmate whose crime preceded the amendment. The State asks reversal; Morales asks affirmance. The Ninth Circuit reversed the denial of the ex post facto claim and ordered judgment for Morales requiring the Board to follow the law at the time of his crime. It affirmed rejection of his claims about allegedly false information and an unlawful sentence. Its disposition expressly did not approve or disapprove release on parole. Those rejected claims supply background, not additional merits questions presented for present review.

The minimum distinct questions are: what the amendment actually changes; how its individualized finding requirement bears on the loss of a previously mandatory annual opportunity; whether any legally available, meaningful interim review changes that loss; and what proof of increased punishment the Constitution requires. Any disposition must specify its consequence for the Ninth Circuit's hearing-frequency relief, without converting review of a hearing schedule into an entitlement to release.

### Statute and chronology in their own terms

At the time of the 1980 offense, the operative baseline required an annual hearing after refusal to set parole. The petition, printed page 3, quotes the 1977 language: “The board shall hear each case annually thereafter.” The 1981 session law is reproduced in the joint appendix. Its amendment to § 3041.5(b)(2) provides:

> The board shall hear each case annually thereafter, except the board may schedule the next hearing no later than three years after any hearing at which parole is denied if the prisoner has been convicted, in the same or different proceedings, of more than one offense which involves the taking of a life, and the board finds that it is not reasonable to expect that parole would be granted at a hearing during the following years and states the bases for the finding.

This is a permission, not a command to postpone every qualifying inmate. Annual consideration remains the default. Both the multiple-life-taking-offenses condition and an individualized predictive finding with stated bases are required. The outer interval is three years after the denial hearing; the statute permits a shorter postponement. It does not itself say that the inmate may demand reconsideration on new facts at any time.

The surrounding provision requires a written statement within 20 days after a hearing at which no date is set, giving the reasons and suggesting beneficial activities. The appendix also supplies procedures for file review before the hearing, a written response, attendance and a statement at the hearing, questions, a hearing record, and representation when another law requires it. These protections concern the hearing that occurs. Their existence does not by itself establish an opportunity during a postponed year.

The 1982, 1990, and 1994 amendments reproduced in the appendix must not be collapsed into the challenged 1981 provision. The 1982 legislation extended two-year postponements more broadly. The 1990 provision had its own offense-date transition and provisions for a five-year postponement and later file review. The 1994 enactment, effective January 1, 1995 according to the supplied session-law material, includes its own two-to-five-year criteria and review mechanism when a five-year period is selected. It is not a textual source for an interim-review guarantee in the 1981 enactment. No present ruling on a later amendment, its application to Morales, or mootness is established by this packet. If a later amendment is to alter the present dispute, its applicable transition, actual application, and source admission must be established first.

### Record facts and their status

Morales was convicted of first-degree murder in 1971. After release from a halfway house and parole in 1980, his wife disappeared; he later pleaded no contest to her second-degree murder and received fifteen years to life. His earliest parole-eligibility date was August 2, 1990. At his initial 1989 hearing the Board denied parole and deferred the next hearing for three years. The Ninth Circuit dates that hearing July 25; the petition and merits briefs say July 15. The quoted suitability report is dated August 22, 1989. The precise hearing-day discrepancy is unresolved and should be disclosed if a day is published, rather than silently reconciled. The year, denial, and three-year interval are common ground.

The State quotes the Board's reasons for unsuitability: the particularly cruel offense and mutilation; violent criminal history and failure on parole; limited constructive institutional programming; and psychiatric assessments identifying a continuing threat and need for treatment and observation. The separately stated postponement reasons were the offense's nature and resulting need for observation, a prior first-degree murder, the longer assessment warranted by his criminal history, and psychiatric evaluations calling for longer observation, evaluation, or treatment. These are Board findings as reproduced in the petition and State's brief. They are not findings that release in an intervening year was legally impossible. Morales contests whether these largely overlapping grounds meaningfully establish futility of another hearing.

The record supports distinguishing the following propositions:

* The State asserts that the parole matrix and ordinary date-setting process make earlier release highly unlikely. Its reliance on a nineteen-year matrix term is not proof of a mandatory minimum of nineteen years: the appendix's regulation permits a different term justified by individual circumstances and requires reasons for that choice. The minimum eligibility date is also distinct from a Board-set release date.
* Morales argues that presentence credit and possible discretionary credits could yield a materially earlier release date. The State disputes the proper use of those credits and whether they would actually be awarded. No actual earlier release date or entitlement to those discretionary credits is established.
* The State cites aggregate rejection rates described in *In re Jackson*: about 90 percent at initial suitability hearings and about 85 percent thereafter. These derive from legislative analyses described by the sources, not an independently supplied dataset about this statutory cohort. They are neither Morales-specific findings nor a measured probability that his postponement would prolong custody.
* There is an important distinction between no right to release and no meaningful right to consideration. The absence of a vested parole right does not itself resolve the federal claim under the actual entering law.

### Actual entering law and date-eligible authorities

**Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993, is binding actual law.** The original candidate's statement that there is no direct case-specific Supreme Court rule is superseded. The precise public reading copies are `../entering-law/OT_1994CHUNK5_CAVANAUGH_MERITS.md`, the parole-frequency standard in `OT_1994CHUNK5_A.md`, and the Cavanaugh public writings in `OT_1994CHUNK5_A_PUBLIC_WRITINGS.md`.

Cavanaugh holds that retrospectively removing alternate mandatory annual opportunities for discretionary parole consideration, replacing them with an unqualified two-year interval where no retained ordinary interim review was established, makes punishment more onerous. The prisoner need not prove that the omitted hearing would have resulted in release. Unchanged initial eligibility, maximum sentence, and ultimate release discretion do not independently answer the loss of a binding meaningful opportunity. A limited medical-release route, conditioned on a life expectancy of one year or less, did not replace ordinary review. Cavanaugh expressly left open individualized postponement supported by findings, meaningful interim review, changes in voting or suitability rules, and other distinct mechanisms. It did not adopt a rule that every procedural disadvantage or any possible postponement is unconstitutional, nor a general numerical probability formula.

O'Connor's controlling merits writing was joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, and Souter. Scalia and Thomas opposed its merits result, distinguishing mandatory gain-time and binding sentencing ranges from discretionary parole and requiring a further demonstrated punitive operation. Ginsburg conditionally agreed with the constitutional conclusion but maintained a separate objection to the procedural vehicle; she did not join the Court's Parts II or III and supplies no seventh vote for their rationale. Cavanaugh's § 1983 holding permitted prospective consideration-frequency relief when the complaint sought neither release nor an entitlement to shorter custody. Morales is already in habeas; that prior vehicle dispute must not be imported as an unraised obstacle or converted into a current vote.

The complete official predivergence opinions additionally establish:

* **Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (February 24, 1981).** Marshall's Court opinion, joined by Brennan, Stewart, White, Powell, and Stevens, held that retrospectively reduced mandatory gain-time credits made punishment more onerous. The test concerns the law's operation, not a vested right or whether the provision is formally part of the sentence. New discretionary credits did not substitute for lost mandatory credits. The decision does not equate every administrative change with a change in punishment.
* **Miller v. Florida, 482 U.S. 423 (June 9, 1987).** O'Connor wrote for a unanimous Court, including Stevens and Scalia. A retrospectively higher binding presumptive sentencing range violated the Clause although the statutory maximum and possible ultimate sentence were unchanged. The old departure process required specified reasons and proof and allowed review. The opinion distinguished genuinely flexible guideposts. Mere advance warning that a law might change did not authorize retroactive harsher punishment.
* **Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (June 21, 1990).** Rehnquist's Court opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy, confined the inquiry to the Ex Post Facto Clause's recognized criminal-law concerns. A procedure allowing removal of an unauthorized fine, while leaving a valid conviction and authorized imprisonment, did not increase punishment. It rejected a free-standing rule against all retrospective procedural disadvantages and displaced the broader approaches in Kring and Thompson v. Utah. Stevens's concurrence in the judgment, joined by Brennan and Marshall, defended protection against substantial procedural deprivations but found no such deprivation there. That concurrence is evidence of Stevens's own reasoning, not the Court's controlling formulation.

### Competing lawful paths and required answers

**Apply Cavanaugh to affirm the hearing-frequency relief.** A possible reading is that California removes a binding annual opportunity on the strength of a prediction made before circumstances develop, without an established ordinary route to reconsider that prediction. An initial finding of present unsuitability and a reasoned longer-term prediction may remain insufficient substitutes for a later opportunity. The strongest objection is that Cavanaugh deliberately reserved individualized findings and that this statute operates only on a defined class after such findings. An affirming analysis must explain why those additional safeguards still leave a material binding opportunity lost; it cannot rely only on the proposition that more hearings necessarily yield more releases or invalidate every minor scheduling change.

**Distinguish Cavanaugh on individualized postponement.** A possible reading gives legal weight to the statutory annual default, the defined multiple-offense condition, a hearing already held, and a specific reasoned determination concerning the intervening years. The strongest objection is that a finding predicting no release cannot by itself restore the mandatory future opportunity Cavanaugh protected. A reversing analysis must explain why these findings and the amendment's operation differ in kind or legal effect from Cavanaugh's unqualified loss. It may not adopt the State's broad suggestion that discretionary parole can never involve protected punishment or require proof that the prisoner necessarily would have been released; those propositions conflict with the actual controlling rule unless the Court expressly reconsiders it.

**Resolve a material missing operational premise through further proceedings.** If availability and adequacy of interim review are necessary to the chosen rule, the Court can distinguish the statute from counsel's unsupported practice assertion and identify the unresolved premise for a limited remand. Such a disposition must say what legal question or factual development remains, whether existing frequency relief survives, and what the lower court is to do. It must not invent the practice or decide that a letter, initial appeal, or collateral writ is equivalent to an ordinary hearing.

Each path must keep relief at the hearing-frequency level. Neither affirmance nor a limited remand establishes suitability for release, a release date, damages, or the validity of later statutory amendments.

## 53. United States v. Williams — No. 94-395

### Identity, route, question, and relief

The contemplated event is a merits decision on April 25, 1995; argument occurred February 22, 1995 according to the supplied neutral identity material. The judgment under review is *Williams v. United States*, 24 F.3d 1143 (9th Cir. May 19, 1994), No. 92-56207. The district court dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction on September 2, 1992, entered September 3. The Ninth Circuit reversed and remanded. Both complete lower opinions are in the clean petition appendix, printed pages 1a–18a. No nonparticipation is established by the permitted materials.

The question is whether 28 U.S.C. § 1346(a)(1) allows Williams to sue for refund of money she paid to remove federal tax liens from property she was selling when the underlying taxes were assessed against her former husband and she was not personally liable for them. The Government seeks reversal and a jurisdictional dismissal; Williams seeks affirmance allowing the suit to proceed. Jurisdiction over this refund suit, the validity of the liens, the validity of the husband's underlying assessments, and the amount recoverable are different questions. No merits refund judgment is before the Court.

### Relevant statutory language and distinct mechanisms

**Section 1346(a)(1), title 28**, gives the district courts original jurisdiction, concurrent with the Court of Federal Claims, of:

> Any civil action against the United States for the recovery of any internal-revenue tax alleged to have been erroneously or illegally assessed or collected, or any penalty claimed to have been collected without authority or any sum alleged to have been excessive or in any manner wrongfully collected under the internal-revenue laws.

**Section 7422(a), title 26**, prohibits maintaining a suit for those recoveries until a refund or credit claim has been duly filed with the Secretary according to governing law and regulations. Section 7422(b) separately permits maintaining a suit or proceeding whether or not payment was made under protest or duress. That latter provision does not itself answer the eligible-plaintiff question, but it prevents silently making formal protest or duress a universal statutory prerequisite.

**Section 6511(a)** provides the administrative time limit for a claim concerning a tax for which the taxpayer must file a return: the taxpayer must file within three years of filing the return or two years of payment, whichever expires later; if no return was filed, within two years of payment. Its separate stamp-tax sentence requires the taxpayer's claim within three years of payment. The dispute is whether the use of “taxpayer” also restricts who can invoke the refund machinery. **Section 7701(a)(14)** defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax.” The definitions govern title 26 unless otherwise distinctly expressed or manifestly incompatible with the statutory intent. Neither the absence of “taxpayer” from § 1346(a)(1) nor its presence in § 6511 can be assessed without that cross-statutory dispute.

The other remedies have distinct triggers:

* **28 U.S.C. § 2410(a)(1):** a suit to quiet title to property on which the United States has or claims a lien. It is not an automatic refund action after proceeds have been paid over.
* **26 U.S.C. § 7426(a)(1):** a wrongful-levy action by an interested person other than the person against whom the underlying tax was assessed. A lien and a levy are different acts. No levy occurred here, and the direct wrongful-levy route cannot be assumed to apply merely because a lien existed.
* **Section 6325(b)(3):** the Secretary may discharge a lien on sold property under an agreement that holds proceeds as a fund subject to federal liens and claims with the same priority as against the property. “May” and the agreement requirement matter. This is not an unconditional statutory command to provide a discharge on demand.
* **Sections 7426(a)(3) and (b)(4):** following that substitute-fund arrangement, an interested claimant may litigate rights in the fund, and the court may award all or part of the amount to which the claimant is entitled. These provisions do not establish that Williams actually obtained such an agreement or that her payment became such a fund.
* **Section 7426(c):** for adjudication under § 7426, the underlying tax assessment is conclusively presumed valid. This limit must not be silently attached to § 1346(a)(1), nor erased when describing the Government's argument that the specialized remedy would be bypassed.

These are the core statutory provisions quoted or discussed in the complete merits briefs and official Dalm opinion. Exhaustion of a refund claim is distinct from the Government's argument that third-party remedies were the only available routes. A choice of another possible route is not automatically a statutory exhaustion duty.

### Facts, competing submissions, and reserved merits

Williams and Rabin owned a home. Tax liabilities were assessed against Rabin in connection with a partnership. He transferred his interest to Williams by an October 20, 1988 agreement and a quitclaim deed recorded October 25. The first notice of federal tax lien was recorded November 10; additional assessment and notice dates differ among the components of the asserted debt. In 1989 she arranged to sell the house, and the IRS recorded a nominee lien shortly before closing. She authorized payment from escrow to clear title, filed an administrative refund claim, and sued after denial. Her lack of personal liability for the underlying partnership taxes is common ground.

The asserted coercion is a pending sale, a threat of buyer litigation or failed closing, and the need to remove the liens from property she claimed as her own. It does not rest on an actual levy. The Government argues that she acquired property already burdened by tax liens and had a substitute-collateral route; Williams argues that she provided adequate consideration, owned the property free of the asserted liens, and lacked a realistic timely alternative. Those competing merits and practical assertions must retain their source status.

In particular, the Ninth Circuit says the Government conceded in open court that its 1989 lien was invalid. Williams's brief describes an ultimate stipulation of wrongful collection. The June 8, 1992 hearing includes counsel's and the judge's statements about agreed facts and erroneous collection, and characterizes the matter as a trial on stipulated facts concerning standing. The appendix contains competing proposed findings with different legal conclusions. The Government's reply, page 4 note 2 continuing onto page 5, expressly disputes the claimed concession concerning lien validity and also disputes Williams's assertions that she was unaware of the substitute-fund provision and received no suggestion of it from the IRS. The record must not be flattened into either a universal concession of invalidity or an adjudication that every lien was valid. The scope of any binding concession can remain for remand if the Court decides only the legal availability of a refund suit.

The sources also differ on the original house-acquisition year: the appellate opinion says April 1975; the district opinion and proposed facts say April 1987. Claimed refund and escrow figures are not uniform: the district complaint amount is $41,369.58, the appellate opinion rounds to $41,369, and appendix submissions include $41,937.04 and a separate $1,308.30 item. The neutral question requires actual payment claimed to have been wrongfully collected, not resolution of these arithmetic differences. A jurisdiction-only decision should not publish a fixed recovery without reconciling the underlying components.

The district court acknowledged that jurisdiction was the dispositive threshold in the submitted posture and dismissed under its reading of sovereign immunity. The Ninth Circuit adopted Martin's plain-language reading of § 1346(a)(1), distinguished Nordic Village's different text, rejected the contrary circuit reading, and ordered further proceedings. The lower-court descriptions of preexisting Supreme Court law are arguments and sources in this case, not a substitute for the actual postdivergence law.

### Parties' strongest grounds and conditional alternatives

**Government.** Consent to sue the United States must be unequivocal. Section 1346(a)(1) must be read with § 7422(a), § 6511(a), and the Code's definition of taxpayer. In this reading only the person subject to the tax liability can file the claim that unlocks refund jurisdiction. Williams was never that person. The statutes created specified remedies for nonassessed owners, including quiet-title, wrongful-levy, and substitute-fund proceedings; allowing ordinary refund suits would let third parties bypass their limits and disturb final collection, priority, and limitation rules. The Government does not rely on statutory language declaring every § 7426 remedy expressly exclusive: its reply, pages 11–12, argues exclusivity because no other remedy was authorized. Its substitute-fund argument admits statutory discretion, while denying any showing of abuse here.

The Government answers the “assessed or collected” argument by giving examples of taxes lawfully assessed but unlawfully collected, including collections outside the limitation period. It also distinguishes the assessment that enables administrative enforcement from tax liability or a judicial collection suit; a formal assessment is not always indispensable to the latter. Thus its narrow interpretation need not make every collection reference redundant. It denies that payment alone transforms a person into a taxpayer and rejects a text-free distinction between a true volunteer and someone who could have used substitute collateral.

**Williams.** The broad words of § 1346(a)(1) and § 7422 encompass money alleged to have been unlawfully collected from her and do not expressly confine suits to assessed persons. Section 6511 supplies timing requirements, not an unstated exclusion of this claimant; she filed a timely administrative claim. Alternatively, a person whose own property and payment are subjected to tax collection can fall within “subject to” an internal-revenue tax for this limited purpose. The title 26 definition cannot simply be read into title 28 without examining the connected provisions and purpose.

Williams distinguishes an unsolicited voluntary payment for another from an IRS collection against property claimed by the payer. Quiet title before a rapidly approaching closing, waiting for a levy, and a discretionary substitute-fund agreement were, she argues, not adequate remedies in her circumstances. She invokes avoidance of a serious remedial gap and Stahmann's treatment of a nonvolunteer whose goods were held to secure another assessment. Those arguments do not establish the disputed facts about her knowledge, IRS advice, the validity of the lien, or the amount due. They also do not authorize a constitutional damages claim or automatically open refund jurisdiction to every person who pays another's tax.

### Actual law and verified primary authorities

**United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., actual February 25, 1992 decision.** The public entering-law slice `OT_1994CHUNK5_B.md` supplies the rule: general bankruptcy jurisdiction did not itself waive federal immunity from an affirmative money judgment under the then-applicable § 106(c). Plausible readings limited to nonmonetary determinations prevented finding unequivocal consent to that monetary remedy; legislative history could not repair the missing clarity. The different waivers in § 106(a) and (b), identified-property claims, and defensive recoupment were kept distinct. Scalia's Court opinion was joined by Stone, White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Its force here depends on identifying the scope of the express tax-refund waiver, not assuming that a plausible litigating position always defeats every express waiver regardless of statutory context.

**United States v. California, actual April 26, 1993 decision.** O'Connor's unanimous Court opinion, available in the same slice, declined to create a federal common-law refund right merely because the United States reimbursed a contractor's state taxes. Subrogation supplied only the contractor's existing right, with its existing infirmity, and did not revive an expired claim. It preserved direct federal tax and express congressional-remedy questions. It cautions against creating a remedy outside governing law but does not decide Williams's express statutory claim.

**United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (March 20, 1990), complete official opinion.** Kennedy's Court opinion, joined by Rehnquist, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, and Scalia, held that an independent gift-tax refund action filed after the statutory claim period lacked jurisdiction; equitable recoupment did not create jurisdiction absent an independently timely action. Section 1346(a)(1)'s broad wording must be read with § 7422(a)'s claim requirement and § 6511's time limit. Bull and Stone v. White involved recoupment in proceedings with an independent jurisdictional basis. The meaning of overpayment includes payments exceeding what is properly due, regardless of the reason, and the limitation runs from payment rather than later discovery. The decision did not decide whether a nonassessed person who timely makes an administrative claim may sue for money collected from that person's property. It did not decide whether the Tax Court itself could have entertained the particular recoupment claim.

Stevens's dissent, joined by Brennan and Marshall, read Bull and the broad wrongfully-collected language to provide relief from inconsistent double taxation, emphasized the unusual pay-first structure and effective remedy, and objected to enlarging immunity through an artificial characterization of the claim. That dissent does not displace the controlling claim and timing requirements, but it supplies Stevens's own date-eligible reasoning. Neither side may turn Dalm's actual late-claim holding into an already-decided answer to Williams's plaintiff-identity question.

**Stahmann v. Vidal, 305 U.S. 61 (November 7, 1938), complete official opinion.** Roberts's Court opinion held that cotton producers could maintain an action against the collector after paying a tax assessed against the ginner in order to obtain their cotton. They had made a timely refund claim and timely sued under the governing predecessor statute. The Bankhead Act was designed to place the burden on producers and to immobilize their cotton until payment; the Court expressly described that scheme as setting the case apart. It also said the producers were not volunteers because payment was made under duress of goods, whether or not the tax was imposed on them. These are both parts of the actual reasoning. Williams can argue for the nonvolunteer principle; the Government can argue for the tax's intended incidence and the distinct collector-action setting. Neither may erase the other part, assume that Stahmann construed the present statutory combination, or use it as a decision on the underlying tax's constitutionality, which remained for further proceedings. Reed did not participate; no present Justice participated.

### Lawful paths and what each must resolve

**Affirm jurisdiction within a stated scope.** The Court could read the express refund waiver, together with duly satisfied claim requirements, to cover a payment collected through liens on property the payer claims as her own despite another person's assessment. This must explain why the connected “taxpayer” provisions do not exclude Williams, why specialized third-party remedies do not foreclose this claim, and how the sovereign-immunity rule is satisfied by text. A narrow holding must identify its actual trigger rather than promise a remedy for every volunteer. It must preserve ordinary merits defenses and specify that further proceedings concern lien validity, any binding concession, wrongfulness, and the amount, as necessary.

**Reverse for lack of waiver as to this claimant.** The Court could treat the connected statutes as restricting the refund route to the person liable for the tax, leaving others to the specifically supplied remedies. It must explain the broad “collected” and “any sum” language, address Stahmann's actual rationale, and explain why timely submission of a claim does not satisfy the relevant requirement for this plaintiff. The strongest objection is that an express collection-based waiver would then leave this asserted coerced payer without this remedy although no levy occurred. The analysis may uphold a statutory boundary despite hardship, but may not falsely say that a levy occurred, that § 6325 commanded an agreement, or that the Government proved an available agreement and timely practical remedy in this case.

**A limited clarification or remand.** If a narrower statutory rule makes the nature of collection or a concession essential, the Court may identify the legal rule and reserve factual application. It should not first decide refund merits merely to classify the plaintiff. Conversely, if the plaintiff category is resolved as a legal matter on undisputed payment and nonliability, existing disputes over liens and amounts need not block the threshold decision.

No path in this packet decides the validity of the husband's assessments, awards a fixed sum, prescribes interest or fees, establishes damages, or treats every alternative statutory remedy as a mandatory exhaustion step. Those require their own legal grounds and record support.
