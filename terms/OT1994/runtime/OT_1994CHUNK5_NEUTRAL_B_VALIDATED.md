## 54. United States v. Lopez — No. 93-1260

### Judgment under review, record, and distinct remedial questions

The complete Fifth Circuit opinion is 2 F.3d 1342, No. 92-5641, September 15, 1993, reproduced at petition Appendix A, 1a–54a and in `tmp/ot1994_chunk5_b/lopez_lower_IA.txt`. On March 10, 1992, Lopez, a twelfth-grade student, brought an unloaded .38 handgun and five bullets to Edison High School in San Antonio. The lower opinion reports a statement that he was to deliver it after school for $40 for a gang conflict. That report does not turn paid delivery into an element of the possession offense or establish that every member of the regulated class is engaged in a commercial transaction. The parties stipulated that an ATF agent would testify the weapon was manufactured outside Texas; the indictment did not allege a commerce nexus.

State charges were dropped when federal prosecution followed. The district court rejected the commerce-power objection and, after a bench trial on stipulated evidence, imposed six months' imprisonment and two years' supervised release. The appellate judgment reversed and remanded with instructions to dismiss the indictment. Respondent's merits brief, p4 n2, describes the district court's subsequent dismissal and identifies his otherwise unresolved sentencing objections: the length of supervised release and the special assessment, based on §924(a)(4)'s direction that the offense be deemed a misdemeanor for other-law purposes. A decision rejecting his constitutional ground therefore does not automatically establish the validity of every sentence component.

The lower judgment contains two matters a merits decision must account for. It held the Act unsupported by adequate commerce findings or legislative foundation for its full reach, but expressly did not decide that Congress could never regulate school-zone possession, that similar legislation with an adequate foundation could not survive, or that every possible application was unconstitutional. It also held that, if a commerce nexus were necessary to sustain the offense, proof of this gun's travel could not cure an indictment omitting that necessary allegation. It invoked *Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212, and *Russell v. United States*, 369 U.S. 749, and directed dismissal. Respondent renews the indictment point in merits pp18–20 and associated notes. A judgment reversing only a supposed categorical findings requirement must still explain its treatment of that ground.

### Offense actually enacted and charged

The controlling charging-period text is original §922(q)(1)(A), not the renumbered 1994 provision or a later jurisdictional-element statute: an individual may not knowingly possess a firearm at a place the individual knows or has reasonable cause to believe is a school zone. A zone includes the grounds of a public, parochial or private school and the area within 1,000 feet; a school provides elementary or secondary education as determined under state law. There is no express commerce element in this possession subsection.

The seven possession exemptions must travel with any statement of statutory breadth: private property outside school grounds; a relevant state or political-subdivision license issued under a law requiring prior law-enforcement verification of the individual's qualification; an unloaded firearm in a locked container, or in a locked firearm rack on a motor vehicle; use in a school-approved program; possession under a contract with a school by the individual or the individual's employer; official law-enforcement possession; and an unloaded firearm carried across school premises to hunting lands with school authorities' permission to enter. The lower court found none applicable here.

The separate discharge prohibition requires knowing discharge or attempted discharge, or reckless disregard for another's safety, at a place known to be a school zone. Its four exemptions are private property outside school grounds, participation in a school-approved program, the school-contract situation, and official law enforcement. Its exemption list is not the possession list. The original nonpreemption paragraph preserves state/local school-zone enactments. This possession charge does not itself decide every discharge application.

Original §924(a)(4) allowed up to five years, a fine of no more than $5,000, or both; any imprisonment could not run concurrently with any term imposed under another provision; except for the authorized five-year maximum, a §922(q) violation was deemed a misdemeanor for purposes of any other law. The 1994 Code's general-fine wording is a later amendment, not the original fine clause. See the source supplement.

Pub. L. 103-322 §320904, September 13, 1994, added findings and renumbered the original paragraphs. Its full findings concern the interstate movement of guns, ammunition, drugs and gangs; effects of crime on travel; school violence, education and commerce; and interstate limits on local enforcement. The Government's September supplemental submission expressly says enactment does not resolve the conflict over prosecutions under the existing statute. The new findings are an enacted predecision source, not a retroactive new indictment or an automatically cured constitutional defect. Admission of the enactment as needed remains the operator's task.

### Supported legal paths and their counterarguments

**Sustain the original offense under the commerce power.** The Government invokes class-based regulation and rational legislative judgment: school gun violence affects interstate travel and national insurance costs, and impairs education and the productivity of the national workforce. Its opening merits summary and complete reply, pp1–16, argue that formal findings are not an indispensable condition of commerce legislation and that courts may consider the legislative setting and rationally predictable aggregate consequences. The complete lower opinion discusses the contrary applications of *Wickard*, *Perez*, *Heart of Atlanta*, *McClung*, and *Hodel*. Their verified propositions concern aggregation, rational basis, permissible means and the absence of a universal formal-findings requirement; they do not supply a holding already sustaining this exact statute. Congress regulates private persons here; the Government argues the local-license and school-program exceptions preserve local choices without compelling state legislation.

The strongest contrary argument is that crime costs and educational productivity connect virtually every local act to commerce through several causal steps, with no statutory nexus or commercial-class boundary preventing a general police power. A sustaining rationale must identify why this class and this means fall within an enumerated power. It may not treat the mere desirability of safe schools, a federal spending program, or the interstate origin of all objects as a self-proving source of commerce jurisdiction. If the original statute is held valid without a nexus element, the indictment's omission of a nonrequired element would not sustain dismissal; remaining sentencing issues still need a remand or an independently supported resolution.

**Invalidate the possession provision as applied through this indictment, with a stated scope.** The lower opinion and respondent's merits argument present the lack of an express nexus, local possession, traditional state responsibilities, attenuation, and absence of an adequate contemporaneous legislative foundation. A constitutional ruling must explain why the existing commerce cases do not cover this statute rather than simply label education or crime exclusively local. *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, recognizes federalism and a clear-statement construction in the particular regulation of state judicial qualifications. The actual *New York v. United States* preserves *Garcia* and generally applicable regulation of state activity while barring compulsory state regulation/ownership; it does not make every federal criminal law in a traditional state field invalid. *Allied-Bruce* recognizes broad actual commercial activity while expressly reserving the outer constitutional boundary and remote interstate history alone. Whether an economic/non-economic line is adopted here remains an issue; it is not supplied as an already controlling rule.

The strongest contrary argument is that *Wickard* and *Perez* permit regulation of a class notwithstanding a trivial individual instance, and a judicial insistence on formal findings mistakes Congress for a record-bound administrative agency. A narrow invalidation can preserve those cases while explaining the missing commerce connection in this statute. It must state whether it reaches the charged possession provision, the challenged class, or other applications; it cannot silently decide the separate discharge offense or every conceivable school gun regulation.

**Narrow statutory construction and indictment path.** *Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, supplies a federalism-sensitive construction of ambiguous firearms language; the full lower opinion and respondent expressly discuss adding a commerce requirement here. The Government's complete reply maintains the present language is unambiguous and therefore cannot be redrafted by the clear-statement canon; compare *Gregory*'s actual ambiguity and the current *X-Citement Video* division over permissible construction. If an added nexus is legally permissible and necessary, the omitted indictment allegation remains a distinct obstacle under the lower court's *Stirone/Russell* ground; stipulated trial proof does not automatically replace a grand-jury allegation. The packet does not declare this construction available or foreclosed. A remand must identify the indictment and sentencing consequences of the chosen rule, not merely say “further proceedings.”

## 55. Travelers consolidated matters — Nos. 93-1408, 93-1414, 93-1415

### Actual scope, lower mandate, and statutory measures

Supplied merits review concerns ERISA preemption of the hospital differentials in the consolidated New York Blues, Pataki and Hospital Association matters. The complete amended Second Circuit opinion, January 14, 1994, following October 25, 1993 issuance, appears at A1–A34 of docket 93-1414's appendix and in `travelers_lower_IA.txt`. Appellate dockets: 93-7132L, 93-7134CON, 93-7148CON and 93-7194XAP. The district decision is 813 F.Supp.996, on cross-motions for summary judgment.

The lower court held the 13% hospital differential, additional 11% commercial-insurer surcharge, and conditional HMO assessment of up to 9% preempted by ERISA. It also sustained FEHBA preemption of the 13% and 11% measures for the experience-rated federal employee plans at issue; rejected the Tax Injunction Act bar after classifying the 11% and 9% charges as taxes but finding no adequate state remedy under its exclusive-ERISA-jurisdiction analysis; and rejected laches. It affirmed preemption of paragraphs 1, 2, 3 and 5 of Actuarial Letter No.5 concerning stop-loss insurance, reversed as to paragraph 4 to find it preempted too, and did not decide unchallenged paragraphs 6–7. The complete mandate was affirmance in part, reversal in part and judgment consistent with the opinion. It was not an undifferentiated affirmance of all claims.

Blues merits p13 n17 expressly identifies FEHBA and the Actuarial Letter as outside this review. Deciding the ERISA surcharge question therefore must not overturn those independent components. The lower Tax Injunction Act jurisdictional analysis is disclosed, not silently adopted as a new holding. No reviewed merits passage establishes a renewed jurisdictional challenge; if jurisdiction is revisited, the operator must obtain the precise preserved jurisdictional submissions and current relevant authority rather than use this packet's merits alternatives as a jurisdictional ruling.

Self-funded-plan applications require more care. Commercial respondents pp48–49 argue that the complaints, district relief and stay included them and that the deemer clause independently defeats the 13% charge if insurance saving otherwise applies. Blues reply pp18 n18–19 acknowledges those applications were covered and says the deemer issue is properly before the Court, but argues that hospital-payor regulation does not deem plans insurers. HANYS reply p3 n2 instead adopts the State's request for remand if deemer resolution is necessary, saying the issue was not decided below. Thus a merits decision may have to choose between resolving that preserved alternative and remanding it; it cannot declare self-funded applications uncontestedly absent or automatically covered by every insurer holding.

The state text, reproduced A101–A113 and fully read, distinguishes: (1) the 13% addition retained by hospitals for listed expense-incurred payors; (2) an additional 11% on the commercial-insurer category, routed to state pools/general fund; and (3) a direct monthly HMO assessment up to 9%, reduced or eliminated through specified Medicaid enrollment/participation conditions. The precise exceptions, targets, alternative rate arrangements, point-of-service rule, annual cap and date boundaries appear in the supplement. “All plans pay 24%” and “all HMOs pay 9%” are both false descriptions.

The appendix's §2807-c(1) reproduction covers January 1988–December 1993; §11(i) expressly addresses discharges April 1, 1992–March 31, 1993 and contains an ellipsis after its total-24% language. The controversy about the challenged charges and lower injunction may be decided on its supplied record without inventing a 1995 command. Otherwise remand preserves these implementation questions.

### Federal text and genuinely distinct paths

Section 514(a), subject to subsection (b), supersedes state laws insofar as they relate to a §4(a) plan not exempt under §4(b). Section 514(b)(2)(A), subject to subparagraph (B), saves state insurance, banking and securities regulation. Section 514(b)(2)(B) prevents a covered nonexempt plan, other than a plan established primarily to provide death benefits, or its trust, from being deemed an insurer/bank/trust or investment company or engaged in insurance/banking for the listed state-regulatory purposes. Coverage, relation, insurance saving and deemer application are separate inquiries.

**No prohibited relation on this record.** *Shaw*, 463 U.S.85, supplies “connection with or reference to” and reserves effects too tenuous, remote or peripheral; *Mackey*, 486 U.S.825, preserves general garnishment despite costs while preempting the provision expressly singling out ERISA plans. The actual *Greater Washington Board of Trade* holding concerns use of covered-plan content as a mandatory legal benchmark, not all indirect economic effects. State/Blues/HANYS argue that the differentials regulate the prices and obligations of providers/payors without ordering ERISA plan benefits, plan structure or administration; both ERISA and non-ERISA customers use each payor category. Differences in market prices and state regulation already prevent identical health-care prices across States. The Blues reply disputes an established finding that its premiums are lower or that plans actually switch providers: cost effects, premium effects, choices and statutory commands are not interchangeable facts.

The strongest contrary path is the lower court's view that targeted, intended and substantial financial pressure on particular coverage arrangements interferes with a core plan choice and nationwide administration even without a literal benefit command. General applicability alone is no immunity; *Shaw* and *FMC*, 498 U.S.52, preempt laws reaching plans along with other entities. A no-preemption rule must say why these effects are insufficient and whether a different record of coercive economic effects or mandated administration could change the answer. It cannot purport to exempt every price law, every provider regulation, or every indirect burden.

**Preemption of one or more charges.** The lower opinion, commercial respondents' summary pp9–14, and HMO respondents pp12–20 present a purposeful-choice and significant-burden account, with HMO respondents separately emphasizing managed-care delivery, constrained capacity, and Medicaid-enrollment pressure. *FMC* addresses a compulsory alteration of plan subrogation and nationwide benefit calculation; Kennedy's *Mackey* dissent identifies onerous multi-state administration as a serious connection, while the majority nonetheless sustained the general procedure. The exact current *Board of Trade* remains a benchmark, but it cannot be expanded merely by calling a price effect a legal requirement. A preempting rationale must identify the legally sufficient connection and explain why routine expenses/taxes/ordinary creditor law remain outside its rule. One may distinguish the direct HMO assessment from hospital charges; equal results on all three require reasoning, not matching percentages.

**Insurance saving for the commercial differentials.** If §514(a) is satisfied, saving remains an independent alternative for the 13%/11% measures. Petitioners stress risk spreading, open enrollment/community rating and access to insurance; respondents stress hospital/provider prices rather than the policy relationship and the inclusion of entities outside insurance. *FMC* supplies the actual saving/deemer architecture and the distinction between indirect regulation of insured plans and direct regulation of self-funded plans. Its discussion of *Metropolitan Life*, 471 U.S.724, distinguishes regulation of policy terms from a mere industry impact. The predecision briefs use the common-sense insurance inquiry and the *Pireno* considerations—risk transfer/spreading, an integral policy relationship, and limitation to insurance-industry entities—as guides; they contest both application and how rigidly those factors operate. Actual *Fabe*, supplied verbatim in the additional slice, protects policy performance and reasonably necessary administration under McCarran-Ferguson's first clause while rejecting tenuous creditor-benefit arguments. It expressly leaves the antitrust clause unchanged. It supports a function-specific inquiry, not the proposition that any public benefit to insurance companies is saved or that the ERISA question has already been answered. A modeler proposing a new categorical rule about the three factors must read the full *Metropolitan Life/Pireno/Royal Drug* opinions before adopting that additional rule. Existing *FMC*, actual *Fabe*, and the complete lower opinion support the bounded alternatives here without silently creating that new rule.

**The 9% assessment has a different preservation map.** HMO merits pp40–41 says petitioners did not preserve insurance saving for the distinct 9% assessment; the SG's earlier suggestion was not pursued as an appeal issue. The dispute whether HMOs perform insurance functions is presented in connection with the 13% differential's reach, and does not itself make a saved-9% disposition available. A modeler must not merge “an HMO may perform an insurance function” with “this 9% Medicaid assessment is preserved and saved.” If the 9% measure relates to covered plans, a different saving disposition needs an independently supported preservation basis.

**Deemer and relief.** If insurer applications are saved, *FMC* presents the separate question whether direct self-funded applications must nevertheless be preempted. The Blues answer that plans are charged as hospital payors, not deemed insurers, and describe direct payment or reimbursement alternatives; respondents rely on the statutory self-insured category and *FMC*. If the Court resolves neither necessary scope nor application facts on this record, remand is supported by HANYS's express alternative. If all surcharges are outside §514(a) on the decided theory, saving/deemer questions may remain unneeded. Any reversal or vacatur must be limited to the ERISA surcharge component decided and account for the independent FEHBA/Actuarial Letter dispositions; no reimbursement amount or newly computed state rate is established here.

## 56. United States v. Harris — supplied No. 94-297

### Record and the three presented claims

The complete lower opinion is *United States v. Harris*, 25 F.3d1275, Fifth Circuit No.93-7554, June 29, 1994, by W. Eugene Davis with Wisdom and Duhe. The supplied Supreme docket and established review are independent simulation inputs. The lower case alone does not establish a historical Supreme act.

The offense occurred December 12, 1992. The borrowed 1976 Buick Electra had been manufactured in Flint, Michigan and titled in Arkansas and Mississippi. Evidence placed Harris at the confrontation with a 12-gauge sawed-off shotgun and Hoskins with an ax handle; they demanded that the occupants leave, Hoskins drove away and Harris chased the occupants. The defendants' gang-dispute/afterthought account and Harris's claimed ignorance of Hoskins's taking were defenses, not accepted findings. The court reviewed sufficiency favorably to the verdict.

Each was convicted of aiding and abetting carjacking and using/carrying a firearm during a crime of violence. Each received **41 months on count one and 60 months on count two, consecutive**. The three supplied claims are commerce power, mens rea/fair notice, and cumulative punishment. The lower court also rejected sufficiency and Rule18 intradistrict venue objections, but those do not automatically enlarge the supplied Supreme questions. No exact jury-charge transcript or full trial indictment is supplied; no claim that a particular instruction omitted a particular word may be invented.

### Original law, not an offense-date substitution

Pub. L.102-519 §101(a),106 Stat.3384, October25,1992, enacted §2119 to punish one who, **possessing a firearm defined in §921**, takes or attempts to take a motor vehicle that has been transported, shipped or received in interstate or foreign commerce, from another's person/presence by force and violence or intimidation. The tiers are up to15 years; if serious bodily injury as defined in §1365 results, up to25; if death results, any term up to life; each permits a fine or both fine and imprisonment. The later intent-to-cause-death-or-serious-injury wording is not this offense's text. Section101(b)'s encouragement of federal/state cooperation and appropriate prosecution consistent with prosecutorial discretion is not another element or an automatic constitutional limit.

The operative §924(c)(1) core imposes five years **in addition to** the punishment for any federally prosecutable crime of violence or drug trafficking during and in relation to which the person uses or carries a firearm, including predicates carrying their own deadly-weapon enhancement. It commands consecutive service with any other term, including the predicate; bars probation or suspension; and contained additional weapon and repeat-conviction tiers. The source supplement supplies the offense-date distinctions and definitions. A sawed-off description does not authorize changing the reported60-month judgment to a ten-year sentence: barrel length/statutory classification and a government sentencing challenge are not established. The cumulative-punishment question concerns the actual convictions and sentences supplied.

### Supported paths and exact prerequisites

**Commerce.** The original carjacking statute contains an express vehicle-commerce condition absent from the original school-zone possession statute. The evidence of manufacture and interstate titling satisfies the lower court's view of that element; whether it provides the constitutionally sufficient relation is a separate question. The lower court invokes *Hodel v. Indiana*, 452 U.S.314, and *United States v. Johnson* on aggregate effects and the national vehicle-theft problem. A sustaining path may distinguish violent taking of an interstate-market article and a theft-prevention scheme from the school-zone provision. Its strongest counterargument is that past interstate movement alone may leave an otherwise local taking indefinitely subject to federal power; a jurisdictional label cannot establish its own constitutional sufficiency. *Allied-Bruce* expressly did not make remote object history conclusive.

An invalidating or remanding path must apply **the actual Lopez rule once available**, including its rationale, exceptions and scope. It may not assume that Lopez invalidates all local violent crime or that the existence of a nexus element necessarily distinguishes it. If the predicate cannot constitutionally support this federal prosecution, the Court must address the dependent §924(c) count's requirement of a federally prosecutable predicate. A remand for actual-Lopez reconsideration remains a possible procedural path; no predicted disposition is supplied now.

**Fault and fair notice.** The complete lower opinion treats knowledge of the facts constituting an offense as ordinarily implied when the statute is silent, distinguishes intent from motive, and rejects limiting carjacking to theft for profit. The actual *Staples*, *Ratzlaf*, *X-Citement* and *Shabani* require contextual statutory analysis: serious criminal liability and apparently innocent conduct matter; a knowing-violation-of-law requirement is not universal; an unlawful agreement or aiding participation is not mere association. A sustaining path reads the ordinary taking/force terms with criminal fault and finds the asserted uncertainty about profit or motive insufficient to make this violent-taking charge vague. A narrowing path may insist on knowledge and intentional participation in the taking, rejecting guilt based only on presence or awareness of another's plan. Those paths can overlap; neither supplies an intent to kill drawn from the 1994 amendment.

The strongest counterargument to an unrestricted affirmance is the danger of imposing this severe felony on a person who did not knowingly participate in the taking; the strongest counterargument to facial invalidation is that the original words and ordinary mens rea presumption give notice of the charged armed taking even though motive is not an element. A particular instruction-based reversal or harmless-error conclusion requires the actual instruction, objection/preservation record and indictment; the lower narrative cannot establish them. The packet supports a bounded statute/fair-notice decision or a properly explained remand, not a fabricated instructional error.

**Cumulative punishment.** *Missouri v. Hunter*,459 U.S.359, fully read, holds that clearly authorized cumulative punishment at one trial does not violate double jeopardy even for offenses that overlap under *Blockburger*. *Whalen* and *Blockburger* supply a construction presumption absent contrary intent; they are not an automatic same-trial bar against clear authorization. The complete lower *Singleton*,16 F.3d1419, reads “any,” the inclusive deadly-weapon parenthetical, “in addition to,” and the consecutive command as extending §924(c) to later-enacted §2119. It treats the1984 amendment's response to *Simpson/Busic* as an express departure from the older cumulative-punishment restriction; later §2119's silence is not an implied repeal of that categorical direction. This is a supported affirmance path, not a Supreme holding already deciding the present pair.

The contrary statutory path argues that firearm possession is integral to original §2119, rather than merely an enhanced penalty, and that the later specific offense's silence leaves ambiguity about multiplying convictions; lenity resolves genuine residual uncertainty after ordinary construction. *Singleton* answers that “including” is nonexhaustive and the command applies to “any” violent federal predicate. A ruling must confront both propositions, not rely on offense overlap alone. The lower court found overlap but also discussed the Government's contrary view of the elements; it is unnecessary to announce that every original §2119 violation invariably satisfies every §924(c) element if the disposition rests on clear cumulative authorization. The actual *Smith* requires use/carrying and a purpose/effect relationship, not accidental possession alone. *Deal* resolves successive firearm convictions within one prosecution, not this two-statute cumulative question.

The actual *Dixon* retained *Grady* for successive prosecutions. The historical statement to the contrary in *Singleton* is excluded from entering law. Harris is one trial with cumulative counts; do not turn that difference into an implicit overruling of current successive-prosecution doctrine. Stevens's join in Marshall's *Hunter* dissent supplies an eligible separate constitutional objection: multiple convictions carry stigma and collateral consequences beyond a permissible aggregate sentence, so the same-offense limitation should constrain the legislature as well. That dissent does not control; changing *Hunter* requires an express, supported reconsideration rather than citing current *Dixon* as if it already did so.
