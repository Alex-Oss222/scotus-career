## 53. United States v. Williams — No. 94-395

**Supplied event:** Merits decision scheduled April 25, 1995; argument February 22, 1995. On review of *Williams v. United States*, 24 F.3d 1143, Ninth Circuit No. 92-56207, decided May 19, 1994. The statutory question is whether 28 U.S.C. §1346(a)(1) permits a refund action by a person who paid another's tax to remove a lien from her own property. The United States seeks reversal; Williams seeks affirmance of the jurisdictional ruling. No refund merits judgment is supplied by this candidate.

### Verified source facts and posture

The Ninth Circuit's opinion identifies the following chronology: Rabin and Williams acquired the house in 1975; assessments against Rabin on June 29, 1987 and March 28, 1988; transfer agreement October 20, 1988; quitclaim deed recorded October 25; first notice of federal tax lien November 10; dissolution petition January 19, 1989; escrow agreement approximately May 9; nominee lien June 26; payment to permit clear title; divorce March 19, 1990. Williams was not personally liable for Rabin's employment taxes. Her administrative refund claim was denied. Her complaint requested a refund plus interest, costs and fees.

The district court ruled for the Government on September 2, 1992. The Ninth Circuit reversed and remanded; it identified jurisdiction as the only issue before it. It read the collection branch of §1346(a)(1) as permitting suit by the person from whom tax was wrongfully collected, followed *Martin v. United States*, 895 F.2d 992 (4th Cir. 1990), and rejected the contrary readings in *Busse*, 542 F.2d 421, and *Snodgrass*, 834 F.2d 537. It described an oral Government concession about its 1989 lien. That description is a lower-court statement, not this handoff's independent resolution of the underlying lien's merits.

The joint appendix, pp. 18–24, reproduces the June 8, 1992 hearing. At its end both counsel agreed there was no triable issue of fact; the judge characterized the matter as a stipulated-fact trial despite the cross-summary-judgment labels. The separate proposed fact/conclusion documents at pp. 7–17 should not be treated as two independently adopted judicial holdings. The district docket at p. 3 reports a June 27, 1994 proceeding after the appellate mandate and an August 19 stay pending Supreme Court proceedings. These are lower-court procedural facts; they do not turn the jurisdictional issue into an adjudication of every refund ground.

### Enacted text reproduced in predecision merits brief

The Government's December 1994 brief, pp. 2–5, reproduces these provisions:

- **28 U.S.C. §1346(a)(1):** District courts have jurisdiction concurrent with the Court of Federal Claims over any civil action against the United States to recover an internal-revenue tax alleged to have been erroneously or illegally assessed or collected, any penalty claimed collected without authority, or any sum alleged excessive or in any manner wrongfully collected under the internal-revenue laws.
- **26 U.S.C. §7422(a):** Such a suit cannot be maintained until a refund or credit claim is duly filed with the Secretary according to applicable law and regulations. **Section 7422(b)** permits the suit whether or not payment was under protest or duress.
- **Section 6511(a):** For a tax requiring a return, the claim is filed by the taxpayer within three years of the return or two years of payment, whichever expires later; if the taxpayer filed no return, within two years of payment. The brief's quotation is expressly of relevant part, not the entire section.
- **Section 7701(a)(14):** Taxpayer means any person subject to any internal revenue tax. Williams additionally invokes §7701(a)'s introduction limiting definitions to use in that title unless otherwise distinctly expressed or manifestly incompatible with its intent.
- **Section 6325(b)(3):** Subject to regulations, the Secretary **may** discharge property sold under an agreement placing proceeds in a fund subject to the United States' liens and claims with the same manner and priority as the property. It is permissive text.
- **Section 7426(a)(1):** A person other than the assessed person claiming an interest in or lien on property wrongfully levied upon may sue after a levy or levy sale, whether or not surrender or sale has occurred. **Section 7426(a)(3)** separately authorizes suit over a fund created by a §6325(b)(3) sale agreement; **§7426(b)(4)** permits judgment for the part of that fund to which the claimant is entitled.
- **28 U.S.C. §2410(a)(1):** The United States may be named in a quiet-title action concerning property on which it has or claims a mortgage or lien. This is distinct from a refund claim after payment.

### The parties' predecision positions

The Government's brief, pp. 10–21, reads §§1346(a)(1), 7422(a), 6511(a) and 7701(a)(14) together as confining refund suits to the person subject to the tax. It invokes *Dalm*, 494 U.S. 596, on timely administrative claims and *Flora*, 362 U.S. 145, on full payment. It invokes strict construction of consent to suit and argues that Congress separately supplied third-party quiet-title, substitution-of-proceeds, foreclosure and wrongful-levy remedies. It concedes no levy or foreclosure occurred here. It argues that allowing third-party refunds could discharge the original liability and liens before a later refund, complicating collection against the taxpayer.

Williams's January 1995 brief, pp. 9–32, relies on the disjunctive assessment/collection language, absence of “taxpayer” from §§1346(a)(1) and 7422(a), her completed administrative claim, and the timing function of §6511. She distinguishes *Dalm* on compliance, disputes the claimed exclusivity of third-party remedies, and stresses that §7426(a)(1) requires a levy while §6325(b)(3) requires discretionary agreement. She asserts the imminent sale and foreclosure made prepayment quiet-title litigation impracticable. She distinguishes a payment compelled by an IRS lien on the payer's property from an unsolicited volunteer payment, citing *Stahmann v. Vidal*, 305 U.S. 61. These are party arguments; their success is not settled here.

## 54. United States v. Lopez — No. 93-1260

**Supplied event:** Merits decision scheduled April 26, 1995; argument November 8, 1994. Review of Fifth Circuit No. 92-5641, September 15, 1993, 2 F.3d 1342. The United States seeks reversal of the judgment invalidating the prosecution under the Gun-Free School Zones Act as enacted. The actual indictment, not a modern amended offense, is the relevant charging instrument.

### Verified lower-court facts, reach and remedy

The Fifth Circuit's complete opinion is reproduced in the Government's petition, Appendix A, pp. 1a–54a. On March 10, 1992, Lopez, a twelfth-grade student, brought a concealed .38-caliber handgun to Edison High School in San Antonio. The gun was unloaded and he carried five bullets. The opinion reports his statement that he was to deliver the gun after school for $40 for use in a gang conflict. These reported circumstances are not a statutory element or an independently resolved characterization of the regulated class.

State charges were dropped in view of federal prosecution. Lopez moved to dismiss for lack of congressional power; the district court denied the motion. A bench trial on stipulated evidence produced conviction, six months' imprisonment and two years' supervised release. He challenged the statute's constitutionality and also his sentence; the Fifth Circuit did not reach the sentencing challenge after reversing the conviction.

The lower court did not claim Congress could never regulate school-zone firearms. It found no statutory jurisdictional element and no adequate congressional findings or legislative-history foundation for this Act's reach. It left open whether legislation of similar scope supported by findings could survive and whether particular applications might be constitutional. The parties had stipulated that an ATF agent would testify the gun was made outside Texas. The Fifth Circuit nevertheless also relied on the indictment's failure to allege a commerce nexus; it cited *Stirone* and *Russell* and directed dismissal of the indictment. The original neutral intake's shorter description omits this additional ground and remedy.

The full lower opinion discusses *Gibbons*, *Wickard*, *Wirtz*, *Perez*, *Heart of Atlanta*, *McClung*, *Hodel*, *Bass*, *Scarborough*, *Gregory*, *Garcia*, and the historical development of firearms regulation. Any post-divergence Supreme Court references in that opinion are source descriptions; only the actual prior simulation holdings in the slice are current Court law.

### Original statutory coverage and exceptions

The respondent's statutory appendix, `lopez_07.txt`, pp. 1a–2a, reproduces original §922(q)(1)(A): knowing firearm possession at a place the individual knows or has reasonable cause to believe is a school zone. Under §921(a)(25) a school zone includes school grounds and the area within 1,000 feet; §921(a)(26) uses elementary or secondary education as determined under state law.

Section 922(q)(1)(B) exempts possession: (i) on private property outside school grounds; (ii) by an individual licensed by the relevant State or political subdivision under a licensing law requiring law-enforcement verification of qualification before licensing; (iii) unloaded and in a locked container or a locked firearm rack on a motor vehicle; (iv) for a school-approved program; (v) under a contract between the school and the individual or employer; (vi) by a law-enforcement officer acting officially; or (vii) unloaded while traversing school premises to access public or private hunting lands, if school authorities authorize entry. The lower opinion states none applies on this record.

The distinct discharge provision, §922(q)(2), prohibits knowing or recklessly unsafe discharge or attempted discharge at a place known to be a school zone and has its own four exemptions: private property outside school grounds; approved school program; school contract; official law enforcement. Those are not interchangeable with the possession exemptions. Section 922(q)(3) preserves state/local gun-free-school-zone enactments. The possession charge here does not decide every separate discharge application.

The September 1994 Government supplemental brief reports enacted Pub. L. 103-322 §320904, adding findings and renumbering the old paragraphs. Its full appendix reproduces findings concerning interstate movement of guns/drugs/gangs, the effects of school violence on education and commerce, and interstate difficulties in enforcement. The brief expressly states that enactment does not itself resolve the conflict concerning prosecutions under the existing statute. Treat the amendment's enactment and terms as a dated noncase-law source; do not silently substitute the amended offense or infer retroactive cure. Admission of any required current-term source belongs to the control operator.

## 55. Travelers consolidated matters — Nos. 93-1408, 93-1414, 93-1415

**Supplied event:** Merits decision scheduled April 26, 1995; argument January 18, 1995. The consolidated identities are New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans, Pataki, and Hospital Association of New York State against Travelers and other respondents. The supplied question concerns ERISA preemption of hospital surcharges; this candidate does not expand it to every part of the lower judgment.

### Verified lower judgment and record description

The amended Second Circuit opinion, dated January 14, 1994 following October 25, 1993 issuance, is reproduced at pp. A1–A34 of the shared appendix in docket 93-1414. It reviews *Travelers Insurance Co. v. Cuomo*, 813 F. Supp. 996, on cross-motions for summary judgment. The appellate dockets are 93-7132L, 93-7134CON, 93-7148CON and 93-7194XAP. All its dispositive sections and footnotes have been read.

The lower opinion identifies three separate measures: the original 13% differential on commercial-insurer hospital payments, retained by hospitals; an additional 11% surcharge with proceeds ultimately paid into the State's general fund; and an HMO assessment of up to 9% for failure to meet Medicaid-enrollment targets. The original differential exempted the Blues, HMOs and specified governmental coverage. The HMO amount was conditional and is not a blanket 9% charge on every HMO. The source describes DRG-based hospital rates and asserts that the differentials were designed to encourage Blues participation and Medicaid enrollment. Those economic assertions are the lower court's record descriptions; they are not statutory commands directing an ERISA plan to purchase specified benefits.

The district court enjoined the challenged charges as applied to appropriate covered payors but stayed the injunction against the original 13% differential. The Second Circuit found all three surcharges preempted by ERISA; it also sustained FEHBA preemption of the 13% and 11% surcharges. It treated the 11% and 9% measures as taxes, yet held the Tax Injunction Act did not bar federal adjudication because its view of exclusive ERISA jurisdiction left no plain, speedy and efficient state remedy. It rejected a laches defense to the original differential. These additional dispositions make the precise Supreme Court scope material.

The opinion further decided Actuarial Letter No. 5 concerning stop-loss insurance purchased by self-funded plans: it affirmed preemption of paragraphs 1, 2, 3, 5 and reversed the district court's refusal to preempt paragraph 4; paragraphs 6 and 7 were not challenged. Its final mandate was affirmance in part, reversal in part, and entry of judgment consistent with its opinion. A generic “the court of appeals affirmed” loses this component distinction.

### Statutory structure and source-attributed arguments

At pp. A18–A19 the opinion reproduces §514(a)'s supersession of state laws insofar as they relate to a covered, nonexempt employee benefit plan, subject to subsection (b). Section 514(b)(2)(A) saves state laws regulating insurance, banking or securities except as subparagraph (B) provides. Section 514(b)(2)(B) prevents a covered, nonexempt plan (other than one established primarily for death benefits), or its trust, from being deemed an insurer, bank, trust or investment company for the specified state-law purposes. The saving and deemer clauses are distinct.

The Second Circuit accepted the insurers' connection-with argument based on intended and significant cost effects and resulting coverage choices. It rejected the State/Blues contention that ordinary hospital-price laws have only indirect effects. It discussed the Department of Labor's position that economic consequences alone did not justify preemption. It rejected insurance saving for the commercial differentials, though acknowledging a risk-spreading effect, because it found the laws directed at hospital rates, not the policy relationship, and not limited to the insurance industry. It separately rejected the HMO saving argument. Footnote 5 identifies differing saving arguments by DOL, the Blues and the State; these positions should not be merged.

The court acknowledged *Shaw*'s tenuous, remote or peripheral exception, *Mackey*'s general garnishment law, *Fort Halifax*, and *FMC*. It rejected reliance on its earlier *Rebaldo* and disagreed with *United Wire*, 995 F.2d 1179 (3d Cir. 1993), which had sustained New Jersey hospital-rate regulation. The actual simulation Greater Washington Board of Trade and John Hancock holdings appear verbatim in the entering-law slice; historical summaries in the lower opinion do not enlarge them.

## 56. United States v. Harris — supplied No. 94-297

**Supplied event and channel:** Established simulated merits proceeding, argument March 1, 1995; scheduled April 27, 1995. No. 94-297 is the supplied assigned Supreme Court docket, not the lower docket. Review concerns federal commerce power, mens rea/notice and cumulative punishment under the carjacking statute and §924(c). The Fifth Circuit docket is 93-7554; the judgment is dated June 29, 1994, 25 F.3d 1275. Defendants Harris and Hoskins seek relief from the convictions or sentences; the United States seeks affirmance. No new historical Supreme Court action enters through this source handoff.

### Verified lower facts, components and sentence

The full Fifth Circuit opinion by W. Eugene Davis, with Wisdom and Duhe on the panel, identifies a December 12, 1992 offense. The 1976 Buick Electra was manufactured in Flint, Michigan and titled in Arkansas and Mississippi. Testimony described Harris carrying a 12-gauge sawed-off shotgun and Hoskins an ax handle; both demanded the occupants leave. Hoskins drove away while Harris pursued the occupants. Defendants argued the confrontation concerned a prior gang dispute, Hoskins's taking was an afterthought, and Harris did not know he would take the car. The court evaluated evidence favorably to the jury verdict rather than accepting those defense characterizations as fact.

The jury convicted each of aiding and abetting the carjacking and using/carrying a firearm during a crime of violence. Each received **41 months on count 1 and 60 months on count 2, consecutively**. The court rejected the sufficiency challenge, rejected vagueness and the proposed limitation to theft for profit, upheld commerce power using *Hodel v. Indiana* and *United States v. Johnson*, and rejected the cumulative-punishment claim by following *Singleton*, 16 F.3d 1419. It also rejected an intradistrict venue challenge under Rule 18; the supplied Supreme Court question does not expressly include that issue. Its judgment affirmed convictions and sentences.

### Original enacted statute

Official Pub. L. 102-519 §101(a),106 Stat.3384, approved October 25, 1992, provides:

> “Whoever, possessing a firearm as defined in section 921 of this title, takes a motor vehicle that has been transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce from the person or presence of another by force and violence or by intimidation, or attempts to do so, shall—”

The three penalties are a fine or imprisonment up to 15 years or both; if serious bodily injury **as defined in §1365** results, a fine or imprisonment up to 25 years or both; if death results, a fine or imprisonment for any term up to life or both. This is the original firearm-possession formulation applicable to the reported December 1992 offense. It does not contain an intent-to-cause-death-or-serious-injury phrase. Section 101(b) urges federal cooperation with state/local officials and prosecution as appropriate and consistent with prosecutorial discretion; it is not an additional offense element.

The same Act separately increases penalties for stolen-vehicle import/export and interstate trafficking, provides forfeiture measures, creates a chop-shop offense, and establishes theft-prevention and titling programs. Its full text was read; existence of those other provisions does not itself settle §2119's constitutional application.

The lower court says knowledge of the facts constituting the offense is ordinarily implied when Congress omits an express mental element. It treats motive as distinct. Its commerce analysis invokes aggregate effects on travel; its cumulative-punishment section relies on Singleton's stated conclusion that Congress intended cumulative punishment despite factual overlap. Those are the lower court's grounds, not new Supreme Court holdings.
