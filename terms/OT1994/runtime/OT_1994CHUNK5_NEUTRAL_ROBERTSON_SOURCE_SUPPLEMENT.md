## Mine activity: support, acknowledgment, and remaining distinctions

### Equipment, supplies, labor, and communications

The lower opinion describes a partnership purchase of mining claims for $125,000, mining equipment for $100,000, and travel expenses for seven employees, paid nearly entirely in cash. It describes a $300,000 Arizona-house-secured loan to finance the 1986 mining season and the Cadillac being driven to Alaska for mining use. In its commerce discussion, it addresses the Government's proof of purchases and hiring outside Alaska and rejects its legal sufficiency as incidental, rather than stating that every such movement failed to occur. The amount of the entire equipment purchase is not a finding that $100,000 of equipment was purchased outside Alaska. (Pet. App.4a–5a, 9a–10a.)

The enterprise must be identified before classifying its contacts. The indictment alleges both the Double Eagle partnership and its successor, Robertson Mining Company, the sole proprietorship, as the enterprise, covering April 1985 through April 1988. It alleges investment from April 1985 through November 1987. (J.A.36–37.) These are allegations, not proof of every date. The jury instructions describe the charged partnership and sole proprietorship as a common-purpose organization functioning as a continuing unit, subject to the jury finding that it existed. (J.A.61.) A supposed absolute line separating the owner from the sole proprietorship would itself require legal justification. Conversely, the proprietor's drug transactions or private travel do not become mining-enterprise activities merely through common ownership.

### Output, nuggets, local sales, and downstream movement

**Lower-court account.** The panel says the evidence established that, with the exception of a few nuggets Robertson kept, none of the gold was sold outside Alaska; it describes approximately $200,000 in production. (Pet. App.9a.) This wording does not state a dollar value for retained nuggets, their destination, an out-of-state sale of them, or a percentage exported. Its qualification about retained nuggets must remain visible. A statement that no gold ever left Alaska would go beyond the panel's statement about sales.

**Government's more specific assertion.** The petition pp.4–5 says production was between $200,000 and $290,000, most gold was sold in Alaska, and Robertson took approximately $30,000 in nuggets out of the State. Petition pp.11–12 n.5 repeats the $30,000 assertion with Tr.774–784 and 1723. The merits brief pp.4–5 repeats it; pp.21–22 n.8 calls that movement a shipment and an additional basis for its commerce argument. These passages establish what the United States claimed the trial record showed. They are not a lower-court finding or the testimony itself. The source-5 OCR gives a differing final transcript number in note 8; do not rely on that OCR pin to certify the missing testimony.

**Respondent's express acknowledgment and its limits.** Respondent merits p.3 states that approximately $200,000 of gold was sold, all purchasers were Alaskan refiners, and Robertson kept about $30,000 in nuggets, citing Tr.453–460, 782–783, 1906–1907. Merits pp.5, 21 says the sales occurred over three years. Thus the approximate retained-nugget valuation is represented by both parties. Respondent's brief does not expressly acknowledge their carriage outside Alaska, identify a destination, or state that he sold them there. It does not expressly rebut the Government's carriage assertion either. Silence on that detail is not a stipulation to an enterprise shipment. The shared representation that he kept nuggets also does not establish the purpose of later transport or their legal attribution as enterprise goods rather than a personal holding.

**Refiners and market connection.** Government merits pp.20–21 says the mine dealt with two of three local refiners; prices followed the London spot price; and General Refining's transaction records displayed Alaska, New York, and Los Angeles addresses. It cites Tr.1004–1015, 774–784, 1011 and Government exhibits 259–262, 264, 266. Respondent accepts sales to Alaskan refiners while contesting their legal significance. None of the cited exhibits or testimony is in the supplied J.A. The brief's account of a refiner's multistate business and internationally determined prices supports its market-effects argument; it does not trace this mine's gold into a particular downstream interstate shipment.

**No numerical synthesis.** Approximately $200,000 in production in the lower opinion, approximately $200,000 in sales in respondent's brief, the Government's $200,000–$290,000 production range, and the parties' $30,000 retained-nugget account are different representations. Do not add them, select a denominator, calculate an export percentage, or describe an exact fraction of output as crossing state lines. No supplied court finding, stipulation, testimony, or exhibit establishes such a fraction. No out-of-state purchaser or out-of-state sale of the retained nuggets is identified in these sources.

## Statutory scope and the actual district submission

The complete §1962(a) text reproduced in Government Appendix pp.5a–6a and Respondent Appendix p.1a agrees in substance with the officially checked text described in the validated packet. It prohibits a person who received income derived directly or indirectly from a racketeering pattern or collection of unlawful debt, in which that person participated as a principal within §2, from using or investing that income or its proceeds, directly or indirectly, in acquiring an interest in, establishing, or operating an enterprise engaged in interstate or foreign commerce or whose activities affect that commerce.

The securities qualification remains exact in substance: an open-market purchase for investment, without intent to control the issuer, participate in its control, or assist another in doing so, is not unlawful under subsection (a) if the postpurchase holdings of the purchaser, immediate family, and their racketeering/unlawful-debt accomplices aggregate less than one percent of any outstanding class and confer no power in law or fact to elect a director. No fact here invokes it. The separate provisions governing acquisition or control through racketeering, conduct of enterprise affairs, and conspiracy are not substituted for subsection (a).

Count Six alleged that the enterprise was both engaged in commerce and affected commerce. (J.A.36–37.) The judge instructed that each element must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the enterprise was engaged in interstate commerce **or** its activities affected interstate or foreign commerce. (J.A.43, 60–61; Tr.2257, 2281–2283.) The complete instruction passage contains no specific finding about nuggets, no finding fixing the interstate procurement amount, and no juror identification of which commerce alternative supplied the verdict. Its general instruction defining interstate commerce as trade or travel between States appears in the discussion of interstate transportation of stolen property; that predicate instruction cannot be converted into a separate finding about the mine. (J.A.63.) The court added a unanimity instruction on the RICO predicate acts after counsel objected. (J.A.71–72.) That addition does not identify the actual predicates or decide the still-unresolved challenge to the refusal of a special verdict.

The Government's principal merits argument invokes the affecting-commerce alternative, aggregate commercial effects, and the full extent of constitutional commerce authority. Respondent's principal merits argument demands a particularized substantial effect because he treats RICO as a general prohibition rather than a statute regulating a congressionally identified class. He invokes federalism, lenity, and the independence of the enterprise-commerce element. The Government's reply contests those distinctions and separately relies on actual use of interstate channels. These are competing arguments. Any party citation to post-divergence historical Supreme Court doctrine must be tested against the actual entering-law slice before it is used as law. The briefs' assertions cannot determine the actual Lopez or Harris refresh.

Direct enterprise purchases and work-related interstate movements therefore require evaluation independently of the claim about exported output and independently of the general aggregation dispute. A direct-commerce analysis cannot be made to depend on an invented export percentage. An insufficiency analysis likewise must address the acknowledged interstate movements, the statutory word “establishment,” and the Government's attribution arguments rather than assuming there were no such contacts. These are framing requirements, not selected conclusions.

## Lower judgment and remaining appellate issues

The source review confirms the existing packet's separation of Count Six from the affirmed cocaine convictions and their independently vacated sentences. The panel reversed Count Six for commerce-proof insufficiency. It did not reach six other RICO grounds: denial of a special verdict; venue; severance of RICO and CCE counts; exclusion of Agent Devetko's testimony; denial of CJA funds to locate the claimed Cadillac owner; and RICO vagueness. (Pet. App.11a n.3.) The broader vagueness discussion in the present merits briefs does not establish that the Court has taken up every unresolved vagueness claim.

The Government's RICO Guidelines straddle argument remained unresolved after the commerce reversal. The drug convictions concerned pre-Guidelines conduct, and their separate Rule 32(c)(3)(D) resentencing order remains distinct. The lower mandate required actual resentencing after compliance, not merely a later statement that the disputed PSR material had not affected the original sentence. (Pet. App.25a–27a.) Counts Five and Eight were acquittals; Count Seven was not decided by the jury and later was dismissed. The supplied district docket independently confirms the acquittals and guilty verdicts, but its reproduced entries end while Count Seven remained pending; its later dismissal is established by the lower opinion and consistent party accounts, not invented from the docket's endpoint. (J.A.27–28; Pet. App.6a–7a.) None of the new sources removes these remaining matters or supplies a later disposition of them.

**Exact sentencing-source difference.** The lower opinion and both merits statements use a shorthand of concurrent twenty-year terms on all five guilty counts. The written June 1, 1992 judgment instead lists 240 months on Counts One and Six, 60 months on Counts Two and Three, and 36 months on Count Four, concurrently for 240 months total; it lists five years of supervised release on Counts One–Four and three years on Count Six, concurrently. (J.A.74–76.) Use the written judgment if exact original per-count terms are material, while disclosing the source discrepancy. The printed form's Sentencing Reform Act boilerplate does not resolve the separate litigated Guidelines question. No sentencing transcript beyond the supplied jury instructions was available, and no correction of the judicial action itself is made here.
