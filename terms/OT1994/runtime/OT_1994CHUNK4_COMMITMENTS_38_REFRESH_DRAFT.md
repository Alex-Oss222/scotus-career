## 38. Anderson v. Edwards — March 22, 1995

**Judgment commitment:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with rejection of the classwide challenge. All eight reject both the categorical no-deeming ground and the independently preserved equitable-treatment ground on the supplied record. The §612 and mandatory-family-inclusion arguments do not independently sustain the injunction. This is a decision on the grounds actually argued, not a conclusion that equitable treatment was waived. Individual resource disputes remain governed by the qualifications below; no hypothetical income receipt is adjudicated as a fact.

### Component A — assistance-unit formation and the availability regulations

**Rule supported by all eight:** A State may combine the otherwise eligible applicants and recipients covered by California's challenged same-household caretaker rules into one AFDC assistance unit and apply its benefit schedule to that unit. A reduction from the sum of hypothetical separate grants does not by itself establish assumed income from a legally nonresponsible person. The unit must comply with federal inclusion and exclusion requirements; neither residence alone nor a state unit label makes an outsider's income available.

The challenged rule covers the specified caretaker relationships, not every person sharing an address. Successor §82-824.1 combines units in the same home when the caretakers are married to one another, when two caretakers have separate children and a child in common, or when there is one caretaker; the litigation principally concerns the third setting. The predecessor's single-caretaker rule similarly applies to eligible children. No holding decides all unproduced exceptions to that former rule or every possible multi-caretaker household. Verna Edwards is herself a nonrecipient, so the result does not rest on treating every caretaker as an aided unit member.

Sections 233.90(a)(1), 233.20(a)(2)(viii), and 233.20(a)(3)(ii)(D) retain distinct work. The first restricts the parental relationship used to establish deprivation and also expressly forbids treating a substitute parent's or other undescribed person's presence as a basis for ineligibility or assumed income. It is not reduced to its first sentence. The second protects a need item against reduction solely because of a nonresponsible individual's presence and bars assumed contributions, subject separately to the stepparent, authorized proration, and sponsored-alien provisions. The third requires the prescribed disregards and current-use availability; income can be actually available or satisfy both legal-interest-in-a-liquidated-sum and legal-ability-to-use conditions, consistently with the chapter. Combining persons who claim assistance together is not itself a finding that an unaided adult contributes money. Nor does the combined grant establish that every member has a private-law support duty to every other member.

The cross-referenced gross-income condition remains distinct: §233.20(a)(3)(xiii) bars aid in a month when the unit's non-assistance income exceeds 185 percent of the State need standard, including special needs, for a family of the same composition, without the identified earned-income and tax-credit disregards except to the extent paragraph (a)(3)(xix) permits. The latter permits the described full-time-student earned-income disregard under the State plan for no more than six months per calendar year; the reproduced JTPA alternatives have a combined six-month limit. No named family's eligibility is calculated here. Likewise, the separate sponsored-alien regime does not carry deemed sponsor income into the need of unsponsored family members unless actually available. These are retained limits, not new adjudications of unpresented income claims.

King, Lewis, and Van Lare protect the boundary against fictitious support; they do not establish an invariable nonsibling assistance-unit boundary. Dandridge permits state benefit-level choices within federal law and does not authorize disregarding those regulations. The strongest contrary argument is that consolidation can conceal exactly the deemed support those cases reject. The answer is limited: the State can measure need for the actual aided unit, but cannot use that proposition to count funds unavailable under applicable federal law. A common label cannot cure a forbidden attribution. The categorical rule is permissible without an adjudication that every future application is lawful.

### Component B — mandatory inclusion, qualified nonapplication, and income scope

**Rule supported by all eight:** Section 602(a)(38) prescribes mandatory inclusion of the co-resident parent and qualifying brother or sister, except as otherwise provided in that part; it does not make those relationships an exclusive list of persons a State may place in one assistance unit. The parent/sibling same-home and dependency conditions, statutory exceptions, and Title II representative-payee proviso remain operative. Gilliard concerns the mandated family grouping and associated child-support treatment; it supports neither an exclusive federal unit ceiling nor an unqualified power to pool every co-resident's resources.

The agency's reproduced 1986 instructions identify otherwise eligible parents and dependent blood or adoptive siblings within the age criteria; exclusions include SSI recipients and the specified alienage, sponsorship, and lump-sum ineligibility settings. A qualifying married stepparent must have the generally applicable support duty described by law; a stepbrother or stepsister is not automatically included merely because that stepparent is. Overlapping mandatory memberships require consolidation. The minor-mother example permits the family not to apply for the grandchild, but if it applies, the required linked units consolidate. These qualifications explain the supported scope; the judicial holding does not independently decide an unpresented exclusion dispute.

An optional nonparent caretaker or nonsibling may refrain from applying only when federal mandatory inclusion does not require that person's inclusion. A child with income who must be included with a sibling cannot withdraw alone while the sibling continues receiving aid; in the supplied example both may have to decline. That cost is not denied or converted into proof that all participation is freely severable. The valid formation of a unit permits considering income legally available to its members under the governing rules after disregards without requiring proof of a separate cash transfer between each pair of recipients. It does not authorize counting an outsider's unavailable income or defeating a particular federal exclusion, restriction, or disregard. A claim concerning an actual restricted award or particular Title II payment would require the recipient, payee, membership, applicable inclusion rules, and availability facts; counsel's hypotheticals do not establish those facts here.

Section 605 is no independent source of a nonsibling support duty. It concerns the AFDC grant's proper use for those for whom it is paid and the qualified availability of counseling, protective payments, guardianship, or authorized penalties. It neither changes state-law general support obligations nor makes a child's or caretaker's non-AFDC income available to others. Its authorized responses are not mandatory cumulative remedies. The judgment rests on lawful unit formation and the income rules, not an invented §605 transfer obligation.

### Component C — the independent equitable-treatment ground

**Rule supported by all eight:** On this classwide record, applying the same grant schedule to the eligible applicants grouped under the challenged caretaker conditions does not violate §§233.10(a)(1) and 233.20(a)(1)(i) merely because consolidation lowers the aggregate amount relative to separate units or makes poor relatives' households receive different grants from households in which a relative does not claim aid. The classifications must remain reasonable, consistent statewide, objective, and equitable in light of the Act's provisions and purposes; administrative convenience cannot authorize arbitrary exclusion or a condition inconsistent with the Act.

The recipients' strongest independent argument is that the Act seeks to support care in relatives' homes, while reduced aid burdens precisely the poor relatives willing to take additional children and may deter that care. The district court found reduced aggregate benefits and resulting hardship; those findings are accepted for this analysis. But §601's purposes do not fix a right to the sum of separate grants, and the regulations do not require the State to disregard the shared need items of a larger aided household. The rules distinguish the assistance applicants whose needs the State is measuring, rather than denying eligibility to an otherwise eligible child because an unrelated adult resides nearby. Using one schedule for units with the same number of aided members is a permissible objective basis. The difference from a household with a nonapplicant caretaker reflects that the caretaker's own needs are absent from that grant; it does not by itself establish arbitrary discrimination against needy relatives. Dandridge supplies the bounded discretion over levels, while King, Lewis, and Van Lare continue to prohibit assumed outside support.

This is an independent construction and application of both regulations. The Court does not treat the equitable-treatment language as surplusage or reduce it to a constitutional rational-basis test. Section 233.10(a)(1)'s ban on arbitrary exclusions, its Act-consistency condition, opportunity to apply, and objective of assisting eligible persons to qualify retain force. Its title XVI uniformity requirement is not imported as an additional AFDC command. The uniform-income clause and its exception for federal statutory authorization do not establish a different result on a record identifying no adjudicated income classification that must be invalidated. The challenged consolidation itself is not such a classification. A demonstrated application counting forbidden income or excluding an eligible group would raise the actual operative rule, not be immunized by this decision.

### Component D — §612 proration and §233.20(a)(5)

**Rule supported by all eight:** Section 612's reasonable proration option for shelter, utilities, and similar needs when a claimant shares a household with people not claiming aid together does not make proration the exclusive permissible recognition of shared costs within one otherwise lawful assistance unit. Its SSI one-third-reduction qualification continues to exclude that recipient from the household for the authorized proration calculation. California's application of a unit-size grant schedule is not, solely because it yields less than separate grants, an invocation of that option.

The implementing §233.20(a)(5) separately prohibits proration with respect to a person receiving SSI subject to the statutory one-third reduction and when a bona fide landlord-tenant relationship exists. If a State elects this option, it must prorate both the need and payment standards on a reasonable basis; its plan must identify the allowances and procedure and specify the circumstances, including who counts as sharing the household with the assistance unit. The election remains permissive. None of those conditional safeguards is removed by recognizing a distinct lawful unit-size schedule, and no landlord-tenant or SSI factual application is decided here.

The strongest contrary reading is that Congress's detailed authorization would be pointless if a State could evade its safeguards by relabeling independent claims as one unit. That concern prevents treating unit formation as unlimited, but the statute's own distinction between those claiming together and those who are not requires separate consideration of unit membership. The federal mandatory-inclusion rule, other eligibility restrictions, and objective/equitable treatment govern that first question. Once this challenged grouping passes those requirements, §612 does not require the State to fragment it solely to conduct a proration calculation. No new approval of a particular outsider-based proration percentage follows.

### Component E — administrative materials and weight

**Rule supported by all eight:** The result follows from an independent reading of the statutes and binding regulations. The supplied 1994 transmittal confirms the distinction between unit composition and available member income and gives useful concrete examples, but no independently sufficient holding of binding deference to that instrument is necessary or adopted. The 1986 material is an excerpt, not a complete transmittal. Neither document can override the regulations or dispose of an objection it does not address.

The 1994 examples are persuasive at their stated scope: a grandmother and five children can form a consolidated aided unit, while a surviving male friend's unavailable resources remain outside; a State that permits separate units cannot presume cross-unit sharing. The transmittal addresses §233.20's equitable determination, but does not separately analyze §233.10(a)(1)'s Act-purpose language. Component C supplies that judicial analysis. NationsBank's statutory delegation holding does not by itself establish the force of every AFDC guidance document. Guernsey's distinct accounting holdings and separate positions require reading the actual regulations; they do not license an administrative instrument to repeal a regulatory condition. No need exists to decide whether a broader agency interpretation would qualify for another form of deference.
