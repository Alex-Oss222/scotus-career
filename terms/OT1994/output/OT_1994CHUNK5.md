## Decisions and Dispositions

### Kyles v. Whitley, No. 93-7927

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 7, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, reviewing 5 F.3d 806 (October 14, 1993), which affirmed denial of federal habeas relief. The question is whether the established suppressed favorable evidence, viewed collectively, is material to Kyles’s conviction under Brady and Bagley.

Dolores Dye was killed and her automobile taken on September 20, 1984. Kyles’s first trial ended in a deadlock; the second produced a conviction and death sentence. No reason for the deadlock beyond disagreement is established. The prosecution used eyewitness identifications, the murder weapon found behind a stove at the apartment where Kyles stayed, ammunition and a holster, the victim’s purse and papers found in garbage, and pet food of brands she had bought. A print linked Kyles to a receipt in the car, but processing destroyed its readable date. The defense presented a theory that Joseph “Beanie” Wallace was implicated and framed Kyles. Adverse credibility findings and evidence supporting guilt remain part of the record.

The established suppression concerns six contemporaneous eyewitness statements, Beanie’s recorded September 22 and written September 23 accounts, prosecutor Strider’s interview notes, a garbage-retrieval memorandum, and the parking-lot plate list. Smallwood’s earlier sequence differed from his trial observation account; Williams’s early description differed from Kyles’s height and build. Beanie’s accounts bear on corroboration, the police investigation, his possible access, and the provenance of physical evidence. Some lawful uses arise through police examination and preparation without calling Beanie or admitting every statement for its truth. The seventeen-plate list omitted Kyles’s plate, but its completeness is disputed. The Court finds neither Beanie’s guilt nor planting, witness perjury, or a complete list.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the denial of relief on the conviction-level Brady claim, 6–3, and remands for a conditional writ. Louisiana must retry Kyles within a reasonable time fixed by the district court or release him under the writ.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conviction-level Brady claim | Reverse and remand, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Conditional writ requiring a new trial within a reasonable period fixed by the district court, or release under the writ. |

The Court does not order acquittal, unconditional immediate release, a fixed number of days, or penalty-only relief. It does not independently adjudicate other claims merely appearing in the briefs.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Dissent | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm denial on the admitted Brady claim | Collective application retaining confidence in the verdict; no per-item threshold, innocence requirement, or separate harmlessness ground. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Collective suppression undermines confidence in this conviction

**Controlling proposition:** Suppressed favorable evidence is material when its collective lawful uses, considered in the whole record and in the defense’s preparation and presentation, create a reasonable probability of a different result sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict. No item must independently satisfy that standard, and the defendant need not show acquittal more likely than conviction or that the remaining evidence is legally insufficient; the established suppression here meets the standard.

**Authority:** Justice Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for the rule, application and conviction-level relief. The dissent accepts collective assessment but disputes materiality on this record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the undisclosed evidence changes the relationship among the prosecution’s apparently reinforcing proofs. Brady requires disclosure of material favorable evidence, and Bagley supplies a probability sufficient to undermine confidence rather than a demand for probable acquittal. Earlier identification accounts permit substantial impeachment; Beanie’s differing accounts support corroboration and examination of the investigation; and the garbage material bears on the provenance of tangible evidence. These uses reinforce one another. They do not all depend on calling Beanie or accepting every statement as true. The remaining identifications, ammunition, holster, pet food and credibility objections remain significant. But counting that evidence as sufficient does not answer how the defense could have challenged its apparent independence after disclosure. The plate list contributes only within its disputed completeness. The Court finds no planting, perjury or factual innocence. It resolves the established cumulative suppression and leaves later evidence and independently unadmitted constitutional claims outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83: applies the duty to disclose material favorable evidence to the established suppression.
- United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667: applies reasonable-probability materiality to the combined defense counterfactual, including lawful impeachment and preparation uses.
- Schlup v. Delo, January 23, 1995: distinguishes its gateway showing; it supplies no additional innocence prerequisite for this Brady claim.

##### The prosecutor’s responsibility includes the investigating team

**Controlling proposition:** A prosecutor must learn of favorable evidence known to police acting on the government’s behalf in the case and disclose it when Brady requires disclosure; personal ignorance does not remove that evidence from the materiality inquiry. This case-specific responsibility does not impute all knowledge of every government office or establish unrestricted investigative or open-file duties.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. The dissent’s consideration of the police-held material does not supply additional votes for an affirmative organizational rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the prosecution cannot avoid its disclosure responsibility by separating the trial lawyer from police who worked on the case. Brady protects the fairness of the trial, and Bagley assesses the material effect of nondisclosure; that protection would fail if the same favorable information counted only when personally recalled by the prosecutor. Here the eyewitness statements, recorded accounts, plate list and investigative memorandum arose within the investigation used to prosecute Kyles. Their lawful defense uses therefore enter the same collective inquiry. The rule does not require every agency to search all government files, and it does not convert every nondisclosure into a completed constitutional violation. Materiality and the evidence’s favorable character still matter. United States v. Williams addresses a distinct federal grand-jury duty and its specified remedy conditions; its personal-knowledge limitation does not narrow trial Brady responsibility. The Court creates no open-file entitlement and excuses no lack of diligence concerning evidence already known to the defense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brady and Bagley: apply their trial-disclosure protection to information held by police acting for this prosecution.
- United States v. Williams, May 4, 1992: distinguishes its confined grand-jury disclosure and dismissal rules; those limits remain effective within their own setting.

##### A completed material Brady violation requires no second duplicative harmlessness showing

**Controlling proposition:** Once this suppression is found material under Brady and Bagley, a second prejudice or harmlessness test may not defeat relief merely by reassessing whether the same nondisclosure affected the verdict. This holding rests on the prejudice built into the completed Brady violation and establishes no universal harmlessness rule for other habeas claims.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes for this claim-specific ground. The dissent finds no material violation and adopts no independent harmlessness ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a second duplicative prejudice inquiry cannot rescue a conviction after material suppression has already undermined confidence in its verdict. Bagley makes the requisite effect part of the constitutional violation itself. The Court has found that effect through the combined disclosure counterfactual, not merely identified an error whose effect remains unexamined. Brecht retains Chapman for its specified preserved protected-silence claim, and O’Neal extends that treatment to its specified personal-intent category; neither decision supplies a general habeas rule displacing Brady’s own materiality requirement. Nor does this decision replace Bagley with Chapman. The State’s surviving evidence has already been considered, and it does not restore confidence after the lawful combined uses are taken into account. Ordinary habeas gates and independently presented claims retain their own rules. For this established violation, the appropriate relief is a conditional new-trial opportunity, not an additional weaker review of the same prejudice or a declaration of innocence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bagley: treats materiality as a defining element of the suppression violation, not an initial screen before a second duplicative prejudice test.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993, and O’Neal v. McAninch, February 21, 1995: preserve their actual category-specific holdings and distinguish them from claim-defining Brady materiality.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Brady and Bagley govern collective materiality and the trial prosecution’s responsibility. Williams remains confined to its distinct grand-jury setting. Schlup remains a gateway rule. Brecht and O’Neal retain their category-specific holdings; this decision neither adopts a general habeas Kotteakos rule nor extends Chapman universally. No precedent is overruled.

Courts must assess the established favorable suppression collectively, including legitimate effects on preparation, corroboration, impeachment and examination of the investigation. Prosecutors bear case-specific responsibility for investigating-police information. A completed material Brady violation carries no second duplicative harmlessness hurdle. The holdings neither require open-file discovery nor treat every nondisclosure as structural error, and they do not excuse defense lack of diligence about known evidence.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. The three accept cumulative reasonable-probability review and consider the police-held material. They find that the supported additional uses do not sufficiently weaken the surviving identifications, physical evidence and adverse credibility findings to undermine confidence in this verdict. They treat the jury’s knowledge of a framing theory as relevant but not conclusive, and Beanie’s absence as a limitation on some uses rather than a bar to every investigative use. Their disagreement concerns the combined application: they reject the majority’s assessment that the new corroboration and provenance challenges materially change the evidentiary picture. They do not demand proof of innocence, impose a materiality threshold on each item, or hold that mere legal sufficiency defeats Brady. Having found no material violation, they would affirm denial of this claim without adopting a second harmlessness ground. Their application is noncontrolling.

Justice Stevens joins the Court’s application of Bagley without withdrawing his earlier request-sensitive criticism of that standard. The Court does not reject that criticism or adopt a new restrictive pretrial-disclosure theory; no additional writing is filed here.

[Fifth Circuit opinion, 5 F.3d 806](https://static.case.law/f3d/5/html/0806-01.html), including the majority and dissent, supplies the trial record and competing materiality assessments. The [filed record and briefs](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0539) support the established suppression and adversarial submissions; disputed later evidence and material not securely identified as a party’s filing supply no additional finding. [Bagley, 473 U.S. 667](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep473/usrep473667/usrep473667.pdf) supplies the reasonable-probability standard. The receipt date, plate-list completeness, alleged planting, later recantations and unadmitted independent claims remain subject to the limits stated above.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for the district court to issue a conditional writ. The district court fixes a reasonable period for Louisiana to retry Kyles; failure to retry within that period requires release under the writ. No immediate unconditional release or acquittal is ordered, and the Court does not predict the result of any new trial.

**End of entry: Kyles v. Whitley, No. 93-7927, Merits decision, April 19, 1995.**

### Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co., No. 93-1631

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, reviewing 2 F.3d 355 (August 23, 1993). The question is whether the federal ban on truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels satisfies the First Amendment. The separately upheld, unappealed advertising restriction is outside the judgment under review.

Section 205(e)(2) prohibits malt-beverage alcohol-content statements unless state law requires them. Its wine clause requires alcohol-content statements only above fourteen percent by volume; it does not prohibit lower-strength wine disclosure. Section 205(f)(2), addressing advertising, contains an alcohol-information exception for malt beverages and wines, without the labeling clause’s state-required-disclosure exception. A later interstate malt-beverage paragraph expressly addresses both labeling and advertising and conditions their application on similar state requirements for comparable intrastate transactions. The agency administers the label ban except where state law affirmatively requires disclosure, but administers the advertising ban where a corresponding state advertising prohibition exists. This is the operating construction, not a holding that the later paragraph mentions only advertising or a new statutory challenge to that construction.

Under the challenged §7.26 regime, state-required statements follow the manner specified by the State; absent such a specification, they use percentage by weight or volume rather than proof, maximums or minimums. Low or reduced alcohol requires less than 2.5 percent alcohol by volume. Non-alcoholic requires the directly conjoined, readily legible, contrasting-background statement that the product contains less than 0.5 percent alcohol by volume. Alcohol-free requires no alcohol. These distinct permissions do not provide all useful numerical comparison information. Sections 7.29(f) and (g), concerning strength descriptions and numerical or design suggestions of strength, are outside this adjudication. Separate outdoor-advertising and publisher/radio provisions are not conflated with the state-law clauses or independently adjudicated. The April 1993 interim suspension of regulatory enforcement complied with the injunction; it was not statutory repeal or a concession of invalidity.

The Government invokes strength competition, possible shifts in consumer preferences, and assistance to state alcohol policy. Coors contests the effectiveness and breadth of suppressing accurate percentages while other channels and beverages receive different treatment. No independently verified fifty-state tally or future strength war is found. The Court assumes the state-assistance rationale may be considered and rejects its unsupported justification on the merits; it does not decide forfeiture.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court unanimously affirms the judgment sustaining the injunction against the challenged prohibition of truthful numerical alcohol-content statements on malt-beverage labels. Eight Justices join the Court’s commercial-speech grounds; Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Challenged numerical label ban | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposing Justice | Preserve the injunction against the challenged label restriction. |

The unappealed advertising judgment remains undisturbed. The Court orders no national label format, alcohol ceiling, tax, or replacement regulation and invalidates no separate state statute or descriptive-strength provision.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirm | Central Hudson/Fox framework, substantial health interest, rejection of unsupported state-assistance justification, failure of advancement, independent failure of reasonable fit, and bounded relief. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | No other Justice | Affirm | Distinct objection to suppressing accurate information because consumers may use it; agrees the scheme fails to advance its stated aim, but does not join the Court’s framework or independent Fox holding. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The challenged scheme does not materially advance its asserted health interest

**Controlling proposition:** The Government may not suppress truthful, nonmisleading numerical alcohol-content statements on lawful malt-beverage labels unless the restriction directly and materially advances a substantial interest. Preventing strength competition and its health harms is substantial, but the challenged scheme fails that requirement on this record; predictive judgment is permissible, while abstract concern and unexplained inconsistencies do not establish the necessary connection.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stevens agrees that the scheme fails in practice but supplies a distinct judgment ground rather than a framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this numerical-label ban has not been shown materially to reduce the strength competition invoked to defend it. Central Hudson requires a real connection between the restriction and the substantial interest; Edenfield requires justification for the actual restraint. The operating scheme permits alcohol information for other beverages, permits malt-beverage advertising where no corresponding state prohibition applies, and yields to state-required label disclosure. Those features leave comparable incentives operating while suppressing useful factual labels. The Court does not demand identical rules for all beverages, controlled studies in every case, or regulation of all sources at once. Edge permits a demonstrated incremental accommodation, while Discovery Network and Lanphere require the chosen distinction to address the asserted harm. The Government’s predictive argument and concern that existing preferences reflect the ban deserve consideration. They do not explain how this scheme materially works despite its inconsistencies. The holding concerns accurate, nonmisleading percentages, not deceptive claims or direct control of alcohol strength.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557: applies its substantial-interest and direct-advancement requirements.
- Edenfield v. Fane, April 26, 1993: applies the requirement of responsive justification for the actual commercial-speech restriction.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, and Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado, April 18, 1995: apply the requirement that the selected speech restriction address the demonstrated harm, without an all-sources-at-once rule.
- United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., June 25, 1993: distinguishes its supported state-based scheme; its holding creates no categorical vice-speech exemption.

##### The ban independently lacks reasonable proportional fit

**Controlling proposition:** Independently of the failure of material advancement, the categorical suppression of accurate numerical beer-label information lacks a reasonable fit to the asserted strength-competition harm in light of its unexplained breadth and workable measures directed more closely at that harm. Reasonable fit requires proportionality, not the least restrictive imaginable means, and the Court prescribes no particular substitute regulation.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight direct joiners adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stevens’s distinct concurrence is not counted as a Fox-framework vote.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the label prohibition is disproportionate to the harm offered to justify it. Fox requires a reasonable fit, not perfection or the least restrictive conceivable choice. Here direct regulation of alcohol strength, focused controls on promotion emphasizing high strength, and a restriction directed to the allegedly affected market segment provide concrete comparisons with a ban on every accurate percentage. The Government answers that national strength limits could displace state choices and that a malt-liquor limitation might miss competition elsewhere. Those objections do not explain suppressing useful comparison information across the challenged category, including information that may assist selection of weaker products. Discovery Network and Lanphere require an account of that actual burden; Ibanez likewise requires a justification responsive to the selected restriction. The existing record and litigated district-court ground permit this alternative holding although the court of appeals stopped at advancement. The Court orders no tax, strength ceiling, national label design or replacement program, and leaves properly justified deceptive-claim regulation available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469: applies reasonable proportional fit and preserves rejection of a least-restrictive-means requirement.
- Discovery Network and Lanphere: apply their distinct fit inquiry to the burden and supported alternatives here.
- Ibanez v. Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, June 13, 1994: applies the need for justification responsive to the actual truthful-information burden.

##### The asserted need to assist state policy supplies no alternative justification on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the Government’s state-assistance rationale is properly considered, its unsubstantiated assertion that federal suppression of accurate label information is needed to facilitate state alcohol policy does not justify this restriction. Legitimate accommodation of differing state policies remains possible when its need and operation are supported; federalism and the Twenty-first Amendment do not alone replace First Amendment scrutiny.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Court opinion and the same eight Justices directly adopt this rejection of the asserted alternative justification. The Court decides no independent forfeiture issue.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government has not established the particular state-policy justification advanced for federal label suppression. Edge recognizes that an effective accommodation of differing state policies may satisfy commercial-speech review. It does not treat an invocation of state authority as proof of the need for a federal information ban. Here the Government has not demonstrated why States cannot operate their own alcohol policies without this suppression of truthful percentages. The statutory state-law provisions and the agency’s differing administration of labeling and advertising describe the scheme; they do not themselves prove its necessity or effect. The Court assumes the rationale may be considered and therefore need not decide Coors’s preservation objection. Nor does it deny the States’ authority to address alcohol harms or hold that federal assistance can never be legitimate. On this record the asserted assistance supplies neither a sufficient independent justification nor an answer to the demonstrated defects in advancement and proportional fit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Edge Broadcasting: preserves the legitimacy of supported state-policy accommodation while distinguishing the missing demonstrated mechanism here.
- Central Hudson: requires the asserted justification to support the actual speech restriction rather than stand as an unexamined label.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Central Hudson and Fox remain the commercial-speech framework. Discovery Network, Edenfield, Ibanez and Lanphere supply the actual requirements of a harm-related classification, responsive evidence, and reasonable fit. Edge preserves supported incremental and state-based regulation at its controlling scope. No precedent is overruled; no categorical vice exception, strict-scrutiny rule for all commercial speech, or least-restrictive-means requirement is adopted.

The challenged truthful numerical malt-beverage label ban fails both material advancement and, independently, reasonable proportional fit. The unsupported state-assistance rationale does not cure either defect. The advertising judgment, distinct strength-description and design rules, state enactments and other applications of §205 remain outside the relief. Lawful direct regulation and properly justified measures against deception remain available without a judicially prescribed replacement program.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Stevens concurs in the judgment. Accurate, unadorned information about a lawful product cannot be suppressed here simply because consumers may use it to choose a stronger beverage. His Edge dissent identifies the objection to keeping consumers uninformed, while Discovery Network requires the selected speech category to relate to an actual harm. He agrees that this scheme fails to advance the stated objective, but does not suggest that greater success at withholding truthful knowledge would cure the underlying objection. His Lanphere joinder in a bounded Central Hudson application did not abandon that distinct ground. He does not deny the Government’s authority to regulate alcohol’s harms directly, forbid deceptive claims, or require properly justified useful disclosures. He declines to join the Court’s commercial-speech framework, its acceptance of the information-suppression interest as framed, or its Fox alternative holding. His truthful-information rationale is noncontrolling; the eight-Justice Court opinion controls without combining separate grounds.

[Tenth Circuit opinion, 2 F.3d 355](https://static.case.law/f3d/2/html/0355-01.html) supplies the litigation path and its advancement-only appellate ground. The [filed briefs and statutory/regulatory appendices](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0507) supply the competing trial-record, fit and state-policy submissions. [27 U.S.C. §205](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title27/pdf/USCODE-1994-title27.pdf) supplies the distinct labeling, advertising and interstate state-law provisions. The agency’s operating construction is distinguished from the statute’s text; no fifty-state survey, unverified state enactment, or unappealed advertising issue is independently adjudicated.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The injunction against the challenged truthful numerical label restriction remains in force. No further merits remand is required. The Government may pursue lawful, appropriately justified alternatives, but the Court commands no particular replacement. The unappealed advertising judgment and separate descriptive-strength provisions are undisturbed.

### Stone v. Immigration and Naturalization Service, No. 93-1199

Merits decision, April 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 28, 1994; decided April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, reviewing 13 F.3d 934 (January 6, 1994). The question is whether a timely agency reconsideration motion filed before judicial review postpones the filing period for review of the original deportation order under former 8 U.S.C. §1105a.

Former §1105a(a) incorporates chapter 158 of Title 28 subject to its stated modifications. Subsection (a)(1) supplies ninety days for this petition and thirty days for an alien convicted of an aggravated felony; this petitioner is not reclassified into the latter category. Subsection (a)(6) requires consolidation whenever review of an original order and review of its reopening or reconsideration are both sought. Its command is mandatory when applicable. Subsection (c) requires exhaustion of remedies available as of right; optional reconsideration is not thereby made mandatory. No departure from the United States is established as a live bar.

Service of a judicial petition stays deportation unless the court otherwise directs, except that an alien convicted of an aggravated felony needs an affirmative judicial stay under subsection (a)(3). Subsection (a)(8) does not require deferral simply because review may be sought. An administrative motion itself supplies no stay under 8 C.F.R. §3.8(a), though the Board or authorized officer may specifically grant one. Section 243.1 addresses administrative finality. Those distinct rules of execution, service, and exhaustion do not alone decide judicial filing finality. The post-September-13-1994 statutory addition applies to proceedings initiated after that date and does not govern this proceeding.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Sixth Circuit’s dismissal of the petition insofar as it seeks review of the original deportation order, 5–4. The separate denial of relief from the reconsideration refusal remains undisturbed.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original-order timeliness dismissal | Affirm, 5–4 | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter, Breyer | The original-order challenge remains dismissed; no merits remand is ordered. |

The separate denial of relief from the reconsideration refusal is outside the admitted question and is left undisturbed, without a new vote on its merits. The dissenting four would reverse only the original-order dismissal and remand for review within the statutory channel. No Justice orders suspension of deportation, decides deportability, or grants a new stay.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg | Affirm original-order dismissal | Separate statutory finality and filing periods, mandatory consolidation of properly pending reviews, and bounded nonreach. |
| Dissent | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter | Would reverse original-order dismissal and remand | Incorporated ordinary finality rule for a timely pre-review reconsideration motion; no equitable extension, late revival, automatic administrative stay, or immigration merits relief. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Reconsideration does not postpone the original-order filing period under this INA scheme

**Controlling proposition:** Under former 8 U.S.C. §1105a, a motion to reopen or reconsider does not postpone the finality or filing period of the original deportation order for judicial review; a petition directed to that order must satisfy its own statutory period. Separately reviewable reconsideration dispositions have their own review period, and subsection (a)(6) requires consolidation when both reviews are properly pending; this rule does not make every reconsideration refusal reviewable.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg: five direct votes for the same statutory ground. The four dissenters adopt a different construction, not a narrower version of this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress preserved separate review periods for the original deportation order and a later reconsideration disposition. Locomotive Engineers supplies the ordinary rule that timely administrative reconsideration postpones finality, but former §1105a incorporates that procedure subject to its own modifications. The added consolidation command treats the two review objects as independently capable of reaching the reviewing court. The shortened original-order period confirms that structure. Consolidation could also do some work under the dissent’s reading when judicial review precedes reconsideration; the Court does not deny that possibility. It finds the amended scheme’s separate-review construction more persuasive. Ibarra governs timely criminal reconsideration, and Darby bars extra exhaustion requirements in its APA setting; neither prevents Congress from specifying a different review arrangement here. Marvin Stone’s March 1993 petition therefore cannot obtain review of the July 1991 order. The Court decides neither the immigration merits nor a new challenge to the reconsideration refusal, and it derives no jurisdictional rule merely from the absence of an administrative stay.

**Precedent treatment:**

- ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S. 270: distinguishes its ordinary administrative finality rule because the amended INA supplies a specific review modification; its separate treatment of original orders and reopening refusals remains intact.
- United States v. Ibarra, October 15, 1991: preserves its timely criminal-reconsideration rule and distinguishes this particular statutory review arrangement.
- Darby v. Cisneros, June 21, 1993: preserves its limits on extra APA exhaustion requirements; it does not decide the filing finality of an INA order.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Locomotive Engineers remains the ordinary administrative reconsideration rule, subject here to the INA’s specific modification. Ibarra retains its criminal-appeal holding, and Darby retains its APA exhaustion holding. No precedent is overruled.

The original deportation order and a separately reviewable reconsideration disposition carry independent filing periods under former §1105a. Optional reconsideration is not a prerequisite to judicial review. Finality for filing, agency enforceability, exhaustion, and a judicial or administrative stay remain distinct. No general equitable-tolling rule, blanket bar to reconsideration review, or immigration merits rule is decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Breyer, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O’Connor and Souter, dissents. Congress expressly incorporated the ordinary administrative-review procedure, and Locomotive Engineers treats a timely reconsideration motion filed before judicial review as postponing reviewable finality. Ibarra supports that treatment of an operative order still under timely reconsideration; Darby cautions against adding an unprovided procedural forfeiture. The dissent reads mandatory consolidation as coordination when an original judicial petition is already pending and review of reconsideration is later sought. It therefore gives subsection (a)(6) work without treating it as withdrawal of the incorporated pre-review rule. On that construction, the March 25 petition was timely after the February 3 denial. The four would restore review of the original order within the statutory channel. They would not revive an expired right through a late motion, grant an automatic administrative stay, impose optional reconsideration as exhaustion, decide an already pending court’s jurisdiction by agency choice, or award immigration relief. ### California Department of Corrections v. Morales, No. 93-1462

Merits decision, April 25, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 9, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing Morales v. California Department of Corrections, 16 F.3d 1001 (February 9, 1994), which reversed denial of his ex post facto habeas claim and required offense-date parole-consideration frequency. The question is whether the 1981 California provision permitting a reasoned postponement of annual reconsideration for at most three years may apply to his 1980 offense. The State seeks reversal; Morales seeks preservation of that consideration-only relief.

At the time of the offense, reconsideration after denial was annual. The 1981 California Penal Code §3041.5(b)(2) amendment retains that default, but permits the next hearing no later than three years after denial if the prisoner has been convicted, in the same or different proceedings, of more than one offense involving the taking of a life, and the Board finds that parole cannot reasonably be expected at a hearing during the intervening years and states its bases. Postponement is permitted, not mandatory. In re Jackson requires reasons separately directed to postponement and permits supporting facts to overlap the reasons for present unsuitability; it establishes review of that initial choice, not a generally available later reopening procedure. Morales's initial hearing occurred in 1989, and his earliest eligibility was August 2, 1990. The Board denied parole and separately deferred reconsideration for three years. Its interval findings addressed the grave offense, prior murder, criminal history, and need for longer observation, evaluation, or treatment. They were predictions, not findings that earlier release was legally impossible.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit’s judgment on Morales’s ex post facto hearing-frequency claim, 5–4. Relief requiring consideration at offense-date annual frequency remains in force. The Board retains discretion to deny parole under lawful suitability standards.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Retrospective application of the 1981 hearing-deferral provision to Morales | Affirm the Ninth Circuit’s ex post facto hearing-frequency judgment, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Breyer | Preserve the Ninth Circuit’s remand for offense-date annual consideration; no release order. |

The previously rejected false-information and sentence claims remain undisturbed and outside this review; they receive no new merits vote. The four dissenters would reverse the hearing-frequency judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with rejecting that claim. None would grant parole.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg throughout | Affirms the hearing-frequency judgment | Part I: statutory and record limits; Part II: the meaningful opportunity actually withdrawn; Part III: claimant’s showing, substantiation of an asserted substitute, and consideration-only relief. Each part has five votes. |
| Concurring opinion | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Fully joins the Opinion of the Court and adds a broader formulation | A substitute for a withdrawn mandatory meaningful opportunity must preserve it in substance through legally available, practically accessible, timely and enforceable consideration; the prisoner retains ultimate constitutional proof. |

An Opinion of the Court controls the two propositions below. The concurrence’s broader substantive formulation is not adopted by the Court. No fractured-decision or Marks inquiry is needed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The initial prediction does not preserve the meaningful later opportunity lost here

**Controlling proposition:** Applying the 1981 California hearing-deferral provision to Morales’s 1980 offense violates the Ex Post Facto Clause because the actual three-year deferral withdraws offense-date mandatory annual consideration during a period when parole is legally available, without an established ordinary substitute for the meaningful intervening opportunities. The separately reasoned prediction of continued unsuitability and review of that initial prediction do not preserve consideration of later rehabilitation, changed circumstances, or a materially mistaken forecast; relief restores consideration, not release.

**Authority:** Part II of O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the same rule, application and relief.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prediction about a future hearing does not supply the meaningful occasion removed on this record. Cavanaugh protects a mandatory avenue to earlier release without proof that parole would have followed, while leaving individualized postponements open. California provides genuine safeguards: annual review remains the default, only prisoners with multiple life-taking convictions qualify, postponement is permissive, reasons must address the intervening years, and the interval cannot exceed three years. Jackson permits review of that initial choice and factual overlap between its reasons and the suitability reasons. Those protections constrain the forecast; they do not hear later developments. Morales became eligible during the postponed interval, and the flexible matrix does not foreclose earlier release as a matter of law. Weaver protects meaningful opportunities to shorten custody; Miller rejects unchanged outer limits as an answer to binding adverse operation. Collins confines the inquiry to punishment. The Court rejects both a categorical procedural exemption and the Ninth Circuit’s broader suggestion that every reduction in frequency suffices. Materially different individualized arrangements remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993: applies its opportunity rule to this reserved individualized setting; its original holding and rejection of proof of the omitted hearing’s outcome remain in force.
- Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981): applies protection of a legally available avenue to reduced custody and the distinction between guaranteed benefits and discretionary alternatives; it supplies no right to parole.
- Miller v. Florida, 482 U.S. 423 (1987): applies scrutiny of binding adverse operation despite unchanged outer limits and a possibly identical result.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990): preserves the punishment boundary; no free-standing rule against every retrospective procedural disadvantage is adopted.
- In re Jackson, 39 Cal.3d 464 (1985): accepts its separate-reasons construction, permitted factual overlap and initial review; its state-court federal characterization does not displace Cavanaugh or establish ordinary later reopening.

##### The claimant proves meaningful loss; the State substantiates a substitute it invokes

**Controlling proposition:** A prisoner challenging retrospective withdrawal of a parole-consideration opportunity must show the earlier legal entitlement, its retrospective actual withdrawal, and a concrete connection to a legally available avenue to earlier release; inconvenience alone is insufficient, and proof that the omitted hearing would actually have produced parole is unnecessary. After that showing, the State must substantiate any replacement mechanism it invokes by identifying its legal basis and establishing its practical availability and timely capacity to provide the relevant consideration; the prisoner retains the ultimate burden of establishing an unconstitutional increase in punishment.

**Authority:** Part III of O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg; five Justices expressly adopt this bounded evidentiary direction and its application. Recognition of a source limitation in the dissent is not an additional join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court requires proof of the meaningful loss before examining an asserted substitute. Morales establishes an annual statutory entitlement at the offense date, its removal by the later deferral, and its connection to discretionary release after eligibility. Cavanaugh does not require him to prove the result of a hearing withheld by law. The State may substantiate its proposed replacement, but must identify a legal basis, a usable means of presenting relevant developments, a responsible decision process, and timely capacity for consideration while earlier release remains possible. The reply’s assertion about reviewing inmate communications and Jackson’s hypothetical extraordinary advancement establish no such ordinary process here. An initial appeal addresses the earlier prediction, not later suitability. Weaver explains why discretionary opportunities cannot be assumed to compensate for guaranteed benefits. Requiring support for the State’s own answer neither transfers ultimate constitutional proof nor demands proof that parole is impossible. The Court prescribes no universal filing procedure or response deadline and does not decide whether a demonstrated different mechanism would suffice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993: preserves meaningful opportunity loss and the absence of an actual-release prerequisite; this decision expressly clarifies substantiation of an asserted replacement.
- Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981): applies its refusal to assume discretionary alternatives compensate for guaranteed benefits; it did not itself prescribe this parole evidentiary direction.
- In re Jackson, 39 Cal.3d 464 (1985): confines review and conceivable advancement to what the opinion establishes; initial review does not become later reconsideration.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The holding concerns Morales’s demonstrated meaningful loss under the challenged 1981 provision. It does not invalidate every individualized postponement, require an interim hearing in every possible individualized scheme, or constitutionalize every scheduling detail. Maximum deferral is permission, not a statutory command or a proved near-uniform cohort practice. The Court neither requires wholly different facts for the two decisions nor treats the initial forecast as unreviewable.

No universal probability threshold, general presumption transferring constitutional proof to the State, numerical response deadline, mandatory filing procedure, suitability finding, release date, damages award, or right to favorable parole is adopted. Adequacy of a demonstrated materially different arrangement, later amendments and their transition or mootness effects are not decided. The false-information and sentence claims are not reopened. Unresolved credit and matrix calculations establish neither release entitlement nor legal impossibility of earlier release.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Cavanaugh remains controlling and its opportunity rule extends to the meaningful withdrawal established here, notwithstanding the genuine individual-prediction constraints. Its reservation is resolved for this record, not erased for every individualized arrangement. Weaver’s protection of legally available opportunities and Miller’s focus on binding adverse operation remain intact; Collins excludes a general procedural-disadvantage rule. Jackson’s separate-reasons construction and initial review are recognized, but its federal characterization cannot override this Court’s intervening law. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

Effective April 25, 1995, California may not impose the challenged three-year interval on Morales on this record. His offense-date annual-consideration relief remains operative, with lawful suitability standards and release discretion preserved. Cavanaugh is unchanged within its original scope; this decision adds its application to the reserved individualized setting established here and the express claimant/substitute substantiation direction. It rejects an automatic rule against every frequency reduction and adopts neither the concurrence’s complete enforceable-substitute formulation for other systems nor the dissent’s view that the prediction suffices here.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela fully joins the Court’s opinion and the opportunity-protecting position joined by Justice Stevens. He adds that when a State removes a legally guaranteed meaningful avenue to earlier release, a replacement must preserve the opportunity in substance. It must be legally available, practically accessible, timely enough to permit consideration before the postponed hearing while earlier release remains legally possible, and enforceable as a usable protection. Discretionary authority to advance a hearing does not, by itself, establish an adequate substitute. Weaver’s treatment of discretionary credits supports distinguishing an enforceable opportunity from officials’ abstract power to act. Lindsey and Miller explain why a possibly identical punishment under former law does not defeat the claim; Cavanaugh supplies the direct opportunity rule.

The prisoner must identify the former entitlement, its retrospective withdrawal and its concrete link to possible earlier release. The State must then substantiate any replacement it relies on, including legal basis, practical availability and timely capacity; theoretical authority requires evidence where operation matters. Uncertainty about that answer cannot justify denying otherwise established relief. Ultimate constitutional proof remains with the prisoner. He accepts the annual default, narrow class, permissive three-year maximum, separate findings, lawful factual overlap and initial review, but finds they do not preserve later rehabilitation, changed circumstances or correction of a materially mistaken forecast. A different arrangement may suffice if it actually preserves the opportunity through an adequate enforceable substitute; no hypothetical system, universal filing procedure or numerical deadline is approved. His full joinder remains intact; the broader formulation has no additional joins and is noncontrolling.

Justice Kennedy dissents, joined throughout by Justices Scalia, Thomas, and Breyer. They would reverse the hearing-frequency judgment and remand consistently with rejecting the claim. They accept Cavanaugh’s binding opportunity protection and absence of an actual-parole proof requirement, but give weight to its individualized-postponement reservation. California retains annual consideration unless a hearing produces a separate, reviewable prediction directed to the intervening years. Permission is restricted to multiple life-taking convictions, requires stated bases, permits a shorter interval and is capped at three years. Jackson requires separately directed reasons while allowing underlying facts to overlap suitability reasons. Morales’s continued-risk findings supply an additional legal decision absent from automatic deletion of alternate hearings.

They acknowledge that forecasts can become outdated and initial review is not later reconsideration. Their sufficient distinction relies on neither unproved ordinary reopening, a binding nineteen-year minimum, aggregate probabilities nor legal impossibility of earlier release. Collins’s punishment boundary and Cavanaugh’s reserved category support their conclusion; a procedural label alone would not suffice. They accept substantiation of an asserted substitute without making equivalent interim review indispensable to every justified delay. Kennedy accepts a bounded method of testing such assertions without joining the Court’s application; Scalia, Thomas and Breyer do not join an independently reusable enumerated allocation rule. Defective, perfunctory, categorical or materially different punitive implementation remains open. They grant no parole and leave rejected claims and later enactments untouched. Their distinction has four votes and does not control.

### United States v. Williams, No. 94-395

Merits decision, April 25, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 22, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing Williams v. United States, 24 F.3d 1143 (May 19, 1994). That court reversed a district-court dismissal for lack of refund jurisdiction and remanded. The question is whether 28 U.S.C. §1346(a)(1) permits Williams to sue for money paid to remove federal tax liens from property she claimed as her own when she was not personally liable for the taxes assessed against Rabin. The Government seeks jurisdictional dismissal; Williams seeks permission to proceed with her refund suit.

Williams paid through escrow to free the house she was selling from liens asserted for Rabin's taxes, timely claimed a refund administratively, and sued after denial. No levy or substitute-fund agreement is established. The lower judgment did not award a refund. The parties dispute lien validity, the scope of an asserted concession, and the amount and components of payment; those disputes are not resolved by the jurisdictional ruling.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit's jurisdictional judgment, 7–2. Williams's refund action may proceed under the ordinary administrative-claim, merits, and proof requirements. The Court awards no refund and fixes no amount.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory availability of Williams's refund action | Affirm jurisdiction, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Kennedy, Thomas | The Ninth Circuit's remand for further proceedings remains in force; no monetary award. The dissent would restore the jurisdictional dismissal. |

The merits of any recovery are not a separate disposition resolved here. The Court leaves lien validity and priority, any binding concession, wrongfulness, proof, and amount for further proceedings as necessary.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

Independent taxpayer-definition ground: five. Distinct third-party-remedy construction: seven. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Express refund consent includes this lien-backed collection claim

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1346(a)(1) permits a refund suit by a person who, although not personally liable for the assessment against another, paid under the coercive force of a federal tax lien to free property claimed as her own and alleges that the Government wrongfully collected that payment. Section 7422(a)'s duly filed administrative claim and §6511's applicable timing requirements remain mandatory; §6511's taxpayer terminology does not implicitly exclude this timely collection claim from the express waiver.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined on this ground by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Breyer: seven direct votes for the stated plaintiff category and jurisdiction-only disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted refund waiver reaches Williams's claim about money collected from her through liens asserted against her property. Section 1346(a)(1) expressly covers wrongful collection as well as erroneous assessment. Nordic Village requires clear monetary consent; this statute supplies it, rather than leaving the remedy to general jurisdiction or fairness. Dalm requires reading the waiver with the administrative-claim and timing provisions, and Williams duly presented a timely claim. Those provisions do not silently remove this collection claim merely because the assessment named someone else. The Government's integrated reading gives the timing provision a claimant restriction that the express waiver does not impose here. Stahmann supports distinguishing a payment to free one's goods from an unsolicited payment for another, while its different tax incidence and collector-action setting prevent treating it as a construction of these statutes. The holding permits adjudication of the alleged wrongful collection. It does not find the liens invalid, fix an amount, excuse an untimely claim, or authorize strangers' and volunteers' suits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., February 25, 1992: applies its demand for unequivocal monetary consent and distinguishes its text permitting nonmonetary readings; the present refund waiver expressly authorizes monetary recovery.
- United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (1990): applies its connected claim-and-timing requirements, while distinguishing its untimely action from this timely nonassessed claimant.
- United States v. California, April 26, 1993: preserves its refusal to create a common-law refund remedy; this action rests on the express congressional remedy it left open.
- Stahmann v. Vidal, 305 U.S. 61 (1938): applies its distinction between collection from one's property and a volunteered payment, while retaining its different statutory incidence and collector-action limits.

Section 6511(a) allows, for a tax requiring a return, a claim within three years of filing the return or two years of payment, whichever expires later; if no return was filed, the period is two years from payment. Its separate stamp-tax clause allows three years from payment. No lateness is alleged here. Section 7422(b) permits suit whether or not payment was under protest or duress. The coercive lien and property connection identify the facts and limited plaintiff category decided; they do not create a universal protest or duress prerequisite or decide every other claimant's eligibility.

##### Williams also falls within taxpayer for these refund provisions

**Controlling proposition:** Independently, a person whose claimed property and own payment are subjected to the lien-backed tax collection presented here is a person subject to an internal-revenue tax within §7701(a)(14) for the connected refund provisions, even though she is not personally liable for the assessment. This construction allows her timely administrative refund claim; it neither makes every payer a taxpayer for all Code purposes nor adjudicates the debt or lien's validity.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's separately identified alternative holding, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, and Breyer: five direct votes. Stone-Zsela and Scalia do not join or decide this independent definitional ground. Kennedy and Thomas reject it. The alternative controls through its own five-Justice coalition, without combining distinct opinions under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds, as an independent ground, that the connected taxpayer provisions include Williams in the limited collection setting before it. Section 7701(a)(14) speaks of a person subject to an internal-revenue tax, and the asserted federal liens subjected property she claimed and her own payment to collection. Personal assessment and subjection to that collection are not identical. The Government correctly insists, under Dalm, that §7422's duly filed claim requirement and §6511 be read together; the Court does so without treating the refund machinery as available only to the assessed debtor. Nordic Village's clarity requirement remains satisfied by the express monetary waiver read with this limited definition. The definitions govern title 26 unless otherwise distinctly expressed or manifestly incompatible with statutory intent; this construction concerns these connected refund provisions rather than an unexamined transfer of every Code definition into title 28. The Court does not decide that any payment creates taxpayer status everywhere, that the underlying assessment may be challenged on every ground, or that Williams has proved a refund entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dalm: applies the integrated treatment of waiver, claim, and timing provisions without converting its late-claim holding into a decision about this plaintiff category.
- Nordic Village: applies clear-consent analysis to the actual refund text and its connected definition; no general jurisdictional or equitable label supplies consent.

##### Distinct third-party mechanisms do not foreclose this refund action

**Controlling proposition:** The cited quiet-title, wrongful-levy, and substitute-fund provisions do not make those mechanisms exclusive of this §1346(a)(1) collection claim or require their exhaustion before her duly claimed refund suit. Their own triggers and restrictions remain in force: a lien is not a levy, discharge into a substitute fund is permissive and requires an agreement, and the fund remedies presuppose the corresponding fund.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined on this construction by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Breyer: seven direct votes. This ground answers the asserted statutory obstacle; remedial hardship does not independently waive immunity.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government's other cited remedies do not withdraw the express refund route for this claim. Section 2410 permits quiet-title proceedings against a claimed federal lien; it does not automatically become a refund action after payment. Section 7426(a)(1) requires a levy, and none occurred here. Section 6325(b)(3) permits the Secretary to discharge sold property upon an agreement holding proceeds as a fund with the same lien priority. It does not command an agreement or prove that Williams obtained one. Sections 7426(a)(3) and (b)(4) address rights and recovery in an actual substitute fund. Section 7426(c) conclusively presumes the assessment valid in proceedings under that section; this distinct restriction is neither erased nor silently transferred to §1346. Dalm preserves the refund claim prerequisite, but supplies no duty to exhaust different proceedings. California forbids creating a common-law remedy; the present suit uses enacted consent. The Court need not resolve disputes about advice, knowledge, or administrative willingness to recognize the statutes' distinct operation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dalm: preserves the statutory refund-claim requirement without adding an exhaustion duty for quiet-title, levy, or substitute-fund proceedings.
- United States v. California: preserves the distinction between an express remedy and one newly created by a court; the practical limitations of other remedies corroborate statutory construction but do not create consent.

The holding is jurisdictional. Lien validity and priority, purchaser status and consideration, the effect and scope of any binding Government concession, wrongfulness of collection, payment components, and recoverable amount remain for further proceedings as necessary. No interest, fees, damages, or unrestricted challenge to Rabin's underlying assessment is awarded or authorized by this decision.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Nordic Village's unequivocal-consent rule remains intact and is satisfied by the express refund text at the stated scope. Dalm's administrative-claim and timing conditions remain binding; its late-claim holding did not decide this timely nonassessed payer. United States v. California retains its bar against manufacturing a common-law refund right and is distinguished by the express remedy here. Stahmann supports treating payment to free one's own goods as different from a volunteered payment, while its tax-incidence and collector-action setting remain material limits. No precedent is overruled, and no general equitable waiver is created.

The express refund waiver permits the stated lien-backed collection claim after satisfaction of the administrative-claim and timing rules. Independently, the five-Justice taxpayer-definition holding includes this collection relationship within the connected refund provisions. The third-party remedies retain their separate statutory triggers and do not foreclose this suit or add an exhaustion step. Jurisdiction does not establish wrongful collection or entitlement to any money. Volunteers, strangers, universal taxpayer status, and an unrestricted challenge to another person's assessment remain outside the holdings.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia concurs, joining the Court's express-waiver and connected timing analysis and its treatment of the distinct third-party remedies. Nordic Village requires clarity in the scope as well as the existence of a monetary waiver. Here the express wrongful-collection language supplies that consent, and the remote timing provision cannot plausibly impose the significant implicit claimant exclusion urged by the Government. He withholds the independently sufficient conclusion that Williams is a taxpayer under §§6511 and 7701 because that alternative is unnecessary to his disposition. He expresses no contrary view on that definition. Ordinary administrative-claim and timing requirements remain effective, and he joins no equitable exception to sovereign immunity.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the Court's primary ground and judgment. The collection was directed against Williams through a lien on property she claimed, and her payment therefore gives her a concrete collection injury even though another person was assessed. The levy, quiet-title, and substitute-fund mechanisms did not provide an adequate route in these circumstances: no levy occurred, quiet title did not furnish a realistic postpayment recovery, and a discretionary discharge requiring an agreement was not an assured substitute for refund review. He treats that remedial setting as confirmation of the enacted waiver's application, not as an equitable enlargement of consent. His ground remains confined to a compelled payment connected with the claimant's property, leaving volunteers and strangers outside the holding. He does not adopt the separate taxpayer-definition alternative. The refund suit proceeds subject to the administrative claim, merits, and proof requirements, without a present monetary award. His additional explanation does not create a general adequacy-of-remedy test.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice Thomas, dissents. Dalm requires the broad refund language to be read with the duly filed claim requirement, the timing provision's taxpayer language, and §7701's definition. In their view those connected provisions do not unequivocally authorize a person concededly not liable for the tax to invoke the taxpayer refund action. Wrongful collection and personal liability are distinct, but collection against claimed property does not itself make that person the taxpayer Congress identified. Nordic Village requires clear consent for the requested remedy and its scope; inconvenience cannot supply it. The specially drawn third-party statutes reinforce that reading without proving that any alternative actually was available to Williams or had to be exhausted. The dissent does not call a lien a levy, treat a permitted substitute-fund agreement as mandatory, or decide lien validity. Stahmann involved a statute intended to burden the producers and an action against the collector; it does not settle this different waiver. The two would reverse the jurisdictional judgment and restore dismissal of this refund action, leaving other lawfully authorized remedies and the merits unadjudicated. Their claimant construction is noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand remain in force. The district court may adjudicate the refund action under the ordinary claim, merits, and proof requirements, resolving lien validity and priority, any binding concession, wrongfulness, and amount as necessary. The Court orders no immediate payment, interest, fees, or damages and predicts no merits result.

### New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co., No. 93-1408; Pataki v. Travelers Insurance Co., No. 93-1414; and Hospital Association of New York State v. Travelers Insurance Co., No. 93-1415

Merits decision, April 26, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 18, 1995; decided April 26, 1995. On writs of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, reviewing its opinion issued October 25, 1993 and amended January 14, 1994. The question is whether ERISA preempts the challenged 13%, additional 11% and conditional up-to-9% New York hospital-payment measures in the reviewed commercial-insurance and HMO applications.

These consolidated cases concern three New York hospital-payment measures: a 13% differential retained by hospitals, an additional 11% charge on the commercial-insurer category, and an HMO assessment of up to 9% with Medicaid participation and enrollment adjustments. The district court granted summary judgment against the State’s ERISA position, 813 F.Supp. 996. The amended Second Circuit judgment also sustained independent Federal Employees Health Benefits Act relief for the experience-rated federal employee plans at issue and resolved portions of Actuarial Letter No. 5 concerning stop-loss insurance. Those independent components are outside this review.

The reproduced state provisions have distinct terms and periods. They are not a flat 24% charge on every payor or an unavoidable 9% charge on every HMO. The appendix reproduces §2807-c(1) for January 1, 1988–December 31, 1993; the additional 11% provision expressly addresses April 1, 1992–March 31, 1993 discharges. The Court resolves the presented ERISA controversy without determining liability under unexamined extensions or fixing a 1995 rate.

**Hospital payment categories.** Section 2807-c(1) excludes Medicare payments and permits alternative reimbursement methodologies authorized under subdivision four, paragraphs (e)–(i). Paragraph (a) covers state governmental agencies, article 43 insurance corporations’ expense-incurred policies, the described payments by those corporations for foreign-corporation subscribers under subdivision 12(d), and article 44 HMO subscribers. The diagnosis-related-group case payment includes reimbursable operating costs, capital expenses, bad-debt and charity-care allowances, the primary-care allowance, the allowance for financially distressed hospitals, the rate-year trend factor and the subdivision four (a)–(d) case-payment adjustments. Paragraph (a-1) gives local-government payments for local correctional inmates the state-agency rates.

Paragraph (b) covers expense-incurred payments under workers’ compensation, volunteer firefighters’, volunteer ambulance workers’ and no-fault laws; self-insured funds paying hospitals directly, except enrollees covered by a rate-method agreement under paragraph (a) of subdivision two; licensed commercial accident-and-health insurers whose policies cover inpatient services on that basis; and out-of-plan benefits authorized under §4406, except those offered by a not-for-profit corporation or an organization qualifying under Internal Revenue Code §501(c). The payment equals the article 43 case-payment basis, adjusted for uncovered services, increased by 13%, and adjusted under subdivision 11(i) where applicable. A hospital may not refuse a qualifying direct-payment arrangement without due cause. Paragraphs (a) and (b) payors may not choose the lower of charges and the case payment or apportion the case payment by excluding a preexisting or acquired condition that must be treated along with the reason for admission. These are statutory payment rules; they are not findings that an ERISA plan changed its benefits.

Paragraph (c) uses hospital charges for patients without an authorized case-payment arrangement under paragraphs (a) or (b), or coverage under subdivision two (a). The subdivision four (d) short-stay definition applies, with that paragraph’s developed payment increased by 13%. For a charge-paying patient the ceiling is 120% of the paragraph (b) diagnosis-related-group case payment, without the subdivision 11(i) adjustment. A hospital must apply its charge schedule uniformly. This is not another application of the full 24% differential.

**Additional commercial-insurer charge.** The reproduced subdivision 11(i) applies the additional 11%, totaling 24% with the existing 13%, to the identified commercial-insurer expense-incurred discharges from April 1, 1992 through March 31, 1993. The appendix contains an ellipsis after that provision’s total-24% statement. Subdivision 14-e creates the statewide supplementary pool: hospitals submit the additional component, and the commissioner deposits it and investment income into the general fund. The complete extension text and resulting current rate liability are not decided here.

**HMO charge and reductions.** Subdivision 2-a(a) addresses article 44 and article 43 HMO inpatient discharges on or after July 1, 1992. Medicaid-eligible subscribers and participants in the 1988 chapter 703 regional pilot projects are excluded. The base is the subdivision one case payment, subdivision four per-diem rate, or negotiated and approved subdivision two (b) rate, whichever applies. The additional factor is 9%, subject to elimination or reduction. Notice was due June 1 for the 1992 rate period and December 1 for each following annual January rate period. An appeal by May 1 can obtain a July 1 adjustment.

Elimination under subdivision 2-a(b)(i) requires status as a Social Services Law §364-j managed-care provider in each social-services district in the service area and enrollment of at least 90% of the sum of the target numbers of Medicaid recipients not exempt from managed care. The reproduced determination dates are May 1 for the 1992 rate period, November 1 preceding the 1993 rate year, and May 1 when an appeal is taken for the six-month period beginning July 1. Reaching 25%, 50% or 75% of total target enrollment gives the corresponding 25%, 50% or 75% reduction, rather than elimination.

An HMO may apply to the social-services commissioner for a district participation exemption by May 1 for the 1992 period or October 1 preceding the 1993 rate year, based on a good-faith effort to obtain a managed-care contract or other commissioner-established criteria. The following nonexclusive good-faith routes apply only to the reproduced 1992 and 1993 rate years. First, the HMO submitted an implementation plan and would have begun enrollment but for the district’s failure or refusal to contract. Second, it submitted letters of intent to all districts, contracted with half by July 1, 1992 and two-thirds by January 1, 1993, and could have contracted with the rest but for Department-agreed implementation delay. Third, the same letters and agreed-delay conditions apply where contracts cover districts containing half and then two-thirds of the service area’s nonexempt Medicaid population on those dates. Fourth, it meets the total enrollment target for its otherwise nonexempt districts despite incomplete contracts. For the fourth route, 1992–1993 fee-for-service enrollment is calculated from annual Medicaid visits to a licensed diagnostic and treatment center operated by the HMO or a distinct licensed nonprofit providing or arranging a majority of its services, divided by commercial subscribers’ annual average visits to those facilities. The commissioner also considers implementation of a plan to capitate a substantial percentage of that enrollment under an approved viable schedule. This fourth route permits only a 75% reduction, not elimination.

An exempt district is removed from the service area for the calculation. An HMO with noncontiguous regions may apply across its whole service area or separately on an aggregate basis for each region. The district target equals the ratio of its commercial subscribers to all HMO commercial subscribers in that district, excluding Medicaid, Medicare and regional-pilot subscribers from both sides, multiplied by the district enrollment objective. That objective is the greatest applicable measure: the approved nonexempt Medicaid enrollment goal as of July 1 of the preceding year; 5% of the district’s nonexempt Medicaid population before completion of the first full year of its approved managed-care plan; or actual nonexempt enrollment on July 1 of the preceding year, except May 1, 1992 for the first period. The actual-enrollment measure excludes providers that are neither HMOs nor entities authorized under §4403-a. The most recent subscriber statistics govern the ratio. Medicaid enrollees under an affiliation contract with a prepaid health-services plan may count when the commissioner approves the contract in consultation with the social-services commissioner.

**Payment and administration.** HMOs remit the adjusted assessment directly to a statewide pool for the preceding month’s discharges; pool funds and investment income go to the general fund. Estimated payment is due within fifteen days after month-end unless the actual payment has been made within that period. Collection stops when the commissioner, consulting the budget director, determines that $31 million has been or will be collected for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1993. Excess collections for that year are refunded in proportion to each HMO’s share of total payments. Arrearages bear subdivision twenty interest and penalties on the basis used for the bad-debt and charity-care pools. The commissioner may contract with the subdivision sixteen (c) pool administrator or, if unavailable, another designated administrator. If a contract is made, annual receipt-and-distribution audits are required; approved reasonable costs may be paid from the pool, with annual personnel services limited to $200,000. The $31 million cap is not declared permanent.

Subdivision 2-a(d) extends the adjusted factor to point-of-service inpatient business when an article 43 corporation offering those benefits was HMO-certified on April 1, 1992 and later transfers to its point-of-service product reduce HMO enrollment by more than 20%. The pre-April-1 subscriber ratio then controls the target. Satisfactory evidence that the transfers occurred for reasons beyond the corporation’s control prevents that provision’s application. The same pool and payment procedure apply. Subdivision 2-a(e) requires the information needed to administer the scheme, including county enrollment data by May 1 each year, identifying Medicaid, Medicare and regional-pilot subscribers separately and reflecting enrollment no earlier than the previous year.

**Federal provisions and review limits.** ERISA §514(a), subject to subsection (b), supersedes state laws insofar as they relate to a §4(a) plan not exempt under §4(b); its effective date is January 1, 1975, and §514(b)(1) excludes earlier causes of action, acts and omissions. Section 514(b)(2)(A), subject to (B), preserves state insurance, banking and securities regulation. Subparagraph (B) prevents a covered nonexempt plan or its trust from being deemed an insurer, bank, trust company or investment company, or engaged in insurance or banking, for the stated state-regulatory purposes; its express exception is a plan established primarily to provide death benefits. A separate exempt workers’ compensation, unemployment or disability plan does not exempt a different covered health plan. Relation, saving and deeming are distinct questions. The direct self-funded application remains for remand; the separate insurance-saving theory for the up-to-9% assessment is not established as preserved. No new ruling on the Tax Injunction Act, laches, FEHBA, or the stop-loss letter is made.

**Lower relief and its limits.** The February 3, 1993 district order enjoined the surcharges against commercial insurers and HMOs in connection with their ERISA-plan coverage. It separately granted FEHBA relief against the 13% and 11% charges and stop-loss Actuarial Letter relief. The February 9 stay order stayed the 13% portion, did not stay the 9% and 11% portions, and required parties subject to the latter charges to pay into interest-bearing escrow pending resolution of defendants’ appeal. Those were different directions, not a single nonpayment rule. The Blues’ reply acknowledges direct self-funded applications and reports a later payment direction including Travelers as fiduciary for the Railroad Plans; its quoted passage contains an ellipsis, and the complete original later order is not available here. The January 14, 1994 amended appellate judgment directs a conforming district judgment. No particular later mandate date, conforming decree, escrow distribution or stay extension or termination is established. The Court therefore identifies the application and legal ground affected by its mandate, without inventing a current payment regime or treating the 1993 comparative-harm findings as findings of present harm.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The reviewed 13% hospital differential is not preempted

**Controlling proposition:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the reviewed 13% hospital differential merely because its price effect may influence a covered plan’s choice of insurer or coverage arrangement. In these commercial-insurance applications the measure neither uses ERISA status or covered-plan benefits as its legal benchmark nor commands plan benefits, eligibility, uniform administration or an alternative enforcement remedy; the Court reserves economic effects sufficiently acute to operate as a substantive coverage command or effectively restrict insurer choice.

**Authority:** Justice Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined on this proposition by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes. The direct self-funded application is outside this merits holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the price influence shown here does not establish the relation to an ERISA plan that §514(a) requires. Shaw supplies connection or reference as the inquiry, while recognizing that some effects are too remote. Mackey permits ordinary legal processes despite their costs to plans. This differential operates through hospital-payment categories that exist whether customers obtain coverage through an ERISA plan or otherwise. It does not use a covered plan’s benefits as the mandatory legal measure, as Board of Trade did, or alter plan reimbursement rights as FMC did. The contrary argument properly observes that the charge is selective and designed to affect choices. But intended economic influence alone does not transform a market price into a plan command. ERISA’s objectives and the presumption against displacement of traditional state health-cost regulation guide that reading, without promising plans uniform market costs or exempting every state health law. Acute effects amounting to substantive compulsion could present a different case. The present record does not establish them; insurance saving and direct self-funded application remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85: applies the connection-or-reference inquiry and its remote-effects boundary without making every cost consequence preemptive.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825: applies the distinction between singling out ERISA plans and ordinary legal effects, including costs, without making general applicability an automatic defense.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguishes its mandatory covered-plan benefit benchmark, which these reviewed payment categories do not contain; that holding remains intact.
- FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52: distinguishes its alteration of plan subrogation and benefit calculation; its separate saving/deemer architecture remains undisturbed.

##### The additional commercial-insurer 11% charge is not preempted

**Controlling proposition:** The reviewed additional 11% commercial-insurer charge likewise is not preempted under §514(a): its revenue destination and additional price differential do not establish a required plan benefit, insurer choice or administrative arrangement on this record. This application adopts neither a numerical safe harbor for combined charges nor immunity for financial pressure that actually operates as a substantive coverage command or effectively restricts insurer choice.

**Authority:** Souter’s Court opinion and the same nine named Justices directly adopt this distinct application. It is not a holding that the additional 11% applies to every payor or that the reproduced period fixes liability under a later extension.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that payment of the additional commercial-insurer component into a state pool does not change the relevant plan relationship. The 13% differential remains with hospitals; subdivision 14-e directs this additional component and its investment income to the general fund. That distinction matters to the statute’s operation, but neither revenue destination defines ERISA benefits or their administration. Shaw requires a sufficient connection, and Mackey shows why added costs alone do not meet that requirement. Board of Trade involved a mandatory measure supplied by an existing covered plan, which is absent here. Respondents emphasize that the additional 11% increases the combined incentive to choose another insurer. The Court considers that argument without finding that premiums fell, plans switched, or every future differential would be harmless. The supplied record shows influence on market choices, not an effective substantive command. Insurance saving is therefore unnecessary. The complete extension law, live prospective relief and any reimbursement implementation remain for proceedings on the actual law and record below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw: applies its relation boundary to the additional charge’s actual operation, rather than treating legislative purpose or percentage alone as conclusive.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between ordinary added economic costs and the relation ERISA preempts; it supplies no quantitative exemption.
- Board of Trade: preserves its covered-plan-content benchmark and distinguishes this additional payor charge, whose amount does not use plan benefits as the legal measure.

##### The conditional HMO assessment is not preempted

**Controlling proposition:** The reviewed HMO assessment of up to 9%, subject to its actual Medicaid participation, enrollment, exemption, reduction and payment conditions, is not preempted by §514(a) on this record. It affects HMO prices and participation choices without a demonstrated command to an ERISA plan’s benefit structure, eligibility, insurer choice or uniform administration; its conditions receive no categorical immunity from preemption if a different application establishes such a connection or effective compulsion.

**Authority:** Souter’s Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes for this separately analyzed assessment. The decision supplies no insurance-saving holding for the distinct 9% measure.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the HMO assessment requires its own analysis rather than treating every surcharge as the same hospital rate. It is paid directly to a pool, excludes Medicaid and regional-pilot discharges, and permits reductions or elimination through the distinct participation and enrollment conditions described above. The good-faith fee-for-service route yields only a 75% reduction. Those rules can influence an HMO’s Medicaid participation and costs, but the record does not show that they prescribe a covered plan’s benefits, eligibility or claims administration. Shaw and Mackey require attention to the actual plan connection; FMC’s compulsory alteration of plan rights cannot be inferred from every provider or payor obligation. The contrary argument stresses the participation conditions’ organizational pressure. That pressure remains relevant, but is not shown here to impose substantive plan coverage or effectively restrict insurer choice. The Court adopts no numerical coercion threshold and no finding of inevitable enrollment. Because §514(a) does not preempt this reviewed application, the separate saving-preservation question need not be resolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw: applies connection-or-reference analysis separately to the HMO measure and preserves the possibility that more acute indirect effects could suffice.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between actual administrative commands and economic consequences without dismissing duties merely because imposed through generally applicable law.
- FMC: distinguishes compulsory changes to plan reimbursement rights from the HMO participation and payment conditions shown here, retaining the insured/self-funded distinction where saving and deemer issues must be reached.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No direct self-funded merits ruling, new insurance-saving test, numerical safe harbor, uniform-cost guarantee, FEHBA ruling, stop-loss-letter ruling, Tax Injunction Act holding, or amended-law rate command is adopted. These are record-specific applications of the relation boundary, not categorical immunity for health regulation, price regulation, generally applicable statutes or indirect burdens.

##### The reserved direct-plan claim remains live after limited vacatur

**Controlling proposition:** Insofar as the reviewed judgment or associated ERISA hospital-surcharge injunction adjudicates or protects a direct self-funded application left unresolved here, that portion is vacated and remanded for consideration under the announced law and any properly presented independent ground. Vacatur removes the prior adjudication’s force as a continuing final entitlement to protection without rejecting the claim, guaranteeing interim relief, disturbing independently surviving FEHBA or stop-loss relief, or imposing a payment or escrow command.

**Authority:** Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes for this component-specific procedural disposition under 28 U.S.C. §2106. No direct-plan relation, saving or deemer merits conclusion commands a vote because those questions are not decided.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that reservation of this application must preserve the claim without silently sustaining the prior final adjudication. Section 2106 permits reversal, vacatur and appropriate further proceedings for matters lawfully under review; the Court uses that authority to separate decided applications from the unresolved one. X-Citement Video preserves both available defenses of a favorable judgment and a lawful remand option. It does not make an available defense an established injunction entitlement. FMC requires respect for distinct insurer and self-funded inquiries, but does not decide this direct-payor application for the Court. Respondents may therefore pursue the reserved ground below. Treating the reserved ground’s availability as sufficient to preserve final protection assumes success on a ground the Court leaves open; categorical merits reversal would make the opposite error. The limited vacatur adopts neither assumption. Its application-defined scope does not invent a freestanding direct-plan decree. Independent FEHBA and stop-loss grounds remain, and the lower courts must assess any supported interim or fund-administration request without a new Supreme Court payment, escrow or timing command.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: applies its distinction between an available judgment-defending alternative and a required immediate decision, preserving a lawful remand without treating the alternative as a proved entitlement to relief.
- FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52: preserves its distinct insured/self-funded statutory inquiries, without deciding the reserved hospital-payor application by analogy alone.

This vacatur is expressly chosen for the reviewed unresolved component. It establishes no universal rule that a remand preserves an injunction, that a reversal automatically dissolves every order, or that reservation requires interim protection.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Shaw and Mackey retain their connection-or-reference framework and limits on remote effects. Board of Trade’s covered-plan benchmark is distinguished, not narrowed; a separate exempt benefit arrangement still does not erase reference to a covered plan. FMC’s subrogation holding and insured/self-funded saving/deemer distinction remain intact. John Hancock’s component-specific statutory qualifications and Fabe’s separate McCarran-Ferguson holding remain undisturbed; neither supplies an unneeded insurance-saving decision here. Wolens’s distinct airline contract rule is not transplanted into ERISA. No precedent is overruled. X-Citement Video preserves alternative defenses and lawful remand without converting an undecided ground into an established injunction entitlement. Section 2106 authorizes the express limited vacatur; the Court creates no general mandate-timing or automatic-injunction rule.

The three reviewed commercial-insurance and HMO measures survive the asserted ERISA relation challenge because the shown price and participation influences do not impose plan terms or administration and do not establish equivalent acute compulsion. The decision clarifies the boundary left open for ordinary indirect economic effects in Board of Trade. Laws mandating plan benefits, specifically referring to ERISA plans, interfering with uniform administration or creating alternative enforcement remedies remain outside this holding. Direct self-funded relation, saving and deemer questions remain for consideration on remand; neither insurer treatment nor the absence of a present saving ruling decides them. The Court fixes no current rate, refund amount or entitlement under other law. The reviewed direct-plan adjudication and associated ERISA surcharge protection are vacated insofar as they concern the reserved application, so they supply no continuing final entitlement to protection. The claim remains live. Independent relief and properly supported interim requests retain their own legal and factual predicates.

### United States v. Lopez, No. 93-1260

Merits decision, April 26, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 8, 1994; decided April 26, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, reviewing 2 F.3d 1342 (September 15, 1993). The question is whether the original Gun-Free School Zones Act’s possession prohibition can sustain this prosecution under Congress’s commerce power.

On March 10, 1992, Alfonso Lopez, a twelfth-grade student, brought an unloaded .38 handgun and five bullets to Edison High School in San Antonio. The lower opinion reports a proposed $40 delivery for a gang conflict. The parties stipulated that an ATF agent would testify that the handgun was manufactured outside Texas. Neither circumstance supplies an element absent from the statute or an allegation absent from the indictment. State charges were dropped after federal prosecution began. Following a stipulated bench trial, the district court imposed six months’ imprisonment and two years’ supervised release. The Fifth Circuit reversed and directed dismissal, which the district court subsequently entered. The unresolved appellate objections concerned supervised release and the special assessment.

The charged original 18 U.S.C. §922(q)(1)(A) forbids an individual knowingly to possess a firearm at a place the individual knows, or has reasonable cause to believe, is a school zone. Sections 921(a)(25)–(26) include the grounds of a public, parochial or private elementary or secondary school, as determined under state law, and the area within 1,000 feet of those grounds. The possession subsection has no commerce element.

Its seven exceptions cover possession: on private property outside school grounds; under a license from the State in which the zone is located or its political subdivision, where the licensing law requires prior law-enforcement verification that the individual qualifies; of an unloaded firearm in a locked container or a locked firearms rack on a motor vehicle; for use in a program approved by a school in the zone; in accordance with a contract between such a school and the individual or the individual’s employer; by an officer acting in an official law-enforcement capacity; and of an unloaded firearm while crossing school premises to reach public or private lands open to hunting, with school authorities’ permission to enter. The lower court found none applicable. That is a finding about this prosecution, distinct from the statutory exceptions themselves.

The separate original §922(q)(2) forbids knowingly, or with reckless disregard for another’s safety, discharging or attempting to discharge a firearm at a place the person knows is a school zone. Its exceptions cover private property outside school grounds, participation in a school-approved program, the school-contract situation, and official law-enforcement action. Those four exceptions are distinct from the seven possession exceptions; discharge liability is not decided. Original §922(q)(3) preserves state and local laws establishing gun-free school zones.

Original §924(a)(4) authorizes imprisonment up to five years, a fine not exceeding $5,000, or both; imprisonment may not run concurrently with imprisonment imposed under another provision. Except for the authorized maximum, it treats the offense as a misdemeanor for purposes of other law. The Court does not apply a later fine provision to this charge, decide the reserved sentencing objections, or adjudicate an amended possession statute.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The original possession offense lacks the required commerce connection

**Controlling proposition:** The original §922(q)(1)(A) school-zone possession prohibition cannot sustain this prosecution on the chain from local firearm possession through crime costs, impaired education and reduced productivity to the national economy. Its stand-alone regulated class and enacted elements supply no adequate commercial connection, and that generalized downstream-effects theory cannot supply one; the holding does not categorically exclude all noneconomic intrastate conduct from federal regulation.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas: five direct votes for this bounded ground and affirmance. The Chief Justice’s additional six-route framework has no additional joins and is not the Court’s exhaustive test. No Marks synthesis is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government’s asserted chain does not establish the necessary connection between this offense and commerce. Wickard permits aggregation in its economic-class setting; Perez likewise sustains regulation of an economic class despite the small effect of an individual instance. Neither makes every local possession offense commercial because its eventual social costs may influence national productivity. Allied-Bruce involved actual multistate commercial operations and expressly left the constitutional boundary open. The reported paid delivery and the handgun’s manufacture outside Texas do not narrow the class Congress enacted or add a commerce condition to this charge. The contrary argument properly emphasizes broad congressional authority and judicial respect for rational legislative judgments. But aggregation cannot itself supply a connection whose asserted logic would reach virtually every serious local problem. The conclusion requires no universal formal-findings prerequisite and does not revive immunity for traditionally local governmental functions. Broad commercial regulation, including appropriate inclusion of intrastate conduct necessary to a valid interstate regime, remains available. The separate discharge offense and materially different enactments remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111: preserves aggregation at its economic-class scope; the school-possession/productivity chain does not extend that holding to this stand-alone offense.
- Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146: preserves Congress’s regulation of an economic class without requiring each instance to have a substantial effect; its regulated commercial setting remains material.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: distinguishes actual interstate commercial operation from remote-object history; its statutory breadth and reserved constitutional ceiling remain intact.
- Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Assn., 452 U.S. 264: preserves broad review of congressional commercial regulation without treating deference as a grant of general police power or imposing a universal formal-findings requirement.
- New York v. United States, June 19, 1992: distinguishes compelled state administration from direct regulation of private persons; neither its anti-compulsion rule nor the traditional local subject alone decides this case.
- Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority, 469 U.S. 528: remains undisturbed in its generally applicable regulation of state activity; traditional-governmental-function immunity is not revived.

##### The possession text cannot be saved by adding a new commerce element

**Controlling proposition:** The Court will not insert an unexpressed commerce element into original §922(q)(1)(A) to sustain this charge. Because the constitutional ground requires affirmance of dismissal under the enacted prohibition, the Court does not decide the independent indictment defect that would arise if a permissible construction made a nexus an element.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas: five direct votes for the no-insertion disposition and the precise reservation. No Justice’s conditional willingness to consider the indictment issue is counted as a separate holding deciding it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that interpretation cannot substitute a different offense for the one enacted. Bass construes ambiguous firearms language in light of federalism; Gregory likewise requires attention to statutory clarity where the usual federal balance is displaced. Those decisions operate through available readings, not a judicial power to add any condition that might avoid a constitutional problem. X-Citement Video concerned the reach of an existing knowledge term and therefore does not authorize insertion of a new commerce term here. This provision identifies possession, culpability and location, subject to its stated exemptions, but includes no nexus phrase to construe. The trial stipulation about manufacture outside Texas cannot become that missing statutory condition. Lopez also renews the lower court’s Stirone and Russell objection that proof cannot replace a necessary grand-jury allegation. That conditional question would require its own decision if an added-element construction were adopted. Because the Court rejects that construction and affirms on the commerce-power ground, it leaves the indictment alternative and the sentencing objections unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336: preserves federalism-sensitive construction of ambiguous firearms language, without authorizing addition of an element absent from this unambiguous provision.
- Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452: preserves its clarity principle within permissible statutory interpretation; it supplies no power to rewrite this offense.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: distinguishes interpretation of an enacted knowledge term from adding a new jurisdictional condition; its separate rules for defending a favorable judgment remain intact.
- Stirone v. United States, 361 U.S. 212: supplies the grand-jury allegation boundary invoked against substituting trial proof for a necessary indictment allegation; the renewed objection remains conditional and is not decided after affirmance on another sufficient ground.
- Russell v. United States, 369 U.S. 749: supplies the grand-jury allegation boundary invoked against curing omission of a necessary offense allegation through later proof; that conditional indictment objection is left unresolved because the commerce ground is sufficient.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Wickard and Perez remain available at their economic-class scope; neither is overruled. Allied-Bruce retains its actual-commerce construction and its express constitutional reservation. New York and Garcia retain their distinct limits concerning state compulsion and generally applicable regulation. Bass and Gregory permit construction, while X-Citement Video interprets an existing statutory term; none authorizes insertion of the missing commerce element. Stirone and Russell are not newly applied to invalidate this indictment because the conditional nexus question is not reached.

Effective April 26, 1995, the original school-zone possession offense cannot be supported by the offered generalized crime-cost, education and productivity chain. The controlling ground is the five-Justice bounded commerce-connection holding, not a categorical prohibition on regulating noneconomic intrastate conduct and not the Chief Justice’s entire six-route framework. No general formal-findings requirement is adopted. Broad national commercial regulation remains intact. The decision announces no Second Amendment rule, decides no firearm’s protected status, and changes no standard under the Elections Clause or Reconstruction Amendments. No amended possession statute or separate discharge prosecution is adjudicated.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court’s limited holdings and concurs. Federal commerce authority is broad but enumerated. His complete account permits regulation of: actual interstate transactions; channels of commerce; instrumentalities, persons and things in interstate commerce; economic classes substantially affecting an actual interstate market; intrastate conduct whose inclusion is necessary to prevent evasion or material impairment of a valid interstate regime; and materially established interstate externalities that individual States cannot internalize. Each route requires its relevant connection to be established. A jurisdictional phrase must perform genuine constitutional work in the individual case. No other Justice joins this complete formulation.

He rejects the chain from guns near schools through violence or fear, impaired education, reduced productivity and the national economy because it supplies no judicially enforceable stopping point. National importance and generalized downstream social costs do not establish the missing enumerated-power connection. The defect is not the word noneconomic: a valid interstate regime or genuinely demonstrated interstate spillovers can support regulation of intrastate conduct under his account. Wickard and Perez remain at their actual economic-class scope. He would not insert a missing element, adjudicate an amended law, or foreclose a materially different enactment supported by its own constitutional authority. Commerce authority and individual rights are distinct inquiries; ordinary state police authority does not settle compliance with an independently applicable right. His concurrence establishes no Second Amendment classification and preserves the Elections Clause and Reconstruction Amendments’ own texts and standards. The additional framework is noncontrolling.

Justice Breyer, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, dissents. Congress could rationally find that the class of school gun possession substantially affects national commerce through disrupted education, workforce preparation and travel. Wickard and Perez require attention to the class rather than treating one defendant’s effect as independently substantial. The dissent would retain judicial review, a substantial class relationship and reasonable means, while deferring to the legislative judgment presented here. It rejects a universal findings prerequisite and does not equate every imaginable consequence with a substantial commercial effect. In its view, the Court discounts the practical national connection by treating the local and noncommercial features of possession as insufficiently connected to the class-wide effects. Safe schools’ desirability alone and the gun’s remote interstate manufacture are not its grounds. The dissent reads the original offense without a new nexus element; on that reading the indictment omits no required commerce allegation. It would reverse the constitutional dismissal and remand for the unresolved supervised-release and special-assessment objections, not approve the entire sentence. Its application is noncontrolling.

### United States v. Harris, No. 94-297

Merits decision, April 27, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit's June 29, 1994 judgment in United States v. Harris, 25 F.3d 1275, No. 93-7554. The questions concern Congress's commerce authority to enact the original armed-carjacking offense, its criminal-fault and fair-notice requirements, and cumulative convictions and consecutive punishment under §§2119 and 924(c). The defendants seek relief from their convictions and sentences; the Government seeks affirmance.

The offense occurred December 12, 1992. The borrowed 1976 Buick Electra was manufactured in Flint, Michigan and titled in Arkansas and Mississippi. The trial evidence recounted by the Fifth Circuit placed Harris with a sawed-off shotgun and Hoskins with an ax handle at the confrontation: the occupants were ordered out, Hoskins drove away, and Harris pursued the occupants. The defendants' retaliation/afterthought account and Harris's claimed ignorance were defenses, not accepted contrary findings. Each defendant was convicted of aiding and abetting carjacking and of using or carrying a firearm during a crime of violence, receiving forty-one months on Count One and sixty months on Count Two, consecutive.

The December 12, 1992 offense is governed by original §2119. A person possessing a firearm as defined in §921 who takes or attempts to take a motor vehicle previously transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation is subject to a fine, imprisonment up to fifteen years, or both. Resulting serious bodily injury, as defined in §1365, raises the maximum to twenty-five years; resulting death permits imprisonment for any number of years up to life, with the fine-or-both alternatives retained. Neither enhanced tier is decided. The later intent-to-cause-death-or-serious-injury language is not part of this offense. The enactment's encouragement of federal/state cooperation and appropriate prosecution consistent with prosecutorial discretion is neither an additional element nor an automatic constitutional limit.

Offense-date §924(c)(1) requires use or carrying of a firearm during and in relation to a crime of violence or drug-trafficking crime for which the person may be prosecuted in federal court. Its inclusive parenthetical reaches predicates with their own deadly-weapon enhancements. The ordinary term is five years in addition to the predicate punishment, consecutive to every other imprisonment term, expressly including the predicate term. Probation and suspended sentence are barred, and the then-effective provision denies parole during its term. A crime of violence under §924(c)(3) is a felony having as an element use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against another's person or property, or by its nature involving a substantial risk that such force may be used in committing it. No later amendment of that definition is adjudicated.

The then-effective separate firearm tiers prescribed ten years for a short-barreled rifle or shotgun; thirty years for a machinegun, destructive device, or firearm equipped with a silencer or muffler; twenty years for a second or subsequent conviction; and life without release for such a repeat conviction involving the listed machinegun, destructive-device, or silencer/muffler category. A sawed-off description in this record establishes neither statutory barrel length nor a basis for increasing the reviewed sentence. The later semiautomatic-assault-weapon tier and later deletion of the parole sentence are not applied to the 1992 conduct.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The vehicle-taking economic class supplies the commerce connection

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may apply original §2119 to the armed forcible acquisition of the vehicle proved here as regulation of the economic vehicle-taking class within the interstate motor-vehicle theft-prevention scheme. The statute's separately enacted requirement that the particular vehicle have been transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce must be proved; neither remote movement alone nor generalized crime, insurance, or productivity costs independently supplies this holding's constitutional ground.

**Authority:** Justice O’Connor's opinion of the Court, joined on this ground by Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: eight direct votes. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment on a distinct instrumentality ground and does not join the economic-class rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the regulated taking has a commercial connection missing from the stand-alone possession offense in Lopez. Congress addresses forcible acquisition of the vehicle itself, a form of theft within the interstate vehicle market. Wickard and Perez permit regulation of an economic class without requiring each instance to produce a substantial interstate effect. Lopez preserves that authority while rejecting a chain through general crime costs, education, and productivity. The defendants' asserted retaliatory motive does not change the enacted acquisition class or create a profit-intent exception. The Buick's Michigan manufacture and Arkansas and Mississippi titling satisfy the existing vehicle-history condition; the Court inserts no new element and finds no intended interstate resale. Allied-Bruce cautions against treating remote object history as constitutionally conclusive. That caution is respected because the taking class, rather than manufacture alone, supplies the selected connection. The holding neither federalizes every property crime nor decides an independent instrumentality theory. Both the carjacking predicate and the dependent firearm count survive this commerce challenge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: applies its retained economic-class boundary and distinguishes its inadequate stand-alone possession/downstream-effects chain; no missing element is supplied.
- Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942): applies aggregation within a regulated economic class rather than demanding a substantial effect from each individual taking.
- Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146 (1971): applies Congress's authority over the economic class despite an individual instance's local character.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: preserves its distinction between actual commercial operation and remote object history; prior manufacture alone is not the constitutional ground here.

##### Knowing participation and intentional assistance give the original offense definite content

**Controlling proposition:** Original §2119 requires knowing armed participation in taking or attempting to take the covered vehicle from another's person or presence by force and violence or intimidation; aiding-and-abetting liability requires intentional assistance in that taking, not mere presence, association, or awareness of another's plan. Read with that ordinary criminal fault, the statute gives fair notice of the charged conduct and defeats the presented lack-of-fault and vagueness challenges without adding a profit motive, interstate-resale intention, knowledge of federal law, or the later intent-to-kill-or-seriously-injure element.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer, directly adopts the complete construction and its bounded application. Stone-Zsela additionally joins the knowing-participation and fair-notice propositions and rejection of a general legal-knowledge requirement; no additional Stone join is attributed to the separately stated intentional-assistance formulation. His separate conclusions concerning knowledge of the vehicle's interstate history and permanent deprivation have no additional joins and are not holdings of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory silence does not make this serious forcible-taking offense a crime of unwitting involvement. Staples preserves ordinary factual culpability, and X-Citement Video requires attention to the whole statutory setting rather than automatic strict liability. The taking and force terms identify the wrongful conduct; participation in that taking must be knowing, and assistance must be intentional. Shabani's rejection of guilt by association supplies a consistent boundary, without converting aiding and abetting into conspiracy. Ratzlaf's known-illegality rule rested on distinct willfulness language and does not add a universal legal-knowledge element here. A claimed retaliatory motive does not negate participation in the taking as a matter of law. The lower record treats Harris's claimed ignorance as a defense, not an accepted finding. No supplied original charge or preservation record establishes an omitted-fault instruction. The Court rejects the presented statute and notice challenge without approving unknown instructions, finding harmless error, or ordering a new trial. Knowledge of interstate history and deprivation duration receive no majority resolution.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Staples v. United States, May 23, 1994: applies contextual factual culpability despite silence while retaining its distinct regulated-characteristic holding.
- United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., November 29, 1994: applies contextual criminal-fault construction without transplanting its depiction elements or deciding every jurisdictional-knowledge question.
- United States v. Shabani, November 1, 1994: preserves the distinction between knowing participation and mere association; its conspiracy holding does not itself define this separate offense.
- Ratzlaf v. United States, January 11, 1994: distinguishes its specific willfulness and known-illegality language rather than converting it into a universal ignorance-of-law defense.
- Lopez: preserves its refusal to insert an absent commerce element; that limit does not eliminate ordinary criminal-fault construction of the enacted taking offense.

##### Section 924(c) expressly adds a consecutive term for this armed carjacking

**Controlling proposition:** The offense-date §924(c) authorizes its additional, consecutive punishment for the original §2119 carjacking predicate even though firearm possession is integral to that predicate's definition. The Government must still establish firearm use or carrying during and in relation to a qualifying federally prosecutable predicate; this cumulative-authorization holding does not equate possession with every §924(c) condition or determine universal element overlap.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined on this statutory holding by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes. The holding sustains the reviewed forty-one-month carjacking terms and additional sixty-month firearm terms, not an unreviewed weapon enhancement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted punishment directions encompass this pairing. Section 924(c) reaches any qualifying federal crime of violence, includes predicates with their own deadly-weapon enhancement, adds punishment to the predicate, and expressly forbids concurrent service. The defendants distinguish an offense defined by firearm possession from an otherwise complete offense carrying an enhancement. But the parenthetical's inclusion is nonexclusive; it does not narrow the general command to the examples it names. Original §2119's later enactment and silence therefore supply no exception for armed carjacking. Ordinary construction resolves this question before residual lenity, which Ratzlaf preserves for genuine remaining ambiguity. Smith keeps firearm use or carrying and relation to the predicate distinct; the Court does not deem incidental possession sufficient. Deal addresses a different question about additional convictions in one prosecution and does not independently decide this statutory pair. The existing five-year firearm terms stand against the presented challenge. No different weapon classification, higher sentence, merger, or concurrent conversion is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ratzlaf: preserves residual lenity after ordinary construction; the inclusive, additional, and consecutive directions leave no residual ambiguity for this pairing.
- Smith v. United States, June 1, 1993: preserves the separate firearm-use and relation requirements; a predicate's possession element does not automatically establish all §924(c) elements.
- Deal v. United States, May 17, 1993: distinguishes its conviction-sequence question, which does not itself decide cumulative punishment under these two statutes.

##### Hunter permits the clearly authorized punishment in this single prosecution

**Controlling proposition:** Applying Missouri v. Hunter, the clearly authorized §2119 and §924(c) convictions and consecutive punishments imposed in this single prosecution do not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause even assuming overlap under Blockburger. This holding applies the governing same-trial precedent to the clear statutory direction and neither decides universal element overlap nor changes the rules for successive prosecutions.

**Authority:** O’Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this bounded application. Stevens joins the application of controlling Hunter while preserving his earlier objection to legislative multiplication of convictions; his reservation does not subtract a vote from this limited holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the clear cumulative direction resolves the double-jeopardy objection under Hunter. Blockburger supplies a method for discerning whether two offenses carry distinct legislative punishment, and Whalen applies that construction principle when contrary authorization is absent. Hunter establishes the governing consequence when the legislature instead clearly authorizes both punishments at one trial. Section 924(c)'s additional and consecutive commands provide that authorization here. The Court therefore need not decide whether every possible original carjacking violation satisfies every condition of the firearm offense. The defendants' overlap argument cannot displace the clear direction in this same-trial setting. Dixon preserves Grady's distinct successive-prosecution rule; neither a common episode nor the present judgment silently changes it. This decision makes no independent claim that multiple convictions lack stigma or collateral consequences and does not endorse every possible multiplication of charges. It applies the existing precedent to this pairing. Both convictions and their supplied consecutive terms remain in force without resentencing.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Missouri v. Hunter, 459 U.S. 359 (1983): applies its governing same-trial rule to the clear cumulative authorization without reconsidering its constitutional foundation.
- Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932): applies its construction role rather than treating possible overlap as an absolute bar to this expressly authorized same-trial punishment.
- Whalen v. United States, 445 U.S. 684 (1980): preserves the presumption against cumulative punishment absent contrary legislative intent and distinguishes the clear direction here.
- United States v. Dixon, June 28, 1993: preserves its successive-prosecution holdings, including the continued operation of Grady.
- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S. 508 (1990): remains governing law for its essential-conduct successive-prosecution setting; this single prosecution does not invoke or displace that rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment. On commerce, he does not join the Court's economic-class rationale. He would sustain this prosecution because Congress may protect an interstate transportation instrumentality from a direct intrastate attack when the offense requires proof of that particular instrumentality's interstate connection. An automobile can carry persons and property through interstate channels; these defendants forcibly seized possession and control of the Buick itself. Its Michigan manufacture and Arkansas and Mississippi titling satisfied the enacted connection. The direct taking and vehicle-specific element together perform constitutional work, rather than attaching remote history to an unrelated local offense. He rejects the Fifth Circuit's general chain through travel, insurance, violent-crime costs, and the national economy. His ground neither reclassifies violent crime as economic activity nor makes every offense in or near a vehicle federal. Past movement alone is not a universal source of authority. This instrumentality application has no additional joins.

On fault and notice, the Chief Justice joins the knowing-participation and fair-notice propositions, requiring proof of the firearm and interstate-vehicle facts as enacted. He additionally concludes that this original statute requires neither knowledge that the vehicle traveled interstate nor a separate intent permanently to deprive. Ratzlaf's distinct willfulness language supplies no such requirements, and Staples's concern with apparently innocent possession turning on an unknown physical characteristic differs from pointing a shotgun at occupants and ordering them out. He finds the jury evidence sufficient to establish knowing participation beyond a reasonable doubt. That explanation does not convert the Court's bounded statute-level ruling into a separate majority adjudication of evidentiary sufficiency or of an unknown instruction. He does not eliminate fault from a fact Congress makes part of the wrongful conduct. His additional nexus-knowledge and deprivation-duration conclusions are noncontrolling. He joins the statutory authorization and bounded Hunter application sustaining the consecutive punishment and reserves a different pair lacking clear authorization.

Justice Stevens concurs. He joins the Court's statutory construction and its limited application of Hunter as governing precedent. He does not withdraw his Hunter dissenting position: multiple convictions can create stigma, risks, and collateral consequences distinct from the total authorized prison term, and his earlier view would give the same-offense protection content independent of legislative authorization. The present opinion neither denies those consequences nor announces unlimited legislative power to multiply convictions. Its application of existing precedent to this clear cumulative command permits his join without deciding again the constitutional foundation on which he previously disagreed. He also joins the Court's economic vehicle-taking ground without withdrawing his broader Lopez dissenting position. His preserved constitutional objection remains noncontrolling; he supports affirmance of both convictions and the consecutive terms here.

### United States v. Robertson, No. 94-251

Merits decision, May 1, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 27, 1995; decided May 1, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing 15 F.3d 862 (February 1, 1994). The question is whether the Alaska gold mine was an enterprise engaged in interstate commerce within 18 U.S.C. §1962(a).

The mine obtained some equipment and supplies in California and transported them to Alaska for mining use. It repeatedly recruited workers from outside Alaska, and Robertson and mine employees traveled between Alaska and the lower 48 States during its operation. Robertson, its sole proprietor, carried approximately $30,000 of its gold nuggets out of Alaska. Most gold was sold to Alaska refiners. No out-of-state sale or precise export percentage is found; most equipment was acquired locally.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit insofar as it reversed the Count Six RICO conviction for insufficient enterprise-commerce evidence, 9–0, and remands for the remaining appellate proceedings.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count Six enterprise-commerce reversal | Reverse and remand, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposing Justice | Remove the insufficient-commerce ground; the RICO conviction may be reinstated subject to remaining preserved appellate claims. |

The Court does not unconditionally reinstate the RICO conviction or sentence. Six undecided RICO claims remain: refusal of a special verdict; venue; severance of the RICO and continuing-criminal-enterprise counts; exclusion of Agent Devetko’s testimony; denial of Criminal Justice Act funds to locate the claimed owner of the Cadillac used for predicate act six; and constitutional vagueness. The government’s unresolved argument that RICO conduct continued after November 1, 1987 and therefore attracted the Sentencing Guidelines remains for appellate consideration. No Guidelines applicability ruling is made.

The convictions on Counts One–Four remain affirmed; their independent sentence vacatur and actual-resentencing mandate under then-Rule 32(c)(3)(D) remain undisturbed. That mandate requires a finding of accuracy or express nonreliance for each disputed presentence-report fact and an appended written record; a retrospective assertion of nonreliance alone does not replace actual resentencing. The acquittals on Counts Five and Eight and dismissal of Count Seven forfeiture remain unaffected. Those dispositions are outside this Court’s commerce ruling, not additional merits holdings.

|---|---|---|---|---|

All nine directly join the same controlling proposition. No separate writing issues; no fractured-decision analysis is necessary.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The mine’s own interstate operations satisfy the enacted enterprise-commerce condition

**Controlling proposition:** An enterprise is engaged in interstate commerce under §1962(a) when it directly participates in interstate acquisition, production or distribution of goods or services. This mine’s actual cross-border procurement, repeated interstate recruitment and mine-work movement, together with its proprietor’s carriage out of Alaska of approximately $30,000 of its own output, establish that connection without a separate showing that this individual mine substantially affected interstate commerce.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: nine direct joins in the rule, cumulative application and bounded remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this mine participated in interstate commerce itself. American Building Maintenance distinguishes direct acquisition, production or distribution across state lines from local purchases of goods whose earlier interstate journey has ended; Gulf Oil likewise keeps direct engagement distinct from local activity with interstate effects. The mine obtained some equipment in California for use in Alaska, repeatedly brought workers across state lines for mining, and its sole proprietor carried a meaningful amount of its gold outside Alaska. Calling the enterprise local mining and local sales does not erase these transactions. The output’s mining origin and its carriage by the proprietor, considered with the continuing interstate operations, answer the attribution objection here without an out-of-state sale or a rule attributing every private owner act to the enterprise. Lopez rejects the unbounded chain from local wrongdoing to national economic consequences; this prosecution needs no such chain because the enacted connection is proved. The Court does not decide the separate affecting-commerce alternative, whether it requires substantial effects, or the sufficiency of retained-output carriage standing alone.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. American Building Maintenance Industries, 422 U.S. 271, 283–286 (1975): applies its distinction between direct interstate goods or services transactions and local purchases after interstate movement has ended; its Clayton Act setting does not erase RICO’s separate affecting-commerce alternative.
- Gulf Oil Corp. v. Copp Paving Co., 419 U.S. 186, 195 (1974): applies its direct-engagement distinction; local economic effects and direct participation remain different inquiries.
- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: distinguishes the stand-alone school-possession offense and generalized social-cost chain from this proved interstate enterprise connection; its bounded holding remains intact.
- Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, January 18, 1995: preserves attention to actual multistate commercial operation and its reserved constitutional ceiling; the FAA’s broader involving-commerce wording is not substituted for §1962(a).
- United States v. Harris, April 27, 1995: preserves the economic-class vehicle-taking holding and refusal to make remote vehicle history alone conclusive; this decision instead concerns the enterprise’s own present interstate operations.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The cumulative ground makes an independently sufficient procurement-only holding unnecessary. The Court does not decide whether a purely intrastate enterprise with only remote economic consequences satisfies §1962(a), establish a universal owner-activity attribution rule, or make a jurisdictional phrase substitute for proof. Predicate drug commerce alone is not enterprise commerce. Neither an out-of-state nugget sale, a precise output percentage, productive use of the wrecked Cadillac, mine use of the airplane, nor traced downstream refinery shipment is necessary or found. The Court decides no other RICO appellate claim, Guidelines issue or claim arising under a different RICO subsection.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

American Building Maintenance and Gulf Oil supply the direct-engagement distinction; their narrower statutory settings remain identified. Actual interstate operation controls this application. Allied-Bruce, Lopez and Harris retain their stated scopes. The commerce condition remains independent under National Organization for Women v. Scheidler; Reves’s direction requirement for §1962(c) is not imported into §1962(a). Holmes’s direct-injury requirement for civil RICO damages does not determine this criminal enterprise-commerce question. No precedent is overruled.

Section 1962(a)’s direct-engagement alternative is satisfied by the proved cumulative interstate operation of this mine. A separate substantial-effects showing for this individual enterprise is unnecessary under that alternative. The separate affecting-commerce route and its constitutional reach remain undecided. The decision adds no independently sufficient procurement-only rule and no rule making every proprietor’s private conduct enterprise activity. Lopez’s negative school-possession holding and Harris’s distinct economic-class holding remain unchanged; the Chief Justice’s complete six-route account in Lopez does not become a Court test through this decision.

Section 1962(a)’s separate income, participation and investment conditions remain intact. Its securities qualification continues to exclude an open-market investment purchase made without intent to control or participate in control of the issuer or assist another in doing so, when the purchaser’s, immediate family’s and racketeering or unlawful-debt accomplices’ aggregate holdings after purchase are less than one percent of any outstanding class and confer no power, in law or fact, to elect a director. That qualification is not implicated by ownership and operation of this mine.

### United States v. Pinson, No. 94-164

Merits decision, May 8, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 20, 1995; decided May 8, 1995. Merits review of the Eighth Circuit’s May 23, 1994 judgment, 24 F.3d 1056, affirming a federal conviction and sentence for manufacturing more than 100 marijuana plants. The question is whether the outside thermal examination directed at heat patterns associated with activity inside Pinson’s home was a Fourth Amendment search requiring a warrant.

Police learned of earlier shipments from hydroponic-equipment suppliers and unusually high electricity use. At approximately 1:20 a.m. on July 25, 1991, a helicopter-mounted FLIR examined heat at a covered third-floor window, roof, and skylight without a warrant. The testimony described relative surface heat, not absolute temperatures or sight through walls. A separate July 30 warrant search found the growing operation and seized marijuana, cash, equipment, and cultivation publications. The search claim was preserved; the circuit expressly reported that affidavit sufficiency was not challenged on appeal and made no determination of that issue.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court holds that the targeted thermal examination was a search and vacates the challenged appellate judgment to the specified extent. Justice Scalia announces the opinion of the Court. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment; Justice Kennedy dissents, joined by Justice Breyer. No additional nonroutine order is issued.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preserved Fourth Amendment search claim | Reject the no-search ruling, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Kennedy, Breyer | Part I: targeted acquisition of otherwise unobservable concealed domestic information is a search on this record. The dissent would reject the claim because the demonstrated information is comparative exterior warmth. |
| Challenged suppression ruling and conviction affirmance | Vacate to the stated extent and remand, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Kennedy, Breyer | Part III: vacatur reaches only the rejection of the preserved suppression claim and conviction affirmance resting on the categorical no-search ground. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. The mandate orders no excision hearing, automatic suppression of seized evidence, acquittal, release, new trial, or replacement sentence. Each controlling portion has its own direct majority. No combination of opposed search rationales under Marks is necessary or adopted. The two dissenters' conditional acceptance of preservation limits does not change the seven-to-two disposition.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The targeted examination of this closed home's heat patterns is a search

**Controlling proposition:** A targeted technological examination of a closed home constitutes a Fourth Amendment search when, as here, its reported heat-pattern information reveals otherwise unobservable information concerning concealed domestic operation. External reception and the absence of an image identifying a person or object do not defeat protection; investigative purpose or use of equipment alone is insufficient, and the Court adopts no general rule governing every sensor or exterior emission.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. This directly controlling application concerns the covered dwelling and demonstrated differential-heat output. Stone-Zsela also states a broader formulation in his separate writing; the other six do not join that formulation merely by joining this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Fourth Amendment protects the privacy of a home against an examination that obtains otherwise concealed domestic information. Katz makes physical penetration unnecessary; Karo protects information about a home's interior that ordinary observation from outside cannot disclose. Police used the instrument to examine heat patterns at a covered third-floor window, roof, and skylight. Although it received surface radiation and did not identify an interior person or object, the resulting pattern conveyed information concerning concealed domestic operation. Treating its physical medium as dispositive would deny the protection Karo recognizes. Hicks likewise rejects an exception for supposedly unimportant concealed information. Riley's naked-eye observation through actual openings does not establish ordinary exposure here. Greenwood's purposeful disposal of garbage does not surrender all information conveyed by escaping heat, and Place's contraband-only sniff differs from information also produced by lawful activity. The Court decides this examination, without assuming greater device capabilities, public availability, particular flight conditions, or a general threshold for other sensing methods.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; privacy protection does not require physical penetration of the home.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied to the reported concealed domestic information; its protection does not depend on the information arriving as an ordinary visual image.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321: applied by analogy to the additional examination of concealed information and its rejection of an unimportant-information exemption; no physical manipulation is found here.
- Florida v. Riley, 488 U.S. 445: distinguished as naked-eye observation through actual openings; its opinions supply none of this record's missing altitude or public-flight facts.
- California v. Greenwood, 486 U.S. 35: distinguished; purposeful placement of garbage for collection does not establish surrender of domestic information conveyed by heat.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696: distinguished; the narrow contraband-information character of the sniff is absent from this heat examination.
- Dow Chemical Co. v. United States, 476 U.S. 227: distinguished; the outdoor industrial photographs do not authorize this examination of a closed dwelling.
- Minnesota v. Dickerson, decided June 7, 1993: consistent with the distinction between information obtained through authorized observation and additional unauthorized probing; no tactile search is presented.

##### II. No warrant exception justifies this scan on the established record

**Controlling proposition:** This search of protected home information required a warrant unless a recognized exception applied. The July 25 scan had no warrant, and no consent, exigency, or other recognized exception is established; the subsequent warrant for physical entry does not retroactively authorize the scan.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: six Justices. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. Kennedy and Breyer do not reach justification because they find no search.

**Controlling explanation:** Once the examination is recognized as a search of protected domestic information, the ordinary warrant requirement applies. Karo preserves recognized exceptions, including genuinely exigent circumstances; it does not authorize warrantless home surveillance merely because the receiving instrument remains outside. Nothing in this record establishes consent, exigency, or another exception for the July 25 scan. The Government's exterior-measurement theory addresses whether a search occurred and supplies no separate justification after that question is resolved. Nor does the limited detail of the output establish an exception for searches considered less revealing. The July 30 warrant authorized a later physical entry; its issuance cannot supply advance judicial authority for an earlier completed search. This conclusion concerns the scan's justification. It neither determines the validity of that later warrant nor requires exclusion of the evidence found during its execution. Those distinct questions remain subject to the record and procedural limits governing the remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347: applied; protected surveillance is subject to the warrant requirement and its established exceptions.
- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part III: applied; the warrant presumption for concealed home information retains recognized exceptions, including true exigency, none established here.

##### III. Correct the preserved search ruling without reopening unpresented warrant questions

**Controlling proposition:** The Eighth Circuit's judgment is vacated insofar as its rejection of the preserved suppression claim and affirmance of the conviction rest on the categorical no-search ground, and the case is remanded for lawful disposition of that claim under the corrected search rule. Pinson's failure to challenge affidavit sufficiency on appeal remains operative: the mandate directs no new excision-and-probable-cause hearing, revives no waived or forfeited argument automatically, and permits further warrant, suppression, or reliance questions only to the extent properly available under the existing record and ordinary preservation and remedial law.

**Authority:** Part III of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg: seven Justices. The mandate controls directly. The remedial authorities below delimit unresolved possibilities; none supplies an independent finding admitting or excluding the evidence. Kennedy and Breyer prefer affirmance on their no-search ground; their acceptance of these procedural limits if the search holding governs does not add votes for vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** The circuit rejected the preserved suppression claim because it considered the scan no search. That premise cannot sustain the challenged conviction affirmance. But its footnote expressly records that Pinson did not challenge affidavit sufficiency on appeal, and the circuit did not decide that question. Correcting the search rule does not erase this limit. Karo's separate treatment of an untainted warrant establishes a possible legal distinction, not a finding about Pinson's affidavit. Olano distinguishes intentional waiver from forfeiture and imposes additional conditions before forfeited error may be corrected. A preserved search question does not establish those conditions for another argument. Leon requires supported objective reliance, subject to its exclusions, rather than automatic validation by a later magistrate. Evans concerns qualifying court-employee clerical error and reasonable reliance; deliberate police scanning is not that error. The lower court must determine what remaining issues are lawfully available. This Court orders neither suppression nor a particular ultimate conviction consequence and does not reopen the separate sentencing question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Karo, 468 U.S. 705, Part IV: retained as the distinct possibility of sufficient untainted warrant information; no determination of Pinson's affidavit or entitlement to a new hearing follows.
- United States v. Olano, decided April 26, 1993: retained; intentional waiver differs from forfeiture. If Rule 52(b) is actually invoked, error, plainness, ordinarily outcome-affecting prejudice, and the separate discretionary inquiry concerning fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings must be satisfied as applicable. This mandate does not establish any predicate or revive an argument automatically.
- United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897: left available only if procedurally open and factually supported. Objective reliance affords no protection for knowing or reckless deception of the magistrate, abandonment of judicial neutrality, an affidavit so lacking probable-cause indicia that reliance is wholly unreasonable, or facial warrant deficiency precluding reasonable reliance. The inquiry includes information-obtaining and supplying officers and assumes execution within the warrant's scope; no final reliance finding is made.
- Arizona v. Evans, decided March 1, 1995: confined to qualifying non-law-enforcement court-employee clerical error plus objectively reasonable reliance. Reserved police or mixed institutional errors supply no technology exception for this deliberate scan, and no final exclusion ruling follows here.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Katz remains the basis for privacy protection without physical penetration.

Karo Part III governs otherwise unavailable home information and retains recognized warrant exceptions; Part IV remains a separate untainted-warrant inquiry whose factual and procedural predicates are not decided here.

Hicks supplies the additional-examination analogy and rejects an exemption for supposedly unimportant information.

Riley retains its scope for naked-eye observation through actual openings and supplies no missing flight fact.

Dow Chemical retains its outdoor industrial setting and does not authorize this closed-home examination.

Place retains its contraband-information limitation; this sensor's information also concerns lawful activity.

Greenwood retains its purposeful-disposal rationale; escaping heat does not establish comparable abandonment of domestic information.

Dickerson retains the boundary between authorized detection and additional unauthorized probing.

Olano retains distinct waiver, forfeiture, plain-error, prejudice, and discretionary-correction requirements, as stated in Part III.

Leon retains objective reliance and all four exclusions stated in Part III, including the inquiry into information-supplying officers and proper execution.

Evans retains its qualifying court-employee clerical-error and reasonable-reliance rule, with police and mixed institutional questions reserved.

No precedent is overruled. The circuit's categorical no-search rationale cannot sustain the affected disposition.

Lower courts must treat this targeted closed-home thermal examination as a search on its demonstrated informational content. Seven Justices support that bounded rule; six additionally hold that no warrant exception is established for this scan. The rule does not depend on an invented capacity to see through walls or a finding about public availability, altitude, or flight frequency. It creates no general rule for every sensor, every emission, or every technological measurement. The absence of a warrant exception does not itself decide exclusion or the validity of a later warrant. The seven-Justice mandate preserves the appellate nonchallenge to affidavit sufficiency and leaves only lawfully available proceedings open. The Chief's broader formulation and the dissenters' limited no-search theory are separate positions, not additional controlling holdings.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The Chief joins Parts I and III: this examination is a search, and the challenged judgment must be vacated with the stated preservation-sensitive remand. He would state the home-information rule more generally: law-enforcement use of sense-enhancing technology from outside a home to obtain information about activity within that ordinary public observation could not otherwise obtain constitutes a Fourth Amendment search. His reasoning does not turn on whether the instrument receives reflected light, sound, heat, or another physical signal. The protection of the interior cannot end at the physical surface of its walls. Treating every measurable escaping emission as abandoned would permit increasingly capable instruments to obtain domestic information while preserving only formal protection against entry. The officers deliberately obtained heat-pattern information connected with concealed activity that their unaided senses could not discern; the absence of a conventional image does not change that conclusion.

The Chief does not classify ordinary visual observation from a lawful vantage, smoke or other conditions perceptible to unaided members of the public, utility records obtained through lawful process, or every technological measurement near private property as a search. His general rule retains its trigger: deliberate technological acquisition of otherwise unavailable information about activity inside a home. He expresses no view on excised-affidavit sufficiency, procedural availability of that issue, ultimate suppression, or Leon's application. His remand does not direct a new probable-cause determination. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and expresses no view on its additional proposition. No other Justice joins his broader formulation.

**Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Breyer.** These Justices would affirm rejection of the search claim and the challenged conviction affirmance because the demonstrated output was comparative exterior warmth. Karo disclosed the presence of a particular object inside a home; this account does not show identification of a person, object, or exact interior activity. The causal inference officers drew from surface conditions does not, in their view, establish acquisition of the concealed fact Karo protected. They recognize that Katz and Karo make neither physical penetration nor the receiver's location dispositive. Their disagreement concerns the content obtained on this record, not immunity for all outside observation. Riley's exposed conditions and Dow's limited external depiction illustrate that distinction without establishing missing public-flight facts or converting a home into industrial premises.

They adopt no general heat-abandonment rule, contraband-only analogy, public-availability test, or technology good-faith defense. More revealing surveillance remains open. They need no alternative holding approving affidavit sufficiency or objective reliance. If the Court's search rule governs, they accept that any further proceedings must respect the same preservation limits; that conditional procedural agreement does not change their votes to affirm. They do not independently revisit the lesser-harms sentence.

### Hubbard v. United States, No. 94-172

Merits decision, May 15, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 21, 1995; decided May 15, 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to review the Sixth Circuit’s February 15, 1994 affirmance, 16 F.3d 694, of Counts V–VII for false statements under 18 U.S.C. §1001. The question is whether the federal court receiving these bankruptcy filings is a department or agency within the statute. The Court does not independently review Counts I–IV for bankruptcy fraud, Counts VIII–X for mail fraud, their evidentiary sufficiency, or the rejected ineffective-assistance claim.

Hubbard filed a Chapter 7 petition on September 25, 1985. Count V concerns the response asserting prior delivery of business records to a former trustee and no later request; Counts VI and VII concern denials about the locations of a well-drilling machine and its parts in an answer to the successor trustee's complaint. The filings were unsworn. The jury found factual falsehoods, and the circuit found the statements capable of affecting identification and collection of estate assets. The circuit rejected the Rule 12 waiver objection as a failure-to-charge argument under then-Rule 12(b)(2), and separately declined a judicial-function exception. It expressly did not decide the Government's alternative contention that the filings concerned administrative rather than adjudicative asset collection.

The offense-date §1001 provided:

> Whoever, in any matter within the jurisdiction of any department or agency of the United States knowingly and willfully falsifies, conceals or covers up by any trick, scheme, or device a material fact, or makes any false, fictitious or fraudulent statements or representations, or makes or uses any false writing or document knowing the same to contain any false, fictitious or fraudulent statement or entry, shall be fined not more than $10,000 or imprisoned not more than five years, or both.

Section 6 supplies **two distinct definitions and qualifications**:

> As used in this title:
>
> The term “department” means one of the executive departments enumerated in section 1 of Title 5, unless the context shows that such term was intended to describe the executive, legislative, or judicial branches of the government.
>
> The term “agency” includes any department, independent establishment, commission, administration, authority, board or bureau of the United States or any corporation in which the United States has a proprietary interest, unless the context shows that such term was intended to be used in a more limited sense.

The reference to former Title 5 §1 was reenacted as §101; the official Code's reference note confirms that change. The department definition's contextual expansion and the agency definition's narrowing proviso are distinct. A ruling on department coverage does not itself resolve every agency theory.

The official 1994 Code records a September 13, 1994 amendment replacing §1001's old $10,000 fine clause with “fined under this title.” The underlying conduct predates that change; no new penalty applies by inference. No later judicial-party exception applies to this decision. Neither offense-date §1001 nor §6 contains an express judicial-function exception.

Section 151's institutional designation, effective July 10, 1984, provides:

> In each judicial district, the bankruptcy judges in regular active service shall constitute a unit of the district court to be known as the bankruptcy court for that district. Each bankruptcy judge, as a judicial officer of the district court, may exercise the authority conferred under this chapter with respect to any action, suit, or proceeding and may preside alone and hold a regular or special session of the court, except as otherwise provided by law or by rule or order of the district court.

The provision's unit and judicial-officer designations do not independently settle Title 18 coverage or the trustee's status. Authority is limited to that conferred under chapter 6 and remains subject to contrary law or district-court rule or order; permission to exercise it or preside alone is not an obligation or unrestricted grant of district-court power.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The court-entity question is properly before the Court

**Controlling proposition:** The Court reaches whether the federal court receiving the filings supporting Counts V–VII is a department or agency within offense-date §§6 and 1001. The governing-law inquiry concerns the same challenged convictions and the question presented; it does not authorize review of a separate judgment, enlarge review merely because merits briefs discuss an issue, or require decision of every unargued antecedent question.

**Authority:** Part I of Thomas's opinion. Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join the complete analysis: eight Justices including the author. Stone-Zsela joins the conclusion that no jurisdictional, preservation, or vehicle bar prevents reaching the question presented; he does not join the additional analysis. The complete procedural explanation therefore has eight votes and the reach conclusion has nine.

**Controlling explanation:** The question before the Court asks whether a federal court is an included entity under §1001. Answering it determines the governing law for the same three convictions challenged here. The Government's account of the petition's narrower focus does not transform this inquiry into review of another judgment. Independent Insurance permits consideration of necessary antecedent governing law with a fair opportunity for adversarial presentation; it does not impose a duty to decide every omitted issue. Yee preserves the difference between a supporting argument and an independent question. Izumi refused to reach a distinct intervention denial, while Caspari permitted its confined subsidiary defense and Madsen required its own supported basis for exceptional review. None makes later briefing sufficient by itself. The entity inquiry is also the premise of the proposed judicial-function exception. The circuit's separate Rule 12 ruling resolved the failure-to-charge objection below; it did not decide this Court's question-presentation issue. The Court reaches coverage without reopening other convictions or unrelated claims.

**Precedent treatment:**

##### II. A federal court is not an included department or agency under this statute

**Controlling proposition:** Under the offense-date §§6 and 1001, a federal court is neither an enumerated executive department nor an agency within the statute's contextual meaning; the department definition's branch-wide contextual alternative does not extend this statute to the court, and the distinct agency definition does not do so either. Counts V–VII therefore lack the asserted court nexus, without deciding whether another Judicial Branch entity could qualify as an agency or whether an independently established nexus to a different included institution would support a different prosecution.

**Authority:** Part II of Thomas's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg, and Breyer: six direct votes. Stone-Zsela reaches the same count disposition under his narrower Article III/adjudicative rule but does not join this broader court-entity construction. O’Connor and Souter would retain Bramblett's contrary construction. No combination of these different grounds is needed for the six-Justice holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress supplied separate definitions of department and agency, and both must be read with their own qualifications. Rowland gives an enacted default genuine force while permitting the context to do its stated work. Here the broad references to any matter and jurisdiction describe the functions of an included body; they do not independently identify which bodies are included. Rodgers construed authorized agency functions, not away the separate entity requirement. A general antifraud purpose and the inconclusive predecessor history do not establish the branch-wide department meaning here. Nor does the word authority in the agency list turn every body exercising governmental power into an agency in this setting. Section 151 identifies the bankruptcy court as a unit of the district court, without independently answering either Title 18 definition. The knowing factual falsehoods found below cannot supply the missing entity nexus. This construction resolves these court filings without creating a judicial-function exception, classifying the trustee, or deciding the status of every Judicial Branch institution.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rowland v. California Men's Colony, decided January 12, 1993: applied for an enacted definition operating subject to a genuine contextual qualification; its association-eligibility holding supplies no categorical result under a different statute.
- United States v. Rodgers, 466 U.S. 475: preserved; jurisdiction includes the authorized functions of an included agency. Footnote 4 reserved the judicial-function question and did not decide these filings or eliminate the entity requirement.
- United States v. Bramblett, 348 U.S. 503: its incompatible all-branch construction is expressly overruled in Part III, not avoided as merely an unnecessary judicial passage.
- Brown v. Gardner, decided December 12, 1994: consistent with enforcing enacted conditions without adding a category the statute does not establish.
- Heintz v. Jenkins, decided April 18, 1995: distinguished in statutory setting; rejection of an added FDCPA litigation exemption does not resolve §1001's entity definition or supply an exception here.

##### III. Bramblett's incompatible all-branch construction is overruled

**Controlling proposition:** Bramblett's construction of department in §1001 to include the executive, legislative, and judicial branches is overruled, including the rationale supporting its legislative-branch result. The decision does not adjudicate the independent House Disbursing Office agency or Treasury-nexus theories Bramblett left open, classify every other judicial entity, or alter §287's separate false-claims coverage.

**Authority:** Part III of Thomas's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg, and Breyer: six votes for this express precedent treatment. Stone-Zsela supports overruling only to the extent stated in his narrower concurrence and does not join the broader disposition of Bramblett. The six associates do not share a controlling extended stare decisis framework: Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer adopt one account; Scalia and Kennedy another; Thomas joins neither complete account. No Marks synthesis supplies a further rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Bramblett's treatment of department cannot remain the governing interpretation alongside the construction adopted today. It considered §6 and the predecessor statutes; the defect is its expansion of the definition, not failure to notice those materials. Its actual dispute involved representations to the House Disbursing Office, but its reason for coverage treated all three branches alike. Calling the reference to courts unnecessary would leave that incompatible interpretive reason in force. The Court therefore rejects the all-branch construction expressly, including the rationale supporting the legislative result. Bramblett declined to decide whether the office separately qualified as an authority or whether Treasury jurisdiction supplied another nexus. Those questions do not become resolved by today's rejection of its chosen ground. Rodgers's meaning of jurisdiction for an included agency remains intact. Six Justices agree on this construction and express precedent treatment, although their additional explanations for departing from statutory precedent differ. The decision establishes this overruling, not a general standard assembled from those separate explanations.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Bramblett, 348 U.S. 503: expressly overruled as to its incompatible all-branch department construction, including the rationale supporting its legislative result; no new ruling resolves the independent agency or Treasury theories it declined to decide.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stevens, concurring, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer.** These Justices join the Court's construction, full Bramblett treatment, and count-specific remedy. They supply an additional case-specific explanation for correcting the precedent. The judicial-function decisions have become an entrenched legal development attempting to protect adversarial proceedings from Bramblett's broad language. Those lower decisions do not authorize vertical disobedience or themselves overrule this Court. Their sustained development nevertheless deserves respect in assessing the consequences of the original construction. Correcting the entity boundary preserves their essential protection while dispensing with a function line the enacted language does not contain.

The statute's definitions and actual context supply the first-instance construction, but disagreement with Bramblett is not the entire precedent inquiry. Hilton and Allied-Bruce show why statutory precedent has institutional force and why settled arrangements matter. Here the combination of the particular definitional defect, the entrenched limiting development, and the qualified reliance record supports correction. The parties agree that prosecution guidance favored other charges in the described court-statement setting, but differ about its scope. That evidence weakens an assertion of indispensable, uniform reliance on litigation prosecutions; it proves neither total nonenforcement nor Hubbard's personal reliance. Congress's silence is not ratification. Crandon supports attention to criminal statutory setting and fair warning, without supplying a rule that every criminal precedent may be discarded.

The ordinary knowledge, falsity, and disclosure-duty requirements protect lawful advocacy but do not remove every cost of threatened prosecution or disputed classification. These Justices recognize that concern without finding an empirically established chilling effect or a right to lie. They adopt no independent lenity holding: genuine residual uncertainty after ordinary construction and precedent treatment is not an operative premise of this decision. Their complete explanation has three votes and is not the Court's general stare decisis standard.

**Scalia, concurring, joined by Kennedy.** These Justices also join the Court's construction, express overruling, and precise remedy. They reject treating the lower courts' limiting decisions as a competing body of authority entitled to displace this Court's statutory construction. Those cases instead demonstrate the serious litigation risks generated over time by the erroneous expansion and the absence of a rational repair grounded in the text. That combination supplies the reason beyond statutory error for overruling here. It would be incoherent to preserve Bramblett's legislative rationale by treating its judicial language as dispensable, because the same all-branch construction produced both.

Patterson requires a special justification; neither Kennedy's Hilton opinion nor his Allied-Bruce join imposes unconditional retention of an erroneous criminal construction. Scalia's Crandon concurrence, which Kennedy joined, takes cautious legal advice and inconsistent executive practice seriously but expressly distinguishes such practice from established judicial precedent. The additional justification here must therefore come from the demonstrated legal difficulties and consequences, not from treating the prosecutor's recommendation as law. The guidance remains qualified, and no assured restraint, universal nonenforcement, or personal reliance is found.

Ordinary offense elements reduce the risk to legitimate adversarial work without logically eliminating investigation and classification costs. Conversely, concern about litigation costs alone cannot invalidate an enacted crime. These Justices conclude that this erroneous construction can be retained only by irrational or textless limitations, and that the statutory boundary should be corrected directly. They do not adopt an independently sufficient lenity ground or a policy-made judicial-function exception. Their complete additional account has two votes. Thomas joins neither this framework nor the competing three-Justice account; his joins remain the Court's statutory construction and express precedent treatment, with practical concerns corroborative rather than independently controlling.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The Chief joins the conclusion that the question presented may be reached and joins the count-specific mandate. He would overrule Bramblett only to the extent it treats an Article III court acting in its adjudicative capacity as a department or agency under §1001. He applies that rule to these adversarial bankruptcy filings and would vacate the resulting §1001 convictions. Section 151 identifies the bankruptcy court as a unit of the district court; that institutional relationship accompanies his application without a new classification of bankruptcy judges as Article III judges or of every Judicial Branch body as a court. His characterization of these filings is his legal application, not a finding that the circuit resolved the Government's administrative-asset-collection theory.

Patterson, Square D, and Illinois Brick give statutory stare decisis unusual force because Congress can amend the law. A different preferred first reading is insufficient. The required special justification is the combination present here: Bramblett's actual facts involved the House, its broad definition was extended to adjudicating courts despite Congress's express statutory usage, and decades of attempts to carve a judicial-function exception show that its broadest language cannot be administered without exceptions the text does not supply. Those lower decisions do not displace Bramblett by attrition. They demonstrate the unworkability of retaining its broadest formulation. A direct correction of the statutory boundary is more candid and administrable than continuing textless exceptions.

Concrete reliance on applying §1001 to adversarial court filings is slight, while perjury, contempt, bankruptcy fraud, obstruction, and other enacted offenses remain available on their own terms. The Chief creates no immunity for falsehoods to executive agencies merely because litigation is pending and does not affect statutes expressly covering court filings or sworn statements. Congress remains free expressly to extend a false-statement offense to all three branches and define safeguards and exceptions appropriate to adversarial proceedings. He supplies no sentence-wide relief and leaves unaffected counts and proceedings subject to the lower court's judgment. He does not join the associates' broader entity rule, their full displacement of Bramblett, or either complete extended stare decisis account. His narrower formulation has his vote alone.

**O’Connor, dissenting, joined by Souter.** These Justices join the Court's conclusion and complete analysis that the entity question is properly presented, but would affirm Counts V–VII. Section 6 expressly allows context to expand the ordinary department meaning. Bramblett considered that provision and the statute's history, and its construction has the institutional force of a statutory precedent Congress may revise. Their disagreement is not a denial of the force of the ordinary definition; it concerns whether the reasons now offered suffice to unsettle a construction already made. O’Connor's Allied-Bruce concurrence and Souter's Hilton and Allied-Bruce joins distinguish a preferred initial reading from the duty to treat statutory precedent seriously. Their Rowland dissent likewise gives actual context substantive work.

They acknowledge the prosecution guidance and the possible burden on legitimate advocacy. The recommendation is neither irrelevant nor binding, and its qualified scope does not establish abandonment of the statute. The difficulties of an added judicial-function exception do not demonstrate that the basic retained coverage rule is unworkable; refusing that extra exception can produce a coherent rule. Neither Congress's silence nor assured prosecutorial restraint supplies an additional ground.

On the retained construction, the proposed judicial-function and private-litigation exclusions lack an enacted basis. Rodgers supports authorized-function coverage but expressly reserved the judicial-function question; it did not already decide the exception's validity. Heintz is a limited analogy against adding a litigation category, not an answer to this criminal statute. The actual counts involve knowing factual falsehoods, not a not-guilty plea or a mere demand for proof. Knowledge and falsity requirements, and a disclosure duty for concealment, protect lawful conduct without eliminating every process cost. These Justices adopt no administrative classification of the filings, no universal legal-knowledge element, and no independent lenity ground. Their affirmance reaches only the three challenged convictions.

### Kansas v. Colorado, No. 105, Original

Decision on exceptions and recommittal, May 15, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 21, 1995; decided May 15, 1995. This original proceeding concerns seven exceptions to the Special Master’s liability report and three unexcepted recommendations. There are no consolidated companion dockets.

This is the continuing original action between Kansas and Colorado under Article III, §2 and 28 U.S.C. §1251(a), on exceptions to Special Master Arthur L. Littleworth’s liability report. The original action began in 1986; there is no lower-court judgment to affirm or reverse and no certiorari disposition. The United States intervened concerning federal reservoir operations. The liability trial occupied 141 days from September 1990 through December 1992. The January 2, 1990 phase order separated remedy while permitting remedial or economic evidence necessary to liability, defenses and counterclaims.

Kansas challenges rejection of its Trinidad and WWSP depletion claims and the usable-water method. Colorado challenges laches, the pre-Compact pumping allowance, the asserted independent Plan credit and the proof standard. The separate WWSP prior-approval ruling and Lake McKinney and Kansas-well counterclaims are unexcepted recommendations requiring express disposition. The Court independently examines the Master’s work, giving appropriate weight to its technical and witness assessment without automatic appellate deference. It adopts the recommendations only on the grounds stated here.

The Arkansas River Compact, signed December 14, 1948 and approved by Congress on May 31, 1949, apportions the defined river waters and John Martin conservation benefits. Article III-B covers waters originating in the natural Arkansas drainage basin above the Stateline, including tributaries, and excludes imports from other basins; IV-A confines the Compact to that definition. IV-B does not affect New Mexico’s or its citizens’ rights in Trinchera Creek or other Purgatoire tributaries in New Mexico. Article III-C uses the official Stateline gage; VI-B also counts Frontier and similarly situated canal flows across the line and places Frontier’s Colorado headworks under Kansas’s exclusive administration for its Kansas irrigation use.

Article IV-D provides:

> This Compact is not intended to impede or prevent future beneficial development of the Arkansas River basin in Colorado and Kansas by Federal or State agencies, by private enterprise, or by combinations thereof, which may involve construction of dams, reservoir, and other works for the purposes of water utilization and control, as well as the improved or prolonged functioning of existing works: Provided, that the waters of the Arkansas River, as defined in Article III, shall not be materially depleted in usable quantity or availability for use to the water users in Colorado and Kansas under this Compact by such future development or construction.

Article IV-C separately permits the Corps’ authorized flood-control releases at necessary or advisable times and rates regardless of either State’s ditch capacities or requirements, conservation-pool releases or suspension of storage required for maintenance, and temporary use of conservation capacity for flood operations. Those are particular operational qualifications, not immunity for all reservoir conduct.

Article V states the ordinary John Martin regime. During November 1–March 31, entering water is stored to available conservation capacity; Colorado may demand river-flow releases up to 100 cubic feet per second, for use without avoidable waste. During April 1–October 31 the same storage rule applies except during the decreed-priority regime in V-F/G. Colorado may demand river-flow equivalents up to 500 cubic feet per second; Kansas may demand the portion between 500 and 750 regardless of Colorado’s demand. Either or both States may demand summer stored-water releases: ordinarily Colorado 750, Kansas 500 and combined 1,250 cubic feet per second, subject to Administration increases for extraordinary conditions. Below 20,000 acre-feet of conservation storage those limits become 600, 400 and 1,000. A–C releases impose no upstream Colorado call except under F/G.

V-E permits simultaneous river-flow and stored-water releases and requires prompt beneficial use unless the Administration authorizes downstream storage. Colorado releases are measured near the dam; Kansas receives the equivalent in Stateline flow, with Administration travel-time allowances. No credits or debits may accumulate. Article VIII procedures govern storage and releases. Under V-F/G an Administration finding that conservation storage will or may be exhausted within fourteen days requires notice identifying a commencement day within that interval. Changed conditions may justify cancellation or modification. Colorado then administers District 67 and upstream decrees by their relative pre-John-Martin priorities until an Administration finding and timely notice that conservation water is again available. While storage is available, upstream users are unaffected by District 67 decrees except as controlled by this and other Compact provisions. Outside priority operation, filed District 67 distribution agreements govern, or relative decrees if none exists. During priority operation Kansas has no portion of river flow entering John Martin, but Colorado-origin water actually crossing the Stateline is apportioned to Kansas.

Under V-H, where material depletion or adverse effect on usable water in District 67 and Kansas would result, District 67 decreed rights cannot be transferred to another Colorado district or upstream diversion or use, and District 67 or Stateline-to-Garden-City ditch rights cannot exceed their existing totals, without Administration findings that no such depletion or adverse effect will result. Notice follows the applicable State’s time and manner for legal proceedings. This is a distinct ditch-right provision. VI-A preserves Kansas jurisdiction over its originating waters and waters crossing into Kansas; its preservation of Colorado administration, distribution, diversion and beneficial use begins “Except as otherwise provided.”

Article VIII-B authorizes bylaws, rules and regulations consistent with the Compact and procedures for its administration; procedures involving John Martin operation require District Engineer approval. VIII-D gives each State one vote and requires unanimity; a divided vote permits arbitration only upon a subsequent unanimous referral. VIII-G provides cooperative data collection and exchange, state-official information on request, and necessary gages. The Administration may require ditch measuring devices at users’ expense if it deems them necessary. VIII-H requires prompt investigation of violations or prejudicial acts brought to its attention, but permits reporting findings and recommendations after investigation when deemed advisable. Investigation is mandatory; reporting is discretionary. VIII-I makes Administration factual findings prima facie evidence, not conclusive. None creates a universal well-metering or record-preservation obligation.

Article IX-A and consent Act §2 reserve federal sovereignty, rights and jurisdiction. The Chief of Engineers is authorized to operate John Martin’s conservation features consistently with the Compact, subject to exceptions jointly approved by the Chief and Administration. IX-B permits modification or termination by unanimous State action while preserving established or recognized rights.

The 1980 Operating Plan separately exchanges release and timing rights through conservation accounts ordinarily divided 60% to Colorado District 67 and 40% to Kansas; it does not divide all river flow or imports. Colorado surrendered winter river-flow demands and Kansas its summer river-flow entitlement. Its other-water accounts concern deliveries under pre-Compact Colorado rights. Under III-A, Amity may store in its account water it could otherwise divert for storage in the Great Plains Reservoir system. Under III-B/C, Fort Lyon and Las Animas Consolidated have agricultural accounts for deliveries under an approved Pueblo winter-storage plan, capped at 20,000 and 5,000 acre-feet respectively and excluding water that otherwise would accumulate in conservation storage. Those two accounts may exchange with existing priorities, may not increase the permanent recreation pool by exchange, transfer, changed use or otherwise, and return unreleased water to conservation storage at the compact year’s end. The Plan transfers 35% of deliveries under III-A/B/C first to replenish Kansas transit-account deficits and then fill that account to 1,700 acre-feet; for excess deliveries, 11% goes to Kansas and 24% to District 67, with Amity excluded from sharing distributions of its own III-A deliveries. Kansas delivery transit losses are replenished from its transit account, with any deficit made up from the next available other-water transfers. Account evaporation is apportioned by stored amounts under jointly approved procedures; physical spills follow the Plan’s order: permanent-pool water above 10,000 acre-feet, III-A/B/C accounts, Section II conservation accounts, the Kansas transit account, then conservation storage, with account spills prorated by amounts at the start. Permanent-pool deliveries are exempt from the III-D transfer but bear their evaporation share. Injury from improper benefits calls for restitution from the benefiting entity’s first available account water, quantified by the engineering committee subject to Administration approval; this provision is not a present award on the pumping claim. Section VI preserves Kansas’s and Colorado ditches’ ability to represent their interests before the Administration and competent courts. After the annual accounting due December 1, either State’s delegation may terminate on the next March 31 by written notice to the Administration by February 1. Previously stored account water remains usable under the Plan during the next irrigation season; unused water reverts to conservation storage November 1. None of these account terms establishes the independent well-liability credit asserted here.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Pumping liability is established without choosing a general proof quantum

**Controlling proposition:** Colorado’s post-Compact well development, including increased use of existing wells, materially depleted water protected by Article IV-D in usable quantity or availability. The substantial pumping violation is established even by clear and convincing evidence, while the separate WWSP claim fails even by a preponderance; the Court therefore overrules Colorado’s proof-standard exception without choosing the general quantum governing disputed Compact-enforcement facts or fixing a compensable quantity.

**Authority:** Part 1 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the competing standards do not change this liability disposition. Nebraska distinguishes enforcement of an established right from creation of a new allocation, but does not settle every factual burden under this Compact. Colorado v. New Mexico requires high probability in its new-diversion setting, not an unerring forecast. Here the pumping evidence is not a small residual produced by one disputed calculation. The States’ differing models show substantial gross depletion, and the record separately addresses actual reservoir operations, imported return flows and the usable portion of the loss. Those considerations support a material violation even under the more demanding proposed standard. Colorado’s concerns about sovereign error and uncertain estimates therefore do not defeat the finding. They remain reasons not to convert illustrative gross totals into an award. The WWSP claim presents different operational and measurement defects and fails the lesser standard. The Court adopts neither a universal preponderance rule nor a new presumption against Colorado; exact quantity and relief remain for the remedy phase.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Nebraska v. Wyoming, April 20, 1993: preserves the distinction between enforcement of an established decree right and modification requiring clear and convincing real and substantial injury; it does not replace this Compact’s material-depletion element or settle its general factual quantum.
- Colorado v. New Mexico, 467 U.S. 310 (1984): distinguishes the proposed new-diversion setting while applying its explanation that clear and convincing proof requires high probability rather than certainty; the general Compact standard remains open.

##### 2. The protected pumping baseline is actual use, not hypothetical capacity

**Controlling proposition:** Article IV-D’s protection against material depletion extends to the improved or prolonged functioning of existing works, and Article VI-A(2) preserves Colorado’s administration only subject to the Compact’s other provisions. The supported 15,000-acre-foot historical actual-use benchmark governs this liability accounting rather than later decreed quantities or hypothetical maximum capacity; it is not an unconditional annual pumping cap or a final damages quantity.

**Authority:** Part 2 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a well’s existence before the Compact does not immunize every later expansion of its use. Article IV-D expressly reaches improved or prolonged operation, and Article VI’s qualification prevents state decrees from enlarging the interstate allowance. Texas v. New Mexico requires enforcement of the agreed allocation rather than judicial replacement of it. Both States accept protection for established use, but that agreement does not establish Colorado’s proposed amount. The Master selected the highest accepted negotiation-period estimate, 15,000 acre-feet, rather than the single wet-year 11,000 figure. The shared Hurr foundation is not five independent measurements, and unavailable original calculations remain a limitation. Colorado’s competing power, acreage and decree reconstructions also depend on disputed coverage, nonelectric use and equipment assumptions. The comparative evidence supports the selected benchmark without making official publication conclusive. Pumping above that benchmark must still cause the material loss of usable Compact water required by IV-D. The Court fixes neither a general groundwater limit nor final compensable depletion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 (1983): applies the duty to enforce the congressionally approved Compact as written; neither state decrees nor preferred allocation policy replaces its express conditions.

##### 3. The laches exception fails for lack of established inexcusable delay

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming laches could apply, Colorado has not established Kansas’s inexcusable lack of diligence and therefore cannot obtain the requested bar to monetary liability for periods before March 1985. The Court leaves laches’s categorical availability in Compact enforcement open, makes no independently sufficient prejudice holding, and reserves the eventual form of relief and properly supported remedy-stage equities.

**Authority:** Part 3 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Colorado has not proved the unreasonable lack of diligence necessary to its asserted defense. Illinois v. Kentucky’s general discussion of state claims does not decide whether laches can ever apply to Compact enforcement. Nebraska likewise shows why specific interstate conduct matters, without making every delay an acquiescence. Kansas knew that wells increased and that gross flow declined, but those facts did not establish knowledge of material depletion in usable Compact water. Drought, reservoir operation, imports and developing regulation complicated that inference. The report’s account of investigation and conflicting information does not show that Kansas possessed a provable violation and inexcusably declined to pursue it. Colorado’s concerns about old records, lost testimony and development reliance cannot substitute for the missing diligence element. The Court need not decide an independent prejudice ground. Texas v. New Mexico rejects good-faith uncertainty as a complete answer to past nonperformance while preserving remedial flexibility. No final damages cutoff or rule excluding every equitable timing consideration follows.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Illinois v. Kentucky, 500 U.S. 380 (1991): distinguishes its concrete prescription and acquiescence setting; its general laches observation does not establish an absolute Compact-enforcement exemption.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming, April 20, 1993: preserves its concrete acquiescence determination without treating knowledge of wells or passage of time as equivalent conduct here.
- Texas v. New Mexico, 482 U.S. 124 (1987): retains enforcement of past Compact obligations despite good-faith uncertainty, while leaving appropriate remedial considerations for the remedy proceeding.

##### 4. The separate reservoir bargain supplies no independent pumping credit

**Controlling proposition:** The 1980 John Martin Operating Plan does not create the asserted independent legal credit against well-pumping liability for benefits Kansas obtained through a separately bargained exchange of release and timing rights. Actual Plan operation and actual imported return flows that reduce the measured loss must remain in the physical accounting; the Court rejects no credit actually created by agreement and awards no final numerical offset.

**Authority:** Part 4 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that performance of the reservoir bargain is not automatically payment for a different Compact violation. Texas v. New Mexico requires attention to the actual undertaking. Colorado surrendered winter river-flow demands, Kansas surrendered its summer entitlement, and both obtained more useful timing through separate accounts. The Plan preserves the parties’ ability to pursue their interests in Administration and court controversies. That bargain does not purchase the independent pumping credit Colorado seeks. The absence of a settlement confirms the agreement’s scope; Colorado’s exception seeks a partial credit, not a complete bar. Its strongest point concerns actual injury: additional water can reduce an actual loss. The Court preserves that point by requiring the hydrologic comparison to reflect the river as operated, including the Plan and actual imported return flows. Imported water remains outside the apportioned source, and no right to future imports follows. Separately purchased benefits cannot become an unagreed legal credit merely because they are valuable. Final causal accounting and supported remedy issues remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Texas v. New Mexico, 482 U.S. 124 (1987): applies enforcement of the actual Compact obligation with remedial flexibility; that flexibility does not create an unbargained credit or establish a present numerical offset.

##### 5. The pleaded Trinidad claim requires the Compact’s material depletion

**Controlling proposition:** The 1967 Trinidad Operating Principles do not establish the asserted independent Compact command making every departure a violation without the material depletion required by Article IV-D. Kansas has not established that depletion by comparing actual operation with conditions without the project, so its pleaded Compact claim is dismissed; independent agreement, third-party-beneficiary and other authorized enforcement theories remain undecided.

**Authority:** Part 5 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the operative source and scope of the asserted obligation control. Texas v. New Mexico respects authorized Compact machinery but does not permit enlargement of the interstate bargain. Article VIII-B permits consistent implementing rules; unanimity and mandatory wording deserve attention, but do not establish that these cooperative project arrangements created the independent Compact prohibition asserted here. Nor does their title alone decide enforceability. Under IV-D the comparison is the river with and without the project, not actual operation against an ideal project complying with every Principle. The offered studies and shortages do not establish material usable-water depletion on that comparison. An assumed inconsistency for purposes of disposition is not an adjudicated breach of every operating term. The contrary argument correctly observes that formal commitments may have legal force. The Court leaves a separate agreement or beneficiary claim, genuine authorized implementation and the broader question of compelling Administration duties to their own sources and proper presentation. It creates neither project immunity nor a rule that all operating commitments are unenforceable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 (1983): applies respect for the Compact’s actual institutional authority while forbidding judicial alteration of its allocation; it does not foreclose every valid implementation rule or separate undertaking.

##### 6. The WWSP depletion claim fails on the particular proof

**Controlling proposition:** Kansas has not proved even by a preponderance that the reviewed Winter Water Storage Program materially depleted protected usable water, and its depletion exception is overruled. The conclusion rests on the comparison’s operational, nonparallel-error and timing defects, not a finding of no possible depletion, a demand for perfect modeling, or a rule that calibration error larger than the modeled effect always defeats proof.

**Authority:** Part 6 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the particular comparison does not establish the prohibited effect. Daubert distinguishes a method’s fit for its task from automatic acceptance of every inference drawn from it; admissibility does not establish persuasive sufficiency. Common errors can cancel in paired runs, so absolute calibration error alone is no answer to Kansas. These runs nevertheless omit actual Plan operation, contain nonparallel winter-evaporation and storage errors, and create artificial storage buildup and late spills. The concentration of modeled accretions in May and June 1985 leaves their usable timing inadequately established; neither all gains nor all losses may simply be assumed usable. Success in estimating the much larger pumping effect supplies no missing precision for this smaller program. Defects in the opposing evaporation calculation do not prove Kansas’s calculation, and conditional output is no concession accepting its assumptions. Kansas had an extensive opportunity to try liability. The Court orders no further liability trial merely because more science is possible and creates no automatic burden shift, metering duty or spoliation inference.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., June 28, 1993: preserves reliability and task-specific fit while keeping preliminary admissibility distinct from the persuasive sufficiency of a particular estimate; no mandatory checklist or perfect-model requirement is imposed.

##### 7. The adopted usable-water method fits this liability record

**Controlling proposition:** Article IV-D protects usable quantity and availability, including supported timing and recharge, rather than gross annual flow alone. For this liability record the Court accepts Durbin’s approach with Larson’s modifications—72% summer, 25% winter, a 9.9% pre-Compact recharge component, and monthly/seasonal ceilings of 40,000/140,000 acre-feet in summer and 7,500/40,000 in winter—and rejects Kansas’s exception without prescribing a permanent formula or foreclosing reliable daily analysis.

**Authority:** Part 7 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the calculation must fit both the protected use and the evidence’s temporal resolution. Daubert’s fit principle does not require choosing whichever computation produces the most detailed output. Spronk’s daily Garden City reconstruction combines daily demands with uncertain monthly depletion estimates, and its recharge attribution can charge Colorado for effects of later Kansas pumping. Kansas rightly argues that seasonal averages may understate loss during periods of greatest need. That concern requires examination of the coefficients and caps, not unsupported daily precision. The accepted approach uses the historical pre-Compact period, excluding the extraordinary 1942 flood, with Larson’s modified percentages, recharge component and ceilings stated above. It is not the unmodified Durbin proposal. The Court does not reject the Garden City gage as inherently inadequate; the discussed 20-cubic-feet-per-second power-plant threshold concerns 1970–1973, while the ordinary threshold discussed is five. Dismissal of Colorado’s Kansas-well counterclaim does not erase Kansas pumping’s separate effect on usability. No report estimate becomes a final award.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., June 28, 1993: applies the importance of fit to the inference sought, without turning its admission rule into a permanent water-accounting formula or choosing the ultimate Compact proof quantum.

##### 8. The separate WWSP prior-approval ruling is confirmed on its narrow ground

**Controlling proposition:** The 1951 Administration policy resolution, the Compact and the cited 1944 and 1962 federal authorizations do not establish the asserted general requirement of prior Administration approval for WWSP. The partial summary judgment is confirmed on that legal ground, while Article IV-D’s material-depletion restriction, Article IV-C’s actual operational qualifications and Article IX’s federal reservation and jointly approved John Martin exceptions remain effective.

**Authority:** Part 8 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted veto requires an enacted or validly exercised source of authority. The 1951 resolution recommends a policy to the governors; it does not itself impose the general approval condition Kansas proposed. Article VIII-B authorizes rules consistent with the Compact, not an unexpressed enlargement of its bargain. The 1944 statute’s provision for state views does not make those views a consent requirement, and the 1962 authorization supplies no such condition. Texas v. New Mexico requires respect for the actual Compact machinery without making administrative division an unlimited obstacle to interstate adjudication. The contrary argument that coordinated storage benefits from prior review does not supply the missing legal command. This conclusion leaves the substantive depletion duty intact and gives federal projects no blanket immunity. The Court adopts no unnecessary laches, estoppel or supersession alternative. Nor does this Administration impasse excuse every future procedural obligation. Confirmation of this unexcepted ruling reaches its necessary ground, not every statement in the earlier report.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 (1983): preserves actual Compact machinery and judicial enforcement despite administrative impasse, without creating either an unexpressed approval veto or a universal exception to administrative procedures.

##### 9. Lake McKinney is dismissed for failure to prove unauthorized retention

**Controlling proposition:** Colorado’s Lake McKinney counterclaim is dismissed because it has not adequately traced protected John Martin releases to unauthorized retention rather than the short retention needed to move water through the pool. Article V-E(2) still requires prompt beneficial use unless the Administration authorizes downstream storage; this record-specific failure of proof supplies no general permission for storage and no universal counterclaim proof rule.

**Authority:** Part 9 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the existence of a downstream pool does not establish the asserted Compact breach. Texas v. New Mexico requires enforcement of the obligation actually agreed, which here includes both prompt beneficial use and the express allowance for Administration-authorized storage. The approximately 3,600-acre-foot pool and its observed level do not by themselves identify the source of the water, the duration of retention or the presence of protected John Martin releases. Short retention necessary to move water through the facility does not establish the unauthorized withholding Colorado alleged. The 1967 episode likewise lacks the required proof. Colorado’s concern that storage could defeat timely use is legally relevant, but cannot replace tracing and duration evidence in this counterclaim. The evidence fails even under the less demanding proposed quantum, so no general counterclaim standard is chosen. The Plan does not erase V-E(2), and dismissal does not legalize every downstream storage practice or determine a materially different future operation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 (1983): applies enforcement of the enacted obligation with its express qualification; the Court neither deletes authorized downstream storage nor adds a categorical storage permission.

##### 10. The Kansas-well counterclaim fails for want of proved Colorado injury

**Controlling proposition:** Colorado’s Kansas-well counterclaim is dismissed because the asserted additional calls, transit or account effects, and lost surplus have not been shown to injure Colorado’s protected supply. The dismissal does not immunize Kansas development, equate all Kansas groundwater with Compact-covered Colorado-origin water, or remove Kansas pumping from the distinct calculation of water Kansas could actually have used.

**Authority:** Part 10 of Justice O’Connor’s opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct votes for this complete, bounded proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that development in Kansas alone does not prove the injury alleged in this counterclaim. Article III identifies the apportioned source, and Article VI preserves Kansas jurisdiction over its originating waters and waters crossing the Stateline subject to the Compact. Texas v. New Mexico requires the actual interstate obligation to supply the rule. After the 1980 Plan, Kansas draws on its own conservation account; the record does not establish the claimed additional burden on Colorado through that account. For the earlier period, the asserted extra calls lack the necessary attribution, and the alleged loss of surplus remains unquantified. Colorado’s argument identifies possible mechanisms of harm, but the evidence does not establish their asserted effect on its protected supply. No broader immunity follows from that failure. Kansas pumping may still affect recharge and the amount of water Kansas could have used, a different question addressed in the usability analysis. The Court confirms this unexcepted dismissal without a new presumption or final remedial calculation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 (1983): applies the actual Compact obligation and protected entitlement as the basis for adjudication; failure to prove this claimed injury creates no general immunity for downstream development.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 (1983): applied to enforce the actual congressionally approved bargain and its authorized machinery; judicial enforcement neither rewrites the allocation nor grants an administrative impasse an unlimited veto.
- Texas v. New Mexico, 482 U.S. 124 (1987): applied to past Compact performance and the distinction between an established obligation and the form of relief; good-faith uncertainty alone is no complete defense, and remedial flexibility creates no unagreed credit.
- Colorado v. New Mexico, 467 U.S. 310 (1984): distinguished as a proposed new diversion; its high-probability account of clear and convincing evidence informs the alternative sufficiency conclusion without deciding the general enforcement quantum.
- Nebraska v. Wyoming, April 20, 1993: preserves enforcement of established decree rights without a new-injury requirement, the separate modification burden, and its concrete acquiescence determination. None replaces Article IV-D or decides every disputed factual burden here.
- Illinois v. Kentucky, 500 U.S. 380 (1991): distinguished in its prescription and acquiescence setting; no categorical sovereign-laches rule is extracted from its general language.
- Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., June 28, 1993: preserves reliability and fit for a particular inference while keeping admission separate from ultimate persuasion; it supplies no universal hydrological method or certainty requirement.

No precedent is overruled. The Compact’s substantive condition, proof of a breach and eventual relief remain separate. No cross-area burden rule displaces the unresolved Compact quantum.

Article IV-D governs both later development and materially depleting increases in the operation of existing works. The 15,000-acre-foot historical allowance and modified usable-water calculation are accepted for this liability record, not imposed as permanent extraction or accounting codes. Colorado’s pumping violation stands under either proposed proof quantum. Laches fails on diligence; the general doctrine’s availability and remedy-stage equities remain open. Separately purchased Plan benefits supply no independent legal credit, while actual operations and imported return flows remain part of causal accounting. The Trinidad and WWSP claims fail on their respective bounded Compact and proof grounds. The narrow prior-approval ruling and two distinct counterclaim dismissals create neither a general approval veto nor immunity from future Compact duties. The Court has not chosen a universal enforcement burden, shifted persuasion because of missing data, or fixed compensation. The original action continues before the Master for the separated remedy phase.

The Master may receive the necessary remedy evidence and submit recommendations consistent with the adopted liability accounting and the Compact’s operative conditions. Final compensable quantity, the appropriate form and timing of relief, valuation, interest, supported offsets, equitable considerations and prospective compliance terms remain for that record and further Court action. No payment, interest, delivery schedule or new operating code is presently commanded. The rejected Trinidad and WWSP liability claims and the two dismissed counterclaims are not automatically reopened; any independently reserved theory requires its own authority and proper procedural presentation. The prior-approval ruling remains confirmed, without eliminating the continuing material-depletion duty. This decision completes the exceptions stage and leaves the original proceeding open for remedy.
