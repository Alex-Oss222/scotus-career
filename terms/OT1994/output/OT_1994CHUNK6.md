### City of Edmonds v. Oxford House, Inc., No. 94-23

Merits decision, 1995-05-15

Argued March 1, 1995; decided May 15, 1995. Oxford House operates a home for 10 to 12 adults recovering from alcoholism and drug addiction in a single-family zone. The District Court held the family definition exempt; the Ninth Circuit reversed. Review asks only whether ECDC section 21.30.010 is an absolutely exempt maximum-occupancy restriction under 42 U.S.C. section 3607(b)(1). The City seeks reversal.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

The Ninth Circuit is affirmed, 6-3.

Affirmance: 6 votes, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Reversal: 3 votes, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. The FHA action may proceed on remand; liability, reasonable accommodation, asserted safety defenses and ultimate relief remain unresolved. The mandate sustains the appellate rejection of the absolute exemption, not a final judgment that the zoning rule violates the FHA.

Ginsburg writes for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer. Thomas dissents, joined by Scalia and Kennedy. Each Court join covers the single exemption holding and bounded remand.

The FHA exemption for reasonable restrictions regarding the maximum number of occupants permitted to occupy a dwelling does not cover Edmonds' family definition, which permits an unlimited number of related residents while limiting unrelated residents to five. The definition regulates household composition, so the absolute exemption does not foreclose the ordinary FHA discrimination and accommodation inquiry.

Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer: six direct votes.

The Court holds that the exemption addresses crowding rather than the membership of a household. Section 3607(b)(1) preserves reasonable restrictions on the maximum number of people occupying a dwelling, while section 3604(f) supplies protection against disability discrimination and requires reasonable accommodation within its terms. Edmonds permits any number of people related by genetics, adoption or marriage, yet limits unrelated people to five. That operation identifies who may live together, not how many occupants the dwelling can safely hold. The City's strongest response is that five is a real maximum for the unrelated household. But a numerical feature does not change the subject of this rule: unlimited related occupancy remains permitted. Reading the exception in its enacted setting keeps genuine crowding controls distinct from household definitions. The Court decides only the exemption. It does not invalidate single-family zoning, find discrimination, order a particular accommodation or determine ultimate relief.


No earlier Supreme Court decision is materially extended, limited or overruled. The enacted FHA coverage and occupancy exception supply the controlling distinction.

The statutory exemption is applied within its terms; no Supreme Court precedent is overruled. Chevron is not reached because no qualifying agency interpretation is established.

Effective May 15, this unlimited-related-family composition rule is outside the absolute crowding exemption. Genuine reasonable occupancy limits remain protected at their actual scope; FHA liability and accommodation remain for remand.

Thomas, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, concludes that the statutory words encompass a reasonable numerical restriction for an unrelated household even if related households follow another rule. The exception contains no express universal-ceiling requirement. Their position does not establish that every classification is reasonable or deny disability protection outside the exemption.

**Source Notes:** 42 U.S.C. sections 3604(f), 3607(b)(1); ECDC section 21.30.010 as set out in the case record. Official United States Reports, 514 U.S. 725. The ruling addresses the exemption only, not ultimate liability.

The appellate judgment stands; the case returns for remaining FHA proceedings.

**End of entry: City of Edmonds v. Oxford House, Inc., Merits decision, 1995-05-15.**

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Financing component: no Opinion of the Court

The responsive-financing judgment has five votes to affirm invalidation but no five-Justice financing framework. Kennedy's four-Justice plurality regards this adverse, proportional legal trigger as a burden; Stone-Zsela agrees to the judgment on the insufficient programmatic connection shown here and rejects categorical invalidity. Their shared judgment does not establish that either complete framework controls. The lower judgment invalidating this arrangement binds the parties. The reporting, contribution and nonprofit holdings below each have direct majorities.

##### The special immediate-notice obligation falls with this trigger

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 10A.20, subdivision 6b's additional immediate affidavit and service obligation cannot be enforced independently of the invalid responsive-financing mechanism on this scheme's demonstrated function and legislative relationship. Under Minnesota section 645.20, the special mechanism is essentially and inseparably connected with the invalid provision such that the legislature cannot be presumed to have enacted it alone; ordinary campaign reports remain enforceable within their separate terms.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct votes. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent on this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the special notice mechanism falls with the responsive program it implements. Buckley recognizes genuine informational, anticorruption and enforcement interests for disclosure, together with protection against serious associational burdens. Minnesota already requires ordinary expenditure reports. Subdivision 6b adds a twenty-four-hour affidavit and service on the candidates and campaign treasurers in the affected race, allowing the Board to administer the amount-linked response. Section 645.20 generally preserves valid provisions, but excepts those essentially and inseparably connected with invalid provisions when independent enactment cannot be presumed. That exception applies to this specially linked mechanism. The State answers that prompt information may still be useful. General usefulness does not establish an independently intended statutory function for this additional obligation once its material implementing purpose disappears. This is a legislative-relationship conclusion, not a finding that notices are physically impossible. Ordinary reports remain intact. The Court does not reach the preserved independent vagueness and overbreadth theories.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Buckley, 424 U.S. 1: preserves distinct disclosure interests and associational protections; no categorical disclosure invalidation follows.
- Gibson, 372 U.S. 539: applies the need for a substantial relation between information demanded and the legitimate subject; does not eliminate ordinary reports.
- Brown v. Socialist Workers, 459 U.S. 87: recognizes genuine disclosure interests with protection for demonstrated associational harms; no new general exemption is created.

##### This $100 intermediary-contribution ceiling lacks sufficient fit

**Holding and operative rule:** Minnesota's $100 annual ceiling on contributions received by the covered political committees and political funds is unconstitutional on the established burden and insufficiently fitted corruption/circumvention justification. Contributions remain distinct from independent expenditures: realistic routes around valid candidate limits may be regulated, but a broadly restrictive ceiling must preserve meaningful collective political association and fit an identified corruption or circumvention mechanism; no permanent dollar minimum follows.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes, at this stated functional application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this unusually low, broadly applied intermediary ceiling cannot stand on the showing made. Buckley distinguishes contributions from expenditures and permits contribution regulation fitted to corruption and its appearance while preserving meaningful association. California Medical sustains regulation of transfers to a multicandidate committee that makes candidate contributions; its fractured reasoning does not settle every independent-advocacy committee's case. The State therefore has a legitimate circumvention route to investigate, and the absence of legislative power in a committee is not dispositive. But Minnesota has not fitted this across-the-board ceiling to an identified route of improper dependence or evasion. Between one-quarter and one-third of MCCL-CSPC's contributions exceeded $100 in the relevant cycle, demonstrating a substantial practical pooling burden. A general reference to special-interest money does not justify it. Buckley's historical amount supplies no constitutional floor or permanent safe harbor. The Court leaves higher limits, coordination controls, candidate transfers and demonstrated conduit restrictions open.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Buckley: applies contribution/expenditure distinction, corruption fit and effective collective advocacy; its historical dollar figure is no minimum.
- California Medical, 453 U.S. 182: preserves candidate-transfer anticircumvention regulation; its plurality is not a categorical answer for every committee.
- Citizens Against Rent Control, 454 U.S. 290: applies protection of pooling for collective political expression; does not immunize a candidate-contribution conduit.
- NCPAC, 470 U.S. 480: preserves independent advocacy at its scope; contributions are not silently equated with expenditures.

##### MCCL receives protection on its actual nonprofit characteristics

**Holding and operative rule:** Minnesota may not prohibit MCCL's independent expenditures solely because its incidental business activity and absence of a formal policy against significant corporate contributions fall outside section 211B.15, subdivision 15's literal safe harbor. On the undisputed minimal-business-income and insignificant-corporate-funding record, its relevant political-association characteristics support as-applied MCFL protection; material commercial accumulation, economic claimants or corporate/union conduit funding requires a different assessment.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by all eight Associates: nine direct votes on this as-applied holding. No general overruling or endorsement beyond Austin's surviving scope is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State must examine MCCL's actual constitutionally relevant characteristics rather than mechanically deny protection because it misses a descriptive checklist. MCFL protects independent spending by a voluntary political organization whose resources reflect political support rather than commercial accumulation and whose structure and funding avoid economic claimants and corporate conduits. Austin preserves regulation of materially different corporate treasury wealth; a nonprofit label alone does not establish an exemption. MCCL's incidental business operations generated minimal income related to its political work, and the State did not dispute that corporate contributions were insignificant. Failure to adopt the statutory policy against significant contributions does not itself establish material corporate financing. The State's strongest argument is administrative certainty and prevention of conduits. Objective safe-harbor conditions can provide certainty, but cannot foreclose as-applied protection where the actual organization lacks the regulatory predicate. Changed activity, business revenue, corporate or union financing may justify reassessment. Direct corporate candidate contributions and ordinary business-corporation treasury restrictions are not decided.

**Precedent treatment:**
- MCFL, 479 U.S. 238: applies protection to actual political-resource, structure and funding characteristics; descriptive facts do not foreclose this narrow as-applied claim.
- Austin, 494 U.S. 652: distinguishes materially different commercially accumulated corporate wealth; its surviving corporate-treasury rule is not overruled.

#### Precedent Treatment

Buckley retains separate contribution, expenditure, disclosure and public-financing treatment. California Medical retains its candidate-transfer circumvention holding and fragmented independent-spending limits; no categorical immunity for intermediary contributions is recognized. MCFL supports the narrow actual-characteristics application; Austin is not overruled. NCPAC remains independent-expression authority rather than a universal ban on public financing. Gibson and Brown retain tailored disclosure/association principles. McIntyre's anonymous-individual-leaflet rule is not extended to decide these organizational arrangements.

#### Law After Decision

Effective May 15, these statutory responsive-financing and linked notice arrangements remain unenforceable, the $100 covered-committee/fund ceiling is invalid, and MCCL receives the established as-applied independent-spending protection. No five-Justice responsive-financing framework is established. The reporting, intermediary-contribution fit and functional MCCL applications have direct controlling majorities as stated. Ordinary disclosure, valid contribution/conduit rules, voluntary public financing and Austin's materially different corporate context remain intact.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy's plurality, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, treats the programmed amount-linked adverse benefit as a legal burden on the independent speaker, even without a spending ceiling. Buckley and NCPAC protect independent advocacy; an ordinarily permissible voluntary grant differs from selecting adverse independent advocacy as the proportional trigger for enlarged spending authority and public funds. The plurality does not recognize a right to escape private rebuttal or prohibit ordinary public grants. It regards Minnesota's asserted participation justification as insufficient on the established record and rejects equalizing influence as a sufficient suppression rationale.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the financing judgment alone. He rejects both categorical rules: public funding of Speaker B ordinarily does not burden Speaker A, yet an independent-expenditure trigger is not automatically valid. His inquiry examines the actual legal burden, whether candidate participation is genuinely voluntary, whether the independent speaker remains legally free to spend, whether the response is bounded rather than indefinitely escalating, whether the State identifies a legitimate program objective beyond equalizing influence, and the demonstrated relationship between trigger and objective. The established over-$100 operational scenario increases an affected candidate's allowance and may yield a half-amount subsidy, subject to statutory eligibility and twice-minimum-match requirements. Participation was already about 96 percent in 1990 and 97 percent among legislative candidates in 1992; these different populations cannot be compared as a common measured trend. This record failed to substantiate the claimed need to preserve participation. Electoral-result eligibility also creates the implementation difficulty the lower opinion identified in relation to statutory payment commands; he does not invent an automatic immediate subsidy or new statutory primary-election exemption. The defect is this particular automatic, potentially escalating response's insufficient programmatic connection, not public funds answering private speech. His framework leaves room for benefits adjusted to genuine electoral circumstances, without financing primarily to neutralize disfavored advocacy. It has one vote and is noncontrolling.

His Court opinion confines special notice invalidity to its material function implementing the invalid mechanism, preserves ordinary informational/anticorruption/enforcement disclosure, and applies Minnesota's actual severability qualification. The contribution opinion rejects the lower court's categorical suggestion that intermediaries pose no corruption concern simply because they lack governmental power. It identifies legally permitted candidate relationships, realistic corruption or circumvention routes, practical magnitude and meaningful pooled advocacy as relevant inquiry subjects without a mandatory universal dollar minimum; the common holding is this ceiling's deficient fit. The nonprofit opinion preserves an objective safe harbor while allowing as-applied protection for an actual voluntary political association outside its literal conditions. Economic claimants, substantial commercial activity, material corporate or union funding and whether advocacy resources reflect voluntary political support are relevant. Minor incidental revenue or a missing formal policy alone is not conclusive; commercial financing or control can require different treatment. These applications do not overrule Austin.

Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents from financing and derivative reporting results. The independent speaker remains uncapped and retains its resources; a voluntary public program facilitates additional advocacy rather than confiscating speech. They reserve materially coercive or punitive applications and do not permit equalization as a basis for direct suppression. Because the trigger should survive, its notice mechanism cannot be invalidated solely as an orphaned implementation provision. They would remand preserved independent vagueness and overbreadth objections rather than affirm disclosure in all respects. They join the contribution and MCCL applications, preserving realistic intermediary corruption controls and regulation of materially different business wealth.

### Reynoldsville Casket Co. v. Hyde, No. 94-3

Merits decision, 1995-05-15

Argued February 27, 1995; decided May 15, 1995. Hyde sued more than three years after an Ohio accident involving the company truck and its driver. Ohio's two-year period was tolled for out-of-state defendants; Bendix held that tolling rule unconstitutional while the suit remained pending. Ohio reinstated the action by withholding Bendix from predecision claims. Review asks whether reliance alone authorizes that temporal exception.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No recusal or nonparticipation is established.

The Supreme Court of Ohio is reversed, 9-0, and the case remanded.

All nine reverse: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Ohio must apply Bendix to this nonfinal action and adjudicate remaining issues without the invalid tolling provision. Independently valid defenses or remedies remain for actual adjudication; no equitable-tolling finding or final dismissal is ordered by this Court. The mandate reverses the reliance-only ground and remands.

Breyer writes for the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg join. Scalia concurs, joined by Thomas. Kennedy concurs in the judgment, joined by O'Connor. Seven Court-opinion votes and two judgment-only votes reconcile to nine reversal votes.

A state court must apply a controlling federal constitutional rule to a case still open on direct review and cannot withhold that rule merely because a litigant relied on the invalidated statute. Independently valid procedural or remedial rules may determine relief, but cannot reproduce selective prospectivity under a different label.

Breyer's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg: seven direct votes; Kennedy and O'Connor concur in the judgment.

The Court holds that Ohio cannot preserve the legal operation of its unconstitutional tolling provision solely for claims accruing before Bendix. Bendix establishes the interstate-commerce defect in that provision. Harper requires a federal rule applied to the original litigants to govern still-open cases within its substantive scope. Beam supplies the distinction between applying the rule and deciding an independently supported remedy. Ohio's reliance ground is tied to the date of Bendix and continues the invalid tolling rule for earlier claims. Calling that treatment a remedy does not create an independent legal basis. Reliance can matter to an actual equitable doctrine with its own lawful prerequisites; this judgment establishes none. Finality, valid procedural bars, immunity, an independently justified equitable rule or a constitutional remedial requirement can present different grounds. Reich concerns withdrawal of an apparent tax remedy and does not guarantee survival of this tort action. The Court remands without deciding whether another valid ground supports relief.


Bendix Autolite, 486 U.S. 888: applies its invalidation of Ohio's interstate-commerce burden; no expansion of that constitutional holding.
Harper, June 18, 1993: applies equal rule treatment on open direct review; final judgments and independently valid remedies remain distinct.
Beam, 501 U.S. 529: applies rejection of selective retroactivity and preserves actual procedural/remedial questions.
Chevron Oil, 404 U.S. 97: cannot support the selective temporal exception Harper displaces; no additional displacement is decided.
Reich, December 6, 1994: distinguishes its apparent tax-remedy withdrawal; creates no automatic tort-claim preservation.

Bendix supplies the constitutional rule; Harper supplies equal application in open cases. Beam's rule/remedy distinction is preserved. Chevron Oil supplies no selective-prospectivity escape. Reich remains confined to its represented tax remedy; no precedent is newly overruled.

Effective May 15, mere reliance on the unconstitutional tolling rule cannot sustain Ohio's temporal exception. Harper is applied without reopening final judgments or foreclosing genuine independently valid procedural/remedial doctrines.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, explains that judicial declaration of governing law supports retroactive application; that account is additional to the Court's narrow reliance rule. Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, agrees to reverse but stresses that reliance may matter under independently justified equitable rules. The judgment cannot stand on reliance alone; their concurrence refuses to treat application of the federal rule as abolition of every lawful reliance-sensitive remedy.

**Source Notes:** Official United States Reports, 514 U.S. 749, supplies the posture. Governing authorities are Harper, June 18, 1993, and Reich, December 6, 1994. No factual entitlement to an independent remedy has been established.

Remand to the Ohio courts for application of Bendix and remaining lawful issues. The action's ultimate timeliness and any actually supported independent ground remain for those proceedings.

**End of entry: Reynoldsville Casket Co. v. Hyde, Merits decision, 1995-05-15.**

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Antecedent assent and delegation

**Holding and operative rule:** Courts independently determine agreement to arbitrate arbitrability unless ordinary applicable contract law establishes clear and unmistakable delegation. Presenting an objection while denying panel jurisdiction does not itself establish delegation; company submission does not itself establish an owner's personal assent. Where parties actually delegate arbitrability, limited award review governs that decision.

**Authority:** Breyer's Opinion of the Court, Part I; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer on the antecedent-assent rule and application; Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer alone on the additional conditional actual-delegation review rule (Stone-Zsela reserves that proposition) directly join this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that arbitration depends on agreement, including agreement about who decides whether arbitration is required. AT&T Technologies supplies clear and unmistakable delegation before arbitrators are treated as the chosen decisionmakers on arbitrability. Volt supplies fidelity to the actual contractual arrangement. A presumption favoring coverage within an accepted arbitration agreement does not answer this antecedent decisionmaker question. MKI signed the workout document containing arbitration language and submitted its dispute, but that conduct cannot automatically establish the Kaplans' personal agreement. Their appearance to object asks the panel to decline authority rather than clearly entrusting authority to it. Independent review therefore governs their objections. Actual delegation instead entails limited review, a proposition eight Justices join. Broad clauses, incorporated rules and independent nonsignatory-binding theories remain for cases presenting them; MKI's submission is distinct. ##### Custody under a continuous consecutive series

**Holding and operative rule:** A prisoner serving a continuous unexpired series of consecutive sentences remains in custody under the earlier conviction when invalidating it would advance release from the aggregate term, although its first segment has been served. Its central rule rejects isolation of one segment from the continuous custody that the judgments together produce. It neither finds the marijuana conviction invalid nor awards relief without the procedural and merits review that remains necessary.

- Maleng v. Cook, 490 U.S. 488: distinguishes a wholly expired independent sentence and preserves the rule that collateral consequences alone do not supply custody under that conviction.

#### Current-Law Effect

The first sentence in an unexpired continuous consecutive series is not jurisdictionally finished when its conviction still affects duration of present custody. Maleng’s rule for an independently expired sentence remains intact. No jurisdiction rests here solely on collateral consequences after all custody ends. Ordinary habeas procedural and merits requirements remain distinct from this custody determination.

He invokes Maleng’s limit on expired-conviction review and preserves custody as a statutory condition distinct from remedial usefulness. He would affirm the jurisdictional dismissal and therefore would not reach the marijuana claim’s merits. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Vehicle privacy and the rental agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** Absence from a rental agreement does not by itself defeat a driver’s Fourth Amendment privacy claim when he possesses and controls the vehicle with the authorized renter’s permission and without theft, fraud or comparable wrongful possession. Courts must evaluate the actual legitimate relationship, including the right to exclude, the owner’s retained rights and any effective termination of possession; renter permission alone does not establish privacy.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, joined in this proposition by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; all nine directly adopt it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a private driver restriction is relevant but does not conclusively decide constitutional privacy. Rakas requires the defendant’s own legitimate interest; Rawlings requires attention to the actual circumstances rather than an automatic property formula. Olson shows that invitation and recognized use can support personal privacy without title. The inquiry here therefore considers the authorized renter’s permission, legitimate possession, practical control, ability to exclude and the owner’s retained rights. Padilla likewise requires individualized rights rather than a proxy claim. The rental company’s request for impoundment may bear on whether legitimate possession continued at search time; the Court does not disregard that fact or decide its legal effect without findings. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Official detention and federal conditional release

**Holding and operative rule:** Time under a federal Bail Reform Act conditional-release order is not official detention under § 3585(b), even when the restrictions are severe, so Koray’s release period requires no conditions-based credit inquiry. This rule concerns the presented federal release/detention distinction, preserves qualifying uncredited detention and judicial review, and does not decide state custody or require physical BOP control for every creditable day.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven directly adopt the status rule and its application without an independently sufficient administrative-deference holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the credit provision must be read with the related release and detention scheme. Sections 3142(c) and 3142(e) distinguish conditional release from detention, and § 3621 supplies the imprisonment context. Restrictive conditions do not convert the established legal status of release into detention under the federal scheme. Wilson assigns initial calculation to the BOP while preserving review and forbidding duplicate credit; it does not answer the present definition by itself. Koray argues that institutional restraint can match jail, but the linked categories resolve this federal period without comparing every alleged restriction or treating allegations as findings. Crandon requires ordinary statutory construction before lenity, and that construction leaves no residual ambiguity here. Administrative consistency and administrability can confirm the reading, but neither independently creates it. Thomas Jefferson University’s own-regulation rule and MCI’s statutory limits do not make the program statement conclusive. The Court reverses the conditions-inquiry order, leaving state custody and unpresented notice claims undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Wilson, March 24, 1992: applies BOP initial computation, no duplicate credit and subsequent lawful review; Wilson did not itself define official detention.
- Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala, June 24, 1994: distinguishes qualified controlling weight for an agency’s own-regulation interpretation from a program statement construing a statute; its own-regulation rule remains intact.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., June 17, 1994: preserves the statutory-boundary requirement; administrative practice does not independently rewrite the credit provision.
- Crandon v. United States, 494 U.S. 152: applies ordinary text and context before lenity; the Court finds no residual ambiguity in the presented federal-release category.

#### Law After Decision

For the presented federal Bail Reform Act conditional-release period, statutory release status does not become official detention under § 3585(b) merely because the conditions are severe. The mandatory credit command for qualifying detention remains intact, including detention resulting from the offense sentenced or another charge for which arrest occurred after that offense, when the time has not been credited against another sentence. The decision does not universally require physical BOP control for every creditable pretrial day and does not decide state custody. No independent administrative-deference ground or new notice holding is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Ginsburg concurs while joining the Court’s opinion in full. She reserves whether an intelligent choice between bail and detention requires notice and comprehension of the consequences for sentence credit. That claim was not presented, so she decides no notice or due-process rule and supplies no new ground for statutory credit.

Stevens dissents without joins. He reads § 3585(b)’s term official detention in light of the restraint actually imposed, rather than treating related Bail Reform Act categories as an exclusive definition for all purposes. Crandon supports considering enacted words, context and purpose before any residual lenity. Court-ordered incarceration in an institution can be official even when a release caption is used, and Wilson allocates calculation without defining qualifying detention. The institutional-control theory does not authorize credit for ordinary curfews or every treatment placement. He would affirm the opportunity for lawful findings, not enter a credit award on allegations.

Stone-Zsela dissents without joins. He would hold that compulsory confinement in an institution under a court order can qualify when equivalent to incarceration, and that the conditional-release label alone cannot defeat the claim. The credit text does not say that only custody committed to the Attorney General counts; Wilson answers who initially computes credit, while Thomas Jefferson University concerns an agency’s own regulation and MCI confines administrative action to enacted authority. Those decisions do not make a program statement conclusive. Stone distinguishes official institutional incarceration from a free-floating hardship comparison. Ordinary home detention, curfew, work-release residence or voluntary rehabilitation is not categorically incarceration. Because the full conditions remain unproved, he would affirm a prompt bounded remand for actual compulsory conditions, the qualifying period and absence of duplicate credit, with the BOP’s computation conforming to § 3585(b) subject to lawful habeas review. He would not automatically award approximately 150 days, order release, revive equality, or replace the BOP as original calculator.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Third Circuit’s conditions-inquiry judgment is reversed and the matter remanded consistently with exclusion of this federal conditional-release period from § 3585(b) credit. BOP initial computation and exhausted judicial review remain available for qualifying detention. No conditions-based credit award for this release period, exact-day award, resentencing, immediate release or equality adjudication is ordered. State-custody treatment and the unpresented bail-choice notice question remain open.
