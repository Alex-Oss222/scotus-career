### Gutierrez de Martinez v. Lamagno, No. 94-167

Merits decision, June 14, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 22, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, which sustained the conclusiveness of certification, substitution of the United States, and dismissal of a diversity tort action arising from a Colombian accident. The question is whether the scope-of-employment certificate is judicially reviewable.

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

Fourth Circuit reversed and remanded, 6–3, for judicial review of employment-scope certification.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Certification conclusiveness | Reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Judicial scope review and proper-defendant determination |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Breyer | Full statutory-review rule, judgment and reservations |
| Dissent | Souter | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm certification finality |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Review of the scope certification

**Controlling proposition:** A Westfall Act scope-of-employment certification is judicially reviewable before substitution of the United States becomes final. Initial substitution on certification does not conclusively establish employment scope; §2679(d)(2) makes certification conclusive for removal only.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Breyer; six Justices adopt the statutory review rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress did not clearly entrust final adjudication of employment scope to the Executive. Abbott Laboratories supplies the ordinary presumption that executive action affecting legal rights remains reviewable. Section 2679 directs initial substitution when certification issues but expressly makes the certificate conclusive only for removal. Reading that limitation as finality for every purpose would confuse the litigation procedure with the predicate for employee immunity. Certification can eliminate a private tort action when the United States is immune under an FTCA exception, making impartial determination of the predicate important. United States v. Smith preserves employee exclusivity once scope is established; it does not decide who conclusively determines that scope. The express employee petition following refusal of certification does not clearly bar a plaintiff’s ordinary judicial challenge. The district court must review scope under applicable law with appropriate evidence and procedure. The Court determines neither actual scope nor liability and creates no federal damages action.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner: applies the judicial-review presumption to an ambiguous statutory finality arrangement.
- United States v. Smith: preserves employee exclusivity after scope is established; does not make the certificate conclusive.
- FDIC v. Meyer: preserves the distinction between immunity, actionable claims and employee exclusivity; supplies no certification-finality rule.

Diversity supplies jurisdiction in this case independently of certification. The Court does not decide whether Article III permits retention of a nondiverse removed case after certification is rejected, or the §904/headquarters matters outside the grant. Review waives no immunity, determines no tort merits and requires no universal discovery or specified trial mode.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No foreign-country exception or federal immunity is displaced.

Courts review the certification’s employment predicate before substitution becomes final. The foreign-country exception and all actual defenses retain their force. No removed-case jurisdiction rule is created.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents. Mandatory substitution, deletion of predecessor plaintiff-review language and the express employee petition on refusal indicate an asymmetric statutory scheme. The removal-finality language can clarify the end of former remand practice without creating a plaintiff challenge. The dissent also regards the possible removed-case jurisdiction anomaly as a reason for caution. It does not find actual employment scope or treat an FTCA exception as proof of it.

#### Source Notes

28 U.S.C. §2679(d); Fourth Circuit, 23 F.3d 402; petition, merits filings and joint appendix in the Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-167. No disputed allegation about the accident is adopted as a finding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded. The district court shall review employment scope under applicable law, permit appropriate evidence and procedure, and determine the proper defendant before addressing immunity and merits. The Court’s merits review is complete; ordinary proceedings remain below.

**End of entry: Gutierrez de Martinez v. Lamagno, merits decision, June 14, 1995.**

### Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation, No. 94-771

Merits decision, June 14, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 24, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, which barred the reviewed fuel tax on tribal retail sales and exempted tribal members’ wages even when they lived outside Indian country. The fuel-incidence and member-income questions are separate.

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

Tenth Circuit affirmed on motor-fuels tax, 9–0; reversed on off-country-resident tribal-wage exemption, 5–4; remanded.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reviewed fuel tax | Affirmed bar, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | No opposing vote | Reviewed retailer-incidence tax unenforceable against Tribe |
| Off-country-resident tribal wages | Reversed exemption, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg | Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Breyer | Remand for ordinary tax law and residence/source predicates |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, fuel and preservation | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Fuel judgment and declined late argument |
| Opinion of the Court, income | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Off-country-resident income judgment and treaty construction |
| Partial dissent | Breyer | Stevens, O’Connor, Souter | Joins fuel ruling; would affirm the treaty income exemption |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Legal incidence of the reviewed motor-fuels tax

**Controlling proposition:** Without clear congressional authorization, Oklahoma cannot enforce a tax whose legal incidence falls on the Tribe or tribal members for Indian-country sales. Under the reviewed statute’s payment, remittance and credit arrangement, incidence falls on the tribal retailer, so the fuel tax is barred in that application.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine Justices support the fuel-incidence rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that legal incidence must be identified before a State relies on economic pass-through or collection machinery. Moe and Potawatomi distinguish a direct tax on an immune tribe from collection of a valid tax on nonmembers. Yakima requires attention to the particular tax Congress authorized; Attea permits reasonable collection controls for valid nonmember taxes, not taxation of the Tribe itself. Here distributors remit on retailers’ behalf, receive credits for retailer taxes they cannot collect, and receive an exemption for the distributor’s own sale. Comparable consumer liability or a mandatory pass-through does not appear in that arrangement. These features support the lower court’s reasonable construction that the retailer bears legal liability; they are not an express statutory declaration naming incidence. The late Hayden-Cartwright argument was not presented below or in the petition and will not be decided first here. A tax lawfully shifted to non-Indians would still require its own federal-preemption and collection analysis.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Moe v. Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes; Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe: distinguish immune tribal incidence from valid nonmember taxation and collection.
- County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes: applies tax-specific congressional authorization, without transferring one tax’s permission to another.
- Department of Taxation v. Milhelm Attea & Bros.: preserves reasonable controls for valid nonmember taxes; supplies no tribal-incidence tax authority.

##### Resident taxation and the surviving treaty promise

**Controlling proposition:** Oklahoma may apply its generally applicable income tax to members residing outside Indian country who earn wages from tribal work in Indian country; the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek does not exempt that employee income. Members residing and earning tribal income in Indian country retain the ordinary protection under McClanahan and Sac and Fox.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s income portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices adopt the off-country-resident treaty construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the treaty’s protection of the Nation’s government does not immunize the income of every member residing outside Indian country. Mescalero supplies ordinary state authority over off-reservation activity absent contrary federal law. McClanahan and Sac and Fox preserve the distinct protection tied to member residence and income in Indian country. Article IV protects jurisdiction over persons and property within the Nation’s western limits and guarantees that state laws will not govern the Nation. Read as a whole, that territorial governmental promise does not prohibit the nondiscriminatory taxation of an off-country resident simply because the Tribe pays the salary. Graves supplies the distinction between taxing an employee’s income and directly taxing the employer. The favorable Indian treaty canon cannot remove the promise’s geographic context. The Court relies on the surviving treaty’s meaning, not implied repeal at statehood. It leaves nonmember wages, nontribal-source income and an independent unpressed infringement claim outside the decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission; Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation: retain the residence-and-source protection within Indian country, including statutory Indian country beyond formal reservation labels.
- Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones: applies ordinary off-reservation state authority absent contrary federal law.
- Graves v. New York ex rel. O’Keefe: distinguishes employee income taxation from taxation of government as employer.

Article IV’s retained council authority is conditioned on consistency with the federal Constitution, treaties and laws; Congress’s laws within its constitutional Indian-affairs authority remain expressly permitted. The Court does not decide Hayden-Cartwright authorization, a replacement tax, amounts, refunds, actual individual residence or an independent unpressed Williams infringement theory.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No broad off-country immunity or universal authorization of non-Indian-incidence taxes follows.

The reviewed retailer-incidence fuel tax is barred. The treaty does not bar the generally applicable income tax on the specified off-country resident wages. Indian-country residence/source protection remains. Proposed bills are not an enacted replacement; a later incidence shift requires independent analysis.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Breyer, joined by Stevens, O’Connor and Souter, agrees with the fuel holding but dissents on income. The treaty’s protection of the Nation and its descendants, read with its history and the favorable-construction canon, protects members’ wages for tribal-government work performed in Indian country despite commuter residence. Taxing that payroll burdens the protected government and its expenditures. The dissent does not revive universal intergovernmental employee immunity or exempt all off-country activities; it rests on this treaty and the member, employer and work-location nexus.

#### Source Notes

Tenth Circuit, 31 F.3d 964; the reviewed Oklahoma motor-fuels provisions; Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, Article IV; Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-771, including respondent’s merits filing and April 18 supplement. Proposed legislation is not treated as enacted law.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Tenth Circuit is affirmed on the reviewed fuel tax and reversed on the specified off-country-resident wage exemption. The case is remanded for application of these separate rulings. Oklahoma may not enforce the reviewed fuel tax against the Tribe or tax covered tribal wages of members residing in Indian country; off-country-resident wages may be taxed subject to ordinary law. No tax sum, refund or individual residence finding is entered.

**End of entry: Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation, merits decision, June 14, 1995.**

### Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston, No. 94-749

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

Argued April 25, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, which sustained application of the public-accommodations law requiring the private organizers to include GLIB. A continuing injunction against the traditional parade and the organizers’ desire to resume it keep the controversy live despite a differently organized 1995 event.

The Court reverses and remands, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support the judgment requiring dissolution of the compelled-inclusion injunction. Justice Souter delivers the single Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. No separate writing is published.

Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

All nine Justices adopt this protection for private expression in Justice Souter’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that the right to speak includes control over the composition of a private expressive presentation. Barnette prohibits compulsory affirmation, and Tornillo protects editorial choice against compelled inclusion. Turner preserves the importance of a speaker’s selection within a particular medium; its separate broadcast context is not mechanically imported here. A parade presents expression through the groups and messages its organizers choose, even if those selections are permissive or varied. GLIB’s bannered unit has its own protected message, but that protection creates no entitlement to insert it into another private speaker’s presentation. Use of public streets under a neutral permit does not convert this parade into a governmental forum. Roberts and PruneYard concern access without the same compelled alteration of expression. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations, with credibility deference, rejects the conclusion that absence of a single theme eliminates protection. The Court invalidates only this compelled expressive inclusion, without finding every customer identity a message or requiring proof that spectators endorse each contingent.

West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette supplies protection against compelled expression.

Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo supplies protection for editorial selection.

The Court applies the medium-specific editorial protection recognized in Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC without importing its unrelated broadcast results.

Roberts v. United States Jaycees and PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins are distinguished because they concern access that does not materially alter a private expressive presentation.

Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union and Fiske v. Kansas supply independent review of characterizations intertwined with the First Amendment issue, while retaining deference to credibility determinations.

The respondents’ state-action/public-forum theory against the City was dismissed and not cross-appealed; it is not revived here. The importance of message misattribution in another setting remains open. No general antidiscrimination exemption, facial invalidation or judgment about the correct political message is announced.

Ordinary nondiscrimination applications remain distinct and enforceable.

Private parade composition receives protection against compelled insertion of an identified expressive unit. Other expressive-association disputes require a comparably material expressive burden; ordinary customer identity alone is not a message.

**Source notes:** Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, 418 Mass. 238, 636 N.E.2d 1293; respondent’s predecision mootness suggestion and petitioner’s response, Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-749. The continuing injunction supplies the live controversy; no later Court action or fee amount is determined.

The Supreme Judicial Court judgment is reversed and the case remanded. The injunction compelling GLIB’s inclusion shall be dissolved. The public-accommodations law remains enforceable where its application does not compel private expression. No fee amount, fine or sanction is decided.

**End of entry: Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston, merits decision, June 19, 1995.**

### National Private Truck Council, Inc. v. Oklahoma Tax Commission, No. 94-688

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

Argued April 19, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Oklahoma Supreme Court. The questions concern federal injunctive or declaratory relief under 42 U.S.C. §1983 and fees under §1988 after the taxpayers obtained invalidation of the taxes and state-law refunds.

The Court affirms, 9–0, both the denial of an injunction or declaration under §1983 and the denial of fees under §1988. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support both components. Justice Thomas delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. Justice Kennedy also files an unjoined concurrence.

Section 1983 does not authorize injunctive or declaratory interference with state tax administration where an adequate legal remedy actually protects the claimant. The limitation arises from federal equitable principles informing §1983, not direct application of 28 U.S.C. §1341 to state courts, and does not restrict otherwise lawful state-law equitable relief.

All nine Justices adopt this remedial rule in Justice Thomas’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that a federal right and the relief available to enforce it are distinct questions. Dennis v. Higgins recognizes an enforceable Commerce Clause right; it does not abolish the established limits on federal tax equity. Dows, Matthews and Great Lakes supply that tradition: courts ordinarily withhold tax injunctions and parallel declarations when legal relief affords effective protection. Section 1341 directly addresses federal district courts and therefore cannot itself withdraw this state court’s jurisdiction. Nor does a State’s preference for uniform procedure defeat federal law. Here the taxpayers obtained invalidation of the discriminatory taxes and an adequate state-law refund remedy. Additional federal equity is unnecessary on that record. The rule preserves extraordinary relief for irreparable injury, a cloud on title or a genuine risk of numerous suits between the same parties involving the same law or facts. It does not impose general exhaustion, decide damages, or permit withdrawal of an actually promised refund.

Dennis v. Higgins remains the source of the Commerce Clause right; the Court distinguishes that right from the availability of equitable relief.

Dows v. Chicago, Matthews v. Rodgers and Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co. v. Huffman supply the tax-equity tradition, including its limits concerning adequate remedies and extraordinary injury.

Reich v. Collins preserves the requirement that a promised postpayment remedy actually be honored; the successful refunds here do not require duplicate equitable relief.

Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation continues to require application of governing tax law and meaningful correction, without fixing a single remedy.

Livadas v. Bradshaw supplies the distinction among a federal right, its enforcement channel and the relief available.

Felder v. Casey and Patsy v. Board of Regents concern hostile state conditions and general exhaustion requirements; those restrictions differ from this limitation inherent in federal equitable relief.

Because the invoked §1983 equitable action affords no relief on this record, §1988 supplies no fee entitlement through that action. Success on state law does not categorically defeat fees where an independently viable fee-eligible federal claim exists.

All nine Justices adopt this fee holding in Justice Thomas’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that section 1988 does not create the federal action whose availability must be established first. Maher v. Gagne permits fees in appropriate federal enforcement proceedings without an express adjudication of federal merits, including when a court resolves the dispute on another ground. The decisive defect here is different: the federal tax-equity limitation leaves no available relief under the section 1983 action the taxpayers invoked. Their state-law refund victory therefore cannot supply a fee entitlement through an unavailable federal remedial route. The Court does not hold that every state-law judgment, or every absence of an express federal merits ruling, defeats fees. No independently viable alternative federal fee-eligible claim is established in this case. The tax invalidity and refund entitlement remain undisturbed. The independent state statutory fee ground is outside review, as are a reasonable fee amount, an unpresented damages action and any case in which the legal remedy fails to afford adequate protection.

Maher v. Gagne remains intact: an available fee-eligible action resolved without deciding federal merits differs from the unavailable federal equitable action here.

An inadequate remedy, including continued unconstitutional taxation after a refund judgment, may require different equity. Mere parallel claims by many taxpayers do not themselves establish the same-party multiplicity exception. The general duty of state courts to entertain §1983 is assumed rather than decided.

Dennis remains law despite Kennedy’s separate objection. No prior right, meaningful refund requirement or generally available §1983 remedy is withdrawn.

Federal tax equity under §1983 and derivative fees are unavailable on this actually adequate-refund record. State-law equitable authority remains intact. This decision does not determine tax validity, refund amounts, a separate damages claim or the independent state fee ground.

Kennedy concurs and joins the Court in full. He preserves his view, expressed in his Dennis dissent, that the Commerce Clause was not meant to create the kind of individual statutory right §1983 enforces. Dennis nevertheless governs this case; his objection supplies no alternative controlling rule and does not alter the adopted remedial analysis.

**Source notes:** Oklahoma Supreme Court, 879 P.2d 137 (1994); petition and party filings in the Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-688; 42 U.S.C. §§1983 and 1988; 28 U.S.C. §1341.

The Oklahoma Supreme Court’s denial of the reviewed federal relief and fees is affirmed. The existing tax invalidity and state-law refund entitlement remain in effect; lawful refund proceedings continue without an additional federal equitable or fee instruction. This merits review is complete.

**End of entry: National Private Truck Council, Inc. v. Oklahoma Tax Commission, merits decision, June 19, 1995.**

### Sandin v. Conner, No. 93-1911

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 28, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed summary judgment for prison officials after recognizing a liberty interest and an unresolved dispute about process under Wolff. The question concerns Hawaii’s disciplinary segregation rules and the predicate for protected liberty.

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

Ninth Circuit affirmed in the reviewed respect and case remanded, 5–4; genuine constraint, meaningful deprivation and process must be determined; no liability finding.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reviewed liberty/process summary judgment | Affirmed reversal of summary judgment; remanded, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Genuine constraint, meaningful deprivation and process determinations |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Sufficient nonexclusive rule; no ultimate application holding; bounded remand |
| Concurrence | Ginsburg | Stevens | Full common rule/remand join; would already find interest and retains direct liberty |
| Concurrence | Breyer | Souter | Full common rule/remand join; would already find interest under whole Hawaii scheme |
| Dissent | O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse reviewed liberty predicate under replacement hardship framework |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Substantive state constraints and meaningful prison liberty

**Controlling proposition:** A State creates protected prison liberty when substantive law meaningfully restricts officials’ authority to impose a significant deprivation. Mandatory wording alone is insufficient: courts must examine the genuine constraint in the whole disciplinary scheme and the meaningful restraint, without making state codification necessary for directly protected constitutional liberty.

**Authority:** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that legal substance matters more than a regulation’s drafting conventions. Hewitt supplies the recognition that substantive state constraints may create protected liberty; Thompson distinguishes genuine predicates from procedures that do not require a particular substantive outcome. The Court narrows reliance on mandatory words alone without discarding real constraints simply because they operate inside prison. The complete scheme must be considered: defined misconduct, an impartial adjudicative process, valid evidence and the relationship between guilt and a significant sanction can confine official authority even when officials retain discretion to select a commensurate penalty afterward. Wolff distinguishes substantial protected liberty from routine management and supplies the separate inquiry into appropriate process. Mere mandatory administration, routine transfers or brief inconveniences do not automatically create entitlements. Nor is this an exclusive requirement of state codification for intrinsic constitutional liberty, an unexpected restraint or a sentence-duration interest. The Court adopts no new multifactor hardship test and leaves the ultimate application to the district court under the stated rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hewitt v. Helms: narrows mechanical reliance on mandatory wording while retaining genuine substantive-predicate liberty protection; no wholesale methodology replacement controls.
- Kentucky Department of Corrections v. Thompson: applies distinction between substantive constraints and procedural directions alone.
- Wolff v. McDonnell: preserves significant prison liberty and context-sensitive procedural protection.
- Meachum v. Fano: distinguishes ordinary sentence-authorized management from a genuine protected constraint.
- Vitek v. Jones; Washington v. Harper: preserve distinct directly protected, qualitatively different restraints; this state-entitlement rule is nonexclusive.
- Farmer v. Brennan; Helling v. McKinney: distinguish Eighth Amendment conditions liability from this procedural-liberty inquiry.

##### The reviewed summary judgment and remand

**Controlling proposition:** The summary judgment rejecting the reviewed liberty-and-process claim cannot stand on a mechanical reading of Hawaii’s regulatory words or dismissal of genuine punitive constraints. The district court must determine whether the rules genuinely constrain authority, whether the deprivation is meaningful rather than routine, and what process is due; the Court makes no liability or ultimate protected-interest finding.

**Authority:** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court and remedial disposition; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg join the bounded rule-and-remand ground. Four concurring Justices separately would find the interest already established.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the dispute requires application of the substantive rule to the complete disciplinary scheme and the actual deprivation. Hawaii’s direction to find guilt on substantial evidence is not rewritten as an express acquittal command; it must be read with the defined offenses, impartial committee, evidence rules and authority for serious sanctions. The record concerns thirty days of punitive isolation and material restriction of ordinary out-of-cell activity. Similarity to separately authorized administrative or protective confinement does not by itself eliminate the legal constraint on punishment, while punitive labeling alone does not create protected liberty. Later expungement of the serious charge does not retroactively supply adequate process. Discretionary parole consequences are not an inevitable sentence extension. The district court must make the necessary determinations and assess process if protected liberty is implicated. Wolff permits refusal of witnesses for irrelevance, lack of necessity or hazards to safety or correctional goals. No automatic witness right, useless-witness finding, damages, immunity or unrelated amended-complaint disposition follows.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wolff: applies witness qualifications and the separate process inquiry once protected liberty is implicated.
- Hewitt; Thompson: require whole-scheme substantive analysis rather than a one-way mandatory-word inference.
- Meachum: preserves ordinary authorized custody without making every punitive restraint legally unconstrained.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Hewitt is narrowed, not wholesale replaced. The proposed atypical-and-significant-hardship framework has four dissenting votes; the concurrences’ positive application has four votes. Neither controls through Marks.

The five-Justice sufficient state-entitlement rule and bounded remand govern. The Court does not hold that protected liberty is absent or already established on this record. Other direct constitutional, unexpected-restraint and sentence-duration protections are not displaced; unrelated claims and defenses remain. No new multifactor prison test is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Ginsburg, joined by Stevens, concurs. They would recognize protected liberty already on the actual punitive confinement record and also preserve an independent constitutional retained-liberty ground. Severe imposed isolation and its punitive character matter without depending on how a State drafts its code; administrative similarity and later expungement do not retroactively remove the need for process. They join the Court’s sufficient state-entitlement rule because it does not make codification necessary or replace intrinsic liberty with an exclusive hardship prerequisite. Breyer, joined by Souter, also concurs. The full defined-misconduct, impartial-decision and valid-evidence scheme constrains serious punishment, and the thirty-day isolation materially restricts ordinary activity. They would find the protected interest already established, while accepting the Court’s narrower remand and leaving actual procedural adequacy open. O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. They would replace the mandatory-regulation methodology with a rule ordinarily limiting state-created interests to atypical and significant hardship relative to ordinary prison life, while preserving direct unexpected-restraint and inevitable sentence-duration interests. They find the reviewed thirty-day confinement insufficient under that rule in light of comparable administrative and protective confinement and discretionary parole. Their proposed framework receives four votes and does not control. The concurrences’ affirmative application likewise has four votes; no Marks aggregation makes it the Court’s application holding.

#### Source Notes

Ninth Circuit, 15 F.3d 1463; Hawaii disciplinary rules §§17-201-7, 12, 13, 16–20; merits filings and record passages in No. 93-1911, including the segregation comparison. The defined-offense, impartial-decision and evidence provisions are distinct from the rule directing a guilt finding on substantial evidence; that wording supplies no automatic acquittal command.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit is affirmed in the reviewed respect and the case remanded. The district court shall determine whether Hawaii rules genuinely constrain officials’ authority to impose punitive segregation, whether the deprivation is meaningful rather than routine, and what process is due. The Court makes no liability or final interest finding; no unrelated amended-complaint claim is swept into the disposition. Properly framed ensuing proceedings remain available.

**End of entry: Sandin v. Conner, merits decision, June 19, 1995.**

### United States v. Gaudin, No. 94-514

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

Argued April 17, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed the reviewed convictions after the district judge decided materiality for the jury. The question concerns the Fifth and Sixth Amendment allocation of that element in the §1001 prosecution.

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support affirmance of the reversal of the reviewed false-statement convictions. Justice Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. Justice Thomas also concurs, joined by Justices O’Connor and Breyer.

When materiality is an element of the charged criminal false-statement offense, the Fifth and Sixth Amendments require the jury to determine it beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge states statutory meaning and the governing legal standard; labeling its application a mixed question does not remove an element from the jury.

All nine Justices adopt this allocation of the offense element to the jury in Justice Scalia’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that the jury must decide every element necessary to establish criminal guilt, including application of an instructed legal standard to the charged facts. Winship supplies proof beyond a reasonable doubt; the constitutional jury guarantee places that determination with the jury. Materiality asks whether a statement has a natural tendency or capacity to influence the relevant governmental decision. That application does not become an abstract legal question simply because legal instruction is needed. Historical practice and functional convenience cannot reserve an offense element to the judge. Sinclair’s contrary criminal pertinency allocation cannot stand to that extent. Kungys concerns a civil denaturalization proceeding and a different appellate allocation, not this criminal jury guarantee. Here the Government concedes materiality is an element and did not challenge the lower court’s error classifications. Those premises preserve this reversal without deciding whether every section 1001 clause includes materiality or every withheld element is structural error. Statutory interpretation and threshold evidentiary sufficiency remain judicial functions.

In re Winship supplies the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt for facts establishing guilt.

Sinclair v. United States is overruled to the extent it assigns a criminal pertinency element to the judge; its unrelated propositions remain in force.

Kungys v. United States is distinguished because it concerns civil denaturalization and the allocation of responsibility on appeal.

Sullivan v. Louisiana retains its constitutional jury and burden-of-proof holding; this decision does not classify every error involving an omitted element.

Hubbard v. United States retains its rule about the entities covered by §1001; it supplies no authority to remove elements of the charged HUD offense from a jury.

The decision does not determine abstract statutory meaning by jury, classify every omitted-element error, adjudicate the exact trial-preservation history, or disturb a separately treated equity-skimming conviction or calculate its sentence.

The treatment accompanying the holding controls; only the incompatible criminal-element allocation of Sinclair is displaced.

A jury must apply the legal materiality standard beyond a reasonable doubt where materiality is an element. No universal materiality requirement for every §1001 clause or universal structural-error rule is created.

Thomas, joined by O’Connor and Breyer, concurs while joining the Court in full. The Government’s concession establishes materiality as an element for this case; the opinion need not construe every §1001 clause. The unchallenged lower error classifications do not settle harmless error, plain error or structural error for all later cases. These reservations qualify the questions decided, not the jury-element holding.

**Source notes:** Ninth Circuit en banc, 28 F.3d 943; §1001; petition in the Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-514. That collection item lacks separately listed merits and appendix components. The lower opinion and the conceded-element premises support the bounded question decided; the decision makes no exact finding about trial preservation.

The Ninth Circuit is affirmed and the reviewed false-statement convictions remain vacated. The Government may retry under instructions submitting every element, including materiality, to the jury. This is not an acquittal or a disposition of the separately treated equity-skimming conviction. This merits review is complete.

**End of entry: United States v. Gaudin, merits decision, June 19, 1995.**

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Truthful targeted mail and the demonstrated fit of the waiting period

**Controlling proposition:** On this record, Florida may not forbid truthful, nonmisleading targeted direct-mail solicitation about lawful legal services until an accident or disaster is more than thirty days old. The asserted privacy and professional-confidence interests are substantial, but Florida has not established that this categorical silence materially and proportionately addresses concrete harm; the referral-service rule is likewise invalid insofar as it incorporates that restriction.

Central Hudson requires material advancement and reasonable fit; Board of Trustees v. Fox does not demand the least restrictive conceivable means. Shapero distinguishes a mailed communication that the recipient can discard from the coercive pressure of an in-person encounter. Florida’s study summary and complaints show disapproval of immediate solicitation and concern for professional reputation. They do not establish that truthful, identified mail deprives recipients of choice or that suppressing all such mail during the fixed period protects privacy in a way proportionate conduct rules cannot. Existing restrictions can address deception, coercion, harassment, unwilling recipients and misuse of confidential information. A materially different record establishing concrete injury and fit remains open.

- Board of Trustees v. Fox: preserves reasonable fit without a least-restrictive-means requirement.

- Bates v. State Bar of Arizona: preserves protection for truthful lawyer advertising.
- Lanphere & Urbaniak v. Colorado; Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co.: apply the current actual-harm, material-advancement and reasonable-fit requirements; supported prediction is permitted, professional distaste alone is insufficient.

Those separate restrictions are not exceptions authorizing premature mail under the challenged waiting-period text. The Bar’s abandoned undue-influence rationale supplies no independent holding.

No professional-advertising exception replaces Central Hudson, and Bates and Shapero are not overruled.

The specific waiting-period restriction and its referral incorporation cannot be enforced on this record. Florida remains free to regulate proven deception, coercion, harassment and confidential-record misuse and to adopt proportionate privacy protections. The evidentiary holding does not forbid every supported temporal restriction or require numerical studies.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Breyer, dissents. The dissent regards the unrequested intrusion on people recently injured or bereaved as a concrete privacy harm supported by the study summary and public submissions. A finite waiting period, with public advertisements and later direct mail available, is reasonably fitted under Central Hudson and Fox. The dissent distinguishes Shapero’s broad mailing ban and rejects any need to exhaust every conceivable alternative. It does not rely on professional distaste alone, revive the abandoned undue-influence theory or overrule protection for truthful advertising.

### United States v. Aguilar, No. 94-270

Merits decision, June 21, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 20, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed Count 8 under §1503 and Count 6 under §2232(c). The questions concern the connection to a judicial proceeding and disclosure after an interception authorization expires. Preserved knowledge-instruction issues remain.

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the §1503 reversal, reverses the §2232(c) expiration ruling and remands.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A known nexus to pending judicial work

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1503 requires a relationship in time, causation or logic between the endeavor and a pending judicial or grand-jury proceeding, including knowledge that the conduct is likely to affect that proceeding. An unsuccessful endeavor can qualify, but false statements to investigators do not suffice merely because they might later testify.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s §1503 portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter and Breyer; six Justices adopt this statutory nexus and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the omnibus clause concerns the due administration of judicial justice, rather than every investigation a federal officer conducts. Pettibone supplies the requirement of a known pending proceeding and a connection between the endeavor and that work. The word endeavor permits punishment even when interference fails; it does not dispense with the statutory object. The conduct must have the natural and probable relation to interference with the proceeding, and the defendant must know it is likely to affect that proceeding. Here the charged false denials were made to FBI investigators not shown to be directed by or subpoenaed before the grand jury. A speculative possibility that their later testimony would relay the statements does not establish the required nexus. The Court does not create a categorical subpoena condition, implied repeal of all witness-related obstruction by section 1512 or a privilege to lie. Direct grand-jury lies and knowingly channeled statements remain distinct. The charged conviction’s reversal stands.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pettibone v. United States: applies known pending-proceeding and nexus requirements.
- United States v. Russell: preserves punishment of unsuccessful endeavors but does not eliminate the judicial object.
- Ratzlaf v. United States; Staples v. United States: preserve offense-specific knowledge or intent construction; their distinct elements do not replace those enacted here.
- Hubbard v. United States: distinguishes §1001 entity coverage from these obstruction counts.

##### Disclosure of a known application and obstructive purpose

**Controlling proposition:** Section 2232(c) requires knowledge of an actual federal application or authorization under chapter 119, purpose to obstruct its interception, and notice or attempted notice of that possible interception to a person. Expiration of the identified authorization does not alone establish a defense; the statute does not require successful interference or that authorization remain formally in force at disclosure.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s §2232(c) portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight Justices support this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted knowledge and purpose requirements cannot be replaced by an additional unexpired-order condition. Knowledge that an officer has applied for or received authorization identifies an actual statutory application; a wholly imaginary application is insufficient. Such interception connects the obstructive purpose to that application, while possible interception and attempted notice anticipate uncertainty and failure. The statute therefore does not turn on proof that interception was actually occurring, that the warning reached only a target or that the endeavor succeeded. The expired order does not alone resolve the offense where the accused acts with the required knowledge and purpose concerning its interception. No knowledge of subsequent orders is imputed, and official notice duties do not extend interception authority. The Court rejects the court of appeals’ automatic expiration bar. It leaves the preserved knowledge-instruction and harmlessness issues for remand, rather than reinstating a final conviction or sentence now. Liability decades later and possible limits following official inventory notice remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Staples; Ratzlaf: apply offense-specific knowledge analysis without grafting statutory-illegality knowledge onto this offense.
- Russell: preserves the distinction between an obstructive endeavor and completed success, without replacing §2232(c)’s actual application and purpose predicates.

##### The as-applied speech objection

**Controlling proposition:** The First Amendment does not invalidate §2232(c) as applied to this purposefully obstructive notice by a judicial officer who acquired sensitive interception information through judicial office. This constitutional conclusion supports enforcement of the enacted application, knowledge and purpose elements; it creates no independent confidentiality offense or source-of-knowledge element.

**Authority:** Ginsburg’s constitutional portion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight Justices support the rationale. Stone-Zsela agrees only with rejection of the objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this case does not concern ordinary reporting by a member of the public. Daily Mail protects public dissemination under the applicable ordinary-speaker principles; it does not decide the obligations attached to information voluntarily entrusted through official position. Seattle Times and Snepp supply the distinction between ordinary acquisition and accepted confidential access. Here the judicial officer received sensitive interception information through judicial office and gave notice with the obstructive purpose required by the statute. Those limited predicates answer the as-applied objection without authorizing a general ban on discussion of surveillance. The Court does not treat an official duty as a separate criminal offense, add a source-of-knowledge instruction or excuse proof of the enacted elements. It does not decide public criticism unrelated to an actual application or lacking obstructive purpose. The retained instruction disputes remain for the lower court. Stevens agrees with this constitutional rejection despite his different construction of possible interception; the constitutional agreement does not erase that statutory disagreement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Smith v. Daily Mail Publishing Co.: distinguishes ordinary public dissemination from accepted official confidential access.
- Seattle Times Co. v. Rhinehart; Snepp v. United States: apply the accepted-access/confidentiality distinction at this as-applied level; create no independent offense here.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No implied wholesale witness repeal, substitute §1001 offense or general official-confidentiality crime is created.

The pending-judicial nexus limits §1503; §2232(c) retains actual application knowledge and purpose without an automatic expiration defense. Jury allocation under Gaudin remains intact. Knowledge-instruction validity, harmlessness and ordinary sentencing issues remain undecided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, would reject the §1503 exclusion. A proved specific corrupt purpose toward a known pending grand jury can support an unsuccessful endeavor without a separately necessary known-probable-effect condition. They retain the pending judicial object, reject wholesale witness repeal and preserve unresolved instruction issues. Stevens would affirm the §2232(c) reversal: possible and such interception objectively identify an interception still lawfully available from the known application. Attempted notice does not, on his reading, turn information about a legally expired authorization into prohibited notice. He nevertheless joins the as-applied constitutional rejection. Neither separate writing revives acquitted counts or resolves the preserved instructions.

#### Source Notes

Ninth Circuit en banc, 21 F.3d 1475; 18 U.S.C. §§1503 and 2232(c); merits briefs and joint appendix in No. 94-270, including Counts 6 and 8. The identified application concerned Tham’s phone and Chapman conversations; it does not establish that Chapman’s own phone was tapped or that Aguilar knew of later orders.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The §1503 Count 8 reversal is affirmed. The automatic expiration ground for Count 6 is rejected and the case remanded for preserved knowledge-instruction, harmlessness and other unresolved claims. The §2232(c) conviction may be reinstated subject to those determinations and ordinary sentencing; it is not reinstated now and retrial is not commanded. Acquitted/dismissed counts and the Government’s sentencing appeal are not adjudicated.

##### I. Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver

**Controlling proposition:** Collection and chemical analysis of a student's urine are Fourth Amendment searches. Enrollment or voluntary athletic participation does not waive that protection; the school and athletic setting instead informs the reasonableness inquiry.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine Justices adopt the search and no-waiver rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that obtaining urine and examining its chemical contents intrudes on interests the Fourth Amendment protects. Skinner recognizes both physical collection and analysis as searches; neither step becomes ordinary nonconstitutional administration merely because the subject is a child. T.L.O. permits school officials to act without a warrant or ordinary probable cause when the school setting justifies that adjustment, but it does not extinguish the student's protection against unreasonable searches. Student athletes accept coaching, examinations directed to fitness, changing facilities and safety rules. Those practices bear on expectations within the activity; they do not amount to surrender of privacy in bodily functions or medical information. The District's permission form therefore cannot settle the constitutional question by describing participation as voluntary. The Court must examine the actual need, intrusion and safeguards. This threshold conclusion does not determine whether the particular program is reasonable, and it does not authorize unrelated medical investigation or police use.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association, 489 U.S. 602: applied; urine collection and chemical analysis each constitute searches.
- New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325: applied; school warrant and probable-cause adjustments preserve constitutional reasonableness rather than extinguishing protection.

##### II. This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable

**Controlling proposition:** In this documented school-athletic setting, objective seasonal and random urinalysis is reasonable without individualized suspicion because the protective safety need, athletic privacy expectations, limited collection and drug information, confidentiality and restricted counseling or athletic consequences together justify the intrusion. The holding concerns this interscholastic program and record; it creates no authority to test every student, conduct unrelated medical searches or use results for ordinary law enforcement.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the balance favors this particular athletic program. T.L.O. recognizes the school's protective responsibilities, while Skinner and Von Raab permit context-specific departures from individualized suspicion when needs beyond ordinary law enforcement justify them. The District had an actual drug and discipline problem, findings that athletes led drug groups, and observed safety concerns; the program is not a symbolic response unsupported by local evidence. Athletic supervision and shared facilities reduce some expectations, without creating a waiver. Collection still intrudes, but observation is limited, laboratory information concerns designated drugs, selection follows an objective draw, and results remain within restricted school channels. Counseling and athletic restrictions replace police transmission or general school punishment. The contrary argument identifies workable suspicion-based measures, but these precedents impose no universal requirement to exhaust every less intrusive alternative before this contextual balance can favor prevention. No confirmed drug-caused catastrophe or quantified efficacy is required. The Court leaves materially different populations, protocols and uses undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325: applied for school responsibilities and contextual reasonableness; its reservation of suspicionless searches is resolved only for this program.
- Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association, 489 U.S. 602: applied; a substantiated protective need can justify suspicionless testing after attention to actual privacy burdens and safeguards.
- National Treasury Employees Union v. Von Raab, 489 U.S. 656: applied; special-needs balancing is contextual, not confined to a universally required catastrophic-risk category.

##### III. The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy

**Controlling proposition:** The prescription-verification provision permits a prescription copy or a doctor's authorization and does not require personal delivery to teachers or coaches. On this facial policy challenge the Court may consider a confidential means of compliance and does not presume the most intrusive implementation; the rule does not approve every specimen form, nonprescription disclosure, physician-name demand or actual personal-disclosure practice.

**Authority:** Part III of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices adopt this limited facial rule. Stone-Zsela does not join it. It independently answers this objection, not the entire testing challenge.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the written verification term does not require the most intrusive delivery method respondents fear. It allows either a prescription copy or a doctor's authorization before testing; it does not command that private medical details be handed personally to a teacher or coach. Skinner treats necessary medication information in the limited testing context as part of the reasonableness assessment. A confidential method, including sealed delivery to the laboratory, is consistent with this policy's text. Considering that possibility in a facial challenge is different from finding that the District actually used it. Nor does confidentiality erase the initial privacy cost of providing information. The specimen form respondents describe, including nonprescription medicines and physician names, was not independently established in complete terms here. The Court therefore does not endorse those demands or decide the constitutionality of an actual intrusive implementation. No distinct, separately surviving medical-disclosure claim is established in this action. The general mass-testing objection remains governed by the separate reasonableness holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association, 489 U.S. 602: applied at the narrow medication-information context; no general approval of unrelated medical disclosure follows.

##### IV. Federal correction requires reconsideration of the Oregon ground

**Controlling proposition:** The Ninth Circuit judgment is vacated and remanded because its Oregon constitutional conclusion expressly followed from its erroneous Fourth Amendment conclusion. The lower court must reconsider the Oregon question independently; this judgment neither decides Oregon constitutional protection nor unconditionally reinstates final dismissal of the action.

**Authority:** Part IV of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the federal decision removes the premise on which the Court of Appeals resolved the Oregon issue, without supplying the answer to that issue. Review is of a federal appellate judgment under section 1254(1), rather than of a state highest court under section 1257. The Ninth Circuit stated that a Fourth Amendment violation necessarily produced an Oregon constitutional violation. Because the federal premise does not survive, its consequent state-law conclusion cannot remain undisturbed on that rationale alone. Respondents requested reconsideration of the independent state question if the federal holding changed. Vacatur and remand preserve that lawful opportunity. The Court does not hold that Oregon must afford only the federal minimum or that the District automatically obtains final dismissal. The school-authority issue previously rejected below is not reopened or separately decided by this federal judgment. No new consent, reinstatement of athletic eligibility or particular state-law remedy is ordered before the remaining question receives lawful consideration.

**Precedent treatment:**

- New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325: supplies only the federal search framework; it does not decide the independent Oregon constitutional question.
- No earlier Supreme Court decision is overruled or extended by this bounded federal-appellate remedy.

##### Actual policy and record qualifications

The challenged policy concerns interscholastic athletes, not all extracurricular participants or the separate voluntary all-student program. Testing occurs initially each season and by weekly random selection of about 10 percent of competing athletes. A student draw with adult witnesses conceals selected identities and leaves no individualized official selection discretion. Boys remain clothed with backs toward a monitor, whose presence permits hearing and sometimes observing production but not viewing genitals. Girls produce in enclosed stalls with a monitor outside who can hear. These are actual privacy intrusions. Initial refusal excludes the athlete for the current season.

The basic laboratory targets are amphetamines, marijuana and cocaine; occasional District-requested LSD testing also occurs. Alcohol and steroids are not tested. Anonymous laboratory numbers, restricted school-result access, one-year retention and no police transmission or suspension, expulsion or general discipline confine the use. Prescription verification permits a prescription copy OR doctor's authorization. Failure to verify combined with a positive result triggers positive-test procedures, not automatic exclusion merely for missing verification. The written policy permits a second test and directs no action if it is negative; testified operation supplies confirmation of positive results. The Court does not rewrite written permission into a mandatory statutory retest rule. Parents are notified and a hearing follows confirmed positives. First offense permits counseling with six weekly tests OR current-and-next-season athletic suspension. Written second-offense consequences are current-and-next-season suspension, while testimony describes a counseling/weekly-testing alternative in practice. Third offense excludes the current and next two seasons. Retesting occurs before the next eligible season.

The trial findings establish increased drug use and discipline difficulties, athletes leading drug groups, observed impaired safety performance and previously attempted educational and deterrent responses. A suspected drug-related wrestling injury is not a toxicologically established causal finding. No finding establishes that Acton uses drugs, that every athlete caused the problem, or that the program has a quantified efficacy.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

T.L.O. preserves student Fourth Amendment rights and school-specific warrant/probable-cause adjustment; its open suspicionless question is resolved only for this athlete program. Skinner establishes search status and contextual protective-needs testing, including limited medication-information reasoning. Von Raab permits contextual special-needs balancing rather than a universal suspicion or catastrophic-risk requirement. None is overruled. Soldal's independent seizure protection and Dickerson's limits on exploratory manipulation remain unaffected; neither supplies a rule governing school urinalysis. 

The Fourth Amendment permits this documented interscholastic-athlete program under its actual collection, selection, confidentiality and response limits. All-student and other extracurricular programs, materially more intrusive practices, police uses and unrelated medical investigation remain undecided. The independent Oregon constitutional question remains open. The Chief Justice's impracticability condition and the suspicion-centered dissent are noncontrolling; no plurality or Marks synthesis is needed.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Ginsburg concurs. She joins the Court's opinion because the actual competitive athletic setting, safety findings and bounded safeguards justify this program. She emphasizes that the decision does not approve compulsory testing of every student or every extracurricular participant. Athlete influence on classmates cannot, without more, extend the rationale to a population with materially different privacy expectations and activity risks. Her caution is a limit on the proposition adopted, not judgment-only agreement or a refusal to join the Court's five-Justice program rule. The prescription holding remains facial and confined; actual intrusive disclosure demands and materially different protocols receive no approval.

Justice O'Connor dissents, joined by Stevens and Souter. They would affirm the federal invalidation while recognizing that children remain subject to legitimate school supervision and that the District faced a genuine drug and safety problem. T.L.O. relaxed warrant and probable-cause demands but did not approve suspicionless testing. Skinner and Von Raab establish exceptions, not an absolute rule requiring suspicion in every conceivable school setting; the dissent disputes whether this record establishes enough need to dispense with workable individualized suspicion. The District's observations identified suspected users and misconduct. Neutral drawing reduces arbitrary selection, but does not itself justify obtaining bodily samples from an innocent child. Collection remains an intrusion even with limited observation, drug-specific analysis and restricted use. Athlete participation is no constitutional waiver. Stevens's concern that school constitutional safeguards teach civic values reinforces, rather than replaces, the required legal justification. The dissent does not demand a universally catastrophic risk, deny every protective school interest, or prohibit reasonable-suspicion testing. It joins the limited facial prescription-verification holding because the policy permits a confidential laboratory method; it does not base invalidation on a demand that prescriptions necessarily be delivered to school personnel. Different evidence and a narrower program remain open.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents. He would affirm the Ninth Circuit's invalidation because the District has not established that individualized reasonable-suspicion testing or materially less intrusive measures are impracticable. Urine collection and chemical analysis are searches. A genuine non-law-enforcement safety need permits adjustment of warrant and probable-cause defaults, but recurring bodily and medical searches of a broad class require a proportionate justification for dispensing with individualized cause. Coaching, fitness examinations, communal changing and athletic rules do not waive excretory or medical privacy. Random selection, some disclosure limits and noncriminal uses reduce burdens without answering whether suspicionless testing is justified. The findings concerning athletes leading drug groups establish serious concern, not that athletes as a whole caused the problem or that observation and suspicion-based testing cannot address sport-specific risks. He would permit ordinary disciplinary rules, individualized reasonable-suspicion testing, testing after a specific safety incident, fitness-directed examinations and materially narrower safety programs supported by evidence that individualized methods cannot address the risk. He does not authorize law-enforcement use or unrelated medical testing. His medical-information concern does not establish or adjudicate a separate surviving disclosure claim. His proposed necessity rule is noncontrolling; no other Justice joins this writing.

The Court’s merits review is complete. The case returns to the Ninth Circuit for proceedings consistent with the federal holding and independent reconsideration of the Oregon constitutional question. The Court does not unconditionally reinstate final dismissal, decide Oregon protection, reopen the statutory-school-authority ruling or prescribe a particular state-law remedy. ### Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon, No. 94-859

Merits decision, June 29, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, reviewing its facial invalidation of the habitat-modification clause of 50 C.F.R. §17.3, 17 F.3d 1463. The District Court had entered summary judgment for the Government. The question is the facial validity of that clause.

All nine Justices participate: Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

#### Judgment

The Court rejects the facial challenge to the habitat-modification clause of 50 C.F.R. §17.3 and reverses and remands, 7–2.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Statutory authority for habitat-caused injury

**Controlling proposition:** The ESA permits the Secretary to define harm to include significant habitat modification or degradation that actually kills or injures protected wildlife by significantly impairing essential behavioral patterns, including breeding, feeding, or sheltering. Habitat change without the required animal injury is outside this clause.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I; Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join this statutory proposition. Seven votes make it controlling independently of Part II's deference rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted definition permits this bounded construction. Congress included harm alongside hunting, shooting, wounding, and killing; harm therefore can reach injury caused through a change in the conditions on which animals depend. The regulation does not turn every land-use decision into a taking. It requires significant habitat modification and actual death or injury through significant impairment of essential behavior. The incidental-take permit provisions reinforce the distinction between causing injury and acting with injury as one's purpose. The separate federal-agency provisions protect against jeopardy and destruction of critical habitat under different conditions; their existence does not erase the individual-animal protection of section 9. MCI requires statutory authority before an agency acts, and that requirement is satisfied here by the enacted definition and the connected provisions. The contrary reading gives the definition's broad injury term too little independent work. The Court decides facial validity, leaving proof of particular violations for appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T: followed as requiring enacted authority; its rejection of removing a mandatory filing requirement does not foreclose this different statutory definition.
- PUD No. 1 v. Washington Department of Ecology: consistent with authority grounded in the particular statute, rather than environmental purpose alone.

##### 2. Reasonable delegated construction

**Controlling proposition:** Where this ESA definition leaves room concerning indirect injury, the Secretary's actual-injury construction is reasonable and receives Chevron deference. This rationale neither dispenses with statutory authority nor establishes that the regulation is the only permissible construction.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II; Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join. Six votes independently control this rationale; Stone does not join it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Secretary reasonably resolved the definition's application to indirect injury. Chevron requires the Court first to examine Congress's enactment; an agency cannot replace a clear command with its preferred policy. Here the word harm, its place in the definition, and the Act's incidental-take provisions permit the causal injury construction without resolving every possible application themselves. The Secretary's regulation narrows that construction by demanding actual death or injury and significant impairment of essential behavior. Ecological expertise helps explain how habitat changes can produce such injury, but expertise alone cannot create the power. MCI preserves that boundary and does not invalidate a reasonable exercise of authority found in another statute. Thomas Jefferson University concerns interpretation of an agency's own regulation and supplies no substitute for this statutory inquiry. Possible unlawful applications do not establish that the regulation has no lawful operation. The Court does not resolve a new criminal knowledge requirement or every disputed causal example in this facial challenge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council: applied to a reasonable construction within statutory ambiguity.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T: followed; deference does not authorize displacement of an unambiguous statutory command.
- Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala: distinguished; own-regulation interpretation does not establish the statutory delegation here.

##### 3. Actual injury and ordinary causal limits

**Controlling proposition:** The habitat clause requires actual death or injury proximately caused, with ordinary foreseeability, by significant habitat modification through significant impairment of essential behavior; bare but-for connection or speculative future harm is insufficient. An omission can be considered only where an independently existing legal duty supplies the obligation, and this decision creates no general duty to maintain habitat.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III; Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join the common rule. O'Connor's concrete reproduction example and Palila reservations are her concurrence, not additional majority applications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the regulation carries ordinary limits on responsibility for injury. Its words require an act that actually kills or injures wildlife, and the significant-behavior requirement connects the habitat change to that consequence. Ordinary proximate causation and foreseeability exclude attribution merely because some distant chain can be traced to a land-use decision. Those limits answer the argument that the rule necessarily imposes responsibility for every ecological effect. They also distinguish an existing legal duty whose breach causes injury from a new obligation to preserve all habitat. Actual injury need not be proved by producing a carcass; appropriate evidence can establish injury or support prevention of injury that will actually occur. Speculation about possible future population effects is insufficient. Gardner's benefit-statute causation analysis does not supply an ESA fault rule. The Court leaves concrete causal applications, the different civil and criminal enforcement requirements, and their questions of required knowledge or intent open. It decides neither a particular logging violation nor a general immunity for lawful economic activity.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brown v. Gardner: distinguished; its benefits provision does not decide ESA proximate causation or civil and criminal knowledge or intent requirements.
- Palila v. Hawaii Department of Land and Natural Resources: no categorical endorsement of every application; O'Connor separately addresses the injury requirement.

##### 4. Distinct statutory protections and conditional permissions

**Controlling proposition:** Facial validity preserves the separate triggers of endangered-species section 9, threatened-species rules under section 4(d), federal-agency section 7, and statutory exceptions and conditional permits under section 10. Neither a no-jeopardy opinion nor otherwise lawful land use alone exempts a taking.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part IV; Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join. The statutory qualifications below are integral limits, not new adjudications of applications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the injury prohibition operates within the permissions and protections Congress actually enacted. Section 9's endangered-wildlife prohibition and section 4(d)'s threatened-species regulation are distinct routes; the latter is not automatically the former. Section 7 separately governs federal agency action and can protect recovery habitat without proof of an individual taking. Section 10 permits incidental taking only through the applicable statutory process, including a conservation plan, public comment, required findings, and compliance with permit conditions. Ordinary economic use is not itself an exemption. The two section 7(o) protections likewise remain distinct: necessary activity associated with an exempted agency action differs from taking that complies with a written incidental-take statement. Tennessee Valley Authority recognizes the Act's operative commands, but does not eliminate Congress's later enacted qualifications. Reading these provisions together supports bounded protection against actual habitat-caused injury without converting the Act into unrestricted habitat management. No particular permit, agency consultation, endangered listing, or threatened-species special rule is adjudicated here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill: relied on for the Act's operative protection, subject to the distinct permissions and exemptions Congress enacted.
- PUD No. 1 v. Washington Department of Ecology: consistent with giving each environmental provision its own statutory trigger.

##### Integral statutory qualifications

Section 3(19) defines take through harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, collect, and attempts at that conduct. Under 50 C.F.R. §17.3, harm means an act actually killing or injuring wildlife; it may include significant habitat modification or degradation where it actually kills or injures by significantly impairing essential behavioral patterns, including breeding, feeding, or sheltering.

Section 9(a)(1) concerns listed endangered fish or wildlife and persons subject to United States jurisdiction, subject to sections 6(g)(2) and 10. Its taking prohibitions cover the United States and territorial sea and the high seas. Section 9(a)(1)(G) separately prohibits violation of regulations lawfully issued for endangered or threatened species. Section 4(d) requires necessary and advisable conservation regulations for threatened species and permits extension of section 9 prohibitions; 50 C.F.R. §17.31 supplies a separate regulatory route. For taking resident fish or wildlife, section 4(d) regulations apply in a State having a section 6(c) cooperative agreement only to the extent the State also adopts them. The Court does not construe the separate section 6(g)(2) exception or decide its application. The terms of that exception are not set out here; stating them requires the official section 6(g)(2) text, and applying them requires the applicable State-agreement record.

Section 7's no-jeopardy and critical-habitat obligations govern federal agency action; they do not substitute for section 9's actual-taking inquiry. Section 7(o)(1) protects activity necessary to an agency action exempted under section 7(h). Section 7(o)(2) protects a taking compliant with the terms and conditions of a written section 7(b)(4) incidental-take statement. A no-jeopardy opinion alone is not a blanket exemption.

Section 10(a)(1)(A) permits scientific or propagation-and-survival permissions. Section 10(a)(1)(B) permits taking incidental to, and not the purpose of, otherwise lawful activity. Section 10(a)(2)(A) requires a conservation plan stating likely impacts; minimization and mitigation steps and funding; alternatives considered and reasons rejected; and other necessary and appropriate measures. After public comment, section 10(a)(2)(B) requires findings of incidental taking, minimization and mitigation to the maximum extent practicable, adequate funding, no appreciable reduction in likelihood of survival and recovery in the wild, satisfaction of other required measures, and receipt of required assurances. Upon those findings the Secretary shall issue the permit with necessary or appropriate terms, conditions, and reporting requirements; section 10(a)(2)(C) requires revocation for noncompliance. These requirements do not grant blanket permission for unpermitted land use.

Section 10(j)'s experimental-population treatment is separate: released populations, including solely their offspring, qualify only while wholly geographically separate from nonexperimental populations of the same species. Release outside the current range requires a finding that conservation is furthered, identification by regulation before release, and a best-available-information determination of essentiality. Members are treated as threatened; a nonessential population is treated as proposed for listing for section 7 other than section 7(a)(1), except within the National Wildlife Refuge or National Park System. Critical habitat may not be designated for a nonessential population. Earlier authorized geographically separate releases are subject to the statutory regulatory determination. No experimental-population application is decided here.

Distinct section 11 civil and criminal channels retain their own conditions, including applicable notice and hearing and the civil good-faith protection for action believed necessary to protect a person from bodily harm. This facial decision establishes no new knowledge or intent test or universal knowledge-of-illegality requirement.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Congress's silence is not treated as automatic ratification. Section 7 and section 9 retain distinct force; the rule is not limited to designated critical habitat.

Significant habitat modification may constitute harm only through actual animal injury and ordinary proximate, foreseeable causation. Mere modification, potential harm, or ordinary land use alone establishes neither liability nor immunity. Threatened-species rules, agency obligations, statutory exceptions, and conditional permissions retain their distinct triggers. Specific causal applications and civil/criminal questions of required knowledge or intent remain open.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone concurs in the judgment and joins Parts I, III, and IV. He independently reads the enacted definition, the conservation structure, and the permission provisions to authorize significant habitat modification that actually injures protected animals by impairing essential behavior. Harm has work beyond direct hunting and capture. Ecological expertise and reasoned regulatory narrowing have substantial persuasive weight, but the statute supplies authority. He does not join Part II's Chevron ground. His position retains actual injury, proximate causation, and the statute's distinct exceptions and permit conditions; it creates no unrestricted habitat power. This separate statutory reading does not announce a universal abolition of deference.

Justice O'Connor concurs and joins all four Parts. She insists on actual injury to identifiable protected animals and a foreseeable, proximate consequence, rather than hypothetical descendants or speculative population effects. Making currently living animals unable to reproduce can injure them by impairing an essential physical function. That differs from descendants never coming into existence, reduced prospects for future population growth alone, or regeneration of habitat not presently sustaining animals. She requires neither a carcass nor a single proof method, and does not equate every decline in reproductive success with actual injury. Her reservations about Palila's habitat-regeneration application concern proof of actual injury and remoteness; erroneous particular applications do not invalidate every lawful operation of the rule. She adopts no new general omission duty or criminal knowledge rule.

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. They would affirm invalidation of the challenged habitat clause, retaining other ESA protections. They read the active verbs and their wildlife object to confine take to conduct directed against animals; the separate habitat provisions confirm that distinction. The incidental-take provisions can address unintended injury arising from directed activities without necessarily authorizing regulation of indirect land-use effects. They accept that the majority states causal limits, but regard those limits as insufficient to establish delegated authority for this habitat rule. Potential omission and population effects and criminal fair-notice concerns reinforce their objection. They announce neither a constitutional property immunity nor a universal knowledge-of-illegality rule, and do not invalidate every part of the harm definition.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The D.C. Circuit judgment invalidating the clause is reversed; the case returns for proceedings consistent with this decision. The Court decides facial regulatory validity, not a specific enforcement claim.

### Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, No. 94-329

Merits decision, June 29, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Fourth Circuit and requires removal of the religious-viewpoint exclusion from the generally available printing benefit.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Viewpoint exclusion and asserted Establishment justification | Reversed, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Viewpoint-neutral eligibility on otherwise lawful program terms; remand |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Religious viewpoint in the private publication program

**Controlling proposition:** The University may not exclude an otherwise eligible private student publication from its generally available printing benefit because its editorial viewpoint is religious. The rule applies to this privately edited, independently attributed publication program on its otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms; it does not require funding every religious activity or every subject.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas; five Justices adopt the program-specific viewpoint rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the University’s decision to support independent student expression does not make each publication its own speech. Perry permits definition of a limited forum and its subjects, but not discrimination against a religious perspective on otherwise included subjects. Lamb’s Chapel and Widmar supply religious-viewpoint equality in genuinely opened private-expression channels. Here the broad publication program leaves editing with the students, requires independence disclaimers and pays approved outside printing expenses. The denial treats primarily religious belief or manifestation as a forbidden activity while allowing comparable student perspectives. That operation excludes viewpoint rather than neutrally defining a government message. The Court does not erase the distinction between recognition, facilities, distribution and money, or equate every expenditure with a speech forum. Nor does it find the organization ineligible under a separate organizational rule. The religious-viewpoint exclusion must be removed; other lawful program terms remain. Actual payment amounts and properly available independent defenses remain subject to the federal viewpoint holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association: preserves limited-forum definition while forbidding viewpoint discrimination.
- Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches; Widmar v. Vincent: apply private religious-viewpoint equality without assuming that facility access decides every subsidy.
- Regan v. Taxation With Representation: distinguishes definition of a government financing program from discriminatory exclusion within this opened private-publication arrangement.

##### Permissible printing support on the actual program terms

**Controlling proposition:** Providing this neutral printing benefit to the otherwise eligible private religious publication does not itself violate the Establishment Clause. Its secular student-expression purpose, broad neutral availability, independent editorial attribution and controlled vendor payments matter together; neutrality alone is not a universal authorization of devotional financing or governmental sponsorship of worship.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas join this concrete program conclusion. Lemon remains governing law; no new universal framework is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the publication’s religious message remains private under the actual safeguards of this program. The fund serves broad student expression rather than government worship, and officials approve printing expenses without adopting the students’ editorial choices. Required disclaimers and independent organizations separate the private message from the University. Vendor payments provide the defined printing service rather than unrestricted grants to churches. Witters and Mueller supply the relevance of neutral benefits and private direction, without making beneficiary neutrality conclusive in every setting. Under the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry, this arrangement does not become governmental religious speech merely because one publication includes devotion and proselytizing. Kiryas Joel’s denominational allocation of governmental power is different; its neutrality limits and retention of Lemon remain. The contrary direct-financing objection gives too little weight to the concrete private-expression structure. Zobrest’s actual procedural remand supplies no aid-merits approval. Compelled-fee refund claims, unrestricted religious support and any particular monetary award remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lemon v. Kurtzman: retains the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry; no universal replacement is adopted.
- Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind; Mueller v. Allen: apply neutral-benefit and private-direction distinctions within the actual program, not neutrality alone.
- Board of Education of Kiryas Joel v. Grumet: distinguishes denominational governmental authority and retains religious-neutrality limits.
- Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills: preserves the actual antecedent-law remand; supplies no religious-aid merits holding here.
- Lee v. Weisman: preserves limits on governmental religious participation and coercion; this private printing benefit compels no student worship.
- Church of Scientology v. Clearwater: distinguishes RFRA burdens and exemptions from constitutional financing authority.

The denial concerned religious activity, defined by the Guidelines as primarily promoting or manifesting belief in or about deity or ultimate reality, rather than an established finding of religious-organization ineligibility. The organization rule separately concerns devotion as organizational purpose. Political restrictions separately exclude principal-purpose lobbying and candidate electioneering while permitting incidental or insubstantial advocacy, nonpartisan study/research and ideological views; those permissions are not exceptions to the religious-activity rule. Permitted ability-to-perform membership limits likewise remain distinct. Abandoned free-exercise/state individual-rights claims and unchallenged equal-protection intent findings are not reopened.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Lemon remains governing; Zobrest is procedural rather than an aid-merits holding. No denominational political power, compulsory worship or unrestricted religious subsidy is authorized.

Otherwise eligible private student publications receive viewpoint-neutral access to this defined printing benefit. Independent expression and the program’s safeguards do constitutional work; this is no rule that every public expenditure creates a forum or every formally neutral religious subsidy is lawful.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor concurs and joins the Court in full at its program-specific level. Organizational independence and disclaimers, approved vendor payments, a vigorous competing forum and the availability of a properly presented objection by students who oppose compelled fees provide safeguards; the last is a possible challenge, not an existing opt-out or refund award. Neutrality alone is insufficient. Thomas also concurs, joining the Court while explaining his own view that equal participation in broadly available civic benefits should not turn solely on a financial-versus-in-kind classification; that broader historical theory is not adopted by the Court. Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents. Mandatory fees pay for devotional production, and routing money to the printer does not turn that support into mere facility access. Ball and Nyquist distinguish direct support of religious activity; the beneficiary’s own editorial choice is not an independent third party’s selection under Witters and Mueller. The dissent does not exclude all religious viewpoints or invalidate every neutral private religious benefit.

#### Source Notes

Fourth Circuit, 18 F.3d 269; Student Activities Fund Guidelines, petition pp. 61a–67a; respondent’s opposition, p. 20 n. 11, and reproduced appendix passages, Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-329. The asserted Virginia funding defense is distinct from abandoned affirmative state-rights claims. Its applicability and available remedies remain unresolved; no payment amount is guaranteed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit is reversed and the case remanded. The University shall remove the religious-viewpoint exclusion and apply otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms. Any properly available independent Virginia funding defense must be examined consistently with the federal viewpoint holding; no applicability, validity or revival of forfeited defenses is declared. No full requested amount, damages, immunity, fee or compelled-fee refund determination is made.
