## Case-specific reasoning

This is the first major test of whether Lopez means federal criminal statutes now automatically fail whenever conduct happens locally — Stone says no. The particular vehicle is an interstate transportation instrumentality/thing in commerce, and the offense directly attacks possession and control of that vehicle; the statute requires a vehicle-specific interstate connection, which is constitutionally different from attaching a remote historical interstate fact to an unrelated local crime.

## Disposition, application and preserved limits

- **Stone's Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** AFFIRM the Fifth Circuit’s judgment.
- **Stone's Remedy and Remand Instruction:** The convictions and consecutive sentences remain in force. No separate remedial instruction is required.

#### Issue 1: Commerce Clause authority

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the constitutional question and affirms on a rationale narrower than the Fifth Circuit’s aggregate-effects theory.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning that carjacking is federal because violent crime affects travel, insurance, or the national economy cannot survive Lopez. Section 2119 is constitutional here for a different reason. Congress may directly protect an instrumentality of interstate commerce from an intrastate attack, and the enacted offense requires the Government in every prosecution to establish that the particular motor vehicle was transported, shipped, or received in interstate or foreign commerce. The prohibited conduct directly attacks possession and control of the vehicle itself. The instrumentality and the offense-specific interstate element therefore perform actual constitutional work rather than merely adding a remote interstate fact to ordinary local crime.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The Commerce Clause permits Congress to protect an instrumentality of interstate commerce against a direct intrastate forcible taking when the federal offense requires proof of the particular instrumentality’s interstate connection. Section 2119 is constitutional as applied to the forcible taking of the Buick proved here. The judgment is affirmed without adopting the Fifth Circuit’s aggregate-effects rationale.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** An automobile is an instrumentality of transportation capable of carrying persons and property through interstate channels. The defendants did not merely commit an offense in which a car appeared incidentally; they forcibly seized possession and control of the vehicle itself. The statute required proof of the Buick’s interstate history, and the record established that it was manufactured in Michigan and titled in Arkansas and Mississippi. That combination places this prosecution within Lopez’s instrumentality category without reclassifying violent crime as economic activity.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not hold that every local offense involving an object that once crossed a state line is federal, that every crime committed in or near a car is federal, or that insurance and productivity effects supply federal authority. Past interstate movement alone establishes no universal jurisdictional rule. The holding rests on the direct attack on a transportation instrumentality and the offense-specific interstate element enacted in § 2119.

#### Issue 2: Mens rea and notice

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the statutory and due-process arguments because they bear on the convictions.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 2119 requires knowing participation in the forcible taking while the firearm element and interstate-vehicle element are proved as enacted. It does not require knowledge of federal jurisdiction or an intent permanently to deprive. The conduct is inherently wrongful, and the statute gives fair notice that forcibly taking a covered motor vehicle with a firearm is criminal.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** The then-operative carjacking statute requires knowing participation in the taking by force, violence, or intimidation and proof of the enacted firearm and interstate-vehicle facts; it does not require knowledge that the vehicle traveled interstate or a separate intent permanently to deprive.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Ratzlaf’s willfulness construction is tied to separate statutory language absent here. Staples protects against criminalizing apparently innocent possession based on an unknown physical characteristic. Pointing a shotgun at occupants and ordering them from a vehicle is not innocent conduct whose criminality turns on an obscure characteristic. The jury evidence established knowing participation beyond a reasonable doubt.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not decide materially different statutory language not governing these offenses or eliminate mens rea from a fact Congress makes part of the wrongful conduct itself.

#### Issue 3: Cumulative punishment under § 924(c)

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the sentencing question because both terms were imposed in the same judgment.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Section 924(c) expressly provides punishment in addition to the punishment for the underlying crime of violence. That instruction controls even though the same firearm conduct also satisfied the then-operative § 2119 element.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Congress authorized a consecutive § 924(c) sentence in addition to the sentence for carjacking under § 2119, so the cumulative punishment imposed in this single prosecution does not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Blockburger is a rule for discerning legislative intent, not a constitutional bar against cumulative punishment Congress clearly authorized. The statute’s additional-and-consecutive language resolves the question.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not decide whether a different charging pair lacking clear cumulative authorization would survive.

#### Issue 1: Whether thermal imaging is a search

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the preserved Fourth Amendment question whether the warrantless thermal scan was a search. He does not reach the separate sufficiency of the warrant affidavit after removal of the thermal information. Pinson did not preserve that issue on appeal, and the Eighth Circuit expressly declined to determine it. The Court therefore has no basis in this case to direct a new probable-cause determination as though that question had been preserved.

- **Stone's Legal Position:** The home's Fourth Amendment protection does not end at the physical surface of its walls. Officers deliberately used sense-enhancing technology from outside the residence to obtain information about patterns of heat associated with activity occurring inside that ordinary public observation could not reveal. The acquisition of that otherwise unavailable information about the interior of the home is a search. The absence of physical entry does not remove the surveillance from the Fourth Amendment merely because the instrument detects heat rather than producing a conventional visual image.

- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Law-enforcement use of sense-enhancing technology from outside a home to obtain information about activity within the home that ordinary public observation could not otherwise obtain constitutes a Fourth Amendment search. The warrantless thermal scan of Pinson's residence therefore was a search.

- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** The home lies at the center of the security protected by the Fourth Amendment. The officers did not merely observe something exposed to ordinary public perception. They employed a technological instrument for the purpose of learning something about activity occurring behind the walls of a residence that their unaided senses could not discern. Constitutional protection cannot depend on whether information about the interior is extracted through reflected light, sound, heat, or some other physical signal. A rule treating every measurable emission escaping a home as abandoned to unrestricted governmental examination would permit increasingly capable instruments to obtain information about domestic activity without constitutional process while leaving the formal protection of the home intact only against physical entry. The Fourth Amendment protects the security of the interior, not merely the walls surrounding it.

  The Eighth Circuit affirmed on the premise that use of the thermal device was not a search. Stone would reject that premise. That resolves the preserved constitutional question. It does not authorize Stone to decide a different question concerning the legal sufficiency of the warrant after the thermal information is removed. The published appellate record states that Pinson did not challenge the affidavit's sufficiency on appeal and that the Eighth Circuit therefore made no determination of that issue. The proper course is accordingly to vacate the judgment resting on the erroneous no-search rule and return the case for whatever further proceedings remain lawfully available under ordinary preservation and remedial principles.

- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not hold that ordinary visual observation from a lawful vantage, smoke or other conditions perceptible to unaided members of the public, utility records obtained through lawful process, or every technological measurement made near private property constitutes a search. The holding concerns deliberate technological acquisition of otherwise unavailable information about activity inside a home. #### Issue 1: Meaning of “department or agency” and statutory stare decisis

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone finds no jurisdictional, preservation, or vehicle bar and reaches the question presented.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Statutory stare decisis carries unusual force because Congress can amend the law, and Bramblett may not be reconsidered merely because Stone would read § 1001 differently in the first instance. The required special justification exists here. Bramblett’s actual facts involved the House of Representatives, while its broad definition was later extended to adjudicating courts; that definition conflicts with Congress’s express statutory usage; and decades of judicial attempts to create a “judicial function” exception show that the construction cannot be administered without making exceptions the text does not contain. Concrete reliance on applying § 1001 to adversarial court filings is slight, while perjury, contempt, bankruptcy fraud, obstruction, and other enacted offenses remain available.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Bramblett is overruled to the extent it treats an Article III court acting in its adjudicative capacity as a “department” or “agency” under § 1001. Statements in the bankruptcy filings at issue therefore do not support these § 1001 convictions.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Patterson, Square D, and Illinois Brick establish that disagreement with a statutory precedent is insufficient. The combination here is unusual: the earlier case’s operative facts did not involve an adjudicating court; the broad construction is difficult to reconcile with the enacted definition; and lower courts have spent years carving a judicial-function exception because applying the construction literally would criminalize ordinary adversarial submissions in ways the scheme does not sensibly regulate. Those decisions do not displace Bramblett by attrition. They demonstrate the unworkability of retaining its broadest language. Correcting the statutory boundary is more candid and administrable than preserving Bramblett while continuing atextual exceptions.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not immunize falsehoods made to executive agencies merely because litigation is pending and does not affect statutes expressly covering court filings, sworn statements, bankruptcy fraud, obstruction, contempt, or perjury. Congress may expressly extend a false-statement offense to all three branches and define the procedural safeguards and exceptions appropriate to adversarial litigation.
