## 26. United States v. National Treasury Employees Union — No. 93-1170

Argument November 8, 1994; event February 22, 1995. The judgment reviewed is the all-Executive injunction affirmed at 990 F.2d 1271. The represented class consists of affected Executive employees below GS–16; Crane is a separately named GS–16 respondent. Not every plaintiff's expression is unrelated to employment. No certiorari reconsideration occurs.

### Exact rule and remedial premises retained

Section 501(b) prohibits receipt of an honorarium by a Member, officer, or employee. Section 505(2) excludes special Government employees as defined in 18 U.S.C. §202. Section 505(3) covers payment of money or anything of value for an appearance, speech, or article and includes a series only if the subject directly relates to official duties or the payment is because of governmental status. That nexus parenthesis does not already qualify every single payment. Actual and necessary travel expenses for the individual and one relative are excluded to the extent paid or reimbursed by another and otherwise deducted to the extent not paid or reimbursed; travel expenses mean transportation, lodging, and meals away from residence or principal employment.

Section 501(c)'s charitable route treats a qualifying payment on the individual's behalf as not received, with a **$2,000 limit on each payment** and no payment to an organization financially benefiting the individual, parent, sibling, spouse, child, or dependent relative. It does not remunerate the employee's expressive labor. Section 504(a) permits an Attorney General civil action and a civil penalty up to the greater of $10,000 or compensation received. Section 504(b) protects the recipient of a written opinion by a §503 entity and another covered individual with materially indistinguishable facts only for action **after issuance, in good faith, in accordance with the opinion's provisions and findings**, from subsection (a)'s sanction. Neither mechanism is a wholesale exemption. Section 501(a)'s different high-level outside-income ceiling is not adjudicated.

All eight associates refuse an avoidance construction that silently relocates the series nexus parenthesis into the entire definition. All preserve the special-employee exclusion, expense treatment, charity qualifications, and advisory-opinion conditions. None invalidates congressional/judicial coverage, decides a civil penalty or damages claim, or nullifies independent valid ethics regulation.

For the five broad-remedy associates, a prospective, comprehensive financial disincentive to protected employee/citizen expression and its audiences imposes a heavier justification burden than an isolated disciplinary application. Legislative predictive judgments deserve substantial consideration, but the record and law's coverage must connect the burden to actual governmental operation and the asserted harms. The record does not justify the sweep over unrelated expression. This is not a categorical ban on preventive ethics laws, a demand for completed corrupt exchanges, or automatic strict scrutiny of every compensation rule. The Government's genuine prevention and appearance interests and the contrary legislative-deference argument remain acknowledged.

O'Connor reaches the same invalidity for unrelated expression under Pickering because this restriction's actual breadth and intrusion outweigh its insufficiently substantiated employment justification. She does not join a rule that prior codification alone automatically increases the constitutional burden for every workplace policy. Scalia and Thomas conclude that the compensation-only restriction and its exceptions, Congress's preventive judgment, appearance concerns, and administration survive the employment balance. They acknowledge an expressive burden and do not characterize public employment as a waiver of all First Amendment rights.

## 27. Harris v. Alabama — No. 93-7659

Argument December 5, 1994; event February 22, 1995. All eight associates reach the admitted objection to the unspecified weight of advisory jury advice. They do not reopen guilt, unrelated trial-presence issues, or other unpresented claims.

### Law and actual findings

The jury recommended life without parole seven to five. The judge's order stated consideration of the recommendation and the evidence, identified one pecuniary-gain aggravator, the no-significant-criminal-history statutory mitigator, other mitigation, and Harris's planning, financing, and benefit. The wording issue concerning statutory mitigation was addressed in the lower court through the order and Rule 10(f) clarification. Independent appellate weighing, proportionality, and review for arbitrary influence were performed. These are reported record events and lower findings; neither constitutional adequacy nor a particular quantity of weight is a stipulated fact. No Justice may call the record wholly unexplained.

Section 13A-5-47(e) requires weighing the found aggravating and mitigating circumstances and consideration of the advisory recommendation unless waived under §13A-5-46(a) or (g); the recommendation is not binding. There was an actual unwaived recommendation here. The statutory command and the judge's actual performance are distinct.

Spaziano permits a judicial final sentencer. Actual Tuilaepa/Proctor rejects fixed numerical weights while preserving effective mitigation and review of independent selection errors. Actual Romano's controlling contextual rule concerning an irrelevant separate death decision does not make every jury recommendation constitutionally binding. Actual Espinosa's invalid-factor rule involves advice carrying legally required great weight; it does not already decide every Alabama jury-advice error. Mandatory consideration gives advice a consequential role, however, so the seven do not regard defects in advice as automatically irrelevant merely because final selection belongs to the judge. This record presents no identified invalid jury factor requiring a cure. The seven find no error, not harmless error.

## 28. Grubart; City of Chicago v. Great Lakes — Nos. 93-762, 93-1094

Argument October 12, 1994; event February 22, 1995. Stevens and Breyer do not participate. The admitted controversy concerns admiralty jurisdiction over the alleged vessel-based piling casualty and ensuing land flooding. The two dockets receive coordinated treatment.

### Final common scope

All six participating associates support affirmance of the appellate jurisdictional judgment and continuation of the limitation proceeding. The alleged negligent vessel operation on navigable water can support jurisdiction despite injury consummated on land. The Admiralty Extension Act's “caused by” boundary is ordinary proximate causation, not a new immediate-impact, fixed-distance, or fixed-time cutoff. The crane is an appurtenance of the actual transport-capable barge; the holding does not make everything floating a vessel. The alleged causation is not a final liability finding. No independent tort negligence, indemnity, contribution, corporate privity/knowledge, contract scope, cap amount, or damages determination is made.

Four associates—O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg—adopt the complete Sisson locality-and-connection route. The incident is damage by a vessel in navigable water to an underwater structure, a general type capable of disrupting maritime commerce. The activity is repair or maintenance work on a navigable waterway performed from a vessel, substantially related to traditional maritime activity. Actual closure corroborates the characterization but is not a universal element. At least one alleged tortfeasor's relevant maritime activity must be an alleged proximate cause of this incident; nonmaritime contributing conduct does not erase that relationship. This resolves Sisson's mixed-activity reservation without declaring that mere joinder of a vessel owner creates jurisdiction over unrelated land conduct. The four reject replacing Sisson with an open-ended multi-factor balancing inquiry.

Thomas and Scalia support the jurisdictional result on the simpler **vessel/locality rule**: a tort occurring on a vessel on navigable waters falls within admiralty, with the Extension Act reaching its land consequences. They do not require the additional potential-disruption and traditional-activity inquiries in this vessel setting. The aircraft exception associated with Executive Jet remains distinct. They do not join the complete Sisson analysis or pretend their alternative has become controlling merely because it reaches the same judgment.
