### Two final grounds, not an aggregated rationale

**G-A — permissible allocation under existing regulations; reversal.** Section 413.20(a)'s ordered provisions require adequate, standardized provider records and reassure providers that their accounting system need not be changed; they do not make every GAAP recognition convention binding on reimbursement. The final sentence treats the accounts as the starting point from which equitable and proper Medicare payment is determined. The structure separating records/reports from later cost and allocation rules supports that construction. Section 413.24 does not settle the dispute merely by requiring accrual accounting. Section 1395x(v)(1)(A) requires consideration of accepted accounting principles, not their unqualified adoption. The actual Administrator's allocation/matching rationale under the existing cost regulations reasonably addresses beneficiary utilization over the relevant periods. Applying that lawful construction through adjudication and explaining it in an interpretive rule does not require a separate legislative rule for every factual detail. The Manual lacks independent legislative force and cannot contradict an existing regulation; the holding is that this treatment does not do so.

G-A supporters reverse the Sixth Circuit's advance-refunding disposition and remand for proceedings consistent with sustaining the Secretary's timing determination. They preserve the distinct offset disposition and all stated allowance/calculation limits. No separate ground says an agency can evade a binding rule simply by calling its action adjudication. The strongest G-B argument—the mandatory accounting language and predictable regulatory method—is answered by the records/reimbursement distinction in the actual text and structure, not by the administrative inconvenience of notice and comment alone.

**G-B — regulatory GAAP default requiring lawful amendment; affirmance.** Section 413.20(a), read as a whole with the regulatory reimbursement process, selects the accepted accounting method as the default for determining costs unless a valid specific regulation provides otherwise. Its mandatory terms and link between the provider's ordinary accounts and costs payable cannot be reduced to a promise about bookkeeping alone. The statutory command to determine costs under regulations supports an actual background method, while still permitting case-by-case application and genuine interpretive guidance. The Secretary may select a reasonable amortization method through lawful regulation, and the statute does not itself require GAAP forever; the defect is using §233 to contradict the governing default without required procedure. Section 413.24 alone is not the basis of this conclusion. An actual adjudication rationale is insufficient if it conflicts with the operative regulation, however rational the alternative accounting policy might be.

G-B supporters affirm the Sixth Circuit's advance-refunding judgment and its bounded immediate-recognition remand, preserving ordinary allowance and calculation limits and leaving the interest offset untouched. They do not require a separate regulation for every conceivable reimbursement detail or treat every interpretive change as unlawful. The strongest G-A argument—legitimate allocation and an elaborate regulatory system—is answered by distinguishing permissible gap filling from alteration of the specific default that these Justices find already adopted.

**Current-law fit:** The same qualified own-regulation rule from simulated Thomas Jefferson University governs both grounds; the dispute is whether this interpretation is inconsistent with the regulation. Good Samaritan permits considered interpretive change within lawful methods, not alteration contrary to text. MCI preserves the boundary of actual authority, not an automatic result for the hospital.
