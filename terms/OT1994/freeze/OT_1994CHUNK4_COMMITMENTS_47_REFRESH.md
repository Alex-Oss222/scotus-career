## 47. Celotex Corp. v. Edwards — April 19, 1995

### New operative source and independent judgment

**Judgment commitment on the supplied sources:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer reverse the Fifth Circuit judgment authorizing enforcement of Northbrook's supersedeas bond while the applicable bankruptcy injunction remains effective, and remand. They require a challenge through the issuing court and authorized reviewing courts on this record; they do not affirm the injunction's ultimate merits or award the bond proceeds to the estate. Stevens and Ginsburg's earlier contrary judgment depended on a narrow reading of the October order. The newly produced operative June order removes that factual and textual predicate.

The chronology is decisive to the order-scope question. The May 3, 1991 Rule 65.1 motion preceded the June 13, 1991 omnibus order, but the Texas enforcement authorization was not entered until May 27, 1992. The June order continued the October §105 stay and expressly barred proceeding against a supersedeas bond without first obtaining relief whether the appellate process ended after court-authorized completion or had already ended when the bankruptcy petition was filed. Thus the independent matured surety obligation and completed tort appeal do not place this proposed enforcement outside the applicable language. No inference about time of day on October 12, 1990 is needed. The October-only construction is no longer an adequate ground for affirmance.

### Component A — applicable injunction, automatic stay, and underlying debt

**All eight join:** The operative §105 injunction reaches the proposed bond enforcement under the June 13 coverage and continuation command. Rule 65.1 supplies an ordinary mechanism to enforce a surety's undertaking by motion and the surety's submission to the bond court; it does not by itself dissolve another court's applicable injunction. The decision does not hold that §362 automatically stays every matured surety obligation or that the bond itself remains estate property after affirmance. Northbrook's obligation remains distinct from Celotex's tort debt, and neither the amount nor finality of the tort judgment is altered.

The strongest affirmance path on scope is that the original October language addressed proceedings involving the debtor, while the Texas motion addresses only Northbrook's independent undertaking. That is a substantial explanation of the Fifth Circuit's reading, not proof of admitted deliberate defiance. But it cannot account for the intervening, already operative June order's express bond-collection command. Giving the June command effect does not retroactively change the words of October or invent notice; it addresses the applicable order in existence when enforcement was authorized. No adjudication of contempt, culpability, or sanctions follows.

### Component B — related jurisdiction, §157, and remedial authority

**All eight join at this bounded level:** Section 1334(b) extends district-court jurisdiction to proceedings arising under title 11, arising in a title 11 case, or related to the case. It is nonexclusive and is not confined to actions against property of the estate. The asserted connections between the bonds, estate collateral, insurance arrangements, and this reorganization present a substantial jurisdictional basis that defeats treating the injunction as transparently unrelated to any bankruptcy competence. That conclusion does not adopt every asserted causal effect, define the outer boundary of related jurisdiction, or make administrative convenience a jurisdictional grant. Section 105 supplies no independent subject-matter jurisdiction.

The lower courts had different factual presentations. The bankruptcy court recorded the scale of the bonded litigation and findings about estate collateral. The Fifth Circuit reported that the particular surety agreement and concrete effect were inadequately shown before it. The latter limitation is preserved; no specific dollar depletion, unavoidable insurance offset, or established avoidance recovery on the Edwards bond is inferred. Nevertheless the question concerns control of a claimed reorganization effect under an existing order, rather than an injunction over a controversy shown to have no arguable relation to the case. Those merits and evidentiary objections belong in the challenge to that order through the statutory machinery.

Section 157 is a distinct condition, not a footnote to relatedness. Under §157(a) matters may be referred to bankruptcy judges. A referred core proceeding under §157(b)(1) may be heard and determined, with appropriate orders subject to §158 review. The issuing court classified its bond determination as core under §157(b)(2)(A), (G), and (O); this decision reports that classification without adopting it as an independently settled merits holding. Section 157(b)(3) directs the bankruptcy judge to decide core status and bars a noncore classification solely because state law affects resolution. That does not give a bankruptcy judge unreviewable power to declare a matter core.

For a noncore but related proceeding, §157(c)(1) ordinarily calls for proposed findings and conclusions and a final district-court order after consideration and de novo review of matters timely and specifically objected to. Under §157(c)(2), all parties' consent permits a district-court referral for the bankruptcy judge to hear, determine, and enter orders subject to §158 review. This record establishes no consent finding either way, so neither consent nor a proven refusal is supplied. A relatedness finding alone does not satisfy these alternatives or validate every final injunction. Conversely, an objection under §157 does not automatically demonstrate that the district court lacks all subject-matter jurisdiction under §1334(b).

The strongest affirmance path is that an Article I judge cannot insulate an unauthorized final adjudication merely by labeling it core and pointing to related district-court jurisdiction. That objection is legally serious. The narrower reversal preserves it for a direct challenge, rather than deciding core classification, consent, or constitutional limits without the necessary record. The order concerns interim preservation and claimed reorganization effects of already entered judgments and bonds; no new personal-injury trial or liquidation of an unliquidated tort claim is authorized. The distinct §§157(b)(2)(B) and (b)(5) restrictions remain intact. No inapplicable 1994 §157(e) jury provision is used in this 1990 bankruptcy.

### Component C — collateral obedience and the route to relief

**All eight join at the validated prior-authority scope:** An applicable injunction addressing a substantial and nonfrivolous jurisdictional question must be challenged through available orderly relief and review, rather than displaced by enforcement proceedings in a different court because the affected party considers it erroneous. It distinguishes an order's operative force pending proper challenge from an adjudication that the order is lawful in every respect. This record supplies an express motion route and does not establish an absence of any substantial jurisdictional claim or frustration of orderly relief that would justify the collateral disposition entered below.

United Mine Workers, 330 U.S. 289–295, supports preserving the status quo while a substantial jurisdictional dispute is resolved; it expressly does not authorize such protection on a frivolous and insubstantial jurisdictional claim. Its distinction between criminal contempt and civil remedial relief also remains: civil relief falls with an erroneously issued injunction, and still more clearly with an order issued without jurisdiction. This case supplies neither a contempt judgment nor a merits reversal of the bankruptcy injunction followed by an attempt to retain civil relief under it. The holding does not convert the rule governing criminal punishment into a universal rule validating civil remedies.

Walker, 388 U.S. 315, identifies its own order as neither transparently invalid nor supported only by a frivolous pretense to validity; that qualification is a reservation, not a fully worked-out universal exception. Its discussion at 318–319 also reserves a different constitutional posture where a timely challenge encounters delay or frustration. This decision does not erase those limits, equate a routine bankruptcy collection dispute with Walker's First Amendment setting, or import that case's no-attempt and deliberate-defiance facts into the Edwards litigation. Here the operative June order's express relief procedure, continuation burden, and statutory review routes provide the supported basis for orderly challenge. No source establishes that these creditors made a timely challenge that was frustrated; none is invented.

The application is an extension of orderly-review reasoning to the present civil proceeding, not an assertion that the contempt cases already decided §§157 or 1334. The bond court's ordinary competence to enforce Northbrook's undertaking does not itself confer appellate authority to set aside an applicable bankruptcy restraint, and leaving the substantial objections for the issuing forum preserves their adjudication rather than extinguishing them. No new surprise forfeiture is announced, no finding of knowing violation substitutes for evidence, and no sanctions are imposed. Walker's treatment of In re Green and its federal-preemption setting is not turned into a general bar to every jurisdictional objection. The decree's reference to all entities supplies no holding that a court can bind strangers otherwise beyond its lawful injunction authority.

The June order expressly invites a successful judgment creditor to seek relief and assigns the debtor the burden of establishing that the §105 stay should continue. The creditors may raise coverage, subject-matter jurisdiction, core/noncore procedure, consent where relevant, statutory injunction authority, the record supporting continued restraint, and duration. The decision neither predetermines those issues nor shifts that stated continuation burden to the creditors. It also does not require completion of every possible appeal before the issuing court may modify its own order.

Under the version of §158(a) applicable to this case, direct appeal from a final bankruptcy order, or with leave from an interlocutory order, proceeds to the district court for the district where the bankruptcy judge serves: here, the Middle District of Florida. Final appellate decisions satisfying §158(d) may then be reviewed by the Eleventh Circuit. No unconditional immediate circuit appeal follows from every bankruptcy ruling. No applicable bankruptcy appellate panel is established, and no panel consent is assumed. Germain separately preserves an otherwise available §1292 route from a district court's bankruptcy appellate order; §158(d) is not an exclusive bar to that route. A §1292(b) appeal still requires the district judge's written statutory findings, timely application, and circuit permission; the application itself supplies no stay. These are possible lawful channels, not findings that their predicates have already been met in this case.

Section 157(d)'s withdrawal of referral is separate: discretionary withdrawal requires cause, and mandatory withdrawal on a timely motion requires the specified consideration of title 11 and other federal laws regulating organizations or activities affecting interstate commerce. No such motion or qualifying finding is invented here. The ordinary direct-review provisions and withdrawal procedure are not interchangeable. The pre-October 22, 1994 statutory version applies to this bankruptcy; exclusive property jurisdiction was then §1334(d), not the later redesignated (e). The current result relies on §1334(b), not exclusive ownership of every bond. The 1994 amendments to review and panel provisions do not retroactively change this case's route.

Swint reinforces the need to identify the actual appellate question and its authorized route, but is not itself a bankruptcy injunction decision. Germain preserves overlapping statutory channels at their true scope. Bancorp distinguishes judicial power over a properly presented judgment from deciding unpresented underlying bankruptcy merits. The existing Morgan Stanley finality rule does not convert this temporary collection restraint into a legislative reopening of the completed tort merits; no damages judgment is vacated here. No unissued Plaut disposition supplies authority.

### Remedy and source-change boundary

Reverse the Fifth Circuit judgment and remand so enforcement is not authorized contrary to the operative §105 injunction while it remains effective. The creditors must seek relief from that restraint through the issuing proceeding and an authorized review route; their independent bond entitlement and challenges to the restraint are not extinguished. No new contempt order, permanent bond release, estate ownership declaration, refund, damages amount, avoidance recovery, or later procedural event is invented. The decision does not dissolve the injunction itself or direct that it continue forever.
