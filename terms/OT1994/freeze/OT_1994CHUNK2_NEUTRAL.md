## 13. Tome v. United States — No. 93-6892

### Event, record, and posture

The admitted question is whether original Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(B) permits substantive admission of a prior consistent statement made after the alleged motive to fabricate arose. The judgment reviewed is United States v. Tome, 3 F.3d 342 (10th Cir. August 26, 1993), No. 92-2104, affirming an aggravated-sexual-abuse conviction under 18 U.S.C. §§1153, 2241(c), and then-2245(2)(A)–(B). The District of New Mexico case was CR-91-314-SC. The defendant seeks relief from that affirmance; the Government seeks to preserve it.

The appellate opinion reproduced in the predecision petition supplies the following facts. Tome and Beverly Padilla divorced in September 1988. The Navajo Tribal Court awarded joint custody of their daughter A.T., with primary physical custody to Tome. A.T. was born June 18, 1985. Padilla unsuccessfully sought primary custody in August 1989 but received custody for summer 1990, when she lived in Colorado. Padilla and A.T. did not attend an August 24, 1990 follow-up custody hearing. On August 27 Padilla reported alleged abuse to Colorado authorities. The charged conduct was alleged to have occurred about June 18, 1989, when A.T. was four. These are allegations and procedural facts, not a new independent finding of the abuse.

A.T. testified at the February 1992 trial at age six and a half. The court examined her understanding of truth and falsehood. Direct examination used short answers, leading questions, and dolls. Cross-examination extended over two days. A.T. answered many questions, sometimes failed to respond audibly, and sometimes said she did not remember. Defense questions emphasized her preference for living with her mother in Colorado. The Tenth Circuit found a sufficient opportunity to cross-examine and an implied charge that she fabricated abuse to avoid returning to her father. Those are the lower court's conclusions, not findings that every later Court must adopt.

Six witnesses recounted prior statements: babysitter Lisa Rocha, mother Beverly Padilla, caseworker Kae Ecklebarger, and pediatricians Karen Kuper, Laura Reich, and Jean Spiegel. Rocha described an initial August 22, 1990 statement, followed by a more detailed August 27 account; Padilla overheard the latter. Ecklebarger interviewed A.T. August 29. Kuper examined her in September and October 1990; Reich on September 21, 1990; Spiegel on September 3, 1991. The pediatricians described physical findings they considered consistent with penetration. That evidence is separate from the admissibility of each child's statement. The claimed motive to obtain maternal custody was alleged to predate the challenged statements. Do not turn the allegation of motive into a finding of fabrication, coaching, or guilt.

The jury convicted on March 3, 1992. The Tenth Circuit rejected a categorical premotive condition. It required probative force beyond mere repetition, assessed under Rules 402 and 403, and considered the alleged motive weak and the first disclosure spontaneous. It also rejected the cross-examination, Confrontation Clause, leading-question, cumulative-error, and fundamental-fairness challenges. The scope admitted here centers on the Rule's temporal condition; it does not automatically reopen every rejected appellate argument.

### Exact operative distinctions and alternative grounds

The relevant original Rule 801(d)(1) requires that the declarant testify at the trial or hearing and be subject to cross-examination concerning the statement. Subparagraph (B) requires that the statement be consistent with the declarant's testimony and offered to rebut an express or implied charge against the declarant of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive. The question is what that rebuttal condition entails; a post-1995 amendment supplies no answer.

The Government offered the physicians' testimony alternatively under Rule 803(4), covering statements made for medical diagnosis or treatment describing medical history, symptoms, pain or sensations, or the inception or general character of their cause or external source insofar as reasonably pertinent to diagnosis or treatment. It offered Rocha's, Padilla's, and Ecklebarger's testimony under then-Rule 803(24). The residual-exception synopsis is supplied only to identify the unadjudicated alternative ground, not to decide its construction or satisfaction. Then-Rule 803(24) addresses a statement not specifically covered by the foregoing exceptions but having equivalent circumstantial guarantees of trustworthiness; it requires that the statement be offered as evidence of a material fact, be more probative on the offered point than other evidence the proponent can procure through reasonable efforts, and serve the Rules' general purposes and the interests of justice. The proponent must make known to the adverse party, sufficiently in advance of trial or hearing to give a fair opportunity to prepare, the intention to offer the statement and its particulars, including the declarant's name and address. Whether the statements qualify and whether those conditions were met remain wholly undecided. The Tenth Circuit expressly did **not** decide either exception. Its use of 801(d)(1)(B) for Ecklebarger was an appellate alternative evidentiary ground even though the Government had not expressly offered her testimony under that subparagraph at trial.

The Tenth Circuit's footnote 6 expressly reserved statements offered solely to rehabilitate live testimony because the Government sought their full substantive effect here. Its footnote 9 did not decide a proved parent-coaching case: Tome had made stray assertions but supplied no supporting record evidence or relied-on coaching theory. Its footnote 10 said the aggregate-prejudice objection to admission of all the statements under Rule 403 had not been made and found no plain error. These distinctions prevent conversion of one preserved Rule-construction question into a categorical exclusion of every statement, a finding of coaching, or a preserved cumulative-prejudice ruling.

### Entering law and supported paths

*United States v. Salerno* (June 19, 1992), under **Former testimony, similar motive and the Government's evidentiary objection**, present operation effective that date, requires satisfaction of the chosen hearsay exception's conditions. It does not decide what 801(d)(1)(B)'s rebuttal language means. *Daubert* (June 28, 1993), under **Scientific expert evidence under original Rules 104(a) and 702**, present operation effective that date, displaces incompatible common-law prerequisites but retains compatible common-law guidance. Its Rule 702 factors do not become factors for prior consistent statements. *Williamson* (June 27, 1994), under **Assertion-specific declarations against penal interest**, present operation effective that date, makes contextual assessment distinct from wholesale admissibility and preserves separate admissibility and relief inquiries. Its Rule 804(b)(3) construction is an analogy, not an answer about timing under 801. The entering slice gives each exact coalition and current force; no independent “Current wording authority” different from those entries is supplied for these rules.

Path A is a premotive construction: rebutting the asserted recent fabrication or improper motive means showing consistency before that asserted cause existed. The common-law distinction between substantive admissibility and limited rehabilitation, and the words “to rebut,” support it. The principal counterargument is the absence of an express temporal phrase, the Rules' displacement of incompatible common law, and situations in which circumstances may rebut an accusation even though some motive already existed. A narrow judgment on this path can set aside the 801 ruling and remand the unaddressed alternative exceptions and appropriate error consequences. It need not decide rehabilitation-only use, guilt, or a new trial immediately.

Path B is case-specific relevance without a categorical premotive limit: the text specifies a rebuttal purpose but leaves timing as an important consideration under ordinary relevance rules. The Tenth Circuit, *United States v. Montague*, 958 F.2d 1094 (D.C. Cir. 1992), and *United States v. Miller*, 874 F.2d 1255 (9th Cir. 1989), support that path. Its principal counterargument is that repetition while the same alleged motive persists may not rebut that motive at all, and general relevance cannot replace a specific nonhearsay condition. Even on this path the judge must identify probative force apart from repetition; consistency alone is insufficient.

The date-eligible circuit counterposition includes *United States v. Quinto*, 582 F.2d 224, 233–234 (2d Cir. 1978), and *United States v. Guevara*, 598 F.2d 1094, 1100 (7th Cir. 1979), discussed in the full lower opinion. A potentially narrower formulation would distinguish the particular alleged motive from an abstract motive every witness might have; it cannot be substituted for an unsupported new finding about when A.T.'s actual motive began.

On remand, *Williamson* preserves the Government's burden under Rule 52(a) and *Kotteakos* for any preserved nonconstitutional evidentiary error: whole-record fair assurance that the error did not substantially sway the judgment; grave doubt prevents a harmless finding. *Chapman* applies only if a constitutional trial error is actually reached and found. The already-presented alternative exceptions can be remanded without deciding their satisfaction. The appellate conclusion of no error meant it did not undertake harmless-error analysis for an erroneous 801 admission.

## 14. Young v. Northern Illinois Conference of United Methodist Church — assigned No. 93-1917

### Event, neutral record, and questions

The admitted questions concern whether the First Amendment bars this probationary minister's Title VII race, sex, and retaliation claims challenging advancement and termination, and whether such a bar is jurisdictional or substantive. The lower-court docket is **93-2157**, not the assigned Supreme Court docket. The judgment reviewed is 21 F.3d 184 (7th Cir. April 7, 1994), argued there January 4, 1994, affirming dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

Darreyl Young, a Black woman, served for several years as a probationary minister of the United Methodist Church. She applied to become a Clergy Member in Full Connection or Elder. A review panel denied advancement and terminated her employment March 4, 1992. She alleged race discrimination, sex discrimination, and retaliation for opposing discriminatory practices, including a departure from procedures the church previously followed. The EEOC found no probable cause and issued a right-to-sue letter. She sought reinstatement as a probationary minister, back pay and benefits, reconsideration of her application, compensatory and punitive damages, fees, and costs. These allegations are not findings that discrimination occurred. Her ministerial status is part of the reported case; the source does not establish facts about every category of church employee.

The defendants sought dismissal because civil review would interfere with internal ecclesiastical government and discipline. The district court found it necessary to reach that constitutional question and treated the limit as jurisdictional. The Seventh Circuit affirmed. It relied on *Milivojevich* and the church-minister distinction in *McClure v. Salvation Army*, 460 F.2d 553 (5th Cir. 1972), *EEOC v. Mississippi College*, 626 F.2d 477 (5th Cir. 1980), *Rayburn v. General Conference of Seventh-Day Adventists*, 772 F.2d 1164 (4th Cir. 1985), and *Scharon v. St. Luke's Episcopal Presbyterian Hospital*, 929 F.2d 360 (8th Cir. 1991). It viewed selection and retention of clergy as protected acts, not only religiously motivated acts, and rejected a civil inquiry into whether the church obeyed its internal process. It found **no allegation of fraud or collusion**. Its explanation of the highest church judicatory means the highest body to which this dispute had actually been carried, not necessarily the denomination's theoretically highest institution.

Young argued that ordinary discrimination questions could be resolved without deciding religious doctrine and that limited review of procedure would not require a deep inquiry into religious polity. Her reliance on discrimination's importance does not amount to a concession that the defendant's actual reasons were religious. Nor does her claim to neutral adjudication establish that all requested remedies can be administered without controlling church office.

### Entering law and independent questions

Title VII's prohibition on race and sex discrimination and its retaliation protection must be kept distinct from its religious-employer exemption. Section 702 permits a qualifying religious corporation, association, educational institution, or society to employ individuals of a particular religion to perform work connected with its activities. That express religious-preference exemption does not itself say that race, sex, or retaliation claims are exempt. The Religion Clauses may impose an independent constitutional limit; the construction and constitutional questions must not be collapsed.

*Kedroff v. St. Nicholas Cathedral*, 344 U.S. 94, 116 (1952), protects church government and the selection of ecclesiastical authorities from state control. *Serbian Eastern Orthodox Diocese v. Milivojevich*, 426 U.S. 696, 708–725 (1976), prohibits reexamining hierarchical ecclesiastical decisions under an arbitrariness test that asks whether internal laws were followed. It leaves the narrow fraud-or-collusion possibility unresolved rather than adopting a general exception. *Jones v. Wolf*, 443 U.S. 595 (1979), permits genuinely neutral principles for property questions but does not authorize resolution of doctrinal issues embedded in those questions. *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872 (1990), is a distinct neutral-general-law rule; whether it reaches government selection or removal of clergy is a question requiring analysis, not an automatic consequence of the employment statute's generality.

*Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 681–683 (1946), separates a substantial federal claim's jurisdictional basis from failure to state a claim on which relief is available. Its stated exceptions concern claims immaterial and made solely to obtain jurisdiction, or wholly insubstantial and frivolous claims. Title VII also has its own jurisdictional provision, §706(f)(3), 42 U.S.C. §2000e-5(f)(3), alongside §1331. The inquiry whether this constitutional limit removes adjudicatory power, precludes particular relief, or supplies a defense cannot be resolved merely by repeating the lower court's word “jurisdiction.” Rule 12(b)(1) and Rule 12(b)(6) have different consequences. A decision resting on the actual claim can leave the procedural treatment of disputed ministerial status or unrelated claims for another case.

The entering slice supplies *Lukumi*, under **Religious targeting, comparable secular conduct and strict scrutiny**, present operation effective June 11, 1993; it preserves Smith's neutral-law rule without deciding ministerial employment. *Kiryas Joel*, June 27, 1994, prohibits the specified denominational allocation of civil power and anomalous particular benefit; it distinguishes permission to accommodate religion from governmental sponsorship. *Lamb's Chapel* addresses equal access to an admitted forum, not clergy employment. None resolves Young by itself.

The already-effective *Clearwater* public supplement requires RFRA's claimant-specific substantial-burden and justification inquiry for future governmental enforcement of an old municipal ordinance. It neither adjudicates RFRA's application to this private Title VII suit nor eliminates an independently supported Religion Clause ground. Whether RFRA has been invoked, preserved, and applies in this procedural setting is not established by the lower opinion. Do not silently add a RFRA holding, invent waiver, or assume the later statutory analysis would decide this case. A constitutional disposition independently supported by the admitted questions remains available.

### Paths, counters, and remedies

1. **Church-autonomy bar to this ministerial employment challenge.** Appointment, advancement, and retention of clergy may lie within protected church government, including where the proposed civil inquiry asks whether the church obeyed its own procedures. Reinstatement and compelled reconsideration directly affect ecclesiastical office; damages based on the same selection decision can also pressure that decision. The strongest counter is that enforcing secular race, sex, and retaliation norms need not adjudicate doctrine, that Congress's express exemption is narrower, and that neutral property adjudication demonstrates the absence of blanket institutional immunity. This path must identify why the actual claim and relief concern clergy selection and cannot be turned into immunity from every contract, tort, property, or lay-employment claim.
2. **Permit a bounded secular discrimination inquiry.** Treat ministerial status as insufficient by itself if liability can be resolved through neutral employment facts without deciding faith, discipline, or polity, reserving coercive remedies that would appoint a minister. The strongest counter is that determining pretext and examining the church's departure from its internal process is itself the prohibited intrusion here, and shifting from reinstatement to damages does not necessarily avoid it. No record finding establishes an independent secular claim detached from the challenged ministerial decision.
3. **Jurisdiction and merits remain separable on either path.** One lawful alternative would preserve subject-matter jurisdiction under Bell but recognize a substantive defense on the face of this reported ministerial claim; another would retain the lower court's jurisdictional treatment if church-autonomy authority supports that characterization. The counter to the first is that ecclesiastical nonreviewability might be understood as an institutional limit on civil adjudication; the counter to the second is that determining applicability of federal law and constitutional defenses is ordinarily an exercise of jurisdiction. Any correction to the form of dismissal should specify whether the judgment is modified or vacated and remanded, and whether further pleadings or findings are actually needed, without granting employment or monetary relief merely from correcting the jurisdictional label.

## 15. Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer — No. 92-2038

### Record, lower dispositions, and admitted scope

The admitted scope concerns former §2543's saved-seed exemption and the consequences for the separately pleaded §2541(6) notice claim. Review is of 982 F.2d 486 (Fed. Cir. December 21, 1992), No. 92-1048; rehearing denied March 25, 1993, 989 F.2d 478. The primary source is the predecision petition's complete lower-opinion appendices, not a retrospective account of this Court's decision.

The Winterboers operated a Clay County, Iowa farm, growing corn and soybeans, selling crops for food/feed and protected soybean seed to other farmers for replanting. Asgrow held certificates on two varieties identified in the appellate opinion as A1937 and A2243. The district opinion uses a different ordering of digits for one variety; no distinct-variety dispute is established, and the construction issue does not require silently resolving that transcription discrepancy. For summary judgment the defendants did not dispute Asgrow's protection or their sale of progeny. They claimed approximately 80% of their aggregate crops were sold for nonreproductive purposes and claimed state-law compliance; Asgrow disputed the latter. Those were party assertions, not findings resolving the state-law condition.

The district court held the exemption limited saving and sale to the amount reasonably necessary for the grower's intended next planting, not mechanically to the prior year's acreage; its illustration included reasonably expected acreage expansion. It granted Asgrow summary judgment September 30, 1991 and enjoined sales except qualifying saved seed. It reserved damages, requested briefing on §2567's notice-related damages limits, and expressly declined to decide labeling liability. Its November 14 clarification preserved the distinction between state labeling and the federal notice provision. The district court described the sales as approximately 10,000 bushels, greatly exceeding own-planting needs. Newman's rehearing dissent described 265 acres and 10,529 bushels, enough for roughly 10,000 acres, as undisputed. Quantity evidences the dispute; it is not a legislative quota.

The Federal Circuit reversed, vacated the injunction as a consequence of reversal, and remanded for a full record on exemption eligibility. It rejected the ensuing-crop limit. It interpreted “primary farming occupation” **crop by protected crop**, allowing less than half a particular protected variety's crop to be sold for planting if the other conditions were met. It construed “marketing” in §2541(3) as extensive or coordinated activity such as advertising, sales representatives, or extended merchandising, distinguishing it from qualifying direct farmer sales. It also concluded that an actually exempt §2543 sale is exempt from §2541(6)'s notice requirement, but made no ultimate finding that these sales qualified. Judge Lourie agreed only with rejection of the ensuing-crop limit and the result; he viewed the rest as beyond the briefed issues. Judge Newman dissented from rehearing denial, arguing that production intended for reproductive sale falls within §2541(3), that “saved” has independent force, and that “marketing” bears its ordinary selling meaning. Rader defended the distinctions and syntax. An April 12, 1993 appellate order stayed the mandate pending the petition and final Court disposition; do not infer a new Supreme Court interim order.

### Applicable former statutory text

The relevant §2541 opening reaches unauthorized acts in the United States or regulated/affected commerce, before expiration of protection but after certificate issuance or distribution with §2567 notice, except as otherwise provided in the subchapter. Its relevant clauses are:

> (1) sell the novel variety, or offer it or expose it for sale, deliver it, ship it, consign it, exchange it, or solicit an offer to buy it, or any other transfer of title or possession of it;
>
> (3) sexually multiply the novel variety as a step in marketing (for growing purposes) the variety; or
>
> (4) use the novel variety in producing (as distinguished from developing) a hybrid or different variety therefrom; or
>
> (6) dispense the novel variety to another, in a form which can be propagated, without notice as to being a protected variety under which it was received; or

Former §2543, as reproduced in petition Appendix E, reads:

> Except to the extent that such action may constitute an infringement under subsections (3) and (4) of section 2541 of this title, it shall not infringe any right hereunder for a person to save seed produced by him from seed obtained, or descended from seed obtained, by authority of the owner of the variety for seeding purposes and use such saved seed in the production of a crop for use on his farm, or for sale as provided in this section: Provided, That without regard to the provisions of section 2541(3) of this title it shall not infringe any right hereunder for a person, whose primary farming occupation is the growing of crops for sale for other than reproductive purposes, to sell such saved seed to other persons so engaged, for reproductive purposes, provided such sale is in compliance with such State laws governing the sale of seed as may be applicable. A bona fide sale for other than reproductive purposes, made in channels usual for such other purposes, of seed produced on a farm either from seed obtained by authority of the owner for seeding purposes or from seed produced by descent on such farm from seed obtained by authority of the owner for seeding purposes shall not constitute an infringement. A purchaser who diverts seed from such channels to seeding purposes shall be deemed to have notice under section 2567 of this title that his actions constitute an infringement.

The opening reservation, the proviso's “without regard” language, “such saved seed,” authorized-seed lineage, occupation requirements for buyer and seller, applicable state law, bona fide nonreproductive channels, and diversion notice all must receive their own treatment. The circuit's interpretation that a brown-bag buyer cannot qualify to save seed because it was not obtained with owner authority is a **lower-court construction**, not additional words of the statute. The own-farm and sale clauses must not be compressed into a nonexistent express numerical cap.

### Enacted 1994 amendment and transition

Pub. L. 103-349 was approved October 6, 1994. Section 10 deletes the first sentence's farmer-sales proviso; section 13 makes gender-neutral changes. Section 15 provides: “This Act and the amendments made by this Act shall become effective 180 days after the date of enactment of this Act.” That date is **April 4, 1995**, after this scheduled event.

Section 14(a), except as provided in §14, continues prior law for any variety whose certificate was issued before the effective date and any variety whose application is pending on that date. Section 14(b) permits refiling a pending application on or after effectiveness; a refiled application's eligibility and protection terms then follow the amended Act, while §42 uses the original filing date. Section 14(c) requires a notice seeking the amended Act's protection to state that amended protection and applies §128 sanctions to false or misleading claims or labels. Section 9's new §2541(b)(3) also expressly extends the specified contract-producer rule to old protected varieties; that contract rule concerns lawn, turf, forage grass, alfalfa, or clover and supplies no soybean-sales exception here. These provisions are enacted future operative text, not current liability rules on January 18. No finding that either certificate was refiled or qualified for a transition exception is supplied.

## 16. United States v. Mezzanatto — No. 93-1340

### Event, facts, and judgment reviewed

The admitted question is whether a defendant can knowingly and voluntarily waive Rules 410 and 11(e)(6) so that plea-discussion statements may be used to impeach his inconsistent trial testimony. Review is of 998 F.2d 1452 (9th Cir. July 7, 1993), No. 92-50261, reversing the Southern District of California conviction and remanding for a new trial. The Government seeks reversal; Mezzanatto seeks preservation of the exclusion and retrial judgment.

Mezzanatto requested a plea discussion with the Government. The prosecutor required, before the discussion, agreement that statements could be used to impeach later inconsistent trial testimony. Mezzanatto agreed. The full lower opinion establishes the agreement, failed discussion, later inconsistent testimony, preserved trial objection, impeachment use, conviction under 21 U.S.C. §841(a)(1), and sentence of 170 months plus five years' supervised release.

The Government's predecision petition adds, with trial-transcript citations, that Mezzanatto and counsel requested the October 17, 1991 meeting; the prosecutor said he need not speak, required complete truthfulness, excluded case-in-chief use, and allowed consultation with counsel before assent. It describes agreed impeachment and subsequent perjury/false-statement uses. It reports that, at trial, Mezzanatto claimed he believed a package contained explosives connected to Shuster's claimed CIA work rather than methamphetamine; the prosecutor cross-examined him on contrary proffer statements, and an agent recounted the statements after he denied them. This is the Government's sourced account, consistent with the lower court's dispositive posture; it does not establish the admissibility of unlitigated uses. No new finding of actual coercion, fraud, or ignorance is supported. Wallace's dissent explicitly treated this waiver as knowing and voluntary; the majority adopted a categorical legal rule instead of resolving enforceability against Mezzanatto on individualized grounds.

Judge Sneed's opinion, joined by Judge Hall, reasoned that the rules' express exceptions omit ordinary impeachment, that Congress rejected a version allowing impeachment generally, and that advance waivers would chill candid bargaining and permit prosecutors to exact the protection as the entry price for negotiations. It invoked the public-interest limit on private waiver from *Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil*, 324 U.S. 697 (1945). It distinguished *Town of Newton v. Rumery* and the general analogy from waivable constitutional rights. The majority separately found the error not harmless because the statements materially undermined the defense. Its footnote 4 did not call every violation automatically reversible: failure to object could forfeit protection and error remained subject to harmlessness. That terminology must be reconciled with simulated *Olano*'s distinction between forfeiture and intentional waiver.

Chief Judge Wallace dissented. He relied on the usual ability to waive personal constitutional and statutory protections, *Peretz*, *Rumery*, and the actual counseled agreement. He found the predicted system-wide bargaining harm speculative, addressed the actual impeachment use, and rejected categorical invalidity solely because prosecutors might misuse waivers. He would affirm without reaching harmlessness. The panel rejected Mezzanatto's other appellate contentions; it did not identify another conviction-reversal ground. Its sentencing reference to historical *Dunnigan* is a lower-court citation, not independent adoption of post-divergence Supreme Court law.

### Rule text, exceptions, and entering law

Then-Rule 410 provides that, except as otherwise provided in the rule, the following is not admissible against the defendant who made the plea or participated in discussions in any civil or criminal proceeding: (1) a guilty plea later withdrawn; (2) a no-contest plea; (3) statements in Rule 11 or comparable state proceedings regarding either foregoing plea; and (4) statements during discussions with an attorney for the prosecuting authority that do not result in a guilty plea or that result in one later withdrawn. Such a statement is admissible (i) when another statement from the same plea/discussion has been introduced and fairness calls for contemporaneous consideration, or (ii) in a criminal perjury or false-statement proceeding if made **under oath, on the record, and in counsel's presence**. Rule 11(e)(6) is substantively parallel. The perjury exception is not an exception permitting ordinary impeachment merely because trial testimony conflicts. General admissibility, express exceptions, and consensual waiver are different questions.

Simulated *Salerno*, present operation effective June 19, 1992, preserves actual Rule conditions and rejects treating independent contradiction as selective disclosure. It does not hold all evidentiary protections nonwaivable. Simulated *Godinez*, **Capacity to plead or waive counsel and independent valid waiver**, present operation effective June 24, 1993, distinguishes competence from an actually knowing, intelligent, voluntary waiver; it does not decide whether this Rule is waivable at all. Simulated *Olano*, under **Forfeited error and discretionary correction under Rule 52(b)**, present operation effective April 26, 1993, distinguishes a failure timely to assert a right from a valid intentional relinquishment of a known right. The latter extinguishes error; the former permits correction only when the error is plain and affects substantial rights, ordinarily with an outcome-prejudice showing by the defendant, and correction remains discretionary under the separate fairness, integrity, or public-reputation inquiry. Right-specific waiver requirements remain: Olano did not decide even Rule 24(c)'s waivability and supplies no rule that every evidentiary protection can be prospectively waived. Because the reported trial objection here was preserved, a finding of unlawful admission would not become forfeited error merely because the advance agreement is disputed. *Williamson* preserves applicable nonconstitutional harmless-error rules; neither a categorical no-waiver theory nor enforcement of a particular waiver warrants enlarging the question to case-in-chief or derivative use.

Surviving *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), supplies knowing and voluntary plea principles; it is not a rule that bargaining pressure always invalidates a choice. *Ricketts v. Adamson*, 483 U.S. 1 (1987), enforces the actual consequences of a breached agreement and distinguishes the terms made from terms the parties could have made. *Peretz*, 501 U.S. 923 (1991), permits consensual magistrate jury selection in its statutory and Article III setting. *Rumery*, 480 U.S. 386 (1987), rejects automatic invalidity of every release-dismissal agreement while retaining voluntariness, prosecutorial-overreaching, and public-interest concerns. *Brooklyn Savings* supplies the counterprinciple that private consent may not defeat a statutory policy intended for public protection. Their scope, rather than a ranking of which right is more important, must perform the legal work.

### Paths and strongest counters

**Enforce a valid, limited impeachment waiver.** The Rules prescribe a protection for the defendant but do not expressly prohibit relinquishing it; the ordinary waiver background may apply to a counseled choice. A narrow decision could resolve only use after the defendant gives inconsistent trial testimony, preserving challenges to knowing and voluntary assent, fraud, coercion, scope, and other evidentiary grounds. The counter is that allowing the prosecutor to make waiver a condition for any discussion may routinely eliminate the confidence Congress sought to protect, and Congress's rejection of an impeachment exception may be undermined in practice. The response must address statutory policy, not merely assert that the defendant may not lie.

**Treat prospective bargaining-entry waiver as incompatible with these rules.** The carefully defined exceptions and legislative choice to protect unsuccessful negotiations, alongside *Brooklyn Savings*, may support a rule that this protection cannot be sold away before discussions. The counter is that a prohibition on admission does not necessarily prohibit a defendant's waiver; many mandatory protections can be relinquished, and the text contains no express no-waiver command. A narrow version might concern this pre-discussion condition rather than every agreement made at any procedural stage, but it must identify a legal basis for that distinction instead of inventing a factual coercion finding.

**Individualized public-interest assessment** is also an available narrower rationale if the Rules permit waiver but *Rumery*'s safeguards are thought necessary. The record does not establish that this waiver was involuntary. Remand for a genuinely unresolved material validity issue must identify the missing issue and cannot assume coercion simply from bargaining. Enforcing the limited agreement would remove the panel's stated evidentiary ground for retrial; a mandate must distinguish that correction from a new sentencing ruling. Declining enforcement can preserve the panel's new-trial remedy on its finding of nonharmless error, subject to the actual issue scope. No route supplies an acquittal.

## 18. NationsBank / Ludwig v. Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co. — Nos. 93-1612, 93-1613

Argument December 7, 1994; merits event January 18, 1995. Consolidated review of *Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co. v. Clarke*, 998 F.2d 1295 (5th Cir. August 26, 1993), reversing 786 F. Supp. 639 (S.D. Tex. 1991). Do not reproduce the erroneous D.C.-Circuit metadata currently displayed on one mirror: the opinion's appellate origin, panel, and Southern District of Texas case establish the Fifth Circuit.

### Record, agency action, and statutes

On August 8, 1989, the Charlotte-based bank and its brokerage subsidiary sought permission to sell fixed, variable, and combined annuity products as agents for insurers. The Comptroller approved on March 21, 1990, adopting the relevant interpretive analysis. VALIC, which issues and sells annuities and would compete with the subsidiary, sought declaratory and injunctive relief. Cross-motions produced judgment for the Comptroller in district court and reversal in the Fifth Circuit.

The proposed business is agency sales, not underwriting, issuing annuities, or guaranteeing insurers' promises. The Comptroller found no bank actuarial or interest-rate risk from agency sales and required disclosure in advertising, promotional material, and customer descriptions that the annuities are not bank products and are not FDIC insured, plus a signed purchaser acknowledgment of agency status and lack of federal insurance. These are conditions of this approval, not an unqualified authorization of all bank insurance activities.

Annuities are not all lifetime mortality wagers. Deferred products have accumulation periods and may permit a lump-sum distribution, systematic withdrawal, or purchase of an immediate annuity at payout. Fixed contracts provide a guaranteed return but may also credit additional interest at the issuer's discretion. Variable contracts generally segregate funds and link returns to chosen investments. Lifetime payout options do involve mortality risk. The legal classification under §92 is disputed; product-specific features cannot be replaced by a categorical claim that every purchaser must annuitize for life.

Section 24 Seventh authorizes all incidental powers necessary to carry on banking and lists discounting/negotiating commercial paper and debt evidence, receiving deposits, buying/selling exchange, coin and bullion, lending on personal security, and obtaining/issuing/circulating notes under the title. Its securities paragraph distinguishes purchases and sales without recourse solely on customers' orders and for their accounts from bank own-account investment and underwriting. This packet invokes no own-account investment-security authorization or exception as a ground for approving these agency sales; a proposed holding resting on such a provision would require its actual operative terms. Agency brokerage is analytically distinct from impermissible underwriting.

Section 92 begins “In addition to the powers now vested by law” and permits a national bank located and doing business in a place with no more than 5,000 inhabitants under the last preceding decennial census to act, under Comptroller rules, as agent for a fire, life, or other insurer authorized by the State where the bank is located. The authorized functions are soliciting and selling insurance and collecting premiums on the insurer's policies; the bank and insurer may agree to fees or commissions. The two operative provisos are: “That no such bank shall in any case assume or guarantee the payment of any premium on insurance policies issued through its agency by its principal” and “That the bank shall not guarantee the truth of any statement made by an assured in filing his application for insurance.” These are express restrictions. The statute does not expressly say “all larger-place insurance activity is forbidden”; that is the Fifth Circuit's construction from the specific grant and statutory history. Keep command and inference distinct. The quotation and complete conditions are supplied by the authorized statutory supplement's official §92 extract check.

The Fifth Circuit, following *Saxon v. Georgia Association of Independent Insurance Agents*, 399 F.2d 1010 (5th Cir. 1968), construed §92 as an exclusive insurance-agency authorization, classified annuities as insurance, rejected deference because it regarded congressional intent as clear, and held that §24's general incidental power cannot defeat §92's specific limit. It regarded lifetime annuity risk as insurance's inverse, noted state insurance regulation, rejected a general/specialized insurance distinction, and distinguished credit-life insurance closely tied to loan repayment. A dissent from rehearing denial, summarized in the government merits brief, disputed certainty of the insurance classification and favored deference to the Comptroller.

### Entering law and supported alternatives

The simulated *United States National Bank of Oregon*, June 7, 1993, establishes that §92 survived the 1918 amendment and Code omission. No revived repeal argument is open.

Current-term *Brown v. The bank and Comptroller argue that annuity agency sales resemble lawful financial-investment intermediation, fit banking and incidental powers, and need not be “insurance” for this particular banking provision even when regulated as insurance elsewhere. Their alternative is that §92 adds general insurance powers to small-place banks without stripping otherwise valid §24 banking powers elsewhere. VALIC answers that enumeration and the specific insurance grant foreclose unlimited expansion, that mortality pooling remains insurance, and that nonmarketability distinguishes annuities from ordinary brokerage. ## 19. Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson — No. 93-1001

Argument October 4, 1994; event January 18, 1995. Review of 628 So. 2d 354 (Ala. August 13, 1993), affirming denial of a motion to stay and compel arbitration.

### Record and complete statutory qualification

In August 1987 the Gwins replaced an expired local termite bond on their Fairhope, Alabama house with a bond from Terminix Service, an Arkansas corporation operating in multiple States, guaranteed by Terminix International, based in Tennessee. They sold the house to the Dobsons in 1991 under a contract requiring inspection information and transfer of the bond. Terminix supplied a Veterans Administration inspection form reporting no visible active infestation. After discovering termite damage the Dobsons sued the Gwins for fraud and the Terminix firms for fraud concerning the inspection and breach of contract; the Gwins cross-claimed against the firms. The firms invoked the bond's arbitration clause against the Dobsons and Gwins.

The firms supplied evidence of their interstate organization and operations, some materials acquired outside Alabama, execution in Memphis, federal-law language in the bond, and Gwin's stated preference for a national firm. Alabama applied its §8-1-41 rule against specific enforcement of predispute arbitration agreements. It rejected a slightest-nexus test in favor of whether, when contracting, the parties contemplated substantial interstate activity. It found that out-of-state corporate status, the form's language, and these materials did not establish the required contemplation; the service was primarily local. It did not hold that no material ever crossed a state line.

The full operative FAA §2 text is: “A written provision in any maritime transaction or a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or transaction, or the refusal to perform the whole or any part thereof, or an agreement in writing to submit to arbitration an existing controversy arising out of such a contract, transaction, or refusal, shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” The concluding defense qualification must accompany any enforceability formulation. Section 1 defines commerce to include commerce among States and with foreign nations; its employment exclusion is separate and is not factually presented by this home-service bond. Federal procedural directions in §§3–4 raise a distinct state-court reach issue; do not infer that every federal procedure automatically applies in a state tribunal.

### Supported alternatives and earlier authority

The firms argue that “involving commerce” invokes broad commerce power and that actual interstate features suffice without parties' foresight. They oppose turning “evidencing” into a subjective or reasonable-expectations prerequisite and rely on *Southland*, 465 U.S. 1, and *Perry*, 482 U.S. 483. A favorable ruling could reject the coverage test and remand for proceedings under the proper rule without awarding tort damages or deciding every party's assent, scope, or generally applicable defense.

Respondents expressly ask that *Southland* be overruled on the state-court question. Their merits brief invokes O'Connor's *Southland* dissent and the brief of Alabama and nineteen other States, contending that a federal procedural law contains no sufficiently clear state-court command. Alternatively, they defend contemplation as a construction of the whole phrase “contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce.” Their further alternative construes “involving” more narrowly than “affecting,” reaching the actual stream of interstate commerce rather than every local contract with effects on it. These are separate defenses of the judgment, not invented alternatives.

*Southland* is governing pre-divergence law unless lawfully reconsidered. It treats §2 as substantive and applicable in state court, subject to the commerce condition and general contract defenses. *Perry* rejects a state wage-claim rule that bypasses arbitration and preserves generally applicable contract defenses. *Volt*, 489 U.S. 468, enforces an actual contractual choice of state arbitration rules; it does not make every forum's antiarbitration rule such a choice. *Bernhardt*, 350 U.S. 198, held the FAA inapplicable where the record did not show the particular employment transaction involved commerce; parties dispute the breadth of its language. *Prima Paint*, 388 U.S. 395, distinguishes fraud in the arbitration clause from fraud in the whole contract in the federal setting; no need exists to manufacture a fraud-in-the-inducement ruling on this source record. Later *Mastrobuono*, *First Options*, *Lopez*, or *Sky Reefer* are not entering law.

The strongest counter to broad coverage is the statute's transaction/contract language and the local service facts; broad commerce power alone does not answer what Congress enacted. The strongest counter to contemplation is the absence of a knowledge/expectation term and the practical difference between a transaction's actual interstate character and contracting parties' prediction. The strongest counter to overruling *Southland* is statutory stare decisis and the existing substantive-enforceability precedent; the competing argument relies on text, procedural structure, and the specifically presented request for reconsideration. No disposition may make arbitration agreements immune from defenses applicable to contracts generally.

## 20. Schlup v. Delo — No. 93-7901

Argument October 3, 1994; event January 23, 1995. Review of *Schlup v. Delo*, 11 F.3d 738 (8th Cir. November 15, 1993). The second federal §2254 petition alleges independent constitutional trial errors, chiefly ineffective assistance and suppressed exculpatory material, with innocence offered to pass otherwise applicable successive/abusive-claim barriers. This is not a request to choose a general freestanding innocence merits standard.

### Corrected posture and contested evidence

Schlup was convicted of participating in the February 3, 1984 prison murder of Arthur Dade and sentenced to death. Two correctional officers identified him: Flowers testified; Maylee's account came by deposition. Their descriptions differed in material details. The defense used a dining-hall videotape showing Schlup entering before an apparent distress response and O'Neal arriving later. The prosecution maintained that the attack preceded the response long enough for Schlup to reach the hall. The tape itself was trial evidence, not newly created evidence.

New affidavits bear on both identification and timing. Inmate clerk John Green said he saw the attack, did not see Schlup, identified a different participant, and promptly called for help. Former lieutenant Robert Faherty described Schlup's movement before entering the hall in a way said to undermine his ability to have participated. Other inmates said Schlup was absent or identified the actual attackers. These submissions are evidence offered by the prisoner, not adjudicated truth. The majority below emphasized inconsistencies between Green's affidavit, his earlier interview, and testimony in another participant's trial, and between Faherty's timing estimates and earlier sworn accounts. It questioned delay and reliability and credited the existence of the two trial identifications. The dissent argued that live examination could resolve contradictions, the affidavits were not uniformly inconsistent, prison witnesses were the natural eyewitnesses, and counsel had failed to interview available exculpatory witnesses.

The district court denied the second petition without the requested evidentiary hearing, dismissed August 23, 1993, and denied the Rule 59(e) motion September 13. The Eighth Circuit applied its *McCoy v. Lockhart*, 969 F.2d 649, and *Cornell v. Nix*, 976 F.2d 376 (en banc), extension of *Sawyer*'s clear-and-convincing no-reasonable-juror standard to guilt innocence. It affirmed denial and rejected a stay. Its majority also treated witness credibility as for the trial jury and discussed why counsel's first-petition ineffectiveness adjudication should stand. Heaney dissented, urging hearing and arguing that the evidence could satisfy even the stricter standard. Do not erase that additional merits discussion, but do not convert it into a fully developed district-court credibility hearing.

The current review is confined to the asserted innocence gateway and its application/remedy. Respondent's merits brief states that Schlup no longer presses cause and prejudice. ### Entering law and standards

Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, addresses trial-record legal sufficiency, with favorable inference to the prosecution; whether that approach is appropriate to newly supported innocence is disputed here.

The simulated register “Capital eligibility innocence as a gateway to barred habeas claims,” present operation effective *Sawyer*, June 22, 1992, requires clear and convincing proof that absent constitutional error no reasonable juror would find death eligibility. It expressly leaves crime innocence unchanged. It does not automatically control guilt innocence simply because the prisoner faces death. It also supplies no categorical exclusion of impeachment evidence.

It concerns merits harmlessness of a properly available claim, not this gateway, and must not be replaced by the historical Brecht rule. ### Supported alternatives and remedy boundaries

Schlup argues that guilt innocence invokes *Carrier/Kuhlmann*'s probability-based exception, that *Sawyer*'s eligibility standard is a distinct branch, and that the whole probative record requires actual reliability assessment rather than asking whether some inculpatory evidence could support the original verdict. ## 21. McKennon v. Nashville Banner Publishing Co., No. 93-1543

**Scheduled event:** Merits, January 23, 1995; argued November 2, 1994. Review of the Sixth Circuit's November 15, 1993 judgment, 9 F.3d 539, affirming summary judgment reported at 797 F. Supp. 604. Question: whether after-acquired evidence of employee wrongdoing bars all ADEA relief and, if not, its effect on available remedies.

### Record and posture

Christine McKennon worked for the Banner from May 1951 until October 31, 1990, principally as a secretary; the appellate opinion reports consistently excellent evaluations. She was 62 when discharged and sued on May 6, 1991. During her December 1991 deposition the Banner learned that, while secretary to its comptroller, she copied confidential documents accessible through that employment, removed copies from the premises, and showed them to her husband. The documents included payroll and financial records and management communications. She attributed the copying to concern about job security and a desire for protection in an anticipated discharge. The Banner then sent another termination letter stating it would have fired her immediately had it known.

The employer **assumed ADEA liability only for purposes of its summary-judgment motion**. Age discrimination is disputed in the underlying testimony and has not been adjudicated. The Sixth Circuit regarded the would-have-fired showing as undisputed: officers submitted affidavits, and McKennon acknowledged in deposition that the conduct would have caused termination. This is more specific than a showing that discharge was permissible. The judgment under review does not establish that every policy violation qualifies, that copying was the actual motive in 1990, or that discrimination is already conclusively proved for trial.

The Sixth Circuit applied the complete-remedy bar recognized in Summers v. State Farm, 864 F.2d 700 (10th Cir. 1988), and its own Johnson and Milligan-Jensen decisions. It treated the wrongdoing as eliminating compensable injury even though discovered after discharge, rejected a distinction between application fraud and employment misconduct, and rejected the claimed nexus to anticipated discrimination. Its footnote 7 separately recognized that genuinely protected opposition or participation under §623(d) could not become a lawful reason to escape liability, but regarded this copying and removal of confidential documents as unprotected. Do not turn that bounded ruling into a general rule against obtaining evidence of discrimination.

### Entering law and paths

The ADEA makes discharge because of age unlawful, 29 U.S.C. §623(a)(1). Section 626(b) incorporates specified FLSA enforcement provisions, deems amounts owing as a result of a violation unpaid wages, limits liquidated damages to willful violations, and authorizes appropriate legal or equitable relief to effectuate the Act's purposes, including employment, reinstatement, promotion, and unpaid-wage liability. Section 626(c)(1) provides an aggrieved person's civil action subject to termination of that individual right when the EEOC commences an enforcement action for that employee; no such EEOC action is established here. These textual remedial limits are separate from discrimination's liability elements.

The entering Hazen Paper holding requires age actually to motivate the employment decision and have a determinative influence; other impropriety is not automatically age discrimination. It preserves liability when an ostensibly different criterion is an age proxy or age also motivates the decision. Its Thurston willfulness rule requires knowledge or reckless disregard of the ADEA violation, not merely awareness that the statute may apply. St. Mary's Honor Center preserves Burdine's allocation and rejects automatic liability from disbelieving an employer's explanation; it supplies no new replacement persuasion allocation. Neither holding adjudicates after-acquired misconduct.

Supported alternatives requiring independent assessment:

1. Affirm the complete bar by treating the conceded legitimate basis for termination as defeating recoverable injury. The strongest objection is that an unknown ground did not cause the challenged act and a universal bar could remove both compensation for the earlier unlawful period and statutory deterrence.
2. Separate unlawful motivation from remedial entitlement: reverse the all-relief bar while allowing proved disqualifying wrongdoing to limit prospective relief and some backpay. The strongest objection is that an employee should not obtain continued pay or employment that lawful discovery would have ended. The actual cutoff, proof requirement, role of equitable circumstances, and treatment of distinct relief must be specified rather than borrowed from an unexamined general fairness rule.
3. Confine the decision to rejection or acceptance of the categorical summary-judgment ground and leave individualized remedy calculation for remand. If a broader remedial rule is adopted, identify why the record permits it. No amount, willfulness finding, front-pay award, reinstatement order, or final discrimination finding follows automatically.

Mt. Healthy, 429 U.S. 274 (1977), and Price Waterhouse, 490 U.S. 228 (1989), concern actual decisionmaking with lawful and unlawful considerations, and require careful treatment of the difference between a contemporaneous lawful reason and a later-discovered reason. The 1991 Title VII amendments must not be silently applied to this ADEA discharge. The October 1990 discharge predates those amendments.

## 22. Fargo Women's Health Organization v. Schafer, No. 93-1712

**Scheduled event:** Merits, February 13, 1995; user-established argument December 12, 1994. No. 93-1712 is the assigned Supreme Court docket, distinct from Eighth Circuit No. 93-1579. Review of 18 F.3d 526 (February 10, 1994), affirming summary judgment at 819 F. Supp. 862 (D.N.D. 1993). No grant date or order is invented.

### Questions, record, and disputed propositions

The granted question encompasses informed consent, the twenty-four-hour interval, definitions, the medical-emergency exception, facial review, and the pre-enforcement record and remedy. The actual challenges below concerned the new informed-consent provisions and emergency definition, and the preexisting abortion definition and general penalty. Parental-consent provisions were **not challenged**. The opinion expressly says the Organization pressed **vagueness**, not an undue-burden challenge to the medical-emergency definition. Treat preservation and the supplied question scope explicitly before expanding that component. A vagueness challenge to the emergency definition asks about fair notice and enforcement standards under the applicable date-eligible law; it is not converted into a general access challenge merely because emergency care also forms part of the protected baseline. A challenge to informed-consent obligations and delay may separately require the complete Casey access inquiry.

The district court issued a preliminary injunction on August 23, 1991 against informed consent and waiting provisions. The parties agreed to await Casey and later litigated summary judgment. The district court granted the State's motion, dissolved the injunction and dismissed the action. The Eighth Circuit affirmed. The supplied established merits posture does not require importing any other Supreme Court action in this litigation.

**Summary-judgment record:** The supplied lower-court account reports no district-court fact findings, but does report a substantial affidavit/deposition record submitted over a three-month period. The appellate majority assumed the nonmovants' factual assertions true, distinguished those from conclusory assertions, and held them legally insufficient under its two stated approaches. The dispute is not simply whether any evidence exists. It includes whether the majority's assumed facts and telephone construction dispose of every material burden under the governing law and whether additional findings or proceedings are needed.

Patients' travel distances, lodging costs, work and appointment constraints, protest-related exposure, and physicians' presence only on Thursdays and Fridays appear in the reported record. Do not assign numerical magnitudes or find the alleged burdens proved. The majority read the Act to allow information by telephone and written certification at the procedure, eliminating a statutory two-visit requirement. It therefore attributed remaining travel and clinic-day constraints to circumstances rather than the State's command. The competing question is whether the required interval nevertheless adds clinically or practically consequential delay or other interference to those circumstances, especially for a defined affected subgroup. That causal question requires the complete six-step rule below, not an assumption that all geographic difficulty is either attributable to the State or constitutionally irrelevant.

The lower court considered a March 1993 Attorney General opinion persuasive. It cited Johnson v. Baker, 21 N.W.2d 355, 364 (N.D. 1945), for an Attorney General opinion's force until contrary court ruling, and Svanes v. Grenz, 492 N.W.2d 576, 579 (N.D. 1992), for respectful consideration of persuasive opinions. The court independently adopted the same text construction. ### Construction and qualifications that must remain separate

- The lower court permits the performing or referring physician or an agent to communicate the required information by telephone. The attending physician must exercise medical judgment to determine probable gestational age; an agent may collect the relevant history, including last menstrual period, and communicate the physician's determination. There is no categorical exclusive-physician delivery rule.
- Written patient certification and receipt by the physician or agent precede the abortion, but need not precede the woman's arrival by twenty-four hours. The duty does not compel acceptance, reading, or endorsement of state materials. Materials must be provided if she elects to view them; statutory state supply is free.
- “May be available” does not guarantee benefit eligibility or payment. A legal child-support duty is not a promise of collection. The express freedom to comment or dissociate concerns printed materials; the lower court additionally construes the Act to permit comment on assistance and paternal-liability statements. Distinguish enacted text from this interpretation.
- The North Dakota text supplied here contains **neither Pennsylvania's separate §3205(c) disclosure-health defense nor its rape-related permission to omit paternal-support information**. Do not import either into North Dakota. Their absence may matter when applying the entering Casey holdings, whose qualifications must be respected.
- Emergency exemption and clinical-judgment language do not require catastrophic deterioration. The lower court reads the purpose clause and clinical standard to mean compliance may not significantly threaten the woman's life or health. That is a protective lower-court construction, not extra enacted words. The “prior to abortion if possible” proviso qualifies the emergency explanation. Written certification is a separate obligation, not authority to delay necessary care.
- The challenged abortion definition has both a pregnancy-termination object and a purpose distinction. The providers' example was amniocentesis inadvertently resulting in termination. The lower court rejected applying the definition to an unintended termination by reading its terms and criminal fault requirements together. No claim about a particular completed prosecution is presented.

#### Protected choice and the six-step standard for previability regulation

**Governing question:** Whether a legal obligation regulating previability abortion impermissibly interferes with the patient's protected decision or access to timely, clinically appropriate care.

**Current rule:** Before viability the patient decides whether to continue pregnancy without proving medical necessity or obtaining official approval of her reasons. The State may not prohibit that choice, confer effective control on another person, or prevent timely necessary life-and-health care. After viability, prohibition requires the life-and-health safeguard, including individualized physical and mental health considerations. Viability is individualized and medically supported, not a fixed constitutional week. Clinical appropriateness concerns safe and suitable care, not official approval of the patient's reasons; not every preference is a medical emergency. The following complete standard governs throughout previability:

1. **Identify the duty and protected baseline.** Identify the enacted command, authoritative construction, exceptions, enforcement consequences and relevant medical circumstances. Distinguish an actual obligation from assumed future administration. A previability prohibition, effective third-party veto or denial of timely necessary life-and-health care violates the baseline and cannot be rescued by justification. Necessary care must remain available before imminent death or catastrophic deterioration. Defining all noncompliant treatment as unsafe does not establish medical necessity.
2. **Identify actual function and purpose.** Identify what the obligation does and the asserted permissible objective, including safety, informed consent, truthful information, permissible parental involvement, health administration or financial accountability. The State may prefer childbirth and communicate truthful information. Moral disapproval does not independently justify obstruction, punishment of protected choice or burdens imposed for their deterrent effect. An obstructive purpose established by objective evidence of text, design, context and operation independently invalidates the requirement; a legislator's opposition to abortion alone does not establish that purpose. Voluntary persuasion differs from prevention through barriers. Evaluate each justification on its terms: medical claims need medical support, and deliberative claims need an explanation linking the particular obligation to informed decisionmaking.
3. **Establish actual interference, comparison and affected population.** Challengers must establish concrete additional interference caused by the challenged obligation. Relevant effects include extra appointments, material expense, consequential delay, loss of usable provider capacity, clinical risk, coercion and disclosure consequences affecting choice or access. Compare the actual system with and without the obligation while holding other relevant law and circumstances constant. For a challenged interacting package, identify the package and compare the system without it. Consider cumulative effects without counting the same burden twice. Define the affected population from the duty's operation before deciding constitutional injury, include evidence-supported subgroups, and keep the population stable. Do not average the burden across everyone pregnant or define the group as only those who prevail. Poverty, distance, transportation, employment and abuse matter insofar as they explain the State's added condition; identify that causal link. Exceptions, bypasses and alternative providers count only if actually usable. Meaningful interference means concrete, nontrivial additional burden on obtaining care or exercising the protected decision. It triggers review rather than establishing invalidity. No universal dollar, mileage or hourly threshold applies. Reliable clinical evidence, records, testimony and reasonable predictions can establish sufficiently demonstrated prospective injury before access is lost.
4. **Apply the substantive protections in order.** First, a baseline violation, unconstitutional obstructive purpose or substantial obstacle is independently invalid. A substantial obstacle seriously obstructs the affected population's practical opportunity for timely, clinically appropriate protected care; absolute impossibility is unnecessary, and success by some patients does not establish adequate access for others. A compelling interest cannot rescue an established obstacle. Second, remaining severe interference requires a compelling permissible justification and narrow tailoring. Identify the severe intrusion and explain why the independent prohibitions did not already decide the issue; overlapping protections require no artificial boundary, but an obstacle may not be relabeled as justifiable severe interference. Serious intrusion into clinical judgment, health or protected confidentiality may require this scrutiny despite usable access. Third, for remaining meaningful interference the State must substantiate a permissible justification and its fit to the added obligation. Abstract interests and conclusory assertions are insufficient. When challengers identify a concrete, practicable, substantially less burdensome alternative adequately serving the objective, the State must answer it; inadequacy, unavailability or material countervailing consequences may answer that showing. The State need not select the cheapest imaginable policy or disprove every hypothetical alternative. Reliable experience and reasonable predictions may suffice; particular legislative findings, a new study in every case, scientific certainty and numerical benefit calculations are not required. Once justification and fit are established, courts may not substitute their preferred policy. Below meaningful interference this heightened access inquiry ends, while prohibited-purpose claims, independent constitutional protections and ordinary lawful-regulation requirements remain available.
5. **Protect clinical judgment and truthful communication.** Evaluate medical justifications against competent evidence, accepted professional standards, the patient's circumstances and substantial contrary evidence. Substantial professional agreement receives serious evidentiary weight, without a professional association's veto or conclusive weight for a clinician's bare assertion or an official's unsupported preference. Necessary care must remain timely; unnecessary committee vetoes and documentation functioning as prior permission may not displace appropriate clinical judgment. Duties must be sufficiently definite and applicable fault requirements respected. Later reasonable medical disagreement alone does not establish every element of a punishable offense; lawful enforcement against fraud, malpractice and professional misconduct remains. Required medical information must be accurate, nonmisleading and material to informed consent. Concise factual assistance notices may inform choice without ideological assent; clinicians may explain limitations and correct misinformation. Accuracy does not alone validate burdensome timing, delivery or certification. Distinguish useful confidential oversight records from public disclosure and records enforcing invalid obligations.
6. **Identify the violation and tailor relief.** Specify the duty or application that fails, why, and the corresponding relief. Preserve independently lawful requirements under applicable severability law and expressly address dependent certifications, report fields and sanctions. An individual claim does not fail because others comply; broader relief requires a corresponding showing. An indivisible mandatory condition may be invalidated when its ordinary operation imposes demonstrated meaningful interference and the State fails to justify it. Voluntary compliance in some circumstances does not alone validate compulsory compliance. Otherwise confine relief to the established applications or groups. This qualification of a strict no-valid-applications approach does not remove proof of causation, scope or inadequate justification. For cumulative burdens, remedy the challenged obligation's or package's contribution without automatically invalidating all regulation. Replacing the trimester timetable does not dissolve injunctions or authorize enforcement of every challenged provision; the mandate must specify the resulting duties.

## 23. Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp., No. 93-1525

**Scheduled event:** Merits, February 21, 1995; argued November 7, 1994. Review of 12 F.3d 388 (2d Cir. December 27, 1993), reversing the district judgment reported at 811 F. Supp. 993. Question: whether Amtrak is a governmental actor for First Amendment purposes despite its statutory disclaimer.

### Record, judgment below, and remedy limits

Michael Lebron makes billboard works commenting on public affairs. In November 1992 he contracted with Transportation Displays, Inc. (TDI), Amtrak's advertising manager, for two months on the large “Spectacular” display at Penn Station, for $16,500 monthly. The lease reserved approval of copy and a termination right if Amtrak found it objectionable. Before signing, Lebron disclosed that his work was generally political but withheld its precise subject. The submitted photomontage criticized the Coors family's political activity and support for the Nicaraguan contras. Amtrak rejected it under a policy against political advertising on that sign.

After expedited discovery and trial on documentary submissions, the district court found governmental action and an unconstitutional speech restriction, and ordered two months' display after any stay ended. It did not reach the alternative contract claim. The Second Circuit stayed that order, then directed dismissal of the complaint on the federal-action threshold. It did **not** decide the First Amendment merits. It also directed leaving the contract dispute to state court; TDI's counterclaim concerning termination of the lease was not decided.

The majority did not dispute the findings of substantial governmental involvement in Amtrak's structure, financing and property. It treated the statutory designation as private as the starting point and required a sufficient connection between government and the challenged action, relying on Myron v. Conrail, 752 F.2d 50, and related employment cases. It regarded general funding, regulation, stockholding and board representation, without federal supervision of day-to-day decisions, as inadequate. Judge Newman's dissent distinguished direct political-content control from employment disputes; he nevertheless would not restore the mandatory-display injunction, considering damages or declaratory relief possible while disputing the sign's forum status and the appropriate scope of equity. That disagreement confirms that the actor threshold and speech remedy are separate questions.

### Statute, entering law, and live alternatives

The lower opinion quotes former 45 U.S.C. §541: Amtrak was to be operated and managed as a for-profit corporation and “will not be an agency or establishment of the United States Government.” The 1994 recodification, Pub. Do not treat recodification wording as a new constitutional adjudication.

The 1994 §24302 fixes a nine-member board: the Secretary of Transportation; Amtrak's president; three Presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed individuals representing specified labor, state-executive and business interests; two Presidential selections from commuter-authority nominees under the statutory conditions; and two preferred-shareholder selections. The governmental preferred shareholder supplies those two selections on the reported record. Amtrak's president is chosen within the corporate structure and chairs the board; do not collapse this into nine direct Presidential appointments. The lower court reports six Presidential appointments, two government selections as preferred shareholder, and the ninth selection by the board. The Secretary's ex-officio role and appointment of the Secretary explain the difference between that shorthand and the statute's detailed categories.

A federal corporation's statutory classification, statutory jurisdiction, constitutional actor status and sovereign immunity are distinct. The refreshed focused slice supplies Red Cross's charter-specific original-jurisdiction and removal rules, effective June 19, 1992. **Path A:** Treat statutory creation, governmental purpose and continuing governmental control of corporate direction as sufficient for constitutional governmental status despite the classification clause. Strongest counter: Congress gave a distinct corporate form and left ordinary operating choices to the company; a rule based on financial aid or appointment alone risks swallowing private entities. The proposed criterion must distinguish permanent public control from subsidized or regulated private activity.

**Path B:** Accept separate private status and ask whether this particular refusal has a sufficiently close governmental nexus under Jackson, 419 U.S. 345, Burton, 365 U.S. 715, and the corporate precedents. Strongest counter: demanding an external command for every act of a government-created, government-directed institution may let the Government avoid constitutional obligations through incorporation.

**Path C:** Test conduct-specific attribution for this use of the institution's advertising resources, while preserving separate decisions about other activities. This requires a supported governmental nexus or public-function basis for the challenged act, not a general preference for speech claims. Strongest counter: identifying expressive content does not itself establish governmental responsibility, and the lower findings must support the particular attribution ground. This path leaves whole-entity status analytically distinct and may overlap with Path B's conduct-specific inquiry.

If the threshold is resolved for Lebron, remand for the unadjudicated First Amendment merits, forum classification, contract issues and appropriate relief is available. Reinstating the district injunction needs a separate supported basis; actor status alone does not command display. If the threshold fails, dismissal of the federal claim and disposition of supplemental contract proceedings must fit the lower mandate. No damages amount is established.

**Preservation:** The federal-action claim was raised and decided; the precise distinction between an argument that Amtrak itself is governmental and an argument attributing a private actor's conduct requires the petition/appellate briefing if it becomes dispositive. ## 24. Milwaukee Brewery Workers' Pension Plan v. Jos. Schlitz Brewing Co., No. 93-768

**Scheduled event:** Merits, February 21, 1995; argued December 5, 1994. Review of Jos. Schlitz Brewing Co. and Stroh Brewery Co. v. Milwaukee Brewery Workers' Pension Plan, 3 F.3d 994 (7th Cir. August 13, 1993), Nos. 92-3811 and 92-3894. The granted question concerns when computational interest begins under §1399(c)(1)(A)(i), not a new review of every allocation or fiduciary issue in the appellate opinion.

### Facts, prior proceedings, and exact surviving question

Schlitz left joint bargaining in January 1981 but remained in the pension plan until August 14, 1981. The Plan year coincided with the calendar year. The parties disputed attempted adoption of direct-attribution withdrawal liability; arbitration retained the presumptive method and the parties' actuaries agreed on approximately $23.3 million under assumptions not disputed in this case. The district court upheld the method but added interest for 1981, plus prejudgment interest on that added amount. The court of appeals preserved the method ruling, reversed the 1981 interest requirement and directed enforcement of the arbitral award in full.

The Plan calculated its disputed interest from December 31, 1980, the prior-year valuation date, while Schlitz was still a contributing employer. Schlitz made its first actual payment October 29, 1981. The statutory assumed first-payment date was January 1, 1982. Distinguish these three dates from the August 14 withdrawal, and distinguish interest within an amortization calculation from default interest and prejudgment interest on a disputed assessment.

The Seventh Circuit said the Plan had **not argued for partial-year interest between actual withdrawal in August and January 1982**. That intermediate date cannot be treated as a preserved alternative demand without establishing a lawful basis for reaching it. The appellate court viewed the prior-year valuation rule as simplifying calculation of underfunding, not making Schlitz indebted before withdrawal. It also declined to give controlling deference to an earlier PBGC litigation position absent regulation, ruling, established agency practice or assurance that the position remained the agency's. PBGC had not participated in that appeal. Do not present that limitation as proof that no later date-eligible agency filing exists.

The district judge's initial omission of the amount of prejudgment interest did not leave the reported appeal dismissed: the parties reached agreement, the October 20, 1992 order apparently accepted it, and payment had occurred. ### Statute in terms and qualifications

Official 1994 29 U.S.C. §1399(c)(1)(A)(i):

> Except as provided in subparagraphs (B) and (D) of this paragraph and in paragraphs (4) and (5), an employer shall pay the amount determined under section 1391 of this title, adjusted if appropriate first under section 1389 of this title and then under section 1386 of this title over the period of years necessary to amortize the amount in level annual payments determined under subparagraph (C), calculated as if the first payment were made on the first day of the plan year following the plan year in which the withdrawal occurs and as if each subsequent payment were made on the first day of each subsequent plan year. Actual payment shall commence in accordance with paragraph (2).

Clause (ii) uses the assumptions for the most recent actuarial valuation in determining the amortization period. Subparagraph (B) limits ordinary liability to the first twenty annual payments when the computed period exceeds twenty years; (D) governs termination by withdrawal of every employer or substantially all pursuant to an arrangement and displaces that limit. No mass-withdrawal or twenty-year-cap dispute is supplied here. Subparagraph (C) calculates the annual amount, including its dated plan-election and partial-withdrawal qualifications; that annual-amount formula is not disputed in the supplied question.

Section 1399(b)(1) requires notice of liability and schedule and demand as soon as practicable after withdrawal. Paragraph (c)(2) starts actual payment no later than sixty days after demand notwithstanding review or appeal. Paragraph (c)(3) provides quarterly installments unless plan rules specify other intervals and imposes interest on overdue payments from due date to payment. Paragraph (c)(4) allows prepayment of outstanding annual payments plus accrued interest without penalty, subject to redetermination if the withdrawal later qualifies under the mass-withdrawal rule. Paragraph (c)(5) permits acceleration after default plus interest from the due date of the first untimely payment; statutory default includes nonpayment uncured sixty days after written notice and qualifying events defined in plan rules indicating likely inability to pay. Paragraph (c)(6) ordinarily uses prevailing-market rates under PBGC regulations, except the amortization assumptions in (c)(1)(A)(ii). None of these independently resolves the disputed starting date without construction of (c)(1)(A)(i).
