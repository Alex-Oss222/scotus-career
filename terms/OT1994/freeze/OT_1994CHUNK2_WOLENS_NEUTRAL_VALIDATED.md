## 17. American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens — No. 93-1286

Argument November 1, 1994; scheduled established merits event January 18, 1995. Review of *Wolens v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 157 Ill. 2d 466, 626 N.E.2d 205 (December 16, 1993), following 147 Ill. 2d 367, 589 N.E.2d 533 (1992). The questions separately concern damages claims under the Illinois Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act and judicial enforcement of the airline's own frequent-flyer agreement.

### Source-supported record and scope

Participants in American's AAdvantage program brought consolidated Illinois class actions alleging that May 1988 restrictions devalued mileage credits already earned. Capacity controls limit the number of seats available at an award level; blackout restrictions concern dates on which an award can be used. Mileage accrual can arise through American flights or participating nonairline businesses; available awards include flight and nonflight benefits. The litigated damages theory concerns prior credits and airline travel awards, not a universal challenge to every program product.

The pleadings alleged a right to redeem previously earned credits on the terms existing when earned, and that American induced accumulation of credits before reducing their value. American points to express language reserving alteration or termination of the program and disputes the claimed immunity of earned credits from alteration. Respondents concede prospective alteration but dispute retroactive alteration. These are rival constructions, not established findings of a binding promise, breach, fraud, reliance, or damages. The airline brief calls attention to a further factual limitation: although plaintiffs describe blackout dates generally, American maintains that the challenged retroactive effect principally concerns capacity controls and that blackout dates applied only to newly earned miles. Do not adjudicate that disputed detail as an established fact; the preemption question can be decided without resolving it.

The circuit court denied American's preemption motion to dismiss and certified interlocutory review. Illinois appellate review distinguished an injunction from damages. Plaintiffs' requested injunction was held preempted; only American sought further review. The 1993 Illinois Supreme Court left the injunction ruling undisturbed and sustained the damages claims, reasoning that frequent-flyer programs were peripheral and the damages claims only tangentially related to airline rates or services. Justice McMorrow dissented on both damages branches, reasoning that awards, restrictions, and availability directly concern fares/services and damages can enforce the same obligation as an injunction. Illinois's denial of dismissal is not a final damages award. No new injunctive relief or adjudicated breach follows merely from rejecting a preemption defense. ### Text and entering law

The transaction-period clause, 49 U.S.C. App. §1305(a)(1), bars state enactment or enforcement of a law, rule, regulation, standard, or other provision having the force and effect of law relating to covered air-carrier rates, routes, or services. Section 1506 preserves existing common-law and statutory remedies, subject to the specific preemption clause. Neither the separate Alaska-transportation qualification nor the airport-proprietor qualification is asserted on these facts. Their recodified terms are stated below; they must not be transformed into a general airline exemption from state law.

Congress's July 5, 1994 recodification places the passenger clause at §41713(b)(1) and the general additional-remedies provision at §40120(c). The official revision notes identify the latter as derived from §1506 and the former as derived from §1305(a)(1). The August 23, 1994 amendment, effective January 1, 1995, adds a separate property-transportation paragraph. Those additions are not qualifications newly attached to this passenger-award dispute. The supplied official-source supplement states the distinct recodified qualifications as follows:

- Section 41713(b)(2) excludes air transportation provided entirely in Alaska from paragraphs (1) and (4), unless the transportation is noncharter air transportation provided under a certificate issued under §41102. Thus the certificate condition and charter carveout qualify the Alaska exclusion.
- Section 41713(b)(3) preserves the proprietary powers and rights of a State, its political subdivision, or a political authority of at least two States that owns or operates an airport served by an air carrier holding a Secretary of Transportation certificate. That provision concerns the public airport proprietor's powers and rights.
- Separate paragraph (4)(B) preserves state motor-vehicle safety authority; highway route controls or limitations based on vehicle size or weight or the hazardous nature of cargo; and motor-carrier minimum financial responsibility concerning insurance requirements and self-insurance authorization. It also excludes household-goods transportation as defined in §10102. Paragraph (4)(C) states that paragraph (4) does not limit paragraph (1).

These are the supplied 1994 recodified qualifications, not findings that any applies to the May 1988 passenger-award transactions. Use the transaction-period clause for those pleaded events; do not import later cargo doctrine or decide a retroactivity issue that no supplied dispute presents. No independent transaction-specific Alaska, proprietor, cargo, or household-goods predicate is established.

### Supported legal alternatives and answers

American seeks dismissal of both damages branches. It argues that award availability and the number of credits needed for a flight concern rates and services directly; state damages law may regulate as effectively as an injunction. On contract it argues that law supplies enforceability and that the asserted implied promise would override its reservation clause. Its strongest supported answer to a contract exception is that it could allow state-imposed standards to return under a contractual label.

Respondents seek affirmance of both damages branches. They characterize the program as a voluntary exchange, invoke the savings clause and traditional remedies, and assert that compensation for broken promises does not prescribe future airline rates. They defend the fraud branch as protection against deceptive inducement rather than direct rate setting. The strongest answer is *Morales*: general applicability, retrospective damages, and absence of direct rate fixing do not alone defeat an express connection to rates/services.

A separate branch distinguishing a state consumer-protection command from enforcement confined to an undertaking American actually made has source support in respondents' contract argument and *Cipollone*. That earlier voluntary-warranty rule is a controlling component-specific narrow ground under Marks, not a full majority join in every part of Stevens's amended-Act analysis and not an already-adopted airline-contract exception. It requires a defensible textual treatment of “enact or enforce” and “force and effect of law”; the model must not assume the distinction merely from the label “contract.” Conversely, treating both branches as remote must confront their direct control over award value, flight availability, and redemption terms. If a contract claim survives preemption, the ordinary proceeding must determine the actual bargain and applicable defenses; no binding promise, waiver of the reservation clause, breach, reliance, causation or amount is established at this stage. A proposed voluntary-undertaking rule must distinguish interpretation and enforcement of an actual promise from imposing an external state policy under a contract label. Conversely, it must explain how ordinary formation, interpretation and remedy law operate without automatically treating every judicial enforcement rule as forbidden regulation.
