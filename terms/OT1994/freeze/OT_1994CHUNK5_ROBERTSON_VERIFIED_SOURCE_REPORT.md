## Competing legal positions preserved

The United States question is whether interstate movement of goods and persons associated with operating this mine supplies RICO's commerce nexus. It argues that RICO reaches the full Commerce Clause power; direct interstate employee/equipment movement is sufficient independently (pp. 18-19); intrastate gold sales belong to a broader interstate/foreign commodity market (pp. 19-21); out-of-state financing supplies another connection (p. 22); and interstate calls/mails provide operational connections (pp. 22-23). It argues these routes suffice separately and together and rejects an incidental/minimal distinction. P. 23 n.9 expressly contests the setup/operation distinction factually and legally. P. 24 n.10 says the government's nexus case rests on activities of the enterprise receiving the investment, not merely on predicate acts generating funds.

Respondent's question asks whether §1962(a) requires a particularized substantial-effect determination. He distinguishes Congress's regulation of an identified class from general prohibitions like RICO; disputes that Congress identified a class encompassing gold mining; argues for an individual substantial effect and a Sherman Act analogy; and invokes federalism, clear-statement considerations, criminal lenity, and limits on federalization (pp. 4-30). His pp. 20-23 attribution argument distinguishes (a) the interstate predicate crimes, (b) use/investment of their proceeds, (c) acquisition or establishment of the enterprise, and (d) activities of the enterprise itself, which he identifies as mining and selling gold. He argues that even if the disputed connections count as enterprise activities, they are trivial rather than substantial. His pp. 2-3 facts are compatible with the described interstate equipment/worker movements and part of the Phoenix loan financing operations; they are not a concession to any legal nexus standard. He also retains the Cadillac/airplane qualifications above. His requested disposition is affirmance; the United States requests reversal.

The statute's two alternatives remain distinct: an enterprise **engaged in** interstate/foreign commerce, or one whose activities **affect** such commerce. JA pp. 36-37 charges both; JA pp. 60-61 instructs both and requires the commerce element beyond a reasonable doubt. The parties focus on affecting commerce, and respondent p. 23 asserts the mine was not engaged in interstate commerce. This report establishes source availability for both alternatives without deciding either.

## Posture, existing lower-court disposition, and remaining claims

The Ninth Circuit Nos. 92-50395 and 92-50460 involved the defendant's appeal and the government's sentencing cross-appeal from the Southern District of California. Opinion filed February 1, 1994; rehearing denied April 26, 1994. The merits briefs record the certiorari petition filed August 10, 1994 and a historical grant October 17, 1994 (JA p. 79). Those Supreme Court actions are source chronology, not imported in-world actions under Engine §1.

The jury convicted on Counts 1-4 and 6, acquitted on Counts 5 and 8, and did not decide Count 7 forfeiture, later dismissed. JA pp. 27-28 documents verdict proceedings; pp. 74-78 contains the June 1, 1992 judgment. The lower court **reversed Count 6 for insufficient commerce nexus; affirmed convictions on Counts 1-4; vacated sentences on Counts 1-4 and remanded for resentencing under Rule 32**, because the district judge neither found the accuracy of the disputed PSR statement about Guarderas's killing nor unambiguously declined to consider it (pp. 25a-27a). Its note 10 requires actual resentencing after strict compliance, not a retrospective assertion of nonreliance. The narcotics-sentence vacatur has its own ground and is not automatically displaced by a different commerce determination.

Lower opinion **p. 11a n.3** expressly leaves unresolved these RICO challenges: denial of a special verdict; lack of venue; denial of severance of RICO and CCE counts; exclusion of Agent Devetko's RICO testimony; denial of CJA funds to locate the alleged Cadillac owner; and constitutional vagueness. A determination that the commerce evidence is legally sufficient does not by itself dispose of these claims.

Lower opinion **pp. 26a-27a** expressly does not reach the government's contention that the Guidelines apply because a money-laundering/structuring predicate continued past November 1, 1987. Its nonapplication conclusion rests on reversing the RICO count, while the affirmed drug counts involve pre-Guidelines conduct. Neither this report nor the supplied commerce accounts decides the cross-appeal; remaining proceedings must preserve it if the premise of RICO reversal changes.

### Primary judgment sentencing detail differs from narrative summaries

Both briefs and the lower opinion summarize concurrent twenty-year sentences on all five counts. **JA p. 76**, checked against the PDF image, instead specifies Count 1: 240 months; Counts 2 and 3: 60 months each; Count 4: 36 months; Count 6: 240 months, concurrent for a 240-month total. Supervised release is five years on Counts 1-4 and three years on Count 6, concurrent. This difference does not alter the lower court's recorded disposition or the commerce evidence. The judgment form's boilerplate Sentencing Reform Act heading is not proof that Guidelines were applied, given the explicit sentencing controversy.
