**Former §2244(b), complete:**

> (b) When after an evidentiary hearing on the merits of a material factual issue, or after a hearing on the merits of an issue of law, a person in custody pursuant to the judgment of a State court has been denied by a court of the United States or a justice or judge of the United States release from custody or other remedy on an application for a writ of habeas corpus, a subsequent application for a writ of habeas corpus in behalf of such person need not be entertained by a court of the United States or a justice or judge of the United States unless the application alleges and is predicated on a factual or other ground not adjudicated on the hearing of the earlier application for the writ, and unless the court, justice, or judge is satisfied that the applicant has not on the earlier application deliberately withheld the newly asserted ground or otherwise abused the writ.

The trigger has alternatives: an earlier evidentiary hearing on the merits of a material factual issue **or** a hearing on the merits of an issue of law, followed by federal denial of release or another remedy to a state prisoner. Thus, absence of a newly requested evidentiary hearing does not alone establish that the earlier-proceeding trigger fails. Whether an actual earlier legal or factual adjudication satisfies that trigger must rest on the proceeding identified, not merely the label “second petition.”

The consequence is permission not to entertain; the text does not command dismissal. The two statutory qualifications remain separate and conjunctive: a factual or other ground unadjudicated in the earlier application, and judicial satisfaction that the newly asserted ground was not deliberately withheld or otherwise an abuse. New evidence supporting a previously adjudicated claim does not automatically establish a new ground; lack of deliberate withholding does not automatically disprove every other form of abuse. This packet makes no claim-level classification from those phrases alone.

The official page's subsection (a) concerns federal-court custody and retains its own ends-of-justice wording; it must not be silently substituted for the state-custody subsection (b). Subsection (c)'s separate Supreme Court adjudication rule supplies no new fact or premise here. No current-case Supreme Court history has been admitted to trigger it.

## Predecision record and procedural predicates

Schlup is in state custody under a murder conviction and death sentence for participation in the February 3, 1984 prison killing of Arthur Dade. The second federal §2254 application alleges ineffective assistance and suppressed exculpatory evidence, with innocence offered to overcome otherwise applicable successive or abusive barriers. The earlier federal proceeding included appellate merits consideration of guilt-phase ineffectiveness. The District Court dismissed this second application without the requested evidentiary hearing on August 23, 1993 and denied a Rule 59(e) motion September 13. The Eighth Circuit sustained the denial and denied a stay. Those facts do not establish that every present constitutional ground was previously adjudicated, that every statutory condition is satisfied, or that the court below exercised every possible form of statutory discretion.

Two correctional officers identified Schlup; Flowers testified and Maylee's account was received by deposition. Their descriptions differed. A dining-hall videotape was trial evidence, not newly created evidence. It showed Schlup entering before an apparent distress response and O'Neal arriving later. The prosecution maintained that the attack preceded the response by enough time for Schlup to reach the hall.

Later affidavits addressed identification and timing. Inmate clerk John Green said he witnessed the attack, did not see Schlup, identified another participant and promptly called for help. Former lieutenant Robert Faherty described Schlup's movement before the dining hall in a way said to undermine participation. Other inmates also supplied absence or alternate-attacker accounts. Their statements remain submissions whose reliability is contested. The appellate majority emphasized inconsistency, delay and the two trial identifications; Heaney's dissent urged live examination and identified potential reasons to credit the new material. No current packet finds the affidavits true or false.

The Eighth Circuit applied *McCoy v. Lockhart*, 969 F.2d 649, and *Cornell v. Nix*, 976 F.2d 376, extending a clear-and-convincing standard to crime innocence. It also discussed why the prior ineffectiveness adjudication should stand and treated trial witness credibility as the jury's function. That additional discussion must remain visible when evaluating a proposed independent ground; it is not a substitute for an evidentiary hearing resolving newly disputed facts. ## Pre-divergence construction and current simulated law

*McCleskey v. Zant*, 499 U.S. 467, 487–489, directly examines subsection (b). It explains that satisfaction of both new-ground and nonabuse conditions requires consideration of the subsequent petition **when other barriers, such as nonexhaustion or procedural default, are absent**. That conditional rule must not be presented as an unconditional statutory command to reach the constitutional merits whenever innocence evidence is submitted. The opinion also explains that the text does not define the limits of discretion to entertain deliberately withheld or otherwise abusive claims; judicial habeas principles continue to define and limit that discretion. The permissive phrase therefore supplies neither automatic dismissal nor unrestricted discretion divorced from precedent. The opinion's treatment of Rule 9(b) likewise distinguishes repetitive grounds adjudicated on the merits from new grounds whose earlier omission constitutes abuse. [Pre-divergence opinion, statutory discussion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/499/467).

Under *McCleskey*, deliberate abandonment is not the only form of abuse. The State must plead abuse with clarity and particularity by identifying the prior writ history and newly raised claims and alleging abuse; the burden then shifts to the prisoner. Cause and prejudice ordinarily excuses the omission, while the exceptional miscarriage-of-justice route remains available without cause. Its majority does not treat proof of a potentially meritorious constitutional claim as sufficient by itself to overcome abuse. Nor does its account of discretion remove that exceptional route. The statutory language and the court-announced limitations must therefore be assessed together.

*Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, preserves the exceptional route when constitutional error probably convicted an innocent person. *Kuhlmann v. Wilson*, 477 U.S. 436, supplies plurality discussion of fair probability and all probative evidence; do not label every such formulation a majority holding. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, concerns legal sufficiency of the trial evidence and favorable inference to the prosecution, a distinct task from evaluating new innocence evidence.

The [focused entering-law slice](../entering-law/OT_1994CHUNK2_B_FOCUSED.md) controls the post-divergence authorities. Its register **Capital eligibility innocence as a gateway to barred habeas claims**, effective *Sawyer*, June 22, 1992, requires clear and convincing evidence that without constitutional error no reasonable juror would find a necessary death-eligibility condition. Kennedy, White, Scalia, Souter and Thomas directly support the eligibility-only scope and quantum. The holding expressly leaves crime innocence unchanged, retains independent claim barriers and merits requirements, and neither excludes impeachment evidence categorically nor grants relief merely because a gateway is passed.

Current *Herrera*, January 25, 1993, supplies only its majority-supported record-specific rejection, not a controlling general freestanding-innocence burden. O'Connor's plurality and White's separate concurrence jointly support that bounded result without a common general threshold. Current *Delo v. Blair* is an equally divided application disposition without precedent. Historical lower-court reliance on broader propositions from those cases must be tested against the actual entering law.

Current *Keeney*, May 4, 1992, retains *Townsend*'s deliberate-bypass limitation for materially inadequate factual development of a properly presented claim: ordinary counsel negligence alone does not forfeit the hearing required in that setting. Its authority is O'Connor's opinion joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy. It does not abolish the separate successive or abusive-claim barriers or substitute its factual-development rule for *McCleskey*'s abuse rule. Determine claim availability and any gateway inquiry before treating a hearing as required on the underlying constitutional merits. Current *Brecht*'s Chapman rule for the preserved Doyle claim concerns merits harmlessness, and current *McFarland* concerns capital counsel and individualized preparatory stays; neither supplies an automatic innocence gateway or a new §2244(b) command.

## Distinct statutory and gateway alternatives

**Statutory path A — preserve a lawful refusal of repetitive or abusive review.** The State may argue that an earlier qualifying merits proceeding and the character of the renewed constitutional grounds permit refusal under subsection (b), and that the actual lower-court merits discussion or failure of the innocence showing supports the existing judgment. The strongest counterargument is that permission does not establish a reasoned exercise of discretion under the correct governing law: a decision resting on an incorrectly expanded gateway standard may require renewed assessment, and a previously unadjudicated, nonabusive ground cannot be treated as repetitive merely because it appears in a second petition. This path requires the actual claim classification, statutory predicates, judicial limits on discretion, and any independent ground to be identified. The word “may” is not itself a complete sufficient ground.

**Statutory path B — require renewed consideration under the correct conditions and gateway.** The prisoner may argue that the district court's authority to refuse a subsequent application remains subject to the statutory qualifications and the recognized miscarriage exception, and that the erroneous choice of a guilt-innocence standard infects the refusal. The strongest counterargument is that neither the appearance of new affidavits nor the availability of an exception establishes that its requirements are met, that an asserted ground is new, or that every independent refusal basis fails. This path requires an identified legal or record defect in the actual refusal; it cannot derive mandatory merits review solely from the permissive statutory language.

These paths are separate from selecting the guilt-innocence standard. Schlup favors the *Carrier/Kuhlmann* probability approach and an assessment of the whole probative record; the State favors a uniform strict standard, or alternatively insufficiency even under the less demanding approach, and also invokes independent merits failure. The strongest response to uniformity is current *Sawyer*'s express eligibility limit; the strongest response to automatic remand or hearing is the requirement of reliable material evidence and an identified unresolved factual need. This packet chooses neither legal construction, gateway formulation, assessment of the affidavits nor disposition.
