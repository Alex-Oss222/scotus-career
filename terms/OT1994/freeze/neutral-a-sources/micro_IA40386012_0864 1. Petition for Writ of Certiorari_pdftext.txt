QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
In 1991, 
this Court held unconstitutional 
the veto 
power of the Board of Review (the “Board of Review” or 
the “Board”) of the Metropolitan Washington Airports 
Authority (the “Airports Authority” or the “Authority”) 
in Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority v. Citi- 
zens for Abatement of Aircraft Noise, 501 U.S. 252 
(1991) (“CAAN”). 
Later that year, Congress amended 
the Metropolitan Washington Airports Act of 1986 (the 
“Transfer Act”) to alter substantially the Board’s powers 
and composition. 
Despite Congress’ actions to remedy 
the constitutional deficiencies in the original Act identified 
by this Court, and the Authority’s adoption of those 
changes, the district court held, and the court below af- 
firmed, that the new Board of Review continues to violate 
separation of powers principles and is unconstitutional. 
Therefore, the questions presented are: 
1. Whether Congress may set forth statutory proce- 
dures that require (as a condition of the lease of federal 
property) that a non-federal Authority defer the effective 
date of action on certain decisions for a prescribed period 
of time to allow a supposed agent of Congress the oppor- 
tunity to render advice to the Authority, and, if such ad- 
vice is not followed, to allow Congress a prescribed period 
of time in which to decide whether to overrule such deci- 
sion by joint resolution strictly in accordance with the 
Bicameralism and Presentment Clauses. 
2. Whether the decision of the court below holding 
that an agent of Congress may not render advice in ways 
that could affect the timing of decisionmaking by a non- 
legislative entity, even if that congressional agent does not 
have ultimate control over the actions of that entity, con- 
flicts with the separation of powers jurisprudence articu- 
lated by this Court in Alaska Airlines v. Brock, 480 U.S. 
678 (1987), by the United States Court of Appeals for 
the Ninth Circuit in Lear Siegler, Inc. Energy Prods. Div. 
(i) 

ii 
v. Lehman, 842 F.2d 1102 (9th Cir. 1988), irrelevant 
portion withdrawn 
en banc, 893 F.2d 205 
(9th Cir. 
1989), and by the United States Court of Appeals for the 
Third Circuit in Ameron, 
Inc. 
v. United States Army 
Corps of Eng’rs, 809 F.2d 979 (3d Cir. 1986), cert. 
dismissed, 488 U.S. 918 (1988). 
3. Where Congress prescribes qualifications relating to 
residence, airport use and aviation expertise for the ap- 
pointment of members of a Board of Review established 
by a non-federal airports authority to represent the inter- 
ests of nationwide airport users, whether such Board of 
Review becomes an agent of Congress for separation of 
powers purposes merely because such members are ap- 
pointed by the Airports Authority from non-exhaustive 
lists supplied by the Speaker of the House and the Presi- 
dent pro tempore of the Senate and may, but need not, 
be Members of Congress, even though such Board mem- 
bers are removable only by the Airports Authority.

501 U.S. 252 (1991) (“CAAN”).

B. Statement of Facts 
Pursuant to the recommendation of an advisory Com- 
mission, the Virginia Assembly enacted legislation author- 
izing the creation of an authority to acquire Washington 
National Airport and Washington Dulles International 
Airport (collectively, the “Washington Airports” or “Air- 
ports”) by lease from the federal government. 
See 1985 
Va. Acts ch. 598. The Council of the District of Colum- 
bia passed a substantially identical law. 
See District of 
Columbia Regional Airports Authority Act of 1985, D.C. 
Act 6-90, 32 D.C. Reg. 6093 (1985). 
The reciprocal 
Virginia and D.C. statutes constituted an interstate com- 
pact, creating the regional Airports Authority as a “public 
body corporate and politic,” “independent” of all state 
and federal governmental bodies. See 1985 Va. Acts ch. 
598, §2; D.C. Act 6-90, § 3; Transfer 
Act § 6007 
(b)(1). 
The two jurisdictions also agreed to consent, 
subject to gubernatorial and mayoral approval, to lease 
conditions to be set forth by Congress “that are not in- 
consistent with [the Virginia or District] Act.” See 1985 
Va. Acts ch. 598, § 3; D.C. Act 6-90, § 4.* 
* The Virginia and D.C. statutes provided that the Authority was 
to consist of 11 members, five of whom are appointed by the Gov- 
ernor of Virginia, three by the Mayor of the District of Columbia,

of Transportation should negotiate lease provisions for 
a Board of Review designed to achieve this goal. A lease 
was subsequently negotiated and signed by the Secretary 
of Transportation and the Chairman of the Airports Au- 
thority and approved by the Governor of Virginia and the 
Mayor of the District of Columbia.

The court below concluded that the new Board of Re- 
view, in its view still an agent of Congress, retains con- 
trol over the Authority’s actions in violation of separation 
of powers principles. While the court pointed to several

one branch of government to another, even in the absence 
of ultimate authority to coerce compliance, violates separa- 
tion of powers principles. Even assuming arguendo that 
the Board of Review is an agent of Congress, which peti- 
tioners strenuously dispute, see Section ITI, infra, peti- 
tioners respectfully submit that the court of appeals’ hold-

The distinction 
between 
“control” 
and “advice” 
or 
“influence” was recognized by Madison in his treatment 

15 
of separation of powers in the Federalist Papers. As the 
court of appeals noted, Madison cautioned against the 
“encroachment” of legislative power against the coordi- 
nate branches of government. 
See Hechinger, slip op. at 
15 (citation omitted); App. at 16a. 
But Madison also 
stated that separation of powers “does not require that the 
legislative, executive, and judiciary departments should be 
wholly unconnected with each other.” See THE FEDERALIST 
No. 48, at 308 (James Madison) (Clinton Rossiter ed., 
1961). On the contrary, the Framers regarded the prin- 
ciple of separation of powers as allowing for advice and 
consultation between coordinate branches of government. 
See THE FEDERALIST No. 47, at 302 (James Madison) 
(Clinton Rossiter ed., 1961) (citing the British practice 
of allowing judges to attend and participate in delibera- 
tions of Parliament without allowing them to vote, in 
Support of the idea that some participation by members 
of one branch in activities of another does not necessarily 
threaten virtues associated with separation of powers). 
The holding of the court below thus runs contrary to the 
Framers’ more flexible view. 
Decisions of this Court reflect the historical distinction 
between control on the one hand and advice and in- 
fluence on the other, holding that Congress or its agents 
may properly attempt to advise and influence the Execu-

branch decisions without conforming to the Bicameralism 
and Presentment Clauses of Article I, § 7. In Bowsher

agency actions be submitted to a committee of Congress 
for a report-and-wait period prior to taking effect, recog-
