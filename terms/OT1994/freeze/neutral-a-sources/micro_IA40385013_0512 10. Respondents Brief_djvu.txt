COUNTERSTATEMENT OF 
QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether the lower courts, in a case of prolonged 
State-mandated racial segregation with devastating 
effects on the educational opportunities available to the 
plaintiff schoolchildren, acted within their discretion in 
refusing to terminate remedial educational improvement 
programs as “unitary” where they found that the defen- 
dant seeking termination had not shown (1) that the 
vestiges of segregation which such programs were 
designed to remedy had been eliminated to the extent 
practicable or (2) that release of jurisdiction in these areas 
would not have adverse consequences on other admit- 
tedly non-unitary aspects of the school system; and 
where there was no evidence (3) that the defendant seek- 
ing such termination had demonstrated a good faith com- 
mitment to its constitutional obligation. 


2. Whether the lower courts acted within their dis- 
cretion to extend orders mandating that the State defen- 
dants help maintain school district salaries for non- 
instructional personnel at levels repeatedly found neces- 
sary to successful implementation of a largely voluntary 
desegregation plan, where the deplorably low original 
salaries were in part the result of the State’s constitu- 
tional violation affecting school district funding.

B. Findings Supporting the Orders at Issue 


Since 1985, the district court has conducted periodic 
reviews (usually, at two-year intervals) of various compo- 
nents of the remedy. In 1992, following hearings on two 
such periodic reviews, the district court ordered the con- 
tinuation of two aspects of the remedy — the educational 
improvement plan as a whole and the salary supplement 
aspect of that plan.? Pet.A at 69, 76. 


The court of appeals affirmed the one-year continua- 
tion of the educational improvement programs because 
Missouri had not met, or even attempted to meet, its 
burden under Freeman v. Pitts, 112 S.Ct. 1430 (1992), of 
proving partial unitary status with regard to those pro- 
grams. Pet.A. at 19. In affirming, the court of appeals 
rejected Missouri’s contentions for three reasons: (1) Mis- 
souri presented no evidence either demonstrating the 
extent to which the educational improvement “programs 
had succeeded in improving student achievement” or 
establishing the State’s good faith implementation of the 
remedy. Pet.A. at 21. (2) Consequently, the “record fell far 
short of establishing” that the educational-harm effects of

C. Findings that the Remedy’s Four Components 
Are Necessary to Achieve its Desegregation and 
Educational Improvement Goals 


The preceding description of the time-limited exten- 
sions of the educational improvements plan and salary 
supplement exhausts the facts needed to address the 
issues on which the Court granted certiorari, namely, 
whether those extensions were within the lower courts’ 
discretion. Missouri devotes only a handful of pages in its 
brief to these issues. Many additional pages are spent on 
the question whether the four components of the remedy 
(educational improvements, magnet schools, capital 
improvements, and voluntary interdistrict transfers) are 
proper means of achieving the remedy’s desegregation 
and educational improvement goals.!° This Court has 
previously denied review of the lower courts’ orders 
implementing this basic remedial design, 490 U.S. 1034 
(1989), and it has not directed the parties to the present 
proceeding to address questions other than those pre- 
sented by the Petition. Because Missouri inaccurately por- 
trays the record relating to the remedy’s basic 
components, we here describe the findings supporting 
the prior determinations below that each of the four 
components is necessary and appropriate to redress the

standard. The continuation of the educational programs

was measured in part by whether Missouri had demon-

as in Milliken I, the district court declined to impose a 
remedy upon autonomous suburban school districts 
because those districts had themselves not caused any 
segregation. J.A. at 15. Instead, the remedy has been 
carried out exclusively by the two proven constitutional 
wrongdoers, Missouri and the KCMSD, and the remedial 
acts of those parties have all taken place exclusively 
within the boundaries of the KCMSD. It is true that, as 
the Court explicitly permitted in Hills v. Gautreaux, 425 
U.S. 284, 298 (1976), relying on Milliken I, the remedy has 
some effects outside the boundaries of KCMSD. But those 
effects are entirely on children who voluntarily choose to 
transfer to the KCMSD. Missouri is simply wrong, there- 
fore, when it says without record citations that the rem- 
edy, therefore, requires it to exercise “authority over the 
local autonomous [suburban] districts.” Pet.Brf. at 28 
n.18. Rather, precisely as Milliken dictates, the nature and 
scope of the remedy’s modest and wholly consensual 
effects outside the district are precisely commensurate 
with the nature and scope of the violation and its effects. 
Having found that violations by Missouri and KCMSD 
caused whites to flee to the suburbs, J.A. at 126, the 
district court designed a remedy “aptly tailored to rem- 
edy the consequences of the constitutional violation,” 
Milliken II, 433 U.S. at 287, by using voluntary transfers to 
“regain some portion of the white students who fled the 


49 


district”, 855 F.2d at 1302: Because the desegregative bur- 
den falls entirely on the constitutional violators and 
impinges not a single iota on the autonomy of innocent 
suburban districts and their students, Missouri’s Milliken 
I argument is without merit. 


IV. MISSOURI PROVIDES NO BASIS FOR INTER- 
FERING WITH A REMEDIAL PROCESS THAT IS 
WORKING IN AN APPROPRIATE AND 
ORDERLY FASHION TOWARDS AN EFFECTIVE 
AND TIMELY CONCLUSION 


Both courts below found that Missouri and KCMSD 
discriminated against African-American children for the 
six score years between the Civil War and 1984, and that 
the violation concentrated African-American children 
within the KCMSD’s systematically under-funded, deteri- 
orated, and educationally inferior schools and depressed 
the educational attainment of the children in those 
schools. Both courts below thereafter found that eliminat- 
ing those segregative and educational-harm vestiges to 
the extent practicable required educational improve- 
ments, magnet schools, capital improvements, and a 
modest voluntary interdistrict transfer plan. For the first 
time this school year, the remedy is fully operating. 
Because initial evaluations have shown that the remedy is 
working, the district court has directed the parties to 
begin planning to terminate the remedy. Nothing Mis- 
souri says in its brief provides any basis for interfering 
with this orderly process, or curbing the district court’s 
equitable and remedial discretion, or overturning the dis- 
trict court’s and the court of appeals’ reenforcing fact 
findings, or for modifying the Court's long-established 


50 


and recently reaffirmed doctrine pursuant to which the 
lower courts’ discretion has been exercised and their fact 


findings have been made. 


CONCLUSION 


For the reasons set forth above, the writ of certiorari 
should be dismissed as improvidently granted or, alter- 
natively, the decisions below should be affirmed.
