STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
This controversy arises out of the long-running Kansas 
City, Missouri, intradistrict school desegregation case, in 
which Missouri taxpayers have been obligated by court 
orders to expend more than $1.3 billion to fund a massive 
remedial program with overriding interdistrict features. 
Since the inception of the unprecedented remedy in the 
1985-86 school year, these court-ordered “desegregation” 
expenses—borne largely by petitioner, the State of Mis- 
souri—have grown at an astounding rate: 
annual ex- 
penditures in fiscal year 1986 were $18 million; by fiscal 
year 1993, those annual costs had risen to $206 million. 
As it has grown, the remedial program has assumed all 
the appearances of an interdistrict remedy, despite the 
district court’s express finding that there has been no 
interdistrict violation. At bottom, this extraordinarily ex- 
pansive exercise of judicial remedial authority has far 
outstripped the scope of the constitutional violation on 
which it is based.

Under the 6-C Plan, which

it changed boundary lines; paired

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
I. This case poses two specific remedial 
questions. 
First, does a federal court in an intradistrict desegregation 
case have remedial authority to require district-wide im- 
provement in student achievement—as measured by na- 
tional averages or other standards—before that district 
can be declared unitary. Second, does a federal court in 
such a case have remedial authority to order across-the- 
board salary increases for all school district employees, 
even if there is no evidence that such salary increases 
reasonably promote desegregation within the school sys- 
tem. 
A. Although each question can be dealt with on its own 
terms, both are inextricably connected to the more funda- 
unitary status was that KCMSD students reach the “national 
norm, the 50th percentile’ in achievement on standardized tests. 
Id. A-62 (quoting testimony). 
19 
mental issue of the propriety of the ultimate remedial 
purpose of the Kansas City desegregation decree, decades 
after the end of de jure segregation. The principles that 
govern here are well settled. 
The object of remedial 
orders in the school desegregation context is the elimina- 
tion of unconstitutional de jure school systems. Milliken 
v. Bradley, 418 U.S. 717, 737 (1974) (“Milliken I’). 
Desegregation remedies must be focused on those condi- 
tions or aspects of school operations that offend the Con- 
Stitution; thus, any remedy directed to a condition that 
does not violate the Constitution or does not flow from 
such a violation exceeds appropriate limits. 
Milliken v. 
Bradley, 433 U.S. 267, 282 (1977) 
(“Milliken 
II’); 
Pasadena Bd. of Educ. v. Spangler, 427 U.S. 424, 434 
(1976); Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ., 
402 U.S. 1, 16 (1971). 
Such limits are “tied to the 
necessity of establishing that school authorities have in 
some manner caused” the unconstitutional condition that 
is the subject of the court’s remedial order. 
Spangler, 
427 US. at 434. 
In the course of this protracted litigation, the courts 
below have lost sight of the goal of eliminating one-race 
schools, instead converting the remedial phase of this 
case into an exercise in providing the best (and most 
expensive) school system that the State’s money can pur- 
chase. There is no question that increases in the “quality” 
of education in the Kansas City schools theoretically could 
promote integration, in the sense that superior quality 
schools might further the goal of attracting additional 
non-minority students from the suburbs or private acade- 
mies. But that goal is inherently interdistrict in nature, 
and this case is, and always has been, an intradistrict 
case. 
This fundamental departure from legitimate 
re- 
medial goals has caused the lower courts to pursue a 
course that is inherently misguided and in excess of any 
appropriate measure of their remedial authority. 
B. The best indication that this case has strayed off 
the proper remedial course is the complete absence of any 

20 
meaningful discussion in the decisions below of the rele- 
vant criteria for determining whether the Kansas City 
school system has become unitary. 
In a typical intra- 
district case, see, e.g., Freeman V. Pitts, 112 S. Ct. 1430 
(1992), the remedial analysis focuses on the so-called 
“Green factors,” Green v. County Sch. Bd., 391 US. 
430 
(1968), which 
this Court has identified 
as the 
measure of whether a school district has racially identifi- 
able schools. 
If schools within a district are as racially 
indistinct as practicable; if the schools’ faculties and staffs 
are similarly diverse; and if the schools both have similar 
physical appearances and offer similar means of transpor- 
tation and equal availability of extracurricular activities, 
then under this Court’s decisions, the school district will 
be held to have eliminated the vestiges of its prior de jure 
system. 
As soon as those objectives have been achieved 
as far as practicable, and the government officials involved 
have demonstrated a commitment to fulfilling their con- 
stitutional obligations, then federal court supervision must 
be terminated. 
Board of Educ. v. Dowell, 498 U.S. 237, 
248-49 (1991). 
The well settled approach of Green makes no provision 
for comparing overall “student achievement” standards 
within the KCMSD to some national (or even suburban) 
standard 
or to some 
unstated 
level of improvement. 
Similarly, there is no basis for comparing staff salary 
levels in the KCMSD to suburban, regional, or national 
norms. 
Instead, under the traditional Green factors, the 
remedial goal is focused on the elimination of racially 
identifiable schools to achieve equality within the district, 
not an effort to improve quality measured against stand- 
ards derived either inside or outside the district. 
See 
Freeman, 
112 S. Ct. 1430; Dowell, 498 U.S. 237; 
Spangler, 427 U.S. 424; Green, 391 U.S. 430. 
C. The courts below have employed two flawed ration- 
ales—both of which arise out of mistaken assumptions 
about what constitute “vestiges” of de jure segregation— 
21 
to escape the constraints of the Green factors and vastly 
expand their remedial efforts. 
First, the district court 
augmented its original liability findings to include a “find- 
ing” that “white flight” was a vestige of de jure segrega- 
tion. Because the court lacked authority to order inter- 
district transfers, it adopted the novel approach of making 
the school system so attractive that non-minority students 
would be drawn from the suburban and private schools. 
Such a faculty rationale could, however, justify any ac- 
tion—including mandated improvement in test scores or 
across-the-board salary increases—that might be said to 
improve the school district. 
To be sure, to the extent 
such efforts make a school district more attractive 
to 
white students, they could thereby promote integration. 
But they exceed by far what is proper to remedy the 
constitutional violation. 
Second, the court held that overall student achieve- 
ment was harmed by de jure segregation. 
Based on this 
assumption—itself not supported by the record—the court 
concluded that it could order remedies designed to up- 
grade achievement system-wide. The court made no find- 
ings tying its decision to the existence of racially identifi- 
able schools. 
Under such a rationale, the court clearly 
would deny unitary status solely because achievement test 
scores for black and white students remained below sub- 
urban or national norms or some other unspecified level 
of achievement. 
The flaw common to both of these arguments is that 
the conditions in question simply are not vestiges of the 
former constitutional 
violation. 
“White flight” to the 
suburbs is not a vestige of de jure segregation. 
To the 
contrary, “white flight” is a regrettable consequence of 
desegregation, 
and the record and findings in this case— 
like the record in many other jurisdictions around the 
country—make clear that the departure of non-minority 
students did not occur until the KCMSD began to dis- 
mantle de jure segregation. 

22 
Similarly, although at one point in time, de jure segre- 
gation deprived minority and non-minority students of 
the cultural richness of a diverse educational environment, 
the district court failed to find a causal link between 
that segregation and current system-wide achievement 
levels, especially in light of the many external factors 
that affect achievement. 
There is certainly no reason 
to conclude that de jure segregation of decades ago is 
the cause 
of “reduced” 
test 
scores 
by mon-minority 
students today. Even though school systems can be re- 
quired to provide special remedial programs (e.g., “quality 
education”) to students directly harmed by racial segre- 
gation, Milliken II, 433 U.S. 267 (1977), such authority 
cannot be expanded into a federal judicial mandate to 
“provide the KCMSD with facilities and opportunities not 
available anywhere else in the country.” Pet. App. A-115.

“desegregative

It simply is beyond the 
scope of the constitutional authority of the courts below 
to erect actual achievement as the benchmark of a suc- 
cessful remedial program. 
It is also beyond the scope of 
those courts’ authority to require across-the-board salary 
increases in the absence of any showing of a link be-

“desegregative attractiveness.” 
The lower courts’ expansive intrusions into the man- 
agement of a local school district thus are constitutionally 
unauthorized, and they are detrimental to the independ- 
ence of a State and its school districts in our federal 
system.

A. The Constitutional 
Harms and 
the Court-Ordered 
Desegregation Remedy 
The principal question decided below, whether the State 
has established that the KCMSD is partially unitary, arises 
from the State’s opposition to a motion filed by the plain- 
tiffs and the District seeking to continue for the 1992-93 
school year certain educational improvements. The remedy 
originally was ordered because in September 1984, after a 
92-day trial, the district court (the Honorable Russell G. 
Clark) found both the State and the District liable for 
unconstitutionally segregating the schools of KCMSD for 
a century and for failing for three decades in their affirma- 
tive duty to undo the harm of that segregation. 
J.A. 14, 
46-50. 
The State did not appeal the determination of 
liability. 
1. The Effects of the Constitutional Violations. In his 
initial liability order with respect to the KCMSD and the 
State, entered on September 17, 1984, Judge Clark ex- 
plicitly found that the “inferior education indigenous of 
the state-compelled dual school system has lingering effects 
in the Kansas City, Missouri School District,” J.A. 23, and 
that State-mandated segregation has resulted in the con- 
tinued racial isolation of minority students. 
J.A. 24. In 
a series of subsequent orders, following the submission of 
additional evidence, Judge Clark found more specifically 
— 
3 
that the constitutional violations had caused a variety of 
related injuries to KCMSD students. He explicitly found 
“a system wide reduction in student achievement in the 
schools of the KCMSD,” J.A. 58 (emphasis in original); 
“low achievement” and a “general attitude of inferiority 
among blacks,” J.A. 23; racial isolation in the schools, 
J.A. 24, 126; and severe deterioration in the physical con- 
ditions of the schools. 
J.A. 89, 133, 219-20, 237. The 
State is incorrect when it claims “[t]he sole vestige that 
the district court identified” in the liability order was 
the maintenance of segregated schools, P.B. 5-6 (emphasis 
in original), and that Judge Clark later identified certain 
“racially ‘isolated’ schools as the only vestige of de jure 
Segregation within the KCMSD.” 
P.B. 8. Judge Clark 
made no such statements. 
Judge Clark did find that pervasive segregation remained 
in district schools. By 1984, 30 years after Brown I and 
seven years after the District first implemented on its own 
a largely ineffective busing plan, there were 
still “24 
schools 
. 
. 
. racially isolated with 90+-% black enroll- 
ment” and the dual school system had not yet been dis- 
mantled. J.A. 25. Judge Clark also expressly found that 
the segregated system and various acts of defendants had 
caused the departure of large numbers of white students 
to suburban and private schools. 
J.A. 126. 
The State 
did not challenge the finding that its constitutional viola- 
tion and its failure to remedy it had caused segregation in 
student enrollment. 
The district court also recognized severe deficits in the 
education and basic skills of KCMSD students caused by 
segregation. J.A. 23, 58. Judge Clark specifically found 
that “[the] education process has been further ‘bogged 
down’ in the KCMSD by a history of segregated educa- 
tion. Too often, as a result, a higher percentage of black 
students are among the lower achievers.” Id.. Segregation, 
Judge Clark confirmed, had “ruin[ed] attitudes” of black 
students in KCMSD. J.A. 23.. Moreover, the court found 
that the situation was “correctable.” J.A. 58. On appeal, 

4

v. Missouri, 807 F.2d 657, 685 (8th Cir. 1986) 
(en 
banc), cert. denied, 484 U.S. 816 (1987). 
Judge Clark further found that the school buildings in 
the KCMSD had “literally rotted,” J.A. 237, and that 
the lack of revenue that had caused this deterioration 
flowed directly from the State’s unconstitutional conduct. 
J.A. 220. See also J.A. 133. Segregation led to the identi- 
fication of particular KCMSD schools as “black,” then to 
the identification of the KCMSD itself as a black district, 
then to severe underfunding and the deterioration of the 
entire system, and ultimately to an inferior education for 
all students who remained. In affirming the district court’s 
findings, the Eighth Circuit explained that in this manner 
the State fostered increased segregation long after Brown: 
“The findings of fact demonstrate a spiraling effect of 
white children leaving KCMSD schools and KCMSD’s 
white constituency withdrawing its financial support from 
the system. This process eventually caused the decay of 
KCMSD’s school buildings, which in turn fed the cycle.” 
Jenkins v. Missouri, 855 F.2d 1295, 1305 (8th Cir. 
1988), cert. denied in relevant part, 490 U.S. 
1034 
(1989). 
2. The Origin of the Remedial Components at Issue 
Here. Judge Clark rejected mandatory busing as a remedy 
for these conditions. He explicitly found that “[t]he evi- 
dence is clear, further mandatory reassignment at this 
time will only serve to increase the instability of the 
KCMSD and reduce the potential for desegregation.” 
J.A, 86. He ordered instead a desegregation plan based 
on educational improvements, magnet schools, capital 
improvements, and voluntary interdistrict transfers. 
J.A. 
54-101. 
These programs all have the dual purposes of 
remedying the educational harms within the District and 
desegregating 
its schools through three complementary 
means: (1) to improve the education available to the 
$s 
plaintiff school children; (2) to bring about, through indi- 
vidual family choices, a voluntary redistribution of chil- 
dren within the KCMSD; and (3) to reverse the State- 
initiated pattern of decreasing non-minority enrollment by 
maintaining white students and attracting back voluntarily 
those who had been driven to attend private and suburban

C. The Present Status of the Remedy 
Despite the State’s obstruction and the massive injuries 
caused by the segregation of Kansas City’s public schools, 
the KCMSD has made remarkable progress toward ad- 
dressing the vestiges of segregation. The racial composi- 
tion of the District’s student enrollment has remained 
roughly constant over the last 10 years after decades of 
dramatic declines in the percentage of white students. 
J.A. 410-11. Voluntary student assignment choices have 
resulted in a 50 percent reduction since 1984-85 in the 
number of KCMSD elementary schools that remain more 
than 90 percent black. 
J.A. 417-19, 433-35. 
At a Jan- 
uary 1993 hearing, one of the State’s principal witnesses 
had to admit that the remedy had reduced segregation in 
“all fifteen [magnet] elementary schools that had suffered 
the most intense racial isolation,” made progress in inte- 
grating secondary schools (some of which had “achieved 
high levels of integration”), and “substantially improved” 
on an index of desegregation in the schools. 
P.A. 129. 
The abysmal pre-1985 levels of student achievement in 
the KCMSD have also risen as a result of the District’s 
remedial efforts, although all the educational vestiges of 
the constitutional violation have not been eliminated to 
the extent practicable. J.A. 419-21, 436-38.

CONCLUSION 
_ 
It remains unclear just what the State asks this Court 
to do. In seeking certiorari, the State proclaimed that it 
does not “ask this Court to declare partial unitary status. 
Petitioners seek only correction of the erroneous legal 
standard applied below.” Reply Memo at 8. At the end 
of its wide-ranging brief, however, the State concludes that 
the Court should “reverse” the orders below. 
P.B. 50. 
We cannot believe that the State intends that this Court 
could declare any part of the District “unitary” without 
further proceedings below, because such a process would 
be diametrically opposed to the procedures required by this 
Court in Dowell and Freeman. 
Unless that is what the 
State suggests, however, its request that the Court simply 
“reverse” the decisions below makes no sense. 
For all of the reasons stated herein, the Petition should 
be dismissed as improvidently granted or, alternatively, 
the decision below affirmed.
