under the Appointments Clause of the Constitution, when 
those acts include affirming criminal convictions on direct 
appeal?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Petitioner was a Boatswain's Mate Second Class (pay 
grade E-5), U.S. Coast Guard, at the time of his court-martial 
in the summer of 1989. 
He was tried by a general court- 
martial composed of officer and enlisted members. Contrary 
to his pleas, he was 
convicted 
of various drug-related 

violations of the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ). 
His approved sentence, as affirmed by the lower courts, was 
confinement for three years, forfeiture of $699 per month for 
thirty-six months, reduction to the lowest enlisted grade (E- 
1), and a dishonorable discharge. 
The question presented is 
whether the constitutional invalidity of the appointments of 
two of the three judges of the Coast Guard Court of Military 
Review who heard Petitioner's appeal can be disregarded by 
invoking the de facto officer doctrine.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
This case seeks to ensure that the separation of powers 
requirements and protections embodied in the Appointments 
Clause are available to those tried by courts—martial whose 
cases are reviewed by the Courts of Military Review. 
More 
specifically, the issue is whether the de facto officer doctrine 
can be relied upon to save the acts of military appellate judges 
who were not appointed in accordance with the specific 
requirements of the Appointments Clause when the issue of 
their unconstitutional appointments was raised before the 
court involved, and two of the appellate judges on the case 
were invalidly appointed. 
No case cited by the Government or found by Petitioner 
supports the application of the de facto officer doctrine to a 
situation like this. The cases in which the doctrine has been 
applied are 
all substantially 
different 
from the situation 
presented in this case. None has ever applied the doctrine in a 
direct 
criminal 
appeal where 
the challenge 
is to 
the 
appointment of a judicial officer who sat on the defendant's 
case. Moreover, there are several cases in which application 
of the de facto officer doctrine would have provided a simple 
way to resolve the case, but it was not applied. These cases 
illustrate the limitations of the doctrine. 
Application of the doctrine in this case would deny 
Petitioner the benefit of a favorable ruling on the underlying 
issue of the constitutionality of the appointments of the 
appellate judges involved. Such a result would be contrary to 
this Court's precedents holding that judicial decisions in both 
criminal and civil cases cannot be applied prospectively only, 
and that the law announced in a case must be applied to the 
litigants in that case. 

10 
This case simply does not present the type of problem 
the de facto officer doctrine was created to avoid. 
The 
doctrine was developed as a matter of necessity to avert the 
confusion and chaos which would result from decisions, often 
highly technical, which would invalidate the acts of a public 
official taken months or years before. 
Thus, the doctrine 
protects the public and third parties who relied on the acts of 
the officeholder. 
In a direct criminal appeal such as this, in 
which the issue was raised before the court on which the 
improperly—appointed judges sat, there are no third parties 
who relied to their detriment on the defective appointments. 
In addition, the number of cases that would be affected by a 
ruling in favor of the Petitioner would be small; cases that are 
already final could not be reopened. 
Therefore, no chaos or 
confusion would result. 
ARGUMENT 
THE 
DE 
FACTO 
OFFICER 
DOCTRINE 
CANNOT BE APPLIED IN THIS CASE. 
A. Introduction 
The Appointments Clause of the Constitution, Art. II, § 
2, cl. 2, sets strict limits on the power and methods of 
appointing officers of the United States. The most senior, or 
principal officers, may be appointed only by the President, 
with the advice and consent of the Senate. "Inferior" officers 
may be appointed by the President alone, the heads of 
departments, or the courts of law, if Congress has so provided 
by law. 
U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2; Freytag v. Comm'r of 
Internal 
Revenue, 
501 
U.S. 
868, 
884 
(1991). 
The 
Appointments Clause is part of the "Constitution's structural 

11 
Separation of powers." 
/d. at 873. 
In addition to protecting 
against the encroachment of one Branch's power at the 
expense of another Branch, the Appointments Clause protects 
against the diffusion of the power of appointment. /d. at 878. 
The appointments at issue here represent an impermissible 
diffusion of the appointment power. 
The Appointments Clause limits the potential holders of 
the power to appoint inferior officers to a small group of 
Officials 
who 
are 
themselves 
politically 
accountable. 
Appellate 
military 
judges, 
who 
exercise 
extraordinary 
authority and discretion, are at least inferior officers of the 
United States and are subject to the Appointments Clause. 
Weiss v. United States, 114 S. Ct. 752, 757 (1994). The Judge 
Advocate General of the Coast Guard is neither the President, 
the head of a department, nor a court of law; accordingly, 
appointment of civilians to judicial service in the Armed 
Forces by him does not satisfy the Appointments Clause. Jd. 
The Solicitor General, in his brief in opposition to the 
petition for a writ of certiorari, did not contest the ruling in 
Carpenter that the appointment of the civilian judges to the 
Coast Guard Court of Military Review was invalid. Thus, the 
Only question remaining is whether the de facto officer 
doctrine can be relied upon to affirm the acts of civilians 
improperly appointed to serve as military appellate judges by 
the Judge Advocate General, in a case in which the accused 
raised this issue during the direct appeal of his case.

Doctrine 
to 
Affirm 
the 
Acts 
of

The de facto officer doctrine was developed to limit the 
circumstances in which government actions can be challenged 
by attacking the title of the officeholder responsible for the 
action. 
The doctrine is not simple, nor its limits yet clearly 
determined. 
See Andrade v. Lauer, 729 F.2d 1475, 1496- 
1501 (D.C. Cir. 1984). However, one proposition is clear——it 
has never been applied in a case with circumstances similar to 
the case at bar.

Two federal criminal cases in which the doctrine was 
relied on to sustain convictions on direct appeal are McDowell 
v. United States, 159 U.S. 596 (1895), and Ball v. United 
States, 140 U.S. 118 (1891). In McDowell, the doctrine was 
merely an alternative basis for affirming the conviction, and 
the facts involved were very different from those in this case. 
The defect alleged in McDowell was that proper procedures 
had not been followed in arranging for an assignment of a 
properly appointed federal judge from one district to sit in 
another district within the same circuit. The judge in question 
had not presided over the defendant's trial, nor was he present 
when the grand jury had returned the indictment. 
Rather, his

and impaneled. 
Under those circumstances,

refused to overturn the defendant's conviction for what was, at 
most, a highly technical violation of a statute, if a violation at 
all. 
In Ball, the de facto officer doctrine was cited in a 
situation in which a properly appointed federal trial judge had 
been properly assigned from one district to fill a temporary 
vacancy in another, but his period of service slightly exceeded 
the express length of the assignment. More significantly, the 
convictions involved were reversed on other grounds, so the 
de facto officer doctrine was not essential to the resolution of 
the case.

C. Applying the De Facto Officer Doctrine in This 
Case Would Be Contrary to This Court's Decision 
in 
Griffith v. Kentucky. 
In Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314 (1987), this Court 
held that any newly declared constitutional 
rule for the 
conduct of a criminal case must be applied to all cases then 
pending on direct review. 
Jd. at 321-322. 
The reasons for 
this holding included the nature of judicial review itself, and 
the principle of treating similarly situated litigants similarly. 
Id. at 322-323. 
Even when the new rule is a "clear break" 
with past precedent, it must be applied to criminal cases not 
yet final. Jd. at 326-328; see also Harper v. Virginia Dep't of 
Taxation, 113 S. Ct. 2510 (1993); James B. Beam Distilling 
Co. 
v. 
Georgia, 
501 
US. 
529, 537 (1991)(retroactive 
application of new rule cannot be denied civil litigants in the 
case that changes the rule). 
A decision in favor of the Government in this case would 
allow the Government to introduce through the back door of 
the de facto officer doctrine, what it could not get through the 
front door of a decision with purely prospective effect. 
In 
essence, it would carve out an exception to Griffith for cases 
in which the constitutional defect relates to the appointment 
of an official involved in the case, even when the validity of 
that appointment had been raised, as here, at the time of the 
action by the improperly appointed officer. It would leave no 
remedy for such a constitutional defect. 
Indeed, if the doctrine were relevant in a case like this, it 
would always be determinative of any question conceming 
appointment to office, and would always preclude a ruling on 
the merits of the constitutional claim because to do otherwise 
would be to render an advisory opinion. 
See F.C.C. 
v. 
Pacifica Foundation, 438 U.S. 726, 734-735 (1978)(federal 
courts do not have authority to issue advisory opinions). 
In 

20 
other words, the use of the de facto officer doctrine as it was 
applied by the Court of Military Appeals in Carpenter, supra, 
would effectively remove all such claims from judicial review 
and thereby allow Congress or the Executive to violate the 
explicit 
provisions 
of 
the 
Appointments 
Clause 
with 
impunity, because the de facto officer doctrine would always 
prevent a court from reaching the merits of the issue of the 
challenged appointment. 
It is particularly inappropriate to apply the de facto 
officer doctrine in a criminal case, since it is unlikely that 
anyone could ever bring an anticipatory challenge to the 
appointment of a judge before being subject to the authority 
of that judge with the possibility of being adversely affected. 
See Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95 (1983). Application of 
the doctrine to this case would leave Petitioner without a 
remedy for the violation his right to have his court-martial 
reviewed by a constitutionally—appointed court; that situation 
would conflict with the long-standing principle of judicial 
review that if there is a right, there must be a remedy for its 
violation. 
See Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137, 162-167 
(1805). 
D. 
This Case Does Not Present the Type of Problem 
the De Facto Officer Doctrine Was Intended to Prevent. 
In order to fill most governmental positions, federal or 
State, 
a 
series 
of 
requirements 
must 
be 
met, 
some 
constitutional 
and some 
statutory. 
From 
time 
to time, 
mistakes may be made, such that, when a governmental action 
is challenged, the objecting party may argue that the person 
taking the action did not properly hold the office involved, 
and hence the action is invalid. The de facto officer doctrine 
was developed as a matter of policy and necessity to limit the 
circumstances in which government actions can be challenged 

21 
by attacking the title of the officeholder responsible for the 
action. The primary purpose of the doctrine is to protect the 
public and third parties who have relied on the acts of the 
officeholder and who would be adversely affected by a 
finding that the acts of the de facto officer were invalid, and 
to avoid the confusion and chaos which would result. Norton 
v. Shelby County, 118 U.S. 425, 441-442 (1886). This type 
of harm would be at its worst when the attack on the 
appointment 
of the 
officeholder 
occurs 
long 
after 
the 
appointment is made, and the defect is of a technical nature 
which could have and would have been readily cured had it 
been called to the government's attention in a timely fashion. 
Id. 
There are no "third parties" involved here who have 
relied 
to their detriment 
on the acts of the improperly 
appointed officials involved in this case. 
Petitioner certainly 
did not rely on the acts of the officials; he contested their 
power to act. 
The Government, as the only other involved 
party, is not simply an innocent third party which relied to its 
detriment on the acts of the officials. 
The Government 
created the flawed appointment process in the first place, was 
aware of the issue, and chose to do nothing to correct the 
problem. 
The significant public interest in avoiding confusion and 
chaos 
is protected by the concept of finality of judicial 
decisions. 
Petitioner 
is not advocating the reopening of 
already final decisions of the Coast Guard Court of Military 
Review. 
Only those cases still open on direct appeal and in 
which the issue of the defective appointments of the Court of 
Military Review judges was raised and preserved would be 
affected if this Court holds that the de facto officer doctrine 
does not protect the acts of those judges. 
Cases which are 
already final would not be affected. 
See Art. 76, UCMSJ, 10 

U.S.C. § 876; R.C.M. 
1209, Manual 
for Courts—Martial, 
United States, 1984 (1994 Edition); see also Teague v. Lane, 
489 U.S. 288, 305-316 (1989); Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 
314, 322 (1987); but see United States v. Rice, 36 M.J. 264 
(C.M.A. 1993)(Court of Military Appeals will accept out—of- 
time requests for relief in cases in which petitions for grant of 
review raising Appointments Clause issues were previously 
denied if the Supreme Court affords relief in a case involving 
an Appointments Clause issuc). 
With this in mind, it is clear there would be no serious 
adverse affect on the public and no chaos or confusion. The 
number of cases which would be affected by a decision in 
favor of Petitioner is quite small, because the decision would 
apply only to the Coast Guard, only to cases not yet final, and 
only to those in which the accused preserved the issue in a 
timely manner. 
There are approximately ten other cases in 
which the accused is situated identically to Petitioner (i.e., 
issue raised at the Court of Military Review, preserved in 
further appeals, and the Court of Military Review decision 
occurred before January 15, 1993) which would be directly 
affected by a reversal here. The de facto officer doctrine can 
not be invoked to protect the public against such a minor 
disturbance in the orderly conduct of the business of the 
nation when the right of Petitioner to have constitutionally 
appointed judges decide his criminal appeal is at stake.
