STATEMENT 


1. On Christmas Eve, 1979, the S/S EM Ford was 
moored at a berth at the Port of Milwaukee when a severe 
winter storm arose. The Ford broke away, crashed into 
the headwall of the berth, and sank to the bottom of 
Milwaukee Harbor. Pet. App. 2a; J.A. 26. In 1980, respon- 
dents, the owner and insurers of the Ford, brought suit in 
admiralty in the United States District Court for the East- 
ern District of Wisconsin. They sought $6.5 million in 
damages from petitioner, the City of Milwaukee, for the 
loss of the Ford and its cargo. Pet. App. 2a; J.A. 29.! 
Respondents alleged that the City should have warned of 
the dangers that might occur if a storm arose. J.A. 27. 


| The court of appeals appears to have misstated the 
amount of damages sought. See J.A. 29. 


| 


The City denied liability, urging that a wharfinger 
has no duty to warn of the dangers of storms. The City 
further asserted that respondents were contributorily 
negligent in leaving the Ford in its outer harbor berth 
essentially unattended during the winter, “with no means 
aboard of monitoring weather or summoning tugs or a 
pilot for assistance” (J.A. 27). On this basis the City 
counterclaimed for $250,000 in damage to the dockwall. 
Pet. App. 2a. 


The trial on liability was not conducted until 1986. It 
lasted approximately three weeks. J.A. 35. The district 
court did not issue its decision until 1989. J.A. 36. The 
district court found respondents 96% liable and the City 
4% liable. Pet. App. 3a. The United States Court of 
Appeals for the Seventh Circuit reversed and, without 
remanding, itself reapportioned the parties’ liability, find- 
ing respondents two-thirds responsible and the City one- 
third responsible. Cement Division, National Gypsum Co. v. 
City of Milwaukee, 915 F.2d 1154 (1990). After this Court 
denied certiorari, 499 U.S. 960 (1991), the parties entered 
into a settlement agreement under which the City paid 
$1,677,541.86 in damages to the respondents. Pet. App. 
3a, 17a. The agreement provided that the district court 
would decide whether prejudgment interest was to be 
awarded. J.A. 46. 


2. Respondents then sought an award of 
$5,317,807.70 in prejudgment interest from the district 
court. The district court denied prejudgment interest. Pet. 
App. 20a-23a. The district court began its analysis by 
recognizing that, in admiralty cases, “an award of pre- 
judgment interest .. . is the rule rather than the excep- 
tion” and that “a court may exercise its discretion to deny 


prejudgment interest” only “if unusual circumstances 


exist.” Id. at 21a. But among such unusual circumstances,

The district court also mentioned, as an additional

The court of appeals reversed. Pet. App. la-15a. The 
court of appeals also recognized that admiralty, while 
generally requiring the award of prejudgment interest, 
has historically allowed a district court “discretion to 
deny prejudgment interest where it finds that special or 
peculiar circumstances would render such an award ineq-

The court of appeals held, however, that because of 
this Court’s decision in United States v. Reliable Transfer 
Co., 421 U.S. 397 (1975), “there no longer exists a reason 
to deny prejudgment interest in collisions cases involving 


mutual fault.” Pet. App. 8a. Reliable Transfer overturned 
the “divided damages” rule, which provided that if the 
two parties to a maritime collision are each to some 
degree negligent, total damages must be divided equally 
rather than apportioned according to comparative fault. 
See 421 U.S. at 397. Reliable Transfer replaced this rule 
with a rule of comparative fault, the same rule that had 
long governed personal injury actions in admiralty. See 
id. at 407. 


~ The court of appeals asserted that the discretionary 
authority to withhold prejudgment interest in “mutual 
fault” cases in which there is a “genuine dispute” had 
served to mitigate the sometimes harsh effects of the 
divided damages rule. Pet. App. 8a. Therefore, the court 
concluded, Reliable Transfer had removed the justification 
for this exercise of discretion. Pet. App. 8a-9a. The court 
of appeals recognized that, under prior decisions, uncer- 
tainty about the existence and extent of the defendant's 
liability might also justify denying prejudgment interest. 
Id. at 10a-1la. The court of appeals explained these deci- 
sions as follows (id. at 11a): 
[U]sing uncertainty as a basis to deny interest in 
a mutual fault case made sense under the old 
divided damages regime. This is because under 
that rule, it was not until after trial that there 
would be a determination from the district court 
as to which of the parties would have to come

whole.

But the court of appeals asserted that Reliable Transfer, by 
overruling the divided damages rule, had reduced the 


amount of uncertainty in collision litigation to such an 
extent that “uncertainty as to eventual liability can no 
longer be a factor to consider in determining whether to 
deny prejudgment interest in cases involving mutual 
fault.” Id. at 1la-12a.2 The court accordingly stated: 
“We ... conclude that, as a matter of law, the mutual fault 
of the parties should not constitute a reason to deny an 
award of prejudgment interest in admiralty.” Id. at 9a. 


The court of appeals then discussed the rate of pre- 
judgment interest that was to be assessed. The “best 
starting point,” the court said, was “an average of the 
prime rate for the years in question.” Pet. App. 14a. 
Alternatively, the district court might “set the rate of 
interest to match that which lenders would charge the 
City for short-term, unsecured loans.” Id. (citations omit- 
ted). 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


A. Prejudgment interest has traditionally been 
available in admiralty. But prejudgment interest has 
never been available in admiralty as a matter of right. 
From the earliest decisions, this Court has emphasized 
that an award of prejudgment interest in admiralty is 


2 The court of appeats also rejected the district court’s rul- 
ing that petitioner’s status as a municipal government, limited 
in its ability to hold interest-bearing funds, was a factor justify- 
ing a denial of prejudgment interest. Pet. App. 13a. 


discretionary with the trial court. The Court has, more- 
over, repeatedly reaffirmed the discretionary nature of 
prejudgment interest awards in cases arising under fed- 
eral statutes. Congress has deliberately left this discre- 
tionary and equitable federal prejudgment interest 
regime intact. 


B. The district court in this case exercised its discre- 
tion to deny prejudgment interest because both parties 
were at fault and the City had good reason to believe that 
it would not be held liable. Such an exercise of discretion 
is consistent both with this Court’s decisions and with the 
underlying logic of prejudgment interest. Prejudgment 
interest is not just an element of a plaintiff's compensa- 
tion; it also operates to impose a cost on the defendant 
that results from the defendant’s decision to continue to 
contest the litigation. That is because an award of pre- 
judgment interest forces the defendant to become, in 
effect, an involuntary debtor who must pay the plaintiff a 
market rate of interest for the entire period during which 
it contests liability. When the defendant’s decision to 
continue litigating is a reasonable one - as will often be 
true in a case of mutual fault and good faith dispute over 
the facts — the district court may, in its discretion, decide 
that that reasonable decision should not be burdened by 
an award of prejudgment interest. 


This understanding of prejudgment interest — that it 
is not just an element of compensation but a burden 
imposed on the defendant for continuing to litigate - 
explains why this Court has consistently viewed prejudg- 
ment interest as discretionary. It also explains the univer- 
sal agreement that prejudgment interest may not be 
awarded when a plaintiff delays the litigation. For there 


is only a difference of degree, not of principle, between 
unreasonable delay by the plaintiff and a reasonable deci- 
sion by the defendant to continue litigation. Finally, 
imposing the burden of prejudgment interest on the 
defendant, when the plaintiff was the party primarily at 
fault, offends the notions of fairness and equity that, this 
Court has repeatedly said, are the touchstones in deter- 
mining whether to award prejudgment interest. 


C. The court of appeals recognized that it was over- 
turning a rule that many lower courts had accepted, but it 
concluded that this Court’s decision in United States v. 
Reliable Transfer Co., 421 U.S. 397 (1975), justified it in 
doing so. This conclusion was doubly mistaken. First, 
when the courts wished to amelioriate the inequities of 
the divided damages rule overturned in Reltable Transfer, 
they did so by adopting explicit, well-focused rules, such 
as the major-minor fault doctrine and the in extremis 
doctrine. The prejudgment interest principle at issue here 
would have been a crude, inefficient, and of course disin- 
genuous way to “compensate for” the inequities of the 
divided damages rule. Second, Reliable Transfer in fact 
increased the uncertainty faced by parties to maritime 
collision litigation. If, as the court of appeals seemed to 
accept, uncertainty is a reason justifying withholding pre- 
judgment interest, Reliable Transfer actually strengthens 
the case for the principle that the court of appeals dis- 


carded. 


ARGUMENT 


THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN DENYING THE 
DISTRICT COURT ALL DISCRETION TO WITHHOLD 
PREJUDGMENT INTEREST ON GROUNDS OF 
MUTUAL FAULT. 


A. Trial Courts Have Traditionally Had Discretion 
To Withhold Prejudgment Interest When Its 
Award Would Be Inequitable. 


1. The common law was hostile to awards of pre- 
judgment interest. See Library of Congress v. Shaw, 478 U.S. 
310, 314-15 & n.2 (1986). The traditional common law rule 
is that prejudgment interest is available only on damages 
awards that are liquidated or “ascertainable.” See 1 D. 
Dobbs, Law of Remedies § 3.6(1) at 336-37 (1993); Note, 
Prejudgment Interest: Survey and Suggestions, 77 Nw. U. L. 
Rev. 192, 196-97 (1982) (citing sources). This rule — which 
was widely criticized (see, e.g., Funkhouser v. J.B. Preston 
Co., 290 U.S. 163, 168 (1933)) - precluded prejudgment 
interest in most personal injury cases and in many cases 
involving property damage. See, e.g., D. Dobbs, § 3.63(1) 
at 336 & n.17; Monessen Southwestern Railway Co. v. Mor- 
gan, 486 U.S. 330, 337 (1988). 


Admiralty did not share the common law hostility to 
prejudgment interest, and prejudgment interest has tradi- 
tionally been available in admiralty. See, e.g., The Amiable 
Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, 560 (1818); Geraghty & 
Stiles, Damages, 2 Benedict on Admiralty § 84, at 7-87 n.38 
(7th ed. 1993). But prejudgment interest in admiralty is 
not available as a matter of right. The trial court has 
broad equitable discretion in deciding whether to award 
prejudgment interest. This Court settled this point long 


ago. In The Scotland, 118 U.S. 507, 518-19 (1886), the Court 
upheld a denial of prejudgment interest, and Justice 
Bradley’s opinion for the Court stated: 


The allowance of interest on damages is not an 
absolute right. Whether it ought or ought not to 
be allowed depends upon the circumstances of 
each case, and rests very much in the discretion 
of the tribunal which has to pass upon the sub- 
ject, whether it be a court or a jury. 


In The Maggie J. Smith, 123 U.S. 349 (1887), Justice 
Field’s opinion for the Court brusquely dismissed a con- 
tention that the lower courts had erred in denying pre- 
judgment interest: “[I]t is sufficient to say that the 
allowance of such interest and costs rested in the discre- 
tion of the court below, and its action will not be dis- 
turbed on appeal.” Id. at 356. The courts of appeals have 
repeatedly reaffirmed the rule that while there is a pre- 
sumption in favor of awarding prejudgment interest in 
admiralty, an award of prejudgment interest remains dis- 
cretionary. See, e.g., The Wright, 109 F.2d 699 (2d Cir. 
1940); The President Madison, 91 F.2d 835, 847 (9th Cir. 
1937); 34 ALR Fed 126, 144-46 (1977 & Supp. 1994) (col- 


lecting cases). 


2. Although this Court has not recently considered 
the availability of prejudgment interest in admiralty, it 
has decided a number of cases involving prejudgment 
interest on federal claims. In these cases the governing 
statute was typically either silent or unclear on the ques- 
tion whether prejudgment interest should be awarded. 
These cases are a reliable guide to how prejudgment 
interest should be treated in admiralty. That is because 


there is no general federal statute governing prejudgment 


10 


interest. Cf. 28 U.S.C. 1981 (providing for awards of post- 
judgment interest). As a result, the law on prejudgment 
interest, in the absence of clear statutory directives, is 
essentially judge-made. “[T]his Court has fashioned rules 
which granted or denied interest on particular statutory 
obligations by an appraisal of the congressional purpose 
in imposing them and in the light of general principles 
deemed relevant by the Court.” Rodgers v. United States, 
332 U.S. 371, 373 (1947). Admiralty, of course, is a similar 
combination of statutory policies and “general princi- 
ples” (ibid.) of a common law nature. See, e.g., Miles v. 
Apex Marine Corp., 498 U.S. 19, 24 (1990). 


A consistent theme in these statutory cases is the 
same as that announced by Justices Bradley and Field in 
their admiralty opinions from a century ago: if prejudg- 
ment interest is available, the trial court has broad equita- 
ble discretion to dacide whether to award it. See also D. 
Dobbs, § 3.6(5) at 357, 360-61. This Court has explicitly 
stated, on many occasions, that prejudgment interest may 
be withheld where an award would be “inequitable.” 
Thus in Blau v. Lehman, 368 U.S. 403 (1962), the Court 
affirmed the denial of prejudgment interest on a damages 
award under Section 16(b) of the Securities Exchange Act 
of 1934, 15 U.S.C. 78p(b), on the ground that the “courts 
below denied interest . . . and we cannot say that the 
denial was either so unfair or so inequitable as to require 
us to upset it.” 368 U.S. at 414. The Court further 
explained, quoting an earlier decision, Board of Commis- 
stoners of Jackson County v. United States, 308 U.S. 343 
(1939): 


“{I]nterest is not recovered according to a rigid 
theory of compensation for money withheld, but 


0 ee eT TE eT ee 


11 


is given in response to considerations of fair- 
ness. It is denied when its exaction would be 
inequitable.” 


Blau, 368 U.S. at 414, quoting Board of Commissioners, 308 
U.S. at 352. Board of Commissioners, in turn,-relied on 
United States v. Sanborn, 135 U.S. 271, 281 (1890), and 
Billings v. United States, 232 U.S. 261 (1914). See also Miller 
v. Robertson, 266 U.S. 243, 258 (1924): “[W]hen necessary 
to arrive at a fair compensation, the court in the exercise 
of a sound discretion may include interest or its equiva- 
lent as an element of damages.” 


In Osterneck v. Ernst & Whinney, 489 U.S. 169 (1989), 
the Court sounded the same note: a district court has 
equitable discretion in deciding whether to award pre- 
judgment interest. Indeed, the discretionary character of 
the district court’s decision was central to the Court's 
reasoning in Osterneck. Osterneck held that the filing of a 
motion for prejudgment interest in the district court con- 
stitutes a motion to alter or amend the judgment, under 
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 59, and therefore renders 
ineffective a previously filed notice of appeal. 489 U.S. at 
170. The Court noted that “prejudgment interest tradi- 
tionally has been considered part of the compensation

its decision not to settle, a decision that on the facts of 
this case can scarcely be regarded as unreasonable.

properly seen, in part at least, as a burden imposed on 
the defendant as a result of its decision to continue to 
litigate. Once prejudgment interest is seen in this way - 
as it has been for centuries — the discretionary regime that

sometimes reasonably believe that it might not be held 
liable at all. Cf. S. Rep. No. 97-275 at 12 (the proposed 
general prejudgment interest statute allowed prejudg-

defendant itself has suffered an injury, for which the 
plaintiff might be held responsible. The defendant’s belief 
that it might not be held liable is even more reasonable 
when it is clear that the bulk of the fault resides with the 
plaintiff. That is indisputable in this case; the issue 
between the district court and the court of appeals was 
whether respondents were two-thirds responsible or 96% 


responsible. 


When, as here, the defendant had a reasonable belief, 
throughout the litigation, that it might not be held liable 
because of the plaintiff’s fault, it may be reasonable for 
the defendant to continue to defend itself. And if the 
decision to continue the litigation is reasonable - in the 
judgment of the district court, which is of course in the 
best position to make that determination — then the dis- 
trict court should also be allowed to detemine that the 
defendant will not be burdened, by an award of prejudg- 
ment interest, for continuing to litigate.

1. The court of appeals’ first reason amounts to 
saying that the reason courts adopted the mutual fault 
principle in prejudgment interest cases was so that they 
could subvert the divided damages rule by manipulating 
the award of prejudgment interest. Indeed, the court of 
appeals reasoned that this was the only function served 
by the mutual fault rule in prejudgment interest cases, so 
that, according to the court of appeals, once the divided 
damages rule was overturned, “there no longer exists a 
reason” to allow the denial of prejudgment interest on 
this ground. Pet. App. 8a. 


There are several difficulties with the court of 
appeals’ reasoning. First, manipulating awards of pre- 
judgment interest is too crude a way of compensating for 
the divided damages rule. Courts operating under the 
divided damages regime did develop principled and 
well-focused means of limiting the inequities of that rule. 
They developed the major-minor fault rule, under which 
the divided damages doctrine would not apply when one 
party was only minimally at fault. See Reliable Transfer, 
421 U.S. at 406; G. Gilmore & C. Black, The Law of 
Admiralty 492-93 (2d ed. 1975). They also developed the 
in extremis rule, under which a vessel responding to an 
emergency not of its own creation would be excused from 
any resulting negligence. See Gilmore & Black at 491-92. 
But any court concerned about the inequity of the 
divided damages rule would be ill-advised to try to use 
awards of prejudgment interest as a corrective. The 
amount of prejudgment interest depends on interest rates 
and the duration of the litigation. Neither of these factors 


30 


bears any necessary relationship to any damages award. 
It would be entirely fortuitous if even the most unprin- 
cipled district judge were able to use a prejudgment 
interest award to “compensate for” the perceived ineq- | 
uities of the divided damages rule. 


Second, the divided damages rule was, before Reliable 
Transfer, a rule that the lower courts were obligated to 
apply. The court of appeals’ approach therefore attributes 
to the lower courts a degree of disingenuousness. Rather | 
than forthrightly seeking to limit the divided damages | 
rule, as through the major-minor fault doctrine or the in 
extremis rule, the lower courts — according to the court of 
appeals — invented a rule solely to enable themselves to 
award or deny prejudgment interest when, under the law 
then in effect, there was no legitimate reason to do so. 
Many lower court judges expressed intense disagreement 
with the divided damages rule (see Reliable Transfer, 421 
U.S. at 404-05; Gilmore & Black at 530-31), but they then 
proceeded to apply the rule, not evade it. There are, to be 
sure, a few cases from the Second Circuit in which 
awards of prejudgment interest were viewed as a way to 
circumvent the divided damages rule. See Iberian Tankers 
Co. v. Gates Construction Co., 504 F.2d 747, 748 (1974); 
Afran Transport Co. v. The Bergechief, 285 F.2d 119 (1960). 
But it is implausible to say that the only function served 
by this prejudgment interest principle was to undermine, 
in a crude and inefficient way, a principle that the judges 
were supposed to apply. 
Finally, the court of appeals’ account cannot be rec- 
onciled with the fact that even in personal injury cases - 


where comparative fault, not divided damages, has long 
been the rule in admiralty —- courts have applied the

principle that prejudgment interest can be denied in 
mutual fault situations. See, e.g., Inland Oil and Transport 
Co., 696 F.2d at 328, citing Pluyer v. Mitsui O.S.K. Lines, 
Ltd., 664 F.2d 1243 (5th Cir. 1983), and Ceja v. Mike Hooks, 
Inc., 690 F.2d 1191, 1197 (5th Cir. 1982). This demonstrates 
that the mutual fault rule for determining when prejudg- 
ment interest would be awarded served purposes beyond 
the highly questionable one suggested by the court of 
appeals — purposes that it continues to serve after Reliable 
Transfer. 


2. The court of appeals’ second reason for conclud- 
ing that Reliable Transfer warrants a change in prejudg- 
ment interest law is not only mistaken but amounts to an 
argument in favor of the mutual fault rule. The court of 
appeals acknowledged that “there are decisions stating 
that, at least where fault is mutual, prejudgment inter- 
est .. . should be denied due to the uncertainty as to 
which party will bear the ultimate liability to make pay- 
ment to the other.” Pet. App. lla. The court of appeals 
went on to reject these decision on the following ground 
(id. at 1lla-12a): 


[U]sing uncertainty as a basis to deny interest in 
a mutual fault case made sense under the old 
divided damages regime. This is because under 
that rule, it was not until after trial that there 
would be a determination from the district court 
as to which of the parties would have to come 
up with the extra money to make the other party 
whole. But now with the abolition of the 
divided damages rule and the adoption of com- 
parative fault both parties are liable to each 
other in relation to their respective degree of 
fault. In other words, although the exact amount 


32 


of damages may not be known until after trial, 
neither party can now claim pretrial uncertainty 
as to whether he will in fact be liable to the 
other. Thus, after Reliable Transfer, it only makes 
sense that uncertainty as to eventual liability 
can no longer be a factor to consider in deter- 
mining whether to deny prejudgment interest in 
cases involving mutual fault. 


This reasoning is precisely backward. Reliable Transfer 
increased the uncertainty faced by parties to litigation. 
Often in collision cases, as the court of appeals appears to 
recognize, the total amount of damage is easily ascer- 
tained, at least within a narrow range. That is because the 
damage is mostly to property, not to persons, and asses- 
sors can fix its value. Before Reliable Transfer, as soon as it 
became clear that both parties were significantly at fault, 
both knew, more or less exactly, the extent of their lia- 
bility - each would be responsible for half of the damage. 


Now, however, in a regime of comparative fault, the 
parties cannot know their liability until the end of the 
litigation, when the precise degrees of fault have been 
determined. The court of appeals appears to have recog- 
nized that uncertainty is a reason to mitigate or withhold 
prejudgment interest. It follows that Reliable Transfer, by 
increasing the degree of uncertainty, strengthens, rather 
than weakens, the case for allowing district courts to 
withhold prejudgment interest when there is mutual fault 


and a genuine dispute.
