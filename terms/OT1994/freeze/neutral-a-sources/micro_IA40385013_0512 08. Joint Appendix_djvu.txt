Theories 


Plaintiffs’ theories involving the SSDs are three-fold: 
first, that the SSDs, as agents of the state, were guilty of 
operating a regional system of segregated schools that 
centered on and impacted Kansas City with blacks and 
made the suburbs whiter before 1954; second, that the 
SSDs failed in their affirmative duty to eliminate the ves- 
tiges of the dual school system; and third, that the SSDs 
are liable for the effects of actions by the KCMSD and 
other defendants and can therefore be included in an inter- 
district remedy. Essentially, plaintiffs seek the realign- 
ment or consolidation of the existing KCMSD and sur- 
rounding SSDs, which would purportedly produce an 
integrated educational environment for each child attend- 
ing school in the KCMSD and surrounding school districts. 
These theories, when considered in light of the evidence 
and the prevailing legal standards, must be rejected. 


Because this is an interdistrict case, all the evidence 
comprising this voluminous record must be weighed by 
that standard. Intradistrict cases are inapplicable. The 
linchpin of an interdistrict case, as declared by the Su- 
preme Court, is whether there has been a racially dis- 
criminatory act by each defendant that substantially 
caused segregation in another district. Milliken v. Bradley, 
418 U.S. 717, 745 (1974) (Milliken I). 


Boundaries 


Plaintiffs seek to racially balance the public school 
in the metropolitan area. As Milliken ex- 

plained, however, plaintiffs’ only right is to attend school 
in a unitary school system in the district in which they 


—A-726— 


reside, not to attend school in a racially balanced metro- 
politan area. 418 U.S. at 746-47. There is a tremendous 
distinction between looking at racial imbalance as evi- 
dence of a segregated school system in an intradistrict case 
and equating racial imbalance on an interdistrict basis 
to a constitutional violation: “[T]he use of significant 
racial imbalance in schools within an autonomous school 
district as a signal [in intradistrict cases] which operates 
to shift the burden of proof is a very different matter 
from equating racial imbalance [between two school sys- 
tems in an interdistrict case] with a constitutional viola- 
tion calling for a remedy.” Milliken, 418 U.S. at 741 n.19 
(emphasis added). See also, Lee v. Lee County Board of 
Education, 639 F.2d 1243, 1254-55 (Sth Cir. 1981). 
This being an interdistrict case necessarily requires rejec- 
tion of intradistrict presumptions. 


In order for plaintiffs to prevail, they had to prove that 
the school district boundaries as originally established or 
later maintained, evidenced a racially discriminatory mo- 
tivation; inferences from discriminatory impact would not 
suffice. Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229 (1976). Lack 
of proof of discriminatory intent in the establishment or 
changing of any school district boundary lines requires the 
Court to reject plaintiffs’ arguments and effectively distin- 
guishes the interdistrict cases granting relief upon which 
plaintiffs rely. Cf. Morrilton School District No. 32 Vv. 
United States, 606 F.2d 222, 225-27 (8th Cir. 1979), cert. 
denied, 444 U.S. 1071 (1980); United States v. Missouri, 
515 F.2d 1365 (8th Cir.), cert. denied, 423 U.S. 951 
(1975) (the Kinloch litigation); and Haney v. County 
Board of Education, 410 F.2d 920 (8th Cir. 1969) 
(pre-Milliken). All of these Eighth Circuit cases evi- 
denced racial gerrymandering, an action specifically recog- 
nized in Milliken as justification for an interdistrict rem- 
edy. 418 U.S. at 745. It is axiomatic, however, that 
absent a constitutional violation in one district producing 


a significant segregative effect in another district, the

The hub, therefore, of plaintiffs’ argument is that the 
SSDs are not separate and autonomous but are instead 
agents of the state whose boundaries are constantly dis-

appropriate remedy. Guided once again by Milliken, 
plaintiffs’ position is rejected:

418 U.S. at 741-42. Applying the local control measure 
to Detroit, the Supreme Court concluded there were no 
constitutional violations by those districts that would 
justify “consolidation of 54 independent school districts 
historically administered as separate units into a vast new 
super school district.” 418 U.S. at 743. A review of 
school district existence in Missouri leads the Court to 
conclude the SSDs are more autonomous than those dis- 
cussed in Milliken and numerous other desegregation 
cases. 


Local Autonomy 

Establishment of school district boundaries in Missouri 
is controlled at the local level to a greater degree than in 
Michigan. Compare Mo. Rev. Stat. §§ 162.222, 162.441 


(1978), § 162.431 (Supp. 1983) (requiring approval of 
the qualified voters in each school district affected) with

Bradley v. Milliken, 484 F.2d 215, 247-48 (6th Cir. 
1973) (Michigan school districts are not highly locally 
autonomous because of various Michigan statutes giving 
the Michigan State Board of Education the power to 
change boundary lines and to merge school districts), 
rev'd, 418 U.S. 717 (1974). The state of Missouri does 
not have the power to require a merger or consolidation of 
school districts. Based upon a review of Missouri stat- 
utes and those cited by the Supreme Court in Milliken, 
418 U.S. at 742, and those relied on by the Sixth Circuit 
in Bradley v. Milliken, 484 F.2d 215, the Court finds 
that Missouri school districts have more authority and 
autonomy than Michigan school districts. The independ- 
ence of the local districts in Michigan was sufficient for 
the Supreme Court to conclude interdistrict relief was 
improper. 


The Court finds that school districts in Missouri are 
governed by locally elected boards of education and have 
the authority and autonomy to control boundaries, cur- 
riculum, finance and administrative matters. Based on 
these facts, and on a review of the facts relied upon in 
Lee v. Lee County Board of Education, 639 F.2d 1243, 
1256 (Sth Cir. 1981), the Court finds that the school dis- 
tricts in Missouri have at least as much independence and 
autonomy as school districts in Alabama where inter- 
district relief was denied. 


Public education in Missouri is structured differently 
than public education in Indiana. In Indiana, the state 
has far more authority and control over school district 
boundaries. For instance, in United States v. Board of 
School Commissioners, 637 F.2d 1101, 1124-25 (7th 
Cir), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 838 (1980), the court found 
that legislation affecting the boundary lines of the India- 
napolis Public Schools had been enacted with a racially 
discriminatory purpose. There is no such evidence here. 


Public education in Missouri is also structured differently 
than public education in Delaware. There, the state has

substantially more authority and control over local school 
district internal matters and school district boundaries. 
See, e.g., Evans v. Buchanan, 393 F.Supp. 428, 438 (D. 
Del.), aff'd mem. 423 U.S. 963 (1975). In Evans, the 
court determined that the enactment of the Education

Likewise, public education in Missouri is structured 
differently than public education in Kentucky. There, the 
court referred to school district lines as being “artificially

had been ignored in the past for the purpose of aiding and 
implementing continued segregation. 


There was no credible evidence that any of the bound- 
aries of any defendant school district were established or 
maintained with any racially discriminatory intent. The 
only evidence regarding boundaries concerned the KCMSD 
and Independence, over the Van Horn attendance area, 
and is discussed infra. The boundaries have been properly 
established and maintained by each SSD, and each district 
manages its own school affairs within its own boundaries. 
Establishment of school district boundaries is a local mat- 
ter in Missouri, and the Court finds that each of the bound- 
aries of the school districts involved in this case were 
established through local initiatives, with no taint of any 
racial animus. This fact alone distinguishes the instant 
case from many upon which plaintiffs rely including New- 
burg, Evans, Hoots v. Pennsylvania, 672 F.2d 1107 (3d 
Cir.), cert. denied, 103 S.Ct. 55 (1982) and Morrilton 
School District No. 32 v. United States, 606 F.2d 222 
(8th Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 1071 (1980).

Reviewing plaintiffs’ evidence, the Court is not per- 
suaded by the testimony of Raphael Nystrand to the effect 
that Missouri school districts do not have local control of 
education. Mr. Nystrand based his opinion on standards 
set by the federal government and private accrediting 
agencies, entities over which the state of Missouri has no 
control. (Tr. 6177-78). Actions by these entities have 
no effect on the autonomy of Missouri school districts. 
Moreover, Mr. Nystrand agreed that no single tradition in 
public education was more deeply rooted than local con- 
trol. (Tr. 6178). Indeed, he agreed that the principles 
of local control are a significant theme in school politics 
across the nation. (Tr. 6195-96). 


The Court finds that local school districts in Missouri 
have the following characteristics, powers and authority: 


1. School districts are governed by local boards of 
education that are elected by the voters residing in 
the district. (Tr. 6179). 


2. School districts, not the state, hire superintend- 
ents and teachers. (Tr. 6179-80). 


3. School districts, by a vote of the residents, estab- 
lish their own tax levy. (Tr. 6180; P. Ex. 1737). 


4. Reorganization is a decision made by the local 
school district; a reorganization plan could be sub- 
mitted to the voters of a school district even though 
the Missouri State Board of Education did not ap- 
prove the plan and the voters could adopt or reject 
the reorganization. (Tr. 6184). 


5. The Constitution of Missouri, Art. 3, § 20, 
prohibits the General Assembly from enacting “any 
special law changing the boundaries .. . of any 


school districts.” (Tr. 6181). 


6. Local school districts have the plenary power to 
establish school attendance zones (e.g. P. Ex. 2267).

Eleventh Amendment immunity as an agent of the state 
but must be sued instead in their own names. Mount 
Healthy City School District v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274 
(1977); Miener v. Missouri, 673 F.2d 969 (8th Cir.),

The autonomy of the local districts is evidenced in the 
pre-1954 period by each local school district deciding 
whether to educate their black students, if any, at schools 
within the district or to transfer them to schools in an- 
other district. (Tr. 372, 378, 622, 624, 696-97, 871-72,

to transfer their high school students to high schools in

black and white and usually considered tuition costs and 
space in making such decisions. (P. Ex. 1768, P. Ex.

is a locally autonomous and independent entity. Because

from the state of Missouri, and from all other school dis-

In regard to the first theory, plaintiffs point to interdis- 
trict student transfers made by some of the SSDs pursuant 
to state mandate before Brown v. Board of Education, 
349 U.S. 483 (1954) (Brown 1). The fact that such 
transfers were made before the Supreme Court declared 
separate schools unconstitutional does not entitle plaintiffs 
to relief. Instead, plaintiffs had to prove first that the 
segregated schools existing before 1954 were the direct 
and substantial cause of blacks leaving each defendant 
district; second, assuming that occurred, that it had a 
Significant segregative effect in the KCMSD. See, 418

clear proof of cause and effect and a careful delineation 
of the extent of the effect”). Plaintiffs’ proof was weak, 
speculative and in any event de minimus. 


The “vestiges” theory also fails. First, the affirmative 
duty to eliminate vestiges is premised upon the notion 
that each defendant district produced the segregation 
sought to be eliminated and therefore has a duty to undo 
its damage. Second, the affirmative duty plaintiffs would 
impose (assuming one exists) is far more expansive than 
that required by the Constitution. The Court in Milliken

While it may be within the province of another Court 
to review and rewrite the standard, it is this Court’s ob-

Court to reject plaintiffs’ theories and grant the suburban 
school district defendants’ motions for judgment pursuant 
to Rule 41(b). 


I. Pre-1954 Evidence 


The defendants admitted, and the Court judicially 
noticed, that Missouri mandated segregated schools for 
black and white children before 1954. See, Mo. Const. 
Art. IX, § I(a) (1945) (rescinded 1976) and §§ 163.130,

not immediately and formally abrogated after the Brown 
decision was announced; however, the State’s Attorney 
General issued an Opinion in 1954 declaring them un- 
enforceable. (P. Ex. 2232). The statutes were repealed 
in 1957 and the constitutional provision was finally re- 
scinded in 1976. This historical background is recounted 
in more detail by the courts in Adams v. United States, 
620 F.2d 1277, 1280-81 (8th Cir.) cert. denied, 449 
U.S. 826 (1980); and United States v. Missouri, 363 
F.Supp. 739, 746-47 (E.D. Mo. 197? affd 515 F.2d 
1365 (8th Cir.) cert. denied, 423 U.S. 951 (1975).

House Bill 171 


House Bill 171 (P. Ex. 977) is an example of such a 
measure. It was passed by the Missouri General Assembly 


—A-746— 


and signed by the Governor in 1957. 1957 Mo. Laws 
454. Prior to the enactment of H.B. 171, § 165.563 
provided in part: 


Every city in this state now having or which may 
hereafter have 500,000 inhabitants or over, together 
with the territory now within its limits, or which 
may in the future be included by any change thereof, 
shall be and constitute a single school district. . . . 


Mo. Rev. Stat. § 165.563 (1949) (emphasis added). 
H.B. 171 amended this section so that it would apply to 
Missouri cities having 700,000 or more inhabitants. 


The legislative history demonstrates that H.B. 171 did 
not amend the population level prescribed by § 165.563 
for the first time; the section’s predecessor was enacted in 
1897. Act of March 23, 1897, § 2, 1897 Mo.Laws 221 
(1897). It provided that a city exceeding 300,000 per- 
sons shall constitute a single school district. That statute 
was then amended in 1909 to change the population level 
from 300,000 to 500,000 persons. Act of May 28, 1909, 
§ 1, 1909 Mo.Laws 846 (1909). There is no evidence 
nor did plaintiffs argue that the Missouri General As- 
sembly chose to establish the population level at 300,000 
in 1897 for racial reasons or to amend it to 500,000 in 
1909 for racial reasons.

finding that House Bill 171 was not discriminatorily en-

representative from a legislative district within the KCMSD 
(Tr. 4653), voted for H.B. 171. Representative Neal 
had long been active in promoting civil rights organiza- 
tions such as the NAACP and the Urban League (Tr. 
4653), and he introduced the legislation which led to the 
creation of the Missouri Commission for Human Rights 


in 1957. (Tr. 3130). Second, not one state representa- 
tive or senator, black or white, cast a vote against H.B. 
171. (SSD Ex. 4). Third, the Court finds that the state

Employment Practices 


In an intradistrict setting, courts often consider faculty 
composition as one indicator of a segregated school sys- 
tem. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Educa- 
tion, 402 U.S. 1 (1971). Plaintiffs argue in this inter- 
district context, however, that faculty imbalance between 
the SSDs and the KCMSD is a vestige of the prior dual 
school system and that the lack of black teachers in the 
suburbs discourages blacks from moving to these areas 
and thereby affects the racial composition of the student 
body. The Court rejects plaintiffs’ arguments for two 
reasons: first, the evidence does not support plaintiffs’ 
conclusions that there has been discriminatory hiring by 
the SSDs; and second, there is no credible evidence that a 
link exists between faculty composition and student body 
composition. 


Because this is not a Title VII employment discrimina- 
tion suit of an intradistrict nature, plaintiffs had to draw 
a link between employment practices by the SSDs and 
the racial composition of its student body thereby imply- 
ing an interdistrict effect. Plaintiffs’ evidence, however, 
fails to provide that link. 

Plaintiffs rely primarily on the testimony of their statis- 
tician, Dr. Robert Carlson. Essentially, Dr. Carlson’s

analysis shows that between 1973 and 1980, ten percent 
of the teachers available for hire in the metropolitan area 
were black yet few of the SSDs hired that percentage of 
minority teachers. The conclusion drawn is that employ- 
ment decisions (and ensuing racial composition of the 
faculty) was not a chance occurrence. (Tr. 6448, 6529). 
While the analysis may eliminate chance as a reason for 
the number of black hires, it does not provide a basis for 
plaintiffs’ further conclusion that racial animus motivated 
the districts’ employment decisions and that in turn led 
to an imbalanced student body. 


For the following reasons, the Court finds Dr. Carlson’s 
methodology unsound and his conclusions therefrom un- 
persuasive: first, the analysis assumes each district re-

of the actual number of applications submitted to each 
defendant district from which faculty was hired. (Tr. 
6506). Second, the analysis did not consider if black 
applicants turned down job offers (Tr. 6515) or whether 
there was a personal preference by black teachers to teach 
children of a particular races (Tr. 6483). Instead, Dr. 
Carlson assumed all black applicants would be willing to 
work in the SSDs. (Tr. 6502). Third, Dr. Carlson did 
not consider if the KCMSD, or another district, such as 
Kanasas City, Kansas was hiring an inordinately large

Kansas City School District has appreciably more black

there would be more black students residing in the de-

and staff. In any event, plaintiffs have no constitutional 
right to attend school in a school district with a teaching 
staff of any particular racial composition. Oliver v. Kala- 
mazoo Board of Education, 706 F.2d 757, 762 (6th 
Cir. 1983). As the Sixth Circuit declared, “[AJIl that 
the students are entitled to is the ‘sustained good faith 
effort to recruit minority faculty members so as to remedy 
the effects of any past discriminatory practice.’ (citations

omitted).” Jd. at 762 (footnote omitted). The Court 
notes that the school district in Oliver had already been 
found guilty of discriminatory hiring practices. Certainly, 
a student’s rights in districts not found guilty of constitu-

Grandview 


Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. James Anderson, established that

CONCLUSION OF LAW 


For ease of discussion and understanding, various legal 
standards have been included in the pertinent fact-finding 
sections of this order supra. They are hereby incorporated 
as conclusions of law. In some instances, only brief 
remention of certain cases may be made. 


As indicated earlier, the touchstone of an interdistrict 
school desegregation case is Milliken v. Bradley, 418 U.S. 
717 (1974). Milliken teaches that before district bound- 
aries may be ignored or school districts consolidated for 
remedial purposes, there must be evidence of a constitu- 
tional violation in one district that produces a significant 
segregative effect in another district. The racially dis- 
criminatory acts must be a substantial cause of inter- 
district segregation. 418 U.S. at 744-45. The absence of 
an interdistrict violation and interdistrict effect requires 
interdistrict relief to be denied. /d. 


The theories offered here have been considered—and 
rejected—by the Supreme Court in Milliken. For exam- 
ple, upon finding violations within the predominantly 
black city district but not in the surrounding predomi- 
nantly white suburban districts, the Court declared, 
“Where the schools of only one district have been af- 
fected, there is no constitutional power in the courts to 
decree relief balancing the racial composition of that 
district’s schools with those of the surrounding districts.” 


—A-811— 


418 US. at 749. After assessing the evidence, the Su- 
preme Court held, “Disparate treatment of white and 
Negro students occurred within the Detroit school system, 
and not elsewhere, and on this record the remedy must 
be limited to that system.” /d. at 746. To award the 
relief plaintiffs seek here would be contrary to the express 
mandate from the High Court because it “would impose 
on the outlying districts, not shown to have committed 
any constitutional violation, a wholly impermissible rem- 
edy based on a standard not hinted at in Brown I and II 
or any holding of this Court.” /d. 


The cause and effect nexus cannot be overemphasized. 
As the Fifth Circuit recognized, “[t]he court’s deliberate 
choice [in Millinen] of phrases such as ‘substantial’ or 
‘direct cause’ and ‘significant segregative effect’ expresses 
an insistence that in cases where an interdistrict remedy 
is requested, there must be clear proof of cause and effect 
and a careful delineation of the extent of the effect.” Lee 
v. Lee County, supra, 639 F.2d at 1256. Not only is 
plaintiffs’ evidence here blurred as to cause and effect. 
there is no “careful delineation of the extent of the 
effect.” 


Another recent Fifth Circuit decision elaborated on 
the Supreme Court’s requirement of an “interdistrict vio 
lation”: “The requisite segregative intent must be not 
simply to discriminate within the offending system but 
further a clear intent to cause segregative effects within 
the neighboring district.” Taylor v. Ouachita Parish 
School Board, 648 F.2d 959, 969 (Sth Cir. 1981). As 
clearly evident by the findings of fact made herein. plain- 
tiffs have failed to prove that conduct by any SSD met 
this standard. 


Furthermore, the Court has rejected plaintiffs’ “effects” 
argument which would have held the SSDs liable and 
incorporated them in an interdistrict remedy on the basis 
of alleged wrongs committed by other actors, e.g.. the 
state. The Taylor court also rejected the “effects” argu- 
ment reasoning if: 


—f$iS— 


[iJnterdistrict effect alone transform otherwise intra- 
district unconstitutional actions into an interdistrict 
violation, then the Milliken insistence that ‘without 
an interdistrict violation and interdistrict effect, there 
is no constitutional wrong calling for an interdistrict 
remedy’ contains a redundancy: the only interdistrict 
thing about the violation would be its effect. 


648 F.2d at 969. 


As articulated in Columbus Board of Education Vv. 
Penick, to make out a fourteenth amendment equal pro- 
tection claim, plaintiff must show a discriminatory pur- 
pose, not just disparate impact and foreseeable conse- 
quences. 443 U.S. 449 (1979). Plaintiffs’ evidence sim- 
ply does not make the intentional cause and effect connec- 
tion. 


This Court is compelled to follow the frequent admoni- 
tion from the Supreme Court to tailor the remedy to fit 
the scope of the violation. Dayton Board of Education 
v. Brinkman, 443 U.S. 526 (1979) (Dayton if), Hills 
v. Gautreaux, 425 U.S. 284, 293-94 (1976), Swann v. 
Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 US. ie 
16 (1971). The Court in Dayton specifically criticized 
the lower court for aggregating a series of violations ‘o 
add up to a significant “cumulative” violation—a proce- 
dure plaintiffs would have us adopt here. The Court notes 
Dayton was not an interdistrict case, however, the mes- 
sage gieaned is to correct only the violations found, and 
nothing more expansive is justified or permissible. There- 
fore, the constitutional violation must be substantial. Nei- 
ther cumulative nor de minimus violations will justify 
interdistrict relief. 433 U.S. 406, 413 (Dayton I). At 
most, plaintiffs’ evidence is only de minimus and is there- 
fcre legally insufficient to justify the relief sought against 
the suburban school district defendants. 


A review of the standards applicable to various 
issues raised by confirms the Court’s conclusion. 


—A-8 13— 
Pre-1954 


The parties stipulated to the existence of the state man- 
dated dual school system and the Court took judicial 
notice of the fact that private discrimination and segrega- 
tion were prevalent in years past. Nonetheless, plaintiffs 
presented months of testimony regarding discriminatory 
conditions existing from the turn of the century to the 
Brown decision in 1954. For the Court today to find a 
constitutional violation based on that evidence, however, 
plaintiffs needed to prove by a preponderance that the 
SSDs were the original segregative actors and that vestiges 
of those past acts remain. It is in this latter respect that 
plaintiffs’ proof floundered. Regarding physical segrega- 
tion in schools during the early decades of this century, 
the Supreme Court stated “[bJoth by reason of the sub 
stantial time that [had] elapsed and because these prac- 
tices have ceased, . . . the foregoing will not necessarily 
be deemed to be evidence of a continuing segregative 
policy.” Dayton I, supra, 433 U.S. at 411. The Court 
has found nocontinuing segregative policy by these 
defendants. 


Another factor negating the importance of plaintiffs’ 
voluminous pre-1954 evidence is simply its age. Our 
society is dynamic; myriad factors produce a multitude 
of simultaneous decisions and consequent effects. If it 
were a Stagnant entity, the pernicious effects of de jure 
segregation would be obvious. Due to its fluid nature, 
however, acts now 30 years past have negligible present 
effects. Such is the fate of the discriminatory practices 
plaintiffs took months to develop. Too many events have 
intervened, reshaping earlier actions. Lee v. Lee County, 
supra, 639 F.2d at 1259. 


The Brown Duty 


Plaintiffs’ evidence also supports the Court’s conclusion 
that all SSDs have met their constitutional obligation to 
operate a unitary school system. That obligation was ini 
tially articulated in Brown II 349 U.S. at 300: “At stake 


—A-814— 


is the personal interest of the plaintiffs in admission to 
public schools as soon as practicable on a nondiscrimina- 
tory basis.” Defendants are required to “make a prompt 
and reasonable start toward full compliance with our May 
17, 1954, ruling. Once such a start has been made, the 
courts may find that additional time is necessary to carry 
out the ruling in an effective manner.” Id. (emphasis 
added). As the findings in the individual district sections 
show, each suburban district, within a maximum of four 
years of Brown, was sending its students to school without 
regard to race. Most districts were doing so within one 
year of the Brown decision. (See individual findings 
supra). Prior to Brown and pursuant to state statute, the 
local school districts had been operating a dual school 
system. Certainly such prompt action dismantling the 
dual system following Brown was accomplished “with all 
deliberate speed.” 349 U.S. at 301. The duty of each 
SSD was to dismantle its dual school system; not the dual 
system in some other district. 


And once a racially neutral school system had been 
established, racial imbalance resulting from other than 
purposely segregative acts is beyond the authority of the 
Court to correct. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board 
of Education, 402 U.S. 1 (1971). See also Ross V. 
Houston Independent School District, 699 F.2d 218, 225 
(5th Cir. 1983) (“[s]chool officials who have taken effec- 
tive action have no affirmative 14th amendment duty to 
respond to the private actions of those who vote with 
their feet.”) The constitution does not compel school 
officials to correct conditions over which they have no 
control, like residential choices. Berry v. School District, 
442 F.Supp. 1280, 1326 (W.D. Mich. 1977) aff'd 698 
F.2d 813 (6th Cir.), cert. denied 104 S.Ct. 235 (1983). 
The Court well remembers the observation of the Supreme 
Court that there is just so much baggage that a school 
case can carry. Swann, supra, 402 U.S. at 22-23. More- 
over, evidence of housing discrimination was presented in 
the Milliken case and discussed in the concurring and dis- 
senting opinions (418 U.S. at 753, 757, 762 & 781) yet 


evidence 


aiheis— 


the majority opinion was based on findings regarding 
conduct by school officials, not housing actors.

As discussed earlier, to find the SSDs vicariously liable 
for acts of the state, the Court must make the threshold 
determination that the SSDs are mere agents or arms of 
the state and not independent, locally autonomous entities. 
After applying the test for autonomy as directed by Milli- 
ken, the Court already concluded the SSDs are independ- 
ent and their boundaries must be respected. 


Whether plaintiffs’ evidence was sufficient to sustain a 
finding of liability against other actors in the housing 
market is irrelevant to the instant motions to dismiss filed 
by each SSD defendant. The nexus necessary must be 
between the conduct of the defendant school districts and 
actions of those responsible for shaping residential pat- 
terns. The absence of such a connection forbids a find- 
ing of liability against the SSDs to remedy any racial 
imbalance that may be attributed to those housing mar- 
ket actors. Bell v. Board of Education, 683 F.2d 963 


(6th Cir. 1982).

the law as applied in this Circuit. Probably the most

_As discussed earlier, plaintiffs’ reliance on the older 
Eighth Circuit cases of Morrilton, Kinloch, and Haney 
is misplaced because those cases involve racial gerry- 
mandering of school district lines, an issue absent here. 
Likewise, plaintiffs’ reliance on the Indianapolis and 
Pennsylvania cases is inappropriate because there, state 
legislation having obvious multidistrict racial effect was 
found as the basis for interdistrict relief. See, United 
States Vv. Board of School Commissioners, 637 F.2d 1101 
(7th Cir.) cert. denied 449 U.S. 838 (1980) and Hoots 
Vv. Pennsylvania, 672 F.2d 1107 (3rd Cir.) cert. denied, 
103 S.Ct. 55 (1982). 


Recent cases applying the interdistrict standards of 
Milliken have summarily rejected all-encompassing al- 
legations unsupported by evidence of significant inter- 
district violations. See, e.g., Bronson v. Board of Educa- 
tion, 578 F.Supp. 1091 (S.D. Ohio 1984), Goldsboro 
City Board of Education v. Wayne County Board of 
Education, No. 81-43-CV-8 (E.D.N.C. June 10, 1983). 
and Armour v. Nix, No. 16708 (N.D. Ga. Sept. 24, 
1979) affd mem., 446 U.S. 930 (1980). The similarity 
between plaintiffs’ theories and evidence in those cases 
and the case at bar confirms the Court’s conclusions 
herein. Even if the Court were to view plaintiffs’ evi- 
dence liberally, which under Rule 41(b) it is not required 
to do, the violations proved are at most, de minimus 
and far removed in time. Therefore, interdistrict relief 
must be denied and the Rule 41(b) motions of the SSDs 
granted.
