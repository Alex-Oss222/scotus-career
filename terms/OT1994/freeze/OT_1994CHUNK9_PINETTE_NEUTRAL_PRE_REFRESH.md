## Material facts and the judgment below

Capitol Square is state-owned land surrounding the Ohio Statehouse in downtown Columbus. The Statehouse dominates the square and is visible from every point on it. The Board regulates its use. Longstanding access includes speeches and gatherings by diverse religious, secular, commercial, and unpopular speakers. Temporary unattended displays have included a privately sponsored menorah, the United Way campaign thermometer, and arts-festival booths. The district court found a traditional public forum and no operative general policy forbidding freestanding displays. These are actual findings, not a conclusion that every location in a government building or every permanent monument is an open forum. The precise full text of every permit criterion is not reproduced here; no disputed criterion is treated as an independent ground for this denial.

On November 18, 1993, the Board voted against December displays. The stipulated record says that vote was invalid because it lacked the statutorily required five votes. On November 23, the Board approved a Christmas tree and menorah. This is more precise than treating November 18 as a valid categorical closure later reopened. Rabbi Capland's November 29 application for a menorah for December 8–16 was granted that day. Carr applied for the Klan that same day to erect a cross for Christmas, December 8–24. Keller denied the application December 3 on stated federal and state constitutional grounds. The administrative appeal was heard December 17; the Board adopted the hearing officer's recommendation denying access December 21. No security risk from the cross was asserted. The district court ruled that an alleged failure to post a bond was not a proper ground for denial and was not properly before it; this packet supplies no contrary finding.

The proposed cross was approximately ten feet high and six feet across, white-painted lumber held upright by a freestanding tripod. Counsel's December 9 letter offered a sign readable at a distance identifying private erection without governmental support, describing respect for the holiday season, and asserting equal expression of religious views on public property. Counsel expressly offered to negotiate its content. The stipulation and district findings establish that offer. They do not establish the size, legibility from every vantage, or effectiveness of the sign later placed on the cross.

Both merits briefs discuss an actual identifying sign. Ohio argues that it was small, buckled in the wind, and ineffective for distant viewers, motorists, children, non-English speakers, and persons unable to read; it also argues that no disclaimer could overcome the Statehouse setting. Respondents say the sign identified the display as private, deny favorable governmental treatment, and treat the sign as one contextual consideration. Their brief concedes that a disclaimer may fail when powerful contrary evidence of endorsement, such as privileged placement or elevation, exists. These are litigation positions, not new judicial findings resolving the actual sign's effectiveness. The lower appellate opinion characterizes the display as privately funded and maintained and expressly disclaiming government support. Its attribution conclusion remains the legal conclusion under review.

The Christmas tree remained throughout the relevant December period. The menorah had already left when the cross was erected on the evening of December 21. Respondents also invoke the State's seasons-greetings banner and the publicity about official opposition. Neither simultaneous co-display of the cross and menorah nor universal public knowledge of the dispute is a fact here. Publicity and the Klan's identity cannot be converted into conclusive public understanding of sponsorship.

District Court, December 21, 1993, Case C2-93-1162: the court consolidated the December 20 preliminary-injunction hearing with trial on the merits of Count One under Rule 65(a)(2). It held access constitutionally protected and rejected the establishment defense. Its entered judgment, Joint Appendix 178, directs issuance of a permit for the remainder of December 21 through December 24, 1993. It is not a perpetual permit, a general prospective ban on enforcement, or an order for future holiday seasons. The consolidated Count One judgment is the injunction reviewed; no adjudication of additional claims or damages is inferred.

Respondents' brief in opposition, page 2, reports a separate January 4, 1994 district order allowing a rally and denying damages following a January 3 hearing. It expressly identifies the cross injunction alone as the subject of this review and says the State did not appeal the rally injunction. That scoped account supports keeping the rally and damages outside this event; it does not authorize reopening those dispositions or inventing their grounds.

Sixth Circuit, July 25, 1994, 30 F.3d 675: Ryan's panel opinion affirmed. It treated the cross as protected private speech in an equally open traditional forum, relied on the private sponsorship and disclaimer, and rejected the contention that proximity to the Statehouse itself created endorsement. It distinguished the privileged courthouse staircase in Allegheny. The opinion used contextual observations but also broad language that truly private religious expression in a truly public forum cannot reasonably be perceived as governmental endorsement. Both the application and that broad rationale are subject to review. No panel dissent appears in the supplied opinion. The appellate court reviewed factual findings for clear error and legal conclusions anew following the consolidated merits hearing.

## Threshold issue: expiration and repetition

The cross no longer stood and the injunction had expired by appellate review. The Sixth Circuit expressly invoked the exception for conduct capable of repetition yet evading review. It considered the annual holiday period too brief for complete litigation and found it likely that the Klan or another organization would seek access again, adding that future litigation involving the same parties and issues would take at least as long. This is the actual lower explanation, not an unqualified finding that any recurring controversy among different speakers suffices.

Weinstein v. Bradford, 423 U.S. 147, 149 (1975), requires short duration and a reasonable expectation of recurrence to the same complaining party outside a class action. The annual display practice, the organized claimant's actual request and history of forum use, the continued defense of the denial, and the lower court's recurrence assessment support consideration of an inference of renewed exposure. The competing concern is that possibility of another group's future dispute alone cannot satisfy the second element. The sourced excerpts do not contain a specific new-season application or sworn promise to reapply. No such promise is a categorical doctrinal prerequisite; the model must decide whether the existing record supports the required reasonable expectation without inventing an application, waiver, concession, or categorical standing rule.

Respondents' brief in opposition, page 2 and footnote 3, expressly does not contest the repetition exception and reports public statements that respondents would seek the display again in 1994 if a menorah were permitted. The footnote itself identifies those press statements as outside the record. This is additional party-specific evidence of a claimed conditional intention, not a judicial finding, verified new application, satisfaction of the condition, or jurisdictional stipulation binding the Court. Keep that source qualification in any threshold analysis using it.

If the record sustains that exception, merits review does not revive the expired permit. Affirmance would leave the adjudicated right and rejection of the 1993 denial in place within the stated forum and circumstances; future applications remain subject to applicable lawful rules. If the exception fails, any threshold disposition must address the expired judgment under the applicable mootness and vacatur principles, without deciding establishment. A material factual uncertainty cannot be repaired by an invented future event. No new contempt, damages, attorney-fee, bond, or 1995 permit issue is adjudicated by assumption.

## Law entering each merits issue

The First Amendment's speech and religion clauses apply to the State through the Fourteenth Amendment. Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association, 460 U.S. 37, 45–46 (1983), distinguishes traditional/designated forums from nonpublic forums. A content-based exclusion in the open forum requires a compelling interest and narrow tailoring; neutral time, place, and manner regulation retains its distinct rules. Religion does not remove expressive conduct from protection. Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 269–277 (1981), applies equal access to religious worship and discussion within a generally available university forum, rejects establishment as a justification in that setting, and preserves the distinction between incidental benefit and governmental advancement. Its holding is contextual, including broad access and the absence of empirical religious domination; it does not constitutionalize every form of government religious aid.

**Religious viewpoints in an after-hours school forum**, present operation effective June 7, 1993, Lamb's Chapel: the controlling speech holding bars exclusion of a religious perspective on an admitted family-life subject even assuming a nonpublic forum; genuinely neutral subject and activity limits remain. The separate controlling establishment application permits voluntary, public, after-hours access without school sponsorship under Lemon's secular-purpose, primary-effect, and entanglement inquiries. It does not replace that framework, require official devotion, or decide the separate Sunday-worship application. Seven Justices supported the speech formulation; six supported the Lemon-based establishment formulation. See the exact authority and limits in the entering-law copy.

**Official graduation worship and independent religious pressure**, present operation effective June 24, 1992, Lee: the official selection and incorporation of clergy worship in a secondary-school ceremony is forbidden on a five-Justice sponsorship ground, independently of the five-Justice substantial-pressure ground. Private prayer and private equal-access speech remain distinct. Lee does not make coercion universally necessary for establishment or make ordinary offense coercion.

**Denominational selection for general governmental authority**, present operation effective June 27, 1994, Kiryas Joel: purposeful allocation of general civic authority by denomination and the anomalous particular grant without a neutral basis for equal treatment are independently sufficient grounds with distinct coalitions. Neutral accommodation remains possible; religious concentration alone is insufficient. Lemon remains in force. Allocation of governmental power is not identical to granting a temporary private speaker access.

Zobrest, June 18, 1993, is an antecedent-law and preservation remand, not an Establishment Clause aid-merits holding. Historical post-divergence aid or speech outcomes supply no additional rule. Lebron, February 21, 1995, distinguishes governmental entity status from the still-undecided right to a particular display; it does not automatically attribute every private message on public land to government. Hurley, June 19, 1995, protects private expressive composition and distinguishes use of public streets from governmental authorship; it does not decide this establishment-attribution dispute. McIntyre, April 19, 1995, addresses anonymous political leaflets and supplies no categorical invalidation or approval of an attribution sign for an unattended display.

Surviving pre-divergence Establishment Clause authority includes Lemon, Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668 (1984), County of Allegheny v. ACLU, 492 U.S. 573 (1989), and Board of Education v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 (1990). Allegheny's distinctions among the courthouse creche, combined city display, private ownership, governmental setting, and actual presentation prevent treating private ownership alone as dispositive. O'Connor's separate analysis emphasizes contextual endorsement; Kennedy's different analysis permits neutral accommodation while prohibiting governmental religious coercion and establishment. Mergens has differing establishment rationales: O'Connor's four-Justice Part III uses the private/government distinction and safeguards; Kennedy, joined by Scalia, uses a separate rationale; neither writing alone is an eight-Justice comprehensive test. Its school statute and safeguarded voluntary clubs are not this square's permit code.

## Supported opposing positions and lawful paths

The strongest access position is that Ohio created and maintained a place for diverse private expression, including temporary unattended displays, and denied this application because of its religious meaning. A broad equal-access rule could treat genuinely private speech in an actually neutral traditional forum as lacking governmental sponsorship; no preference, financing, control of devotional content, or security ground is established. Widmar, Mergens, and Lamb's Chapel are direct obstacles to treating a religious viewpoint as uniquely disqualified. The objection to this path is that legal ownership and paper access rules may not resolve the actual message conveyed by an unattended prominent display beside the Statehouse; a rule dispensing with contextual attribution needs support and defined limits.

A narrower access path applies existing purpose/effect/entanglement and contextual attribution principles to this forum, private funding and control, ordinary temporary access, the offered disclaimer, and the absence of a privileged state-selected exhibit. The principal counterargument is that spectators may see the cross and Statehouse without learning the forum history or reading a sign. The model must distinguish a reasonably informed observer from an observer presumed omniscient and must state which facts do legal work. A disclaimer can be considered, or a suitably supported attribution measure can be identified, without finding every sign adequate or commanding government to rebut every possible misunderstanding. Whether a better sign is necessary, merely permissible, or irrelevant is a substantive fork, not a clerical detail. No particular new sign text, dimensions, location, or continuing injunction is ordered by this packet.

The strongest exclusion position is that the Statehouse's visible authority and the cross's unattended, dominant religious message create governmental attribution despite private title and neutral formal eligibility. Allegheny establishes that contextual governmental endorsement can matter without coercion and despite private contribution. Ohio argues no disclaimer, publicity campaign, or equal admission of other faiths cures the message in this location. The access reply is that a genuinely open, diverse forum differs materially from the selected staircase display; excluding religion wholesale instead of applying available neutral attribution measures may fail both the compelling-interest premise and narrow tailoring. A judicial conclusion permitting exclusion must explain that distinction, identify the actual establishment injury, and justify exclusion within the forum's constitutional constraints.

A limited remand is a lawful possibility only if a material attribution fact or lawful remedial condition remains unresolved and necessary under the standard actually selected. The offered sign, actual disputed sign, and expired order cannot be collapsed. The model may not create a record dispute merely to avoid choosing among adequately supported legal paths, impose a general compulsory-disclaimer program without a basis, or treat a neutral closure of all future unattended displays as an already enacted rule. The validity of genuine neutral future rules, permanent governmental monuments, official worship, and other locations is outside the particular application unless necessarily decided.
