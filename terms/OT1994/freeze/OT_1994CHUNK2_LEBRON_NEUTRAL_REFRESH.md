## Material record and proceedings below

Lebron sought to display a photomontage criticizing the Coors family's political activity and support for the Nicaraguan contras on the large Spectacular billboard at Penn Station. He disclosed during negotiations that his work was generally political but withheld its particular content. TDI, Amtrak's advertising agent, signed the lease on November 30, 1992, for January and February 1993. The contract reserved approval of advertising copy and allowed TDI to terminate if Amtrak found the material objectionable. Amtrak's vice president responsible for real-estate operations rejected the submitted work under a policy against political advertising; Amtrak notified TDI by letter of December 23, 1992.

The rent is immaterial to the present actor and reachability questions and is not resolved here: the Second Circuit's paragraph 4 reports $16,500 per month; the district opinion's paragraph 10 reports $18,500. Neither amount is a damages finding. The signed agreement would be needed before any amount-dependent relief.

After expedited discovery and a trial on documentary submissions, the district court found governmental action in Amtrak's control of speech on its billboards and held the rejection unconstitutional. Its analysis rested on the government's pervasive relationship with Amtrak and on the nature of the challenged activity. It did not decide the alternative contract theory. The judgment required two months' display beginning six business days after denial or expiration of any stay by the highest court authorized to issue one. The Second Circuit stayed execution pending appeal.

The Second Circuit accepted the described structural and financial facts but rejected the governmental-action conclusion. Paragraph 17 treats Amtrak as a private corporation and invokes the statutory nonagency clause; paragraphs 18–32 then analyze attribution of the challenged conduct. It relied principally on *Myron v. Consolidated Rail Corp.*, 752 F.2d 50, and related cases finding that funding, regulation, stock ownership and board representation did not establish federal responsibility for ordinary corporate choices without the required governmental connection. It rejected confining *Myron* to employment because that case itself presented a First Amendment retaliation contention. Paragraph 32 expressly leaves the speech merits unaddressed.

The appellate judgment reversed and remanded with instructions to dismiss the complaint. Its discussion left the contract claim for state court and also left TDI's counterclaim for a declaration permitting lease termination undecided. These are the actual lower-court directions under review, not a jurisdictional rule adopted by this packet.

Judge Newman's dissent would have treated the advertising decision as governmental action, distinguishing direct regulation of political content from the employment decisions in *Myron*. He nevertheless would not have sustained the mandatory-display injunction: the forum character and appropriate equitable remedy remained separate, and he viewed damages or declaratory relief as possible responses to a proven violation. His disagreement confirms that success on attribution alone does not establish entitlement to the requested display order.

## Entering law and the two reachability paths

The [focused entering-law slice](../entering-law/OT_1994CHUNK2_C_FOCUSED.md) supplies the actual controlling *Yee* and *Bray* holdings. *Yee v. City of Escondido*, April 1, 1992, distinguishes a new supporting argument for a properly presented federal claim from an unpreserved distinct claim, while independently enforcing the question-presented limit. Its preservation/ripeness holding has O'Connor, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas as direct supporters; its Rule 14.1(a) question-scope holding also has Stone's join. The state-court review setting and the particular taking theories limit direct transfer of its applications. Its holding does not expressly decide the effect of a litigant's deliberate disavowal of an argument in this federal First Amendment setting.

*Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic*, January 13, 1993, declines a separate hindrance-clause theory that was neither adjudicated below nor fairly included in the granted deprivation-clause questions and lacked a necessary finding. Scalia, Stone, White, Kennedy and Thomas directly support that scope and mandate. The surviving rule distinguishes additional arguments from an independent claim outside the presented dispute; it does not convert every change in argument into an additional claim, or erase a claim limit because the same constitutional interest is implicated. *Bankers Life* and *Kamen*, as treated in the copied holdings, inform those distinctions. No current-case result may supply their application here.

**Reachability path 1 — consider the entity argument within the preserved First Amendment claim.** The requested relief and alleged injury remain the rejection of this advertisement; entity status may be characterized as another legal reason the challenged act is governmental, and paragraph 17 expressly addresses private status. The supplied question itself mentions the statutory disclaimer. The strongest counterargument is that express disavowal is materially different from merely failing to advance an additional supporting reason: it may have shaped the adversary's submissions and the lower courts' inquiry. This path requires an explanation of why that particular choice does not foreclose review and why the argument remains within the supplied question, without pretending the choice never occurred.

**Reachability path 2 — give the express disavowal limiting effect and assess only the conduct-attribution theory properly litigated.** On this view, the Supreme Court should not resolve an entity-status alternative deliberately excluded below; the appellate private-status passage can be read as a premise on which the parties proceeded rather than sufficient adversarial adjudication of the newly asserted theory. The strongest counterargument is that the federal claim never changed, the question expressly concerns governmental-actor status despite the disclaimer, and the lower judgment relied on private status. This path requires a supported account of the legal effect of the disavowal; *Yee* and *Bray* do not mechanically compel it. Declining this argument does not automatically dispose of the preserved conduct-attribution claim, and this packet selects no procedural disposition.

If the distinction turns on exact original wording, a supposed additional concession, adversarial prejudice, or a claim that the argument was withdrawn on different terms, the affected conclusion requires the actual pretrial submission, appellate brief and, where pertinent, petition and merits briefs. The bounded facts above permit examination of both paths; they do not support invention of those details.

## Actor-status alternatives and statutory boundaries

The original neutral packet's statutory material remains available, subject to these distinctions. The lower opinions quote former 45 U.S.C. §541 as declaring Amtrak outside the listed governmental-entity categories while organizing it as a private, for-profit corporation. The 1994 recodification, Pub. L. 103-272, places the for-profit directive and the nondepartment/nonagency/noninstrumentality classification in 49 U.S.C. §24301(a). Its revision notes identify the predecessor provision and stylistic revision; recodification supplies no constitutional adjudication.

The reported nine-member structure consists of the Secretary of Transportation, five other direct Presidential selections under statutory categories, two selections by the governmental preferred shareholder, and Amtrak's president chosen within the corporate structure. Section 24302's detailed categories and the Secretary's ex-officio role must remain distinct. The district opinion reports the board's authority over advertising, authorization of its TDI agreements, and direct delegation of control to the officer who rejected the advertisement. It does not establish that the full board itself considered this specific rejection.

If reachable, one merits path treats federal creation, public purposes and continuing public direction as establishing constitutional governmental status despite the classification clause. Its strongest counterargument concerns the distinct corporate form and operational autonomy and the need to distinguish publicly aided or regulated private entities. A second path accepts private status and evaluates the particular act under *Jackson*, *Burton*, *Blum* and the pertinent corporate cases. Its strongest counterargument is that insisting on a separate external governmental command for each act can miss governmental responsibility arising from the institution's own structure. A conduct-specific affirmative attribution path must identify a supported governmental nexus or relevant public function, rather than infer attribution from the importance of speech alone. The preservation choice governs which grounds can be reached; it does not predetermine their merits.

The supplied current law also keeps statutory classification, original jurisdiction, constitutional attribution and sovereign immunity distinct. *American National Red Cross* concerns an express charter jurisdiction clause, not constitutional status. *McCollum* concerns use of governmental jury machinery, not every publicly benefited corporation. Current *Hess* concerns a public entity's claim to state immunity, not this speech obligation. Current *FEC* establishes litigation authority and timing rules without a controlling merits rule about governmental control. No holding is enlarged across those boundaries.
