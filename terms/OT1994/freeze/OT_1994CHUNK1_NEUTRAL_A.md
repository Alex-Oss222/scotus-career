## Other governing law

### Issue 1: Merits

- **Other Governing Law:** 21 U.S.C. § 846; Nash v. United States; Singer v. United States; United States v. Felix.
- **Other Authority Used:** Nash supplies the common-law conspiracy baseline; Felix is distinguished because it did not add an overt-act element to § 846.

### Validation additions controlling the neutral framing

The overt-act question is statutory, not a claim that conspiracy punishes an unexpressed thought. The Government must still establish the intentional agreement and its unlawful narcotics object. The Government's strongest path reads §846's omission against the common-law agreement offense and Congress's express overt-act language in §371. Shabani's strongest competing path invokes the settled circuit element and the traditional requirement of conduct rather than mere intention, with lenity only if the statute remains ambiguous after ordinary construction. The lower court itself recognized the textual weakness of its rule but considered circuit precedent binding.

The Court may affirm the overt-act requirement and preserve reversal for the instructional omission, or reject that added element and reverse the appellate legal ruling with a remand for the expressly unresolved sufficiency and other claims. No record here supports deciding those other claims or ordering unconditional conviction reinstatement. An acquittal would require an independent supported insufficiency ruling.

Entering law: Felix's distinct-agreement rule, effective March 25, 1992 and retained within the June 28, 1993 Dixon register, supports distinguishing agreement from substantive acts; it does not construe §846's element. Ratzlaf (January 11, 1994), Staples and Posters 'N' Things (both May 23, 1994) supply offense-specific criminal-construction comparisons, not an overt-act rule. ### Verified lower record, statute and competing lower rationales

[United States v. X-Citement Video, 982 F.2d 1285 (9th Cir. Dec. 16, 1992)](https://static.case.law/f2d/982/cases/1285-01.json), local `xcitement_lower.json`. Facts, majority scienter reasoning, disposition, operative statutory quotation and Kozinski's alternative were reviewed. The complete judicial text is retained, including other facial challenges and footnotes.

Gottesman and the corporation were prosecuted after 1986-1987 undercover transactions involving tapes featuring Traci Lords. The officer said he wanted tapes made while she was under eighteen. The described transactions were 49 tapes delivered directly and eight sent to Hawaii on the officer's instructions. The judgment followed a **bench trial**, not a jury instruction. The opinion reports conviction for shipping, distributing and conspiring; Gottesman received twelve months and a $100,000 fine. After initial appeal the case was returned for reconsideration in light of Ninth Circuit Thomas, and the district court again upheld the Act.

Canby's majority considered Thomas binding on the statutory reading: knowingly modified the handling acts, not the performer's minority. It rejected §2256 vagueness/overbreadth attacks, but concluded §2252 lacked constitutionally required knowledge of minority and reversed on facial grounds. It therefore did **not** decide the as-applied contention that the tapes were not child pornography, or the indictment scienter objection. Its opinion distinguishes knowledge of a fact (minority) from knowledge of legal classification/illegality. The Government's precise oral concession is described in the lower opinion, and should be attributed to that setting rather than silently carried into Supreme Court briefing.

Kozinski agreed reversal was needed under the strict-liability trial theory but would construe a recklessness requirement and remand for retrial rather than invalidate the Act. His source is Osborne's acceptance of Ohio's independently supplied recklessness element. The lower panel thus offers two different judgment/rationale/remedy routes; merely rejecting facial invalidity does not establish an actual trial finding of the knowledge ultimately required.

Section 2252(a)(1) and (a)(2) are separately quoted in the source. Subsection (a)(2) includes receiving/distributing with its commerce/mail nexus and separately knowingly reproducing for distribution. ### Justice-specific authorities and current doctrine

- Current **Staples**, Holdings criminal-procedure and OT1993 chunk 5: **Thomas**, joined by **Scalia, Kennedy and Souter**, adopts the contextual factual-knowledge requirement for characteristics making the rifle a machinegun. **Ginsburg**, joined by **O'Connor**, concurs on the same narrow knowledge requirement. **Stevens** dissents. The surviving distinction is factual knowledge versus knowledge of nonregistration or illegality; the public-welfare analysis is offense-specific.
- Current **Ratzlaf**, OT1993 chunk 1: **Ginsburg**, joined by **Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter**, adopts the express-willfulness construction and independent residual-lenity alternative. **O'Connor and Thomas** join Blackmun's dissent, which still requires actual reporting knowledge and deliberate evasion rather than strict liability. This is not a universal legal-knowledge rule.
- Current **Posters 'N' Things**, OT1993 chunk 5: **Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg** join the actual factual-knowledge rule; **Scalia**, joined by **Kennedy and Thomas**, agrees with the sufficient likely-use/knowledge ground while retaining an additional seller-intent route. The difference concerns statutory coverage, not elimination of knowledge.
- [United States v. Aversa, 984 F.2d 493 (1st Cir. Jan. 13, 1993)](https://static.case.law/f2d/984/cases/0493-01.json), local `aversa.json`: **Breyer's concurrence was read in full**. He emphasizes avoiding punishment of an innocent state of mind under technical criminal laws, regards tax/currency parallels as significant, and accepts the en banc knowing-or-reckless-duty construction as close enough to that concern. This is evidence of a pre-Court interpretive position; **current Ratzlaf governs the currency offense**, and the Aversa rule cannot displace it or establish §2252's element.
- [Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103 (1990)](https://static.case.law/us/495/cases/0103-01.json), local `osborne.json`: the scienter, narrowing and related procedural passages were reviewed. **O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy** join White; **Stevens** joins Brennan's dissent. The majority relied on a separate Ohio default-culpability statute to supply recklessness and required the other narrowed elements to govern the conviction. Its setting does not itself supply a federal default statute. The dissent includes a distinct objection to treating the scienter-instruction claim as forfeited despite the overbreadth objection. No position on §2252 is attributed to any of these Justices from Osborne alone.
