## Corrected factual and decisional scope

The stipulated transactions are Oklahoma sales by a physically present carrier of tickets for trips originating in Oklahoma and terminating elsewhere. Jefferson is qualified to do business and operates as a common carrier in the State. Local services support sale, loading and travel on Oklahoma roads. Tickets for wholly out-of-state trips, remote purchases, and the separate Greyhound/Eagle record are outside these adjudicated facts.

The four disputed tax periods are September and October 1989 and January and February 1990. The amount in controversy is $46,659.15, a stipulated claim amount rather than a new award. The rate was four percent before the February 1, 1990 increase to four and one-half percent. Other withdrawn permit/withholding amounts remain withdrawn. The claim's treatment remains governed by the Chapter 11 proceeding and confirmed plan; a Commerce Clause ruling does not authorize payment outside lawful bankruptcy priorities and procedures.

The bankruptcy court found substantial nexus and fair relation satisfied. It found external apportionment defective and treated that failure as discriminatory effect, despite facial neutrality and no apparent protectionist purpose. The district court and Eighth Circuit stopped after external apportionment. All four factors, including internal consistency, are actively contested in the Supreme Court briefs. No factor is declared conceded or waived. There is no Oklahoma credit for the hypothetical other-state levy, and there is no proved second assessment.

## Proposition-level grounds adopted by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg

**Substantial nexus.** A physically present carrier's completed local sale of transportation originating in the State supplies substantial nexus for the sales tax at issue. Quill keeps the Commerce connection separate from due process and its particular remote-collection rule; National Geographic's actual local business connection is the closer analogy. The contrary argument identifies travel performed elsewhere, but nexus concerns the taxable transaction rather than a claim to regulate or tax every mile independently. This is not a ruling about a remote vendor or an Oklahoma ticket for transportation wholly outside Oklahoma.

**Internal consistency.** Applying this same tax to the same category of completed local retail sale in every State would tax this sale once. The statutory inclusion of furnishing taxable services in the definition of sale does not make each segment of the ensuing interstate trip a new retail sale of the ticket. Jefferson's contrary definition-based argument is considered and rejected for this actual taxable event, rather than treated as conceded. The conclusion assumes the identical local-sale application, not that any differently designed tax imposed by another State must be valid.

**External consistency.** The complete local retail sale may be measured by its full price although the purchased service includes interstate performance. That is an allocation of the consumer sales event, not a claim that Oklahoma supplied all transportation or that travel outside the State has no value. Central Greyhound's gross-receipts tax on conducting a carrier's multistate transportation business remains subject to apportionment; identifying the different taxable event does legal work beyond changing the nominal taxpayer. Complete Auto's rejection of purely formal labels remains. The absence of a credit does not establish a constitutional defect where no other State can impose the identical sales tax on this same sale; a hypothetical tax on a distinct use or carrier activity is not automatically duplicate taxation of this purchaser's sale. An actual competing levy with a different demonstrated effect remains outside the record.

**Nondiscrimination.** The stipulated same-rate treatment of intrastate and interstate local ticket sales, coupled with the absence of an established additional discriminatory burden, satisfies this requirement. Equal facial rates alone do not invariably end discrimination review, but the bankruptcy court's adverse practical-effect conclusion rested on the external-apportionment premise now rejected. The $100-trip hypothetical assumes that the tax must reimburse transportation miles rather than tax the local sale; it does not establish favoritism to local commerce once that premise is rejected. No compensatory-tax defense, statewide average, unproved credit, or protectionist benefit is invented. Lohman's insistence on the actual locality and burden is preserved.

**Fair relation.** Broad governmental services supporting the locally taxed activity supply the required relation; the Constitution does not demand a dollar-for-dollar or mile-for-mile accounting of each public service received. The stipulation and bankruptcy findings establish local ticketing and loading operations, protection at those places, and travel on Oklahoma roads. Commonwealth Edison and Goldberg supply the relevant general-service connection, without authorizing a State to tax an unrelated event. Jefferson's strongest objection—that Oklahoma supplies no services along the out-of-state portion—does not defeat a tax whose actual occasion is this adequately connected local sale. The price measures the taxable transaction's value rather than the cost of particular governmental protection.

Each of these five associates directly joins each stated proposition. Their affirmative conclusions do not combine Scalia's different ground into a fictitious additional rationale join. Stevens and Breyer's acceptance of nexus/internal consistency is component agreement, not support for the reversal judgment or the five-Justice external-consistency holding. Their nonreach of other unnecessary factors is not an opposing vote on those propositions.

## Enhanced alternative-path and remedy audit

For the five full-rationale reversal supporters, the strongest contrary theory is that a ticket only embodies the multistate service and cannot be localized through who legally pays the tax. They reject that theory because the retail transaction is independently locatable and different from a levy on the carrier's ongoing interstate business; later use of a purchased service does not always divide the sale itself. A formulation based only on consumer-versus-carrier incidence would not satisfy Kennedy, Souter, Thomas or Ginsburg's actual-event requirement, nor O'Connor's complete apportionment inquiry. The formulation above resolves that barrier by identifying the operative sale and retaining Central Greyhound for its different tax.

For Stevens and Breyer, the strongest contrary point is that their transportation-service approach may require apportioning ordinary sales according to subsequent use. They distinguish this purchase as the directly taxed right to interstate carriage, whose location and mileage can be identified, and regard Central Greyhound as stronger than the goods-sale analogy. They would preserve disallowance on that sufficient constitutional ground without directing a judicial rewrite of §1354. The new record does not supply a changed premise warranting moving either to reversal. Their disagreement is legal characterization, not a claim that the additional findings or stipulations do not exist.

Scalia's strongest contrary argument is that Central Greyhound already condemned the same economic burden despite formal incidence. His narrow answer is that the consumer's local retail sale is not indistinguishable from the carrier-business gross-receipts levy. He agrees that no proved discrimination requires invalidity and would reverse without a universal balancing rule. The full framework remains controlling law; his separate method supplies no license for assembly to rewrite it.

The six reversal supporters would remand with direction that the established Commerce grounds do not justify disallowing Oklahoma's stipulated unpaid-sales-tax claims, allowing their allowance and payment treatment to proceed within normal bankruptcy procedures and the operative plan. No direct execution against the debtor, immediate priority payment outside those procedures, revival of withdrawn amounts, or recomputation of the agreed $46,659.15 is ordered. The requested State instruction to direct payment is not mechanically adopted beyond lawful bankruptcy treatment. No additional remand on the already presented constitutional factors is necessary under their positions. Stevens and Breyer would affirm disallowance; neither proposes an alternative assessment.
