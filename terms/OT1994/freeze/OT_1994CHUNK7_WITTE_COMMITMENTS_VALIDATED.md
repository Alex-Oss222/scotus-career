## Two lawful analytical paths

**Path A - sentence for the offense of conviction:** Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576, 584-586 (1959), supports considering other criminal conduct when fixing punishment for the offense before the sentencing court. McMillan, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), distinguishes sentencing considerations within a legally authorized range from a separately prosecuted offense, subject to its own due-process limits. The first judgment imposed punishment for the marijuana attempt within its authorized range. Relevant-conduct cocaine findings affected the severity of that sentence but were not a cocaine conviction, prosecution, or independent sentence. Felix's evidentiary-use distinction supplies an analogy, not an assertion that Guidelines aggravation is literally trial evidence. Grady remains binding: its predicate is a previously prosecuted offense, and the 1989-1990 cocaine offenses were not prosecuted by the 1992 sentencing judgment. The later prosecution's essential cocaine conduct is not the conduct constituting the later 1991 marijuana attempted possession for which Witte was convicted. Affirm the Fifth Circuit judgment permitting prosecution without adopting its alternative successive-punishment-authorization rationale.

**Path B - actual separate punishment despite the caption:** The cocaine findings were not merely descriptive background; Guidelines inclusion substantially increased the imprisonment starting point. A court cannot necessarily avoid repeated-punishment protection by labeling a substantial penalty marijuana punishment. Kurth Ranch's substance-over-label inquiry warrants examination of operative punishment, although its tax holding is narrow. On this characterization, the later cocaine counts seek to prosecute offenses already substantively punished, and the first conviction's broad statutory ceiling alone does not make the extra cocaine penalty disappear. Reverse and remand to restore the cocaine-indictment dismissal to the extent of the same offenses, leaving the marijuana judgment intact. This path must identify the earlier cocaine punishment, not rest on evidence reuse or a general same-transaction prohibition. It cannot automatically turn every criminal-history factor into a previous prosecution.

**Strongest objection to A:** Mandatory relevant-conduct aggregation is more consequential than evidence used to prove intent; cocaine facts actually drove a new offense level and therefore imprisonment. Answer required by A: the legally authorized marijuana punishment is individualized through those facts, and a sentence within that authority does not adjudicate or impose an independent penalty for every fact contributing to it. The distinction requires legal analysis and is not established simply by saying relevant conduct is evidence.

**Strongest objection to B:** The first judgment adjudicated only a distinct marijuana attempt; ordinary sentencing always assesses conduct and offender characteristics. Treating each aggravating fact as a separately punished offense would distort the sentencing process and Felix. Answer required by B: this claim concerns Guidelines aggregation of an independently criminal episode substantially increasing the punishment calculation, not routine background facts or a same-evidence rule. The actual 144-month departure and the counterfactual marijuana-only sentence must not be turned into an invented exact incremental cocaine term.
