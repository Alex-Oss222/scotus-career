## 13. Tome v. United States — No. 93-6892

### Event, record, and posture

The admitted question is whether original Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(B) permits substantive admission of a prior consistent statement made after the alleged motive to fabricate arose. The judgment reviewed is United States v. Tome, 3 F.3d 342 (10th Cir. August 26, 1993), No. 92-2104, affirming an aggravated-sexual-abuse conviction under 18 U.S.C. §§1153, 2241(c), and then-2245(2)(A)–(B). The District of New Mexico case was CR-91-314-SC. The defendant seeks relief from that affirmance; the Government seeks to preserve it.

The appellate opinion reproduced in the predecision petition supplies the following facts. Tome and Beverly Padilla divorced in September 1988. The Navajo Tribal Court awarded joint custody of their daughter A.T., with primary physical custody to Tome. A.T. was born June 18, 1985. Padilla unsuccessfully sought primary custody in August 1989 but received custody for summer 1990, when she lived in Colorado. Padilla and A.T. did not attend an August 24, 1990 follow-up custody hearing. On August 27 Padilla reported alleged abuse to Colorado authorities. The charged conduct was alleged to have occurred about June 18, 1989, when A.T. was four. These are allegations and procedural facts, not a new independent finding of the abuse.

A.T. testified at the February 1992 trial at age six and a half. The court examined her understanding of truth and falsehood. Direct examination used short answers, leading questions, and dolls. Cross-examination extended over two days. A.T. answered many questions, sometimes failed to respond audibly, and sometimes said she did not remember. Defense questions emphasized her preference for living with her mother in Colorado. The Tenth Circuit found a sufficient opportunity to cross-examine and an implied charge that she fabricated abuse to avoid returning to her father. Those are the lower court's conclusions, not findings that every later Court must adopt.

Six witnesses recounted prior statements: babysitter Lisa Rocha, mother Beverly Padilla, caseworker Kae Ecklebarger, and pediatricians Karen Kuper, Laura Reich, and Jean Spiegel. Rocha described an initial August 22, 1990 statement, followed by a more detailed August 27 account; Padilla overheard the latter. Ecklebarger interviewed A.T. August 29. Kuper examined her in September and October 1990; Reich on September 21, 1990; Spiegel on September 3, 1991. The pediatricians described physical findings they considered consistent with penetration. That evidence is separate from the admissibility of each child's statement. The claimed motive to obtain maternal custody was alleged to predate the challenged statements. Do not turn the allegation of motive into a finding of fabrication, coaching, or guilt.

The jury convicted on March 3, 1992. The Tenth Circuit rejected a categorical premotive condition. It required probative force beyond mere repetition, assessed under Rules 402 and 403, and considered the alleged motive weak and the first disclosure spontaneous. It also rejected the cross-examination, Confrontation Clause, leading-question, cumulative-error, and fundamental-fairness challenges. The scope admitted here centers on the Rule's temporal condition; it does not automatically reopen every rejected appellate argument.

### Exact operative distinctions and alternative grounds

The relevant original Rule 801(d)(1) requires that the declarant testify at the trial or hearing and be subject to cross-examination concerning the statement. Subparagraph (B) requires that the statement be consistent with the declarant's testimony and offered to rebut an express or implied charge against the declarant of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive. The question is what that rebuttal condition entails; a post-1995 amendment supplies no answer.

The Government offered the physicians' testimony alternatively under Rule 803(4), covering statements made for medical diagnosis or treatment describing medical history, symptoms, pain or sensations, or the inception or general character of their cause or external source insofar as reasonably pertinent to diagnosis or treatment. It offered Rocha's, Padilla's, and Ecklebarger's testimony under then-Rule 803(24). The residual-exception synopsis is supplied only to identify the unadjudicated alternative ground, not to decide its construction or satisfaction. Then-Rule 803(24) addresses a statement not specifically covered by the foregoing exceptions but having equivalent circumstantial guarantees of trustworthiness; it requires that the statement be offered as evidence of a material fact, be more probative on the offered point than other evidence the proponent can procure through reasonable efforts, and serve the Rules' general purposes and the interests of justice. The proponent must make known to the adverse party, sufficiently in advance of trial or hearing to give a fair opportunity to prepare, the intention to offer the statement and its particulars, including the declarant's name and address. Whether the statements qualify and whether those conditions were met remain wholly undecided. The Tenth Circuit expressly did **not** decide either exception. Its use of 801(d)(1)(B) for Ecklebarger was an appellate alternative evidentiary ground even though the Government had not expressly offered her testimony under that subparagraph at trial.

The Tenth Circuit's footnote 6 expressly reserved statements offered solely to rehabilitate live testimony because the Government sought their full substantive effect here. Its footnote 9 did not decide a proved parent-coaching case: Tome had made stray assertions but supplied no supporting record evidence or relied-on coaching theory. Its footnote 10 said the aggregate-prejudice objection to admission of all the statements under Rule 403 had not been made and found no plain error. These distinctions prevent conversion of one preserved Rule-construction question into a categorical exclusion of every statement, a finding of coaching, or a preserved cumulative-prejudice ruling.

### Entering law and supported paths

*United States v. Salerno* (June 19, 1992), under **Former testimony, similar motive and the Government's evidentiary objection**, present operation effective that date, requires satisfaction of the chosen hearsay exception's conditions. It does not decide what 801(d)(1)(B)'s rebuttal language means. *Daubert* (June 28, 1993), under **Scientific expert evidence under original Rules 104(a) and 702**, present operation effective that date, displaces incompatible common-law prerequisites but retains compatible common-law guidance. Its Rule 702 factors do not become factors for prior consistent statements. *Williamson* (June 27, 1994), under **Assertion-specific declarations against penal interest**, present operation effective that date, makes contextual assessment distinct from wholesale admissibility and preserves separate admissibility and relief inquiries. Its Rule 804(b)(3) construction is an analogy, not an answer about timing under 801. The entering slice gives each exact coalition and current force; no independent “Current wording authority” different from those entries is supplied for these rules.

Path A is a premotive construction: rebutting the asserted recent fabrication or improper motive means showing consistency before that asserted cause existed. The common-law distinction between substantive admissibility and limited rehabilitation, and the words “to rebut,” support it. The principal counterargument is the absence of an express temporal phrase, the Rules' displacement of incompatible common law, and situations in which circumstances may rebut an accusation even though some motive already existed. A narrow judgment on this path can set aside the 801 ruling and remand the unaddressed alternative exceptions and appropriate error consequences. It need not decide rehabilitation-only use, guilt, or a new trial immediately.

Path B is case-specific relevance without a categorical premotive limit: the text specifies a rebuttal purpose but leaves timing as an important consideration under ordinary relevance rules. The Tenth Circuit, *United States v. Montague*, 958 F.2d 1094 (D.C. Cir. 1992), and *United States v. Miller*, 874 F.2d 1255 (9th Cir. 1989), support that path. Its principal counterargument is that repetition while the same alleged motive persists may not rebut that motive at all, and general relevance cannot replace a specific nonhearsay condition. Even on this path the judge must identify probative force apart from repetition; consistency alone is insufficient.

The date-eligible circuit counterposition includes *United States v. Quinto*, 582 F.2d 224, 233–234 (2d Cir. 1978), and *United States v. Guevara*, 598 F.2d 1094, 1100 (7th Cir. 1979), discussed in the full lower opinion. A potentially narrower formulation would distinguish the particular alleged motive from an abstract motive every witness might have; it cannot be substituted for an unsupported new finding about when A.T.'s actual motive began.

On remand, *Williamson* preserves the Government's burden under Rule 52(a) and *Kotteakos* for any preserved nonconstitutional evidentiary error: whole-record fair assurance that the error did not substantially sway the judgment; grave doubt prevents a harmless finding. *Chapman* applies only if a constitutional trial error is actually reached and found. The already-presented alternative exceptions can be remanded without deciding their satisfaction. The appellate conclusion of no error meant it did not undertake harmless-error analysis for an erroneous 801 admission.

## 14. Young v. Northern Illinois Conference of United Methodist Church — assigned No. 93-1917

### Event, neutral record, and questions

The admitted questions concern whether the First Amendment bars this probationary minister's Title VII race, sex, and retaliation claims challenging advancement and termination, and whether such a bar is jurisdictional or substantive. The lower-court docket is **93-2157**, not the assigned Supreme Court docket. The judgment reviewed is 21 F.3d 184 (7th Cir. April 7, 1994), argued there January 4, 1994, affirming dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

Darreyl Young, a Black woman, served for several years as a probationary minister of the United Methodist Church. She applied to become a Clergy Member in Full Connection or Elder. A review panel denied advancement and terminated her employment March 4, 1992. She alleged race discrimination, sex discrimination, and retaliation for opposing discriminatory practices, including a departure from procedures the church previously followed. The EEOC found no probable cause and issued a right-to-sue letter. She sought reinstatement as a probationary minister, back pay and benefits, reconsideration of her application, compensatory and punitive damages, fees, and costs. These allegations are not findings that discrimination occurred. Her ministerial status is part of the reported case; the source does not establish facts about every category of church employee.

The defendants sought dismissal because civil review would interfere with internal ecclesiastical government and discipline. The district court found it necessary to reach that constitutional question and treated the limit as jurisdictional. The Seventh Circuit affirmed. It relied on *Milivojevich* and the church-minister distinction in *McClure v. Salvation Army*, 460 F.2d 553 (5th Cir. 1972), *EEOC v. Mississippi College*, 626 F.2d 477 (5th Cir. 1980), *Rayburn v. General Conference of Seventh-Day Adventists*, 772 F.2d 1164 (4th Cir. 1985), and *Scharon v. St. Luke's Episcopal Presbyterian Hospital*, 929 F.2d 360 (8th Cir. 1991). It viewed selection and retention of clergy as protected acts, not only religiously motivated acts, and rejected a civil inquiry into whether the church obeyed its internal process. It found **no allegation of fraud or collusion**. Its explanation of the highest church judicatory means the highest body to which this dispute had actually been carried, not necessarily the denomination's theoretically highest institution.

Young argued that ordinary discrimination questions could be resolved without deciding religious doctrine and that limited review of procedure would not require a deep inquiry into religious polity. Her reliance on discrimination's importance does not amount to a concession that the defendant's actual reasons were religious. Nor does her claim to neutral adjudication establish that all requested remedies can be administered without controlling church office.

### Entering law and independent questions

Title VII's prohibition on race and sex discrimination and its retaliation protection must be kept distinct from its religious-employer exemption. Section 702 permits a qualifying religious corporation, association, educational institution, or society to employ individuals of a particular religion to perform work connected with its activities. That express religious-preference exemption does not itself say that race, sex, or retaliation claims are exempt. The Religion Clauses may impose an independent constitutional limit; the construction and constitutional questions must not be collapsed.

*Kedroff v. St. Nicholas Cathedral*, 344 U.S. 94, 116 (1952), protects church government and the selection of ecclesiastical authorities from state control. *Serbian Eastern Orthodox Diocese v. Milivojevich*, 426 U.S. 696, 708–725 (1976), prohibits reexamining hierarchical ecclesiastical decisions under an arbitrariness test that asks whether internal laws were followed. It leaves the narrow fraud-or-collusion possibility unresolved rather than adopting a general exception. *Jones v. Wolf*, 443 U.S. 595 (1979), permits genuinely neutral principles for property questions but does not authorize resolution of doctrinal issues embedded in those questions. *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872 (1990), is a distinct neutral-general-law rule; whether it reaches government selection or removal of clergy is a question requiring analysis, not an automatic consequence of the employment statute's generality.

*Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 681–683 (1946), separates a substantial federal claim's jurisdictional basis from failure to state a claim on which relief is available. Its stated exceptions concern claims immaterial and made solely to obtain jurisdiction, or wholly insubstantial and frivolous claims. Title VII also has its own jurisdictional provision, §706(f)(3), 42 U.S.C. §2000e-5(f)(3), alongside §1331. The inquiry whether this constitutional limit removes adjudicatory power, precludes particular relief, or supplies a defense cannot be resolved merely by repeating the lower court's word “jurisdiction.” Rule 12(b)(1) and Rule 12(b)(6) have different consequences. A decision resting on the actual claim can leave the procedural treatment of disputed ministerial status or unrelated claims for another case.

The entering slice supplies *Lukumi*, under **Religious targeting, comparable secular conduct and strict scrutiny**, present operation effective June 11, 1993; it preserves Smith's neutral-law rule without deciding ministerial employment. *Kiryas Joel*, June 27, 1994, prohibits the specified denominational allocation of civil power and anomalous particular benefit; it distinguishes permission to accommodate religion from governmental sponsorship. *Lamb's Chapel* addresses equal access to an admitted forum, not clergy employment. None resolves Young by itself.

The already-effective *Clearwater* public supplement requires RFRA's claimant-specific substantial-burden and justification inquiry for future governmental enforcement of an old municipal ordinance. It neither adjudicates RFRA's application to this private Title VII suit nor eliminates an independently supported Religion Clause ground. Whether RFRA has been invoked, preserved, and applies in this procedural setting is not established by the lower opinion. Do not silently add a RFRA holding, invent waiver, or assume the later statutory analysis would decide this case. A constitutional disposition independently supported by the admitted questions remains available.

### Paths, counters, and remedies

1. **Church-autonomy bar to this ministerial employment challenge.** Appointment, advancement, and retention of clergy may lie within protected church government, including where the proposed civil inquiry asks whether the church obeyed its own procedures. Reinstatement and compelled reconsideration directly affect ecclesiastical office; damages based on the same selection decision can also pressure that decision. The strongest counter is that enforcing secular race, sex, and retaliation norms need not adjudicate doctrine, that Congress's express exemption is narrower, and that neutral property adjudication demonstrates the absence of blanket institutional immunity. This path must identify why the actual claim and relief concern clergy selection and cannot be turned into immunity from every contract, tort, property, or lay-employment claim.
2. **Permit a bounded secular discrimination inquiry.** Treat ministerial status as insufficient by itself if liability can be resolved through neutral employment facts without deciding faith, discipline, or polity, reserving coercive remedies that would appoint a minister. The strongest counter is that determining pretext and examining the church's departure from its internal process is itself the prohibited intrusion here, and shifting from reinstatement to damages does not necessarily avoid it. No record finding establishes an independent secular claim detached from the challenged ministerial decision.
3. **Jurisdiction and merits remain separable on either path.** One lawful alternative would preserve subject-matter jurisdiction under Bell but recognize a substantive defense on the face of this reported ministerial claim; another would retain the lower court's jurisdictional treatment if church-autonomy authority supports that characterization. The counter to the first is that ecclesiastical nonreviewability might be understood as an institutional limit on civil adjudication; the counter to the second is that determining applicability of federal law and constitutional defenses is ordinarily an exercise of jurisdiction. Any correction to the form of dismissal should specify whether the judgment is modified or vacated and remanded, and whether further pleadings or findings are actually needed, without granting employment or monetary relief merely from correcting the jurisdictional label.

## 15. Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer — No. 92-2038

### Record, lower dispositions, and admitted scope

The admitted scope concerns former §2543's saved-seed exemption and the consequences for the separately pleaded §2541(6) notice claim. Review is of 982 F.2d 486 (Fed. Cir. December 21, 1992), No. 92-1048; rehearing denied March 25, 1993, 989 F.2d 478. The primary source is the predecision petition's complete lower-opinion appendices, not a retrospective account of this Court's decision.

The Winterboers operated a Clay County, Iowa farm, growing corn and soybeans, selling crops for food/feed and protected soybean seed to other farmers for replanting. Asgrow held certificates on two varieties identified in the appellate opinion as A1937 and A2243. The district opinion uses a different ordering of digits for one variety; no distinct-variety dispute is established, and the construction issue does not require silently resolving that transcription discrepancy. For summary judgment the defendants did not dispute Asgrow's protection or their sale of progeny. They claimed approximately 80% of their aggregate crops were sold for nonreproductive purposes and claimed state-law compliance; Asgrow disputed the latter. Those were party assertions, not findings resolving the state-law condition.

The district court held the exemption limited saving and sale to the amount reasonably necessary for the grower's intended next planting, not mechanically to the prior year's acreage; its illustration included reasonably expected acreage expansion. It granted Asgrow summary judgment September 30, 1991 and enjoined sales except qualifying saved seed. It reserved damages, requested briefing on §2567's notice-related damages limits, and expressly declined to decide labeling liability. Its November 14 clarification preserved the distinction between state labeling and the federal notice provision. The district court described the sales as approximately 10,000 bushels, greatly exceeding own-planting needs. Newman's rehearing dissent described 265 acres and 10,529 bushels, enough for roughly 10,000 acres, as undisputed. Quantity evidences the dispute; it is not a legislative quota.

The Federal Circuit reversed, vacated the injunction as a consequence of reversal, and remanded for a full record on exemption eligibility. It rejected the ensuing-crop limit. It interpreted “primary farming occupation” **crop by protected crop**, allowing less than half a particular protected variety's crop to be sold for planting if the other conditions were met. It construed “marketing” in §2541(3) as extensive or coordinated activity such as advertising, sales representatives, or extended merchandising, distinguishing it from qualifying direct farmer sales. It also concluded that an actually exempt §2543 sale is exempt from §2541(6)'s notice requirement, but made no ultimate finding that these sales qualified. Judge Lourie agreed only with rejection of the ensuing-crop limit and the result; he viewed the rest as beyond the briefed issues. Judge Newman dissented from rehearing denial, arguing that production intended for reproductive sale falls within §2541(3), that “saved” has independent force, and that “marketing” bears its ordinary selling meaning. Rader defended the distinctions and syntax. An April 12, 1993 appellate order stayed the mandate pending the petition and final Court disposition; do not infer a new Supreme Court interim order.

### Applicable former statutory text

The relevant §2541 opening reaches unauthorized acts in the United States or regulated/affected commerce, before expiration of protection but after certificate issuance or distribution with §2567 notice, except as otherwise provided in the subchapter. Its relevant clauses are:

> (1) sell the novel variety, or offer it or expose it for sale, deliver it, ship it, consign it, exchange it, or solicit an offer to buy it, or any other transfer of title or possession of it;
>
> (3) sexually multiply the novel variety as a step in marketing (for growing purposes) the variety; or
>
> (4) use the novel variety in producing (as distinguished from developing) a hybrid or different variety therefrom; or
>
> (6) dispense the novel variety to another, in a form which can be propagated, without notice as to being a protected variety under which it was received; or

Former §2543, as reproduced in petition Appendix E, reads:

> Except to the extent that such action may constitute an infringement under subsections (3) and (4) of section 2541 of this title, it shall not infringe any right hereunder for a person to save seed produced by him from seed obtained, or descended from seed obtained, by authority of the owner of the variety for seeding purposes and use such saved seed in the production of a crop for use on his farm, or for sale as provided in this section: Provided, That without regard to the provisions of section 2541(3) of this title it shall not infringe any right hereunder for a person, whose primary farming occupation is the growing of crops for sale for other than reproductive purposes, to sell such saved seed to other persons so engaged, for reproductive purposes, provided such sale is in compliance with such State laws governing the sale of seed as may be applicable. A bona fide sale for other than reproductive purposes, made in channels usual for such other purposes, of seed produced on a farm either from seed obtained by authority of the owner for seeding purposes or from seed produced by descent on such farm from seed obtained by authority of the owner for seeding purposes shall not constitute an infringement. A purchaser who diverts seed from such channels to seeding purposes shall be deemed to have notice under section 2567 of this title that his actions constitute an infringement.

The opening reservation, the proviso's “without regard” language, “such saved seed,” authorized-seed lineage, occupation requirements for buyer and seller, applicable state law, bona fide nonreproductive channels, and diversion notice all must receive their own treatment. The circuit's interpretation that a brown-bag buyer cannot qualify to save seed because it was not obtained with owner authority is a **lower-court construction**, not additional words of the statute. The own-farm and sale clauses must not be compressed into a nonexistent express numerical cap.

### Enacted 1994 amendment and transition

Pub. L. 103-349 was approved October 6, 1994. Section 10 deletes the first sentence's farmer-sales proviso; section 13 makes gender-neutral changes. Section 15 provides: “This Act and the amendments made by this Act shall become effective 180 days after the date of enactment of this Act.” That date is **April 4, 1995**, after this scheduled event.

Section 14(a), except as provided in §14, continues prior law for any variety whose certificate was issued before the effective date and any variety whose application is pending on that date. Section 14(b) permits refiling a pending application on or after effectiveness; a refiled application's eligibility and protection terms then follow the amended Act, while §42 uses the original filing date. Section 14(c) requires a notice seeking the amended Act's protection to state that amended protection and applies §128 sanctions to false or misleading claims or labels. Section 9's new §2541(b)(3) also expressly extends the specified contract-producer rule to old protected varieties; that contract rule concerns lawn, turf, forage grass, alfalfa, or clover and supplies no soybean-sales exception here. These provisions are enacted future operative text, not current liability rules on January 18. No finding that either certificate was refiled or qualified for a transition exception is supplied.

Thus the former Act governs for reasons stronger and more specific than an abstract rule against retroactivity. Under simulated *Landgraf* and *Rivers*, **Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct**, present operation effective April 26, 1994, Congress's temporal direction is the first inquiry. The pending appeal does not make future text govern earlier conduct or erase the grandfather provision. Do not announce that the amendment necessarily eliminates all later farmer sales of every old certified variety.

### Paths, counters, and disposition limits

**Own-farm/purpose path:** Construe the opening reservation and “saved” to exclude production undertaken as a step in marketing protected seed for planting, while permitting genuinely retained own-planting seed to be sold under the proviso if plans change and its conditions are met. The chief counter is that “for seeding purposes” grammatically modifies “obtained,” and the express sales proviso and its “without regard” phrase must not be deprived of work by a breeder-protection preference. A purpose rule need not be a fixed percentage or a fixed prior-acreage quota. Evidence of intended use and the actual statutory elements remain necessary.

**Broader sale path:** Treat the proviso as granting qualifying farmers an actual reproductive-sales privilege, limited by farmer status, authorized lineage, purchaser status, state law, and retained restrictions. The chief counter is that broad routine production for seed sale may consume the reserved §2541(3) protection and the limitation “such saved seed.” The Federal Circuit's particular less-than-half, variety-specific rule is a further construction that must be defended separately; it is not automatically equivalent to the farmers' aggregate-farming argument.

**Narrow remand path:** Reject the particular quantitative/syntactic rule or marketing construction without adopting every alternative construction below, then remand eligibility and the notice claim. Lourie's separate opinion supports caution about adjudicating unbriefed provisions. A general rule about saved seed does not itself resolve the buyer's statutory qualification, state-law compliance, every §2541(6) element, marking under §2567, damages, or precise injunction scope. If a sale actually falls within §2543, the omission of §2541(6) from its opening exceptions bears on notice; if it does not, §2541(6) still requires proof of its own terms. Rejecting the broad exemption does not alone prove notice infringement or a dollar award.

## 16. United States v. Mezzanatto — No. 93-1340

### Event, facts, and judgment reviewed

The admitted question is whether a defendant can knowingly and voluntarily waive Rules 410 and 11(e)(6) so that plea-discussion statements may be used to impeach his inconsistent trial testimony. Review is of 998 F.2d 1452 (9th Cir. July 7, 1993), No. 92-50261, reversing the Southern District of California conviction and remanding for a new trial. The Government seeks reversal; Mezzanatto seeks preservation of the exclusion and retrial judgment.

Mezzanatto requested a plea discussion with the Government. The prosecutor required, before the discussion, agreement that statements could be used to impeach later inconsistent trial testimony. Mezzanatto agreed. The full lower opinion establishes the agreement, failed discussion, later inconsistent testimony, preserved trial objection, impeachment use, conviction under 21 U.S.C. §841(a)(1), and sentence of 170 months plus five years' supervised release.

The Government's predecision petition adds, with trial-transcript citations, that Mezzanatto and counsel requested the October 17, 1991 meeting; the prosecutor said he need not speak, required complete truthfulness, excluded case-in-chief use, and allowed consultation with counsel before assent. It describes agreed impeachment and subsequent perjury/false-statement uses. It reports that, at trial, Mezzanatto claimed he believed a package contained explosives connected to Shuster's claimed CIA work rather than methamphetamine; the prosecutor cross-examined him on contrary proffer statements, and an agent recounted the statements after he denied them. This is the Government's sourced account, consistent with the lower court's dispositive posture; it does not establish the admissibility of unlitigated uses. No new finding of actual coercion, fraud, or ignorance is supported. Wallace's dissent explicitly treated this waiver as knowing and voluntary; the majority adopted a categorical legal rule instead of resolving enforceability against Mezzanatto on individualized grounds.

Judge Sneed's opinion, joined by Judge Hall, reasoned that the rules' express exceptions omit ordinary impeachment, that Congress rejected a version allowing impeachment generally, and that advance waivers would chill candid bargaining and permit prosecutors to exact the protection as the entry price for negotiations. It invoked the public-interest limit on private waiver from *Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil*, 324 U.S. 697 (1945). It distinguished *Town of Newton v. Rumery* and the general analogy from waivable constitutional rights. The majority separately found the error not harmless because the statements materially undermined the defense. Its footnote 4 did not call every violation automatically reversible: failure to object could forfeit protection and error remained subject to harmlessness. That terminology must be reconciled with simulated *Olano*'s distinction between forfeiture and intentional waiver.

Chief Judge Wallace dissented. He relied on the usual ability to waive personal constitutional and statutory protections, *Peretz*, *Rumery*, and the actual counseled agreement. He found the predicted system-wide bargaining harm speculative, addressed the actual impeachment use, and rejected categorical invalidity solely because prosecutors might misuse waivers. He would affirm without reaching harmlessness. The panel rejected Mezzanatto's other appellate contentions; it did not identify another conviction-reversal ground. Its sentencing reference to historical *Dunnigan* is a lower-court citation, not independent adoption of post-divergence Supreme Court law.

### Rule text, exceptions, and entering law

Then-Rule 410 provides that, except as otherwise provided in the rule, the following is not admissible against the defendant who made the plea or participated in discussions in any civil or criminal proceeding: (1) a guilty plea later withdrawn; (2) a no-contest plea; (3) statements in Rule 11 or comparable state proceedings regarding either foregoing plea; and (4) statements during discussions with an attorney for the prosecuting authority that do not result in a guilty plea or that result in one later withdrawn. Such a statement is admissible (i) when another statement from the same plea/discussion has been introduced and fairness calls for contemporaneous consideration, or (ii) in a criminal perjury or false-statement proceeding if made **under oath, on the record, and in counsel's presence**. Rule 11(e)(6) is substantively parallel. The perjury exception is not an exception permitting ordinary impeachment merely because trial testimony conflicts. General admissibility, express exceptions, and consensual waiver are different questions.

Simulated *Salerno*, present operation effective June 19, 1992, preserves actual Rule conditions and rejects treating independent contradiction as selective disclosure. It does not hold all evidentiary protections nonwaivable. Simulated *Godinez*, **Capacity to plead or waive counsel and independent valid waiver**, present operation effective June 24, 1993, distinguishes competence from an actually knowing, intelligent, voluntary waiver; it does not decide whether this Rule is waivable at all. Simulated *Olano*, under **Forfeited error and discretionary correction under Rule 52(b)**, present operation effective April 26, 1993, distinguishes a failure timely to assert a right from a valid intentional relinquishment of a known right. The latter extinguishes error; the former permits correction only when the error is plain and affects substantial rights, ordinarily with an outcome-prejudice showing by the defendant, and correction remains discretionary under the separate fairness, integrity, or public-reputation inquiry. Right-specific waiver requirements remain: Olano did not decide even Rule 24(c)'s waivability and supplies no rule that every evidentiary protection can be prospectively waived. Because the reported trial objection here was preserved, a finding of unlawful admission would not become forfeited error merely because the advance agreement is disputed. *Williamson* preserves applicable nonconstitutional harmless-error rules; neither a categorical no-waiver theory nor enforcement of a particular waiver warrants enlarging the question to case-in-chief or derivative use.

Surviving *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), supplies knowing and voluntary plea principles; it is not a rule that bargaining pressure always invalidates a choice. *Ricketts v. Adamson*, 483 U.S. 1 (1987), enforces the actual consequences of a breached agreement and distinguishes the terms made from terms the parties could have made. *Peretz*, 501 U.S. 923 (1991), permits consensual magistrate jury selection in its statutory and Article III setting. *Rumery*, 480 U.S. 386 (1987), rejects automatic invalidity of every release-dismissal agreement while retaining voluntariness, prosecutorial-overreaching, and public-interest concerns. *Brooklyn Savings* supplies the counterprinciple that private consent may not defeat a statutory policy intended for public protection. Their scope, rather than a ranking of which right is more important, must perform the legal work.

### Paths and strongest counters

**Enforce a valid, limited impeachment waiver.** The Rules prescribe a protection for the defendant but do not expressly prohibit relinquishing it; the ordinary waiver background may apply to a counseled choice. A narrow decision could resolve only use after the defendant gives inconsistent trial testimony, preserving challenges to knowing and voluntary assent, fraud, coercion, scope, and other evidentiary grounds. The counter is that allowing the prosecutor to make waiver a condition for any discussion may routinely eliminate the confidence Congress sought to protect, and Congress's rejection of an impeachment exception may be undermined in practice. The response must address statutory policy, not merely assert that the defendant may not lie.

**Treat prospective bargaining-entry waiver as incompatible with these rules.** The carefully defined exceptions and legislative choice to protect unsuccessful negotiations, alongside *Brooklyn Savings*, may support a rule that this protection cannot be sold away before discussions. The counter is that a prohibition on admission does not necessarily prohibit a defendant's waiver; many mandatory protections can be relinquished, and the text contains no express no-waiver command. A narrow version might concern this pre-discussion condition rather than every agreement made at any procedural stage, but it must identify a legal basis for that distinction instead of inventing a factual coercion finding.

**Individualized public-interest assessment** is also an available narrower rationale if the Rules permit waiver but *Rumery*'s safeguards are thought necessary. The record does not establish that this waiver was involuntary. Remand for a genuinely unresolved material validity issue must identify the missing issue and cannot assume coercion simply from bargaining. Enforcing the limited agreement would remove the panel's stated evidentiary ground for retrial; a mandate must distinguish that correction from a new sentencing ruling. Declining enforcement can preserve the panel's new-trial remedy on its finding of nonharmless error, subject to the actual issue scope. No route supplies an acquittal.
