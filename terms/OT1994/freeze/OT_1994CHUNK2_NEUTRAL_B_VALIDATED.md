## 17. American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens — No. 93-1286

Argument November 1, 1994; scheduled established merits event January 18, 1995. Review of *Wolens v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 157 Ill. 2d 466, 626 N.E.2d 205 (December 16, 1993), following 147 Ill. 2d 367, 589 N.E.2d 533 (1992). The questions separately concern damages claims under the Illinois Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act and judicial enforcement of the airline's own frequent-flyer agreement.

### Source-supported record and scope

Participants in American's AAdvantage program brought consolidated Illinois class actions alleging that May 1988 restrictions devalued mileage credits already earned. Capacity controls limit the number of seats available at an award level; blackout restrictions concern dates on which an award can be used. Mileage accrual can arise through American flights or participating nonairline businesses; available awards include flight and nonflight benefits. The litigated damages theory concerns prior credits and airline travel awards, not a universal challenge to every program product.

The pleadings alleged a right to redeem previously earned credits on the terms existing when earned, and that American induced accumulation of credits before reducing their value. American points to express language reserving alteration or termination of the program and disputes the claimed immunity of earned credits from alteration. Respondents concede prospective alteration but dispute retroactive alteration. These are rival constructions, not established findings of a binding promise, breach, fraud, reliance, or damages. The airline brief calls attention to a further factual limitation: although plaintiffs describe blackout dates generally, American maintains that the challenged retroactive effect principally concerns capacity controls and that blackout dates applied only to newly earned miles. Do not adjudicate that disputed detail as an established fact; the preemption question can be decided without resolving it.

The circuit court denied American's preemption motion to dismiss and certified interlocutory review. Illinois appellate review distinguished an injunction from damages. Plaintiffs' requested injunction was held preempted; only American sought further review. The 1993 Illinois Supreme Court left the injunction ruling undisturbed and sustained the damages claims, reasoning that frequent-flyer programs were peripheral and the damages claims only tangentially related to airline rates or services. Justice McMorrow dissented on both damages branches, reasoning that awards, restrictions, and availability directly concern fares/services and damages can enforce the same obligation as an injunction. ## 18. NationsBank / Ludwig v. Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co. — Nos. 93-1612, 93-1613

Argument December 7, 1994; merits event January 18, 1995. Consolidated review of *Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co. v. Clarke*, 998 F.2d 1295 (5th Cir. August 26, 1993), reversing 786 F. Supp. 639 (S.D. Tex. 1991). Do not reproduce the erroneous D.C.-Circuit metadata currently displayed on one mirror: the opinion's appellate origin, panel, and Southern District of Texas case establish the Fifth Circuit.

### Record, agency action, and statutes

On August 8, 1989, the Charlotte-based bank and its brokerage subsidiary sought permission to sell fixed, variable, and combined annuity products as agents for insurers. The Comptroller approved on March 21, 1990, adopting the relevant interpretive analysis. VALIC, which issues and sells annuities and would compete with the subsidiary, sought declaratory and injunctive relief. Cross-motions produced judgment for the Comptroller in district court and reversal in the Fifth Circuit.

The proposed business is agency sales, not underwriting, issuing annuities, or guaranteeing insurers' promises. The Comptroller found no bank actuarial or interest-rate risk from agency sales and required disclosure in advertising, promotional material, and customer descriptions that the annuities are not bank products and are not FDIC insured, plus a signed purchaser acknowledgment of agency status and lack of federal insurance. These are conditions of this approval, not an unqualified authorization of all bank insurance activities.

Annuities are not all lifetime mortality wagers. Deferred products have accumulation periods and may permit a lump-sum distribution, systematic withdrawal, or purchase of an immediate annuity at payout. Fixed contracts provide a guaranteed return but may also credit additional interest at the issuer's discretion. Variable contracts generally segregate funds and link returns to chosen investments. Lifetime payout options do involve mortality risk. The legal classification under §92 is disputed; product-specific features cannot be replaced by a categorical claim that every purchaser must annuitize for life.

Section 24 Seventh authorizes all incidental powers necessary to carry on banking and lists discounting/negotiating commercial paper and debt evidence, receiving deposits, buying/selling exchange, coin and bullion, lending on personal security, and obtaining/issuing/circulating notes under the title. Its securities paragraph distinguishes purchases and sales without recourse solely on customers' orders and for their accounts from bank own-account investment and underwriting. This packet invokes no own-account investment-security authorization or exception as a ground for approving these agency sales; a proposed holding resting on such a provision would require its actual operative terms. Agency brokerage is analytically distinct from impermissible underwriting.

Section 92 begins “In addition to the powers now vested by law” and permits a national bank located and doing business in a place with no more than 5,000 inhabitants under the last preceding decennial census to act, under Comptroller rules, as agent for a fire, life, or other insurer authorized by the State where the bank is located. The authorized functions are soliciting and selling insurance and collecting premiums on the insurer's policies; the bank and insurer may agree to fees or commissions. The two operative provisos are: “That no such bank shall in any case assume or guarantee the payment of any premium on insurance policies issued through its agency by its principal” and “That the bank shall not guarantee the truth of any statement made by an assured in filing his application for insurance.” These are express restrictions. The statute does not expressly say “all larger-place insurance activity is forbidden”; that is the Fifth Circuit's construction from the specific grant and statutory history. Keep command and inference distinct. The quotation and complete conditions are supplied by the authorized statutory supplement's official §92 extract check.

The Fifth Circuit, following *Saxon v. Georgia Association of Independent Insurance Agents*, 399 F.2d 1010 (5th Cir. 1968), construed §92 as an exclusive insurance-agency authorization, classified annuities as insurance, rejected deference because it regarded congressional intent as clear, and held that §24's general incidental power cannot defeat §92's specific limit. It regarded lifetime annuity risk as insurance's inverse, noted state insurance regulation, rejected a general/specialized insurance distinction, and distinguished credit-life insurance closely tied to loan repayment. A dissent from rehearing denial, summarized in the government merits brief, disputed certainty of the insurance classification and favored deference to the Comptroller.

The bank and Comptroller argue that annuity agency sales resemble lawful financial-investment intermediation, fit banking and incidental powers, and need not be “insurance” for this particular banking provision even when regulated as insurance elsewhere. ## 19. Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson — No. 93-1001

Argument October 4, 1994; event January 18, 1995. Review of 628 So. 2d 354 (Ala. August 13, 1993), affirming denial of a motion to stay and compel arbitration.

### Record and complete statutory qualification

In August 1987 the Gwins replaced an expired local termite bond on their Fairhope, Alabama house with a bond from Terminix Service, an Arkansas corporation operating in multiple States, guaranteed by Terminix International, based in Tennessee. They sold the house to the Dobsons in 1991 under a contract requiring inspection information and transfer of the bond. Terminix supplied a Veterans Administration inspection form reporting no visible active infestation. After discovering termite damage the Dobsons sued the Gwins for fraud and the Terminix firms for fraud concerning the inspection and breach of contract; the Gwins cross-claimed against the firms. The firms invoked the bond's arbitration clause against the Dobsons and Gwins.

The firms supplied evidence of their interstate organization and operations, some materials acquired outside Alabama, execution in Memphis, federal-law language in the bond, and Gwin's stated preference for a national firm. Alabama applied its §8-1-41 rule against specific enforcement of predispute arbitration agreements. It rejected a slightest-nexus test in favor of whether, when contracting, the parties contemplated substantial interstate activity. It found that out-of-state corporate status, the form's language, and these materials did not establish the required contemplation; the service was primarily local. It did not hold that no material ever crossed a state line.

The full operative FAA §2 text is: “A written provision in any maritime transaction or a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or transaction, or the refusal to perform the whole or any part thereof, or an agreement in writing to submit to arbitration an existing controversy arising out of such a contract, transaction, or refusal, shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” The concluding defense qualification must accompany any enforceability formulation. Section 1 defines commerce to include commerce among States and with foreign nations; its employment exclusion is separate and is not factually presented by this home-service bond. Federal procedural directions in §§3–4 raise a distinct state-court reach issue; do not infer that every federal procedure automatically applies in a state tribunal.

### Supported alternatives and earlier authority

The firms argue that “involving commerce” invokes broad commerce power and that actual interstate features suffice without parties' foresight. They oppose turning “evidencing” into a subjective or reasonable-expectations prerequisite and rely on *Southland*, 465 U.S. 1, and *Perry*, 482 U.S. 483. A favorable ruling could reject the coverage test and remand for proceedings under the proper rule without awarding tort damages or deciding every party's assent, scope, or generally applicable defense.

Respondents expressly ask that *Southland* be overruled on the state-court question. Their merits brief invokes O'Connor's *Southland* dissent and the brief of Alabama and nineteen other States, contending that a federal procedural law contains no sufficiently clear state-court command. Alternatively, they defend contemplation as a construction of the whole phrase “contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce.” Their further alternative construes “involving” more narrowly than “affecting,” reaching the actual stream of interstate commerce rather than every local contract with effects on it. These are separate defenses of the judgment, not invented alternatives.

*Southland* is governing pre-divergence law unless lawfully reconsidered. It treats §2 as substantive and applicable in state court, subject to the commerce condition and general contract defenses. *Perry* rejects a state wage-claim rule that bypasses arbitration and preserves generally applicable contract defenses. *Volt*, 489 U.S. 468, enforces an actual contractual choice of state arbitration rules; it does not make every forum's antiarbitration rule such a choice. *Bernhardt*, 350 U.S. 198, held the FAA inapplicable where the record did not show the particular employment transaction involved commerce; parties dispute the breadth of its language. *Prima Paint*, 388 U.S. 395, distinguishes fraud in the arbitration clause from fraud in the whole contract in the federal setting; no need exists to manufacture a fraud-in-the-inducement ruling on this source record. Later *Mastrobuono*, *First Options*, *Lopez*, or *Sky Reefer* are not entering law.

The strongest counter to broad coverage is the statute's transaction/contract language and the local service facts; broad commerce power alone does not answer what Congress enacted. The strongest counter to contemplation is the absence of a knowledge/expectation term and the practical difference between a transaction's actual interstate character and contracting parties' prediction. The strongest counter to overruling *Southland* is statutory stare decisis and the existing substantive-enforceability precedent; the competing argument relies on text, procedural structure, and the specifically presented request for reconsideration. No disposition may make arbitration agreements immune from defenses applicable to contracts generally.

## 20. Schlup v. Delo — No. 93-7901

Argument October 3, 1994; event January 23, 1995. Review of *Schlup v. Delo*, 11 F.3d 738 (8th Cir. November 15, 1993). The second federal §2254 petition alleges independent constitutional trial errors, chiefly ineffective assistance and suppressed exculpatory material, with innocence offered to pass otherwise applicable successive/abusive-claim barriers. This is not a request to choose a general freestanding innocence merits standard.

### Corrected posture and contested evidence

Schlup was convicted of participating in the February 3, 1984 prison murder of Arthur Dade and sentenced to death. Two correctional officers identified him: Flowers testified; Maylee's account came by deposition. Their descriptions differed in material details. The defense used a dining-hall videotape showing Schlup entering before an apparent distress response and O'Neal arriving later. The prosecution maintained that the attack preceded the response long enough for Schlup to reach the hall. The tape itself was trial evidence, not newly created evidence.

New affidavits bear on both identification and timing. Inmate clerk John Green said he saw the attack, did not see Schlup, identified a different participant, and promptly called for help. Former lieutenant Robert Faherty described Schlup's movement before entering the hall in a way said to undermine his ability to have participated. Other inmates said Schlup was absent or identified the actual attackers. These submissions are evidence offered by the prisoner, not adjudicated truth. The majority below emphasized inconsistencies between Green's affidavit, his earlier interview, and testimony in another participant's trial, and between Faherty's timing estimates and earlier sworn accounts. It questioned delay and reliability and credited the existence of the two trial identifications. The dissent argued that live examination could resolve contradictions, the affidavits were not uniformly inconsistent, prison witnesses were the natural eyewitnesses, and counsel had failed to interview available exculpatory witnesses.

The district court denied the second petition without the requested evidentiary hearing, dismissed August 23, 1993, and denied the Rule 59(e) motion September 13. The Eighth Circuit applied its *McCoy v. Lockhart*, 969 F.2d 649, and *Cornell v. Nix*, 976 F.2d 376 (en banc), extension of *Sawyer*'s clear-and-convincing no-reasonable-juror standard to guilt innocence. It affirmed denial and rejected a stay. Its majority also treated witness credibility as for the trial jury and discussed why counsel's first-petition ineffectiveness adjudication should stand. Heaney dissented, urging hearing and arguing that the evidence could satisfy even the stricter standard. Do not erase that additional merits discussion, but do not convert it into a fully developed district-court credibility hearing.

The current review is confined to the asserted innocence gateway and its application/remedy. Respondent's merits brief states that Schlup no longer presses cause and prejudice. Passing a gateway cannot by itself establish Brady/Strickland merits, innocence as a fact, or entitlement to release. Later investigation mentioned in advocacy does not automatically become part of the record reviewed below; identify the actual submission and procedural vehicle before using it.

### Entering law and standards

*Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, preserves an exceptional route where constitutional error probably caused conviction of an innocent person despite lack of cause. *Kuhlmann v. Wilson*, 477 U.S. 436, supplies the plurality's fair-probability/all-probative-evidence discussion, not a five-Justice holding on every formulation. *McCleskey v. Zant*, 499 U.S. 467, applies cause/prejudice to abuse while retaining an innocence exception and requiring the State to plead abuse particularly before the prisoner's burden arises. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, addresses trial-record legal sufficiency, with favorable inference to the prosecution; whether that approach is appropriate to newly supported innocence is disputed here.

The simulated register “Capital eligibility innocence as a gateway to barred habeas claims,” present operation effective *Sawyer*, June 22, 1992, requires clear and convincing proof that absent constitutional error no reasonable juror would find death eligibility. It expressly leaves crime innocence unchanged. It does not automatically control guilt innocence simply because the prisoner faces death. It also supplies no categorical exclusion of impeachment evidence.

Simulated *Herrera*, January 25, 1993, has only a majority-supported record-specific insufficiency holding; no whole opinion controls and no uniform freestanding-innocence burden is established. It cannot supply an assumed controlling “extraordinarily high” standard here. Simulated *Delo v. Blair*, July 21, 1993, was an equally divided application disposition, without precedent. The historical lower court's citations to a broader Herrera or Blair rule must be evaluated against this law.

Simulated *Keeney*, May 4, 1992, preserves Townsend's deliberate-bypass rule for materially inadequate factual development of a properly presented claim; ordinary negligence alone does not forfeit the necessary hearing in that setting. Independent successive/abusive/default barriers remain separate. The model must decide gateway availability before using the hearing rule as though the constitutional claim were already available. The register “Preserved protected-silence error on federal habeas review,” present operation effective *Brecht*, April 21, 1993, applies Chapman to the preserved Doyle claim there, with the State bearing the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden. It concerns merits harmlessness of a properly available claim, not this gateway, and must not be replaced by the historical Brecht rule. The register “Capital habeas counsel before a merits petition and discretionary stays,” present operation effective *McFarland*, June 30, 1994, supplies qualifying capital-counsel appointment and individualized preparatory stay authority, not an automatic innocence gateway or indefinite stay. No counsel-appointment or expense issue is adjudicated by this packet.

### Supported alternatives and remedy boundaries

Schlup argues that guilt innocence invokes *Carrier/Kuhlmann*'s probability-based exception, that *Sawyer*'s eligibility standard is a distinct branch, and that the whole probative record requires actual reliability assessment rather than asking whether some inculpatory evidence could support the original verdict. He requests at least remand for evidentiary consideration. His strongest answer to finality is that he alleges both actual nonparticipation and independent trial error; a gateway is exceptional access to constitutional adjudication, not release upon a bare affidavit.

The State argues for a uniform strict clear-and-convincing gateway for guilt and punishment, emphasizing repeat litigation and finality; alternatively it argues that the late and inconsistent submissions fail the less demanding standard too, and that the constitutional claims lack merit independently. The strongest answer to its uniformity argument is the actual scope of simulated *Sawyer* and *Carrier*'s preserved exception. The strongest answer to automatic remand is that a prisoner must submit sufficiently reliable, material evidence and cannot relitigate every credibility contest on a second petition.

Source-supported choices include identifying the guilt-gateway rule and remanding for its application to all relevant evidence; affirming if the showing fails under the legally selected rule without unresolved material factual needs; or addressing a properly established independent ground sufficient to preserve the judgment. The model must explain why any such independent ground survives the correct scope and record. It may not find innocence, issue an unconditional writ, or assume that every affidavit is true. A remand for assessment may leave the need for live evidence to the governing hearing rules and the district court's reasoned treatment of the submissions; a mandatory hearing requires its own basis. Preserve the distinction between “could convict” sufficiency and a gateway's probability inquiry, rather than silently interchanging them.
