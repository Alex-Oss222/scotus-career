## Corrected and bounded facts

The proposed indoor display must be distinguished from the past outdoor display. Chabad owns a fifteen-foot stainless-steel Chanukah menorah. In 1989 it displayed that menorah on the outdoor plaza for the eight-day holiday under permission from the Building Authority and Governor's office. A yellow sign identified Chabad and offered holiday greetings. Daily candle-lighting ceremonies lasted approximately thirty to forty-five minutes; no representative remained at other times. The proposed continued display is therefore unattended between ceremonies, not a proposal that no person ever appear beside it.

For 1990 Georgia refused the eight-day outdoor display but permitted a candle-lighting event followed by removal. A content-neutral rule forbidding unattended objects on the plaza was the district court's ground for rejecting preliminary relief. In January 1991 Chabad renewed its outdoor request and alternatively requested the indoor Rotunda. Its amended complaint separated Count I (plaza) and Count II (Rotunda). Georgia eventually allowed a candle-lighting event but refused an unattended menorah on the grounds or in the Rotunda.

The governing distinction between these spaces is material. Chabad conceded the State's entitlement to judgment on Count I because the unattended-object policy had been neutrally enforced on the outdoor plaza. That policy does not apply inside the Rotunda; the State acknowledged no other content-neutral rule prohibiting unattended displays there. Only Count II was appealed from the December 5, 1991 summary judgment. No judicial relief for the plaza, reversal of the Count I concession, or decision invalidating its neutral rule belongs in the present event.

The Rotunda is the open interior beneath the State Capitol dome. Over approximately a decade the State admitted private secular and religious expressive events on a content-neutral first-come basis. Examples include advocacy press conferences on marijuana law, abortion, parental notification, and mental-health awareness, as well as cultural, educational and religious events. Unattended displays included an eighteen-foot Indigenous structure for five days, forty-one posters sponsored by the Atlanta Jewish Federation for eight days, and thirty-five flags during International Week for five days. The source calls the Indigenous display a wattle-and-daub hut in the en banc opinion and a teepee in the district opinion; no anthropological finding resolving that label is required here. The material agreed facts are its large scale, five-day duration, and unattended admission.

The district court found a designated public forum. The en banc opinion says the parties did not dispute that finding; Georgia's perfunctory suggestion that factfinding could be more thorough did not actually attack it. No exclusive secular-only subject limitation is established. The older source uses the phrase limited public forum to describe the created forum, not to establish an unproved restricted subject category. Governmental speakers have also used the space, including prior state Christmas presentations; the Rotunda's private forum function does not mean every event there has always been private.

The proposed indoor menorah would stand prominently for eight days, generally unattended and without surrounding secular symbols. The State's planned 1991 Christmas tree would not arrive until after Chanukah. Neither the private sign nor the tree may be portrayed as an already tested combined indoor display. The identifying holiday sign names Chabad; it does not expressly disclaim government sponsorship. Judge Kravitch's panel writing reports that Chabad offered at oral argument to erect an appropriate disclaimer. No exact approved disclaimer language, size, placement, or test of its effectiveness is established.

## Actual judgments and reasoning

The District Court granted Georgia summary judgment on both counts and denied Chabad's Count II motion. On Count II it accepted public-forum status and protected religious expression but held that the isolated religious symbol at the center of a core governmental building created establishment endorsement. It relied on its understanding of Allegheny and the likely perception of visitors unaware of the forum's history. It did not perform a distinct developed analysis of whether complete exclusion was narrowly tailored.

The initial appellate panel adopted the district order and affirmed. Judge Kravitch agreed that the Rotunda was a forum and that the proposed sign did not itself disclaim sponsorship. She regarded the establishment concern as sufficient to warrant scrutiny but would remand to consider whether an appropriate disclaimer or other lesser measure could avoid complete exclusion. Her writing supplies a materially supported narrower path; it is not the en banc judgment or a Supreme Court commitment.

The en banc Eleventh Circuit reversed the Count II judgment. Chief Judge Tjoflat's opinion held that genuinely neutral, nonpreferential admission would not establish religion and independently that total exclusion was not narrowly tailored even on the contrary premise. It treated forum access as the relevant state act; discussed the State's ability to explain the forum, enforce neutral time/place/manner rules, or genuinely close its designated forum; and declined to prescribe the wording of any sign. Its broad statements that a true forum necessarily prevents endorsement must be distinguished from the narrower contextual and alternative-tailoring reasoning. They are lower-court reasoning subject to review, not this Court's law.

Judge Anderson concurred, emphasizing a fact-sensitive reasonable-observer analysis, the heightened attribution risk inside a core governmental building, the fact that government itself also speaks there, the ordinary private uses, and the sufficiency of appropriate signage in these circumstances. Judge Birch joined both the court opinion and Anderson's concurrence. Judge Cox concurred in the result only. The supplied en banc source contains no dissent. Do not manufacture a Supreme Court lineup from these lower-court positions.

The en banc operative disposition is reversal. The supplied source does not prescribe an exact new injunction, mandate date, season-specific permit, sign text, damages, or perpetual entitlement to any display configuration. An eventual Supreme Court affirmance must accurately identify the lower judgment affirmed and the relief that must be implemented below; it must not purport to preserve an already entered detailed permanent permit order that the source does not supply.

## Duration and recurrence

The amended claim sought relief for the eight days of Chanukah 1991. Judge Kravitch's panel footnote 1 reports that Chabad confirmed at oral argument that it would apply again. It also explains that the annual request, decision, trial, and appeal could not realistically all be completed before the holiday expired. That statement is a reported actual party representation, unlike an invented later application.
