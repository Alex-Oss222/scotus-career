## 2. Verified lower-court record and precise preservation limits

The complete Sixth Circuit opinion, including its footnotes, was retrieved and read. The following are its account of the record and rulings, not new factual findings by this Court. The complete trial transcript, district-court order and magistrate reports were not independently obtained.

O'Neal was convicted in 1981 of aggravated murder, aggravated robbery and kidnapping following a joint Ohio trial. Henry Podborny's estranged wife induced him to travel from the Chicago area to Cleveland. The prosecution described a plan to hold him in the basement of O'Neal's unlicensed saloon while others robbed his property. A witness saw O'Neal leave the bar with Podborny's wallet and possessions shortly after Podborny entered. Another conspirator reported striking Podborny with a pipe. Podborny's body was discovered later; the medical evidence showed repeated head blows and one gunshot. The appellate opinion also reports testimony that O'Neal admitted that he and another participant had killed a man and placed the body in the field where it was found, blood of the victim's type at the saloon, and paint on the body similar to that in the bar. These descriptions support competing arguments about the error's possible influence; they do not permit replacement of a verdict-effect inquiry with a new finding of guilt.

The lower opinion states that O'Neal unsuccessfully appealed in the Ohio courts and filed his §2254 petition in 1986. The district court conditionally granted relief because of confusing instructions and other trial errors considered together. Its exact retrial period and operative order are not supplied by the reported appellate opinion. The Sixth Circuit reversed that conditional grant; it did not affirm a district-court denial.

### The State's magistrate-report default

The State's objections to the magistrate's first report were approximately twenty days late and were struck. The district court did not adopt the report in full; it recommitted the matter, and the State timely objected to the second report. The Sixth Circuit treated its Walters waiver rule as procedural rather than jurisdictional and exercised its interests-of-justice authority to hear the appeal, citing *Thomas v. Arn*, 474 U.S. 140, and its own precedents. This is a resolved lower-court appeal-waiver issue. It is not proof that O'Neal defaulted his trial claim or that this Court's admitted question includes reconsideration of the State's appeal waiver.

### Instruction, prosecutor's remark, and assumption of error

The district court concluded that a reasonable juror could understand the instruction to impute one conspirator's intent to another, relieving the State of proving O'Neal's intent. The Sixth Circuit applied a reasonable-likelihood inquiry to the complete charge rather than a merely possible erroneous interpretation. Its instructions-only analysis found no constitutional violation. That ruling must remain separate from its alternative assumption about the instruction combined with the prosecutor's closing statement.

The quoted charge first stated that purpose to kill is an element of aggravated murder and defined purposeful action as the specific intention to cause a result. It then required that a specific intention to kill be present in the minds of the “defendants or co-defendants or aiders and abettors.” Similar terminology accompanied aggravated robbery and kidnapping. The appellate court understood those terms as alternative descriptions of the accused rather than an instruction to transfer a different person's intent. It also relied on two directions to consider each defendant's elements separately, as if each were tried separately, and not to let conclusions about one control another's verdict.

The charge further addressed a conspiracy to an unlawful act, such as kidnapping or stealing, whose manner of performance would reasonably and likely produce death. It stated that a conspirator could then be equally guilty of homicide without presence at, or knowledge of, the physical killing or weapon. The Sixth Circuit regarded this as an imperfect statement of Ohio's permitted inference of personal purpose. Its account of Ohio law requires the culpability of the principal offense for accomplice or conspiracy liability. The inference it described arises where participants entered a common design and either the aider or abettor knew an inherently dangerous instrumentality would be used or the felony and manner of accomplishing it would reasonably be likely to produce death. That is a description of the lower court's state-law analysis, not an independent certification of the complete 1981 statutory text or a Supreme Court adoption of every inference described.

The prosecutor described the State's task as establishing that the crimes occurred and that the defendants participated in a course of criminal conduct eventually leading to them. **Defense counsel objected; the trial court overruled the objection.** The Sixth Circuit called the statement a misstatement of Ohio law because it improperly suggested no independent showing of each defendant's intent was needed. It then assumed for argument that the remark, together with possible confusion from the charge, infringed federal constitutional rights and held the assumed error harmless. It relied on evidence of O'Neal's principal participation, the prosecution's portrayal of him as a principal, and the principal-liability charge's express requirements of purpose and prior calculation and design.

The Sixth Circuit's opening harmlessness statement placed on the petitioner the burden of establishing actual prejudice under what it described as Brecht's substantial-and-injurious-effect standard. **That account of historical Brecht is a premise of the lower opinion, not the law of this timeline.** Its dispositive discussion found no injurious influence; it did not expressly find grave doubt, an evenly balanced record, or inability to decide. The choice of burden may matter to review, but an actual equipoise finding cannot be invented from the general burden statement.

The appellate court separately rejected the district court's reliance on evidentiary rulings and inflammatory closing remarks, finding at most state-law errors and no fundamental unfairness individually or together. Those rulings are part of the judgment below. They are not automatically additional Supreme Court questions merely because they appear in the record.

**Preservation boundary:** The report establishes the objection to the prosecutor's statement, presentation of the instructional federal claim in habeas and its adjudication by the lower courts. It does not independently establish a contemporaneous objection to each instruction, the precise grounds of every trial objection, or all state-court exhaustion presentations. No dispositive petitioner-default ruling appears in the reported Sixth Circuit decision. The packet therefore neither invents a default nor certifies every preservation predicate. An assumed constitutional violation is not a State concession, a final constitutional holding, a structural-error finding, or an automatic entitlement to a writ.

## 3. Actual entering law

### A. Brecht's controlling preserved-Doyle rule

The controlling Holdings entry in `state/holdings/criminal-procedure.md`, “Protected silence and habeas harmless-error review / Brecht v. Abrahamson,” and the operative Standards entry **“Preserved protected-silence error on federal habeas review”** agree. **Present operation effective:** April 21, 1993, Brecht. No distinct later current-wording authority changes that component.

The full controlling rule is: using post-Miranda silence to impeach the defendant's trial explanation violates Doyle and is constitutional trial error, rather than merely nonconstitutional error or a structural defect. Permitted prewarning impeachment remains separate. For a **preserved Doyle trial error properly before the federal habeas court**, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict, on the whole record. Collateral posture alone does not replace Chapman with Kotteakos for that claim. Relief is conditional on failure of the State's showing, not automatic on identifying error. Exhaustion, preservation, other habeas gates, structural doctrine and capital-weighing correction remain independent.

White's controlling Parts II–III were joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor and Souter. The classification portion was unanimous. The Court expressly did **not** prescribe a standard for every constitutional collateral claim. Its public render says Kotteakos was not adopted as a general constitutional-habeas standard. Thus neither “Brecht already requires Kotteakos here” nor “Brecht already holds Chapman governs every preserved constitutional trial error” is a faithful statement of entering law. Extending the retained Chapman rule to the present instructional claim or adopting a distinct collateral rule requires a reasoned doctrinal choice.

### B. Underlying instructional violation and structural-error boundary

The criminal-procedure Holdings volume and the published Victor/Sandoval entry preserve the reasonable-likelihood, whole-charge inquiry from Estelle, informed by Winship's requirement that the State prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt. An ambiguous instruction is not unconstitutional simply because a juror could possibly misread a phrase. Correct language elsewhere is not an automatic cure if the complete charge is reasonably likely to lower the required standard.

The operative Standards entry **“A vitiated jury reasonable-doubt standard is structural error”** has **Present operation effective: June 1, 1993, Sullivan v. Louisiana.** An unconstitutional reasonable-doubt instruction vitiating the governing standard for all factual findings leaves no valid jury verdict of guilt beyond reasonable doubt, and appellate speculation about a correct verdict cannot supply one. **Other instructional errors are not thereby structural.** O'Neal's alleged imputation of intent and the lower court's assumption of trial error do not themselves establish Sullivan's predicate. A structural path would require the exact whole-charge defect and proper question scope to be established; those premises cannot be inferred here.

### C. Surviving pre-divergence harmless-error authority

*Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 21–26 (1967), distinguishes federal constitutional error from state-law error; permits some constitutional trial errors to be harmless; and requires the beneficiary of the constitutional error to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that it did not contribute to the verdict. Strong evidence is not a substitute for evaluating the forbidden contribution. Its direct-review origin and Brecht's express collateral holding must remain distinct; its words do not erase Brecht's reserved categories.

*Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760–766, 776 (1946), applies the federal harmless-error statute's predecessor to a federal criminal direct appeal. It examines the error's influence on the actual jury in the setting of the whole proceeding, not evidentiary sufficiency in a hypothetical proper trial. If the conviction was not substantially swayed, harmlessness may be found; if the court remains in grave doubt, its stated rule does not permit the conviction to stand. Its discussion distinguishes merely technical error from error naturally prejudicing substantial rights and cautions against universal presumptions detached from the error's nature and setting. The present question is whether and how that approach governs this constitutional claim in collateral review; the case itself did not decide that application.

Brecht's controlling public treatment of *Rose v. Clark*, 478 U.S. 570, and *Yates v. Evatt*, 500 U.S. 391, identifies their prior collateral use of Chapman as support for continuity, while declining to misdescribe them as squarely deciding the later choice between harmlessness standards. That exact authority characterization enters here. Brecht likewise preserves the independent operation of Sawyer, Herrera and capital-weighing cases rather than deriving a universal innocence condition for preserved trial error.

### D. Current-term Schlup and limits of other burden analogies

The January 23, 1995 Schlup decision establishes a demanding gateway for otherwise barred successive or abusive constitutional claims: reliable new evidence and the whole probative record must show it more likely than not that no reasonable, properly instructed juror would find guilt beyond reasonable doubt. Passing that gateway does not prove constitutional error, award relief, or shift the ultimate burden on underlying claims. Schlup's specific refusal-and-remand holding likewise does not define the harmlessness rule for an ordinary instructional claim. Nothing in this record establishes that O'Neal invokes the Schlup gateway.

Greenwich Collieries's earlier published positions concern APA §556(d), which assigns persuasion to the proponent absent an applicable exception, while retaining genuine affirmative-defense burdens. They can illuminate how individual Justices treat statutory allocations and actual equipoise. Neither that statute nor its benefits context determines this habeas allocation without an additional legal argument.

### E. Term-effective statutes

The official 1994 Code, stating laws effective January 4, 1995, verifies these terms. No later habeas legislation or later judicial review standard enters.

- **28 U.S.C. §2254(a):** federal courts entertain the state prisoner's habeas application only on the ground that the prisoner is in custody in violation of the Constitution, laws or treaties of the United States. Whether this language fixes the risk of uncertainty about the effect of an established trial violation is an issue of construction, not a burden expressly stated in subsection (a).
- **Section 2254(b)–(c):** relief requires exhaustion of available state remedies, unless available state corrective process is absent or circumstances render it ineffective to protect the prisoner's rights; a state procedure still available to raise the question precludes exhaustion under subsection (c). The packet establishes no additional application-specific default or exhaustion defect.
- **Former §2254(d):** its qualified presumption concerns a state-court factual determination after a hearing and written or other adequate indicia. Its exceptions concern failure to resolve the factual dispute, inadequate procedure or development, lack of jurisdiction, unconstitutional denial of appointed counsel to an indigent, absence of a full and fair hearing, other due-process denial, or lack of fair record support. Subject to those terms, it requires convincing evidence to displace a qualifying factual finding. That factfinding provision must not be silently converted into the distinct harmlessness allocation at issue here.
- **28 U.S.C. §2243:** the court is to hear and determine the facts summarily and dispose of the matter as law and justice require. This text must be construed together with the custody requirement; neither a mandatory writ on any trial mistake nor an express universal prejudice burden appears in those words alone.
- **28 U.S.C. §2111:** on appeal or certiorari in any case, the court examines the record and gives judgment without regard to errors or defects not affecting substantial rights. Its application to error in the habeas proceeding is distinct from the question how a habeas court evaluates error in the underlying state criminal trial.
