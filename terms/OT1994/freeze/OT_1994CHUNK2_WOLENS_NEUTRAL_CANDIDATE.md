## 17. American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens — No. 93-1286

Argument November 1, 1994; established merits event January 18, 1995. Review of *Wolens v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 157 Ill. 2d 466, 626 N.E.2d 205 (December 16, 1993), following 147 Ill. 2d 367, 589 N.E.2d 533 (1992). The questions separately concern damages claims under the Illinois Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act and judicial enforcement of the airline's own frequent-flyer agreement.

### Source-supported record and scope

Participants in American's AAdvantage program brought consolidated Illinois class actions alleging that May 1988 restrictions devalued mileage credits already earned. Capacity controls limit the number of seats available at an award level; blackout restrictions concern dates on which an award can be used. Mileage accrual can arise through American flights or participating nonairline businesses; available awards include flight and nonflight benefits. The litigated damages theory concerns prior credits and airline travel awards, not a universal challenge to every program product.

The pleadings alleged a right to redeem previously earned credits on the terms existing when earned, and that American induced accumulation of credits before reducing their value. American points to express language reserving alteration or termination of the program and disputes the claimed immunity of earned credits from alteration. Respondents concede prospective alteration but dispute retroactive alteration. These are rival constructions, not established findings of a binding promise, breach, fraud, reliance, or damages. The airline brief calls attention to a further factual limitation: although plaintiffs describe blackout dates generally, American maintains that the challenged retroactive effect principally concerns capacity controls and that blackout dates applied only to newly earned miles. Do not adjudicate that disputed detail as an established fact; the preemption question can be decided without resolving it.

The circuit court denied American's preemption motion to dismiss and certified interlocutory review. Illinois appellate review distinguished an injunction from damages. Plaintiffs' requested injunction was held preempted; only American sought further review. The 1993 Illinois Supreme Court left the injunction ruling undisturbed and sustained the damages claims, reasoning that frequent-flyer programs were peripheral and the damages claims only tangentially related to airline rates or services. Justice McMorrow dissented on both damages branches, reasoning that awards, restrictions, and availability directly concern fares/services and damages can enforce the same obligation as an injunction. Illinois's denial of dismissal is not a final damages award. No new injunctive relief or adjudicated breach follows merely from rejecting a preemption defense.

### Text and entering law

The transaction-period clause, 49 U.S.C. App. §1305(a)(1), bars state enactment or enforcement of a law, rule, regulation, standard, or other provision having the force and effect of law relating to covered air-carrier rates, routes, or services. Section 1506 preserves existing common-law and statutory remedies, subject to the specific preemption clause. The Alaska and airport-proprietor provisions are separate qualifications; neither is asserted on these facts.

Congress's July 5, 1994 recodification places the passenger clause at §41713(b)(1) and the general additional-remedies provision at §40120(c). The official revision notes identify the latter as derived from §1506 and the former as derived from §1305(a)(1). The August 23, 1994 amendment, effective January 1, 1995, adds a separate property-transportation paragraph and its own motor-vehicle-safety, route-control, financial-responsibility, and household-goods qualifications. Those additions are not qualifications newly attached to this passenger-award dispute. The complete official §41713 extract is retained in the source directory; use the old clause for the pleaded transactions without importing later cargo doctrine.

**Operative register:** “Airline rates, routes and services under the express preemption clause,” present operation effective *Morales*, June 1, 1992. Its connection/reference rule applies to generally applicable laws; §1506 does not undo a specific prohibition. Its separate significant-effects holding concerns the identified fare-marketing package, not every indirect business cost. Tenuous, remote, or peripheral connections and materially different nonprice duties remain outside the resolved boundary.

**Operative register:** “Cigarette-labeling preemption by statutory period and predicate duty,” present operation effective *Cipollone*, June 24, 1992. The voluntary express-warranty holding preserves an otherwise valid voluntarily undertaken substantive promise; state law still supplies formation and other elements. It does not automatically construe the differently worded airline clause. *O'Melveny*, June 13, 1994, limits creation of federal common law in its FDIC setting; it does not erase this express clause or itself create federal airline contract law.

### Supported legal alternatives and answers

American seeks dismissal of both damages branches. It argues that award availability and the number of credits needed for a flight concern rates and services directly; state damages law may regulate as effectively as an injunction. On contract it argues that law supplies enforceability and that the asserted implied promise would override its reservation clause. Its strongest supported answer to a contract exception is that it could allow state-imposed standards to return under a contractual label.

Respondents seek affirmance of both damages branches. They characterize the program as a voluntary exchange, invoke the savings clause and traditional remedies, and assert that compensation for broken promises does not prescribe future airline rates. They defend the fraud branch as protection against deceptive inducement rather than direct rate setting. The strongest answer is *Morales*: general applicability, retrospective damages, and absence of direct rate fixing do not alone defeat an express connection to rates/services.

A separate branch distinguishing a state consumer-protection command from enforcement confined to an undertaking American actually made has source support in respondents' contract argument and *Cipollone*. It requires a defensible textual treatment of “enact or enforce” and “force and effect of law”; the model must not assume the distinction merely from the label “contract.” Conversely, treating both branches as remote must confront their direct control over award value, flight availability, and redemption terms. Any surviving contract claim returns for ordinary adjudication of the actual bargain and applicable defenses; preservation does not authorize enlargement by external consumer policy or establish any particular damages entitlement.
