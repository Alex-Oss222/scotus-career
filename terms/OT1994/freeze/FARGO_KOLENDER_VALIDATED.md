**Primary source:** [Official United States Reports, 461 U.S. 352](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep461/usrep461352/usrep461352.pdf), decided May 2, 1983. Justice O'Connor wrote the Court opinion, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell and Stevens. Brennan also concurred separately. White dissented, joined by Rehnquist. These are pre-divergence authorship and joins, not a Fargo lineup.

**Verified holding and facial-review boundary.** The Court held California's identification statute facially vague because, even under its accepted state-court construction, it did not sufficiently specify what a detained person had to provide to satisfy the identification demand. The statute left virtually complete enforcement discretion with the officer. The majority treated notice and restraints on arbitrary enforcement as distinct concerns and emphasized the legislature's duty to provide minimum enforcement guidelines. A lawful initial detention did not cure the undefined subsequent compliance demand. 461 U.S. at 355–361.

Footnote 8, at 358–359, expressly rejects White's assertion that facial vagueness always requires uncertainty in every possible application, whether protected conduct is implicated or not. The Court identifies protected conduct and the greater certainty demanded by criminal sanctions, cites *Colautti* and *Lanzetta* as examples of facial invalidation despite conceivable valid applications, and distinguishes *Hoffman Estates*' business-regulation setting and *Parker v. Levy*'s military context. This is majority reasoning, not White's dissent. It does not establish that every uncertainty, criminal penalty or connection to protected conduct warrants invalidating an entire enactment.
