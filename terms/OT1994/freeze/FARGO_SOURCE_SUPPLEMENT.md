## North Dakota default culpability: original enactment

Official source: [1973 N.D. Session Laws, chapter 116, section 2, printed pages 220–221](https://ndlegis.gov/assembly/sessionlaws/1973/pdf/CRIMES.pdf), PDF pages 22–23. Downloaded original: `tmp/fargo_source_research/nd1973_crimes.pdf`; extracted text: same basename `.txt`. The complete §12.1-02-02 reads, with line breaks and spacing normalized:

> 12.1-02-02. REQUIREMENTS OF CULPABILITY.) 1. For the purposes of this title, a person engages in conduct:
>
> a. "Intentionally" if, when he engages in the conduct, it is his purpose to do so;
>
> b. "Knowingly" if, when he engages in the conduct, he knows or has a firm belief, unaccompanied by substantial doubt, that he is doing so, whether or not it is his purpose to do so;
>
> c. "Recklessly" if he engages in the conduct in conscious and clearly unjustifiable disregard of a substantial likelihood of the existence of the relevant facts or risks, such disregard involving a gross deviation from acceptable standards of conduct, except that, as provided in section 12.1-04-02, awareness of the risk is not required where its absence is due to self-induced intoxication;
>
> d. "Negligently" if he engages in the conduct in unreasonable disregard of a substantial likelihood of the existence of the relevant facts or risks, such disregard involving a gross deviation from acceptable standards of conduct; and
>
> e. "Willfully" if he engages in the conduct intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly.
>
> 2. If a statute or regulation thereunder defining a crime does not specify any culpability and does not provide explicitly that a person may be guilty without culpability, the culpability that is required is willfully.
>
> 3. a. Except as otherwise expressly provided, where culpability is required, that kind of culpability is required with respect to every element of the conduct and to those attendant circumstances specified in the definition of the offense, except that where the required culpability is "intentionally", the culpability required as to an attendant circumstance is "knowingly".
>
> b. Except as otherwise expressly provided, if conduct is an offense if it causes a particular result, the required degree of culpability is required with respect to the result.
>
> c. Except as otherwise expressly provided, culpability is not required with respect to any fact which is solely a basis for grading.
>
> d. Except as otherwise expressly provided, culpability is not required with respect to facts which establish that a defense does not exist, if the defense is defined in chapters 12.1-01 through 12.1-06; otherwise the least kind of culpability required for the offense is required with respect to such facts.
>
> e. A factor as to which it is expressly stated that it must "in fact" exist is a factor for which culpability is not required.
>
> 4. Any lesser degree of required culpability is satisfied if the proven degree of culpability is higher.
>
> 5. Culpability is not required as to the fact that conduct is an offense, except as otherwise expressly provided in a provision outside this title.

The original source establishes these enacted words. The state opinions below independently confirm the material definitions and title limitation before the cutoff. A complete intervening session-law amendment audit has not been performed; this supplement does not certify the entire criminal code as a consolidated February 1995 edition. No source located here establishes an express Title 14 incorporation overriding the title limitation.

## North Dakota Supreme Court decisions on the provision's reach

These are the actual state opinions, retrieved from Harvard's Caselaw Access Project, not modern commentary. The HTML and extracted text are saved with the names given below. CAP metadata independently supplies the citations and dates.

**City of Dickinson v. Mueller, 261 N.W.2d 787, 789–790 (N.D. December 19, 1977).** Correct caption is *City of Dickinson*, not *State*. The Court, Erickstad writing with Sand, Paulson, Pederson, and Vogel concurring, held that §12.1-02-02(2) must be read with subsection (1)'s introductory title limitation and applies only to offenses described in Title 12.1. It therefore did not add willfulness to Title 5's prohibition on alcohol sales to minors or the corresponding municipal ordinance. The Court expressly reserved whether willfulness in the sense of conduct done voluntarily rather than under coercion could matter in other circumstances. [Primary text](https://static.case.law/nw2d/261/html/0787-01.html); `tmp/fargo_source_research/mueller.html` and `.txt`.

**State v. North Dakota Education Association, 262 N.W.2d 731, 733–734 (N.D. 1978), Part IV.** The Court rejected the requested default-willfulness instruction for a Title 16 election statute. Section 12.1-02-02 was part of the recodified criminal code and applied only to that code, not earlier unrepealed statutes outside it. The election statute contained no culpability requirement. [Primary text](https://static.case.law/nw2d/262/html/0731-01.html); `tmp/fargo_source_research/ndea.html` and `.txt`.

**State v. Goetz, 312 N.W.2d 1, 11–12 (N.D. October 15, 1981; rehearing October 23), issue thirteen.** The securities statute expressly used “willfully” but did not define it. The Court distinguished *Mueller* and *North Dakota Education Association*: those cases concerned attempts to import default culpability into outside-title statutes containing no culpability term. The trial instruction in *Goetz* used intentional or knowing conduct and omitted recklessness. That instruction imposed a greater burden on the State and did not harm Goetz; the Court did not need to decide whether it was erroneous. The opinion does not overrule the title limitation or make default willfulness applicable throughout the Century Code. [Primary text](https://static.case.law/nw2d/312/html/0001-01.html); `tmp/fargo_source_research/goetz.html` and `.txt`.

**Source boundary:** These decisions concern other offenses. They do not themselves construe the abortion definition, the medical-emergency trigger, the scope of §14-02.1-11, or the civil-damages provision. They supply state-law material that a fresh context must reconcile with any federal lower-court assertion about default culpability. This supplement draws no dispositive inference from the tension.

## Earlier Supreme Court vagueness decisions

**United States v. Vuitch, 402 U.S. 62, 69–72 (1971).** The Court construed the D.C. criminal abortion statute to require the prosecution to plead and prove that abortion was not necessary to preserve life or health. It read health to include psychological and physical well-being and held the standard sufficiently intelligible because physicians routinely make such judgments. Only the vagueness ground reached below was decided. The D.C. statute's construction and burden allocation are statute-specific. [Official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep402/usrep402062/usrep402062.pdf); local `vuitch.pdf`/`.txt` under `tmp/fargo_source_research/`.

**Doe v. Bolton, 410 U.S. 179, 191–192 (1973).** With the restrictive statutory reasons already removed below, the remaining requirement of necessity in the physician's best clinical judgment was not vague under *Vuitch*. The permitted judgment considered physical, emotional, psychological, familial, and age factors relevant to the patient's well-being. This is the specific statute and construction the Court adjudicated, not a categorical approval of any medical exception using clinical vocabulary. [Official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep410/usrep410179/usrep410179.pdf); local `doe.pdf`/`.txt`.

**Colautti v. Franklin, 439 U.S. 379, 390–401 (1979).** Blackmun's Court opinion was joined by Brennan, Stewart, Marshall, Powell, and Stevens. White dissented, joined by Burger and Rehnquist. The majority held the viability trigger vague because of two distinct ambiguities and fault not attached to the disputed viability determination; homicide fault concerning causing death did not supply culpability for that separate trigger. A subjective professional standard and culpability for its breach are different questions (395 n.12). The Court separately invalidated the care/technique provision, whose conflicting duties and fault uncertainty left maternal-health priority unclear. The Court reserved what particular fault level a properly drafted statute would require (396–397). It distinguished *Vuitch* and *Doe* because their constructions afforded broad clinical discretion. White disputed the state-law fault interpretation and urged state construction if substantial doubt remained; those objections are dissenting positions. [Official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep439/usrep439379/usrep439379.pdf); local `colautti.pdf`/`.txt`.

**Village of Hoffman Estates v. Flipside, 455 U.S. 489, 494–505 (1982).** Marshall's Court opinion was joined by Burger, Brennan, Blackmun, Powell, Rehnquist, and O'Connor. White concurred only in judgment. **Stevens did not participate.** The no-valid-application formulation at 494–495 expressly assumes no constitutionally protected conduct; the concluding holding at 505 repeats that boundary. Vagueness addresses notice and enforcement standards; economic regulation receives more tolerance, criminal penalties less, and interference with protected rights calls for greater clarity. Scienter may mitigate uncertainty, especially notice. Limiting constructions from state courts or enforcement agencies are considered (494 n.5). The ordinance was sufficiently clear as applied to the plaintiff's conduct even under a criminal or quasi-criminal standard. White would have confined the decision to vagueness of economic regulation and avoided the unnecessary overbreadth discussion. [Official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep455/usrep455489/usrep455489.pdf); local `hoffman.pdf`/`.txt`.

**Ohio v. Akron Center for Reproductive Health, 497 U.S. 502, 514, 518–524 (1990).** Kennedy wrote Parts I–IV for the Court, joined by Rehnquist, White, Stevens, O'Connor, and Scalia. Part V was joined only by Rehnquist, White, and Scalia. Scalia concurred separately; Stevens concurred in part and in judgment; Blackmun dissented with Brennan and Marshall. At 514, constitutional avoidance and facial-review discussion rejected invalidation based on a worst-case bypass delay that might never arise. This was parental-notification/bypass litigation, not a general adjudication of emergency-definition vagueness. Stevens stressed the need for protection in exceptional cases and reserved an assessment informed by implementation. Scalia joined the precedent-based result while maintaining his separate constitutional objection to abortion rights. The later governing law must determine the continuing reach of these propositions. [Official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep497/usrep497502/usrep497502.pdf); local `akron2.pdf`/`.txt`.

**Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352 (1983), source locator only.** O'Connor authored the Court opinion, joined by Burger, Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, and Stevens; Brennan also concurred; White dissented with Rehnquist. The case concerns indefinite police identification demands and enforcement discretion. The official full text has been downloaded; further substantive reliance should use a full read. [Official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep461/usrep461352/usrep461352.pdf); local `kolender.pdf`/`.txt`.
