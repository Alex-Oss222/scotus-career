## Question and procedural scope

Does the federal exclusionary rule require suppression of evidence seized incident to an arrest made on a police computer's report of a warrant that had been quashed seventeen days earlier, without regard to whether court employees or law-enforcement employees caused the erroneous entry to remain? The narrower issues are whether the judgment rests on an adequate independent state ground, whether Leon's objective-reliance rule extends to a court clerk's recording error, whether attribution or system reliability needs further findings, and what disposition follows.

Evans moved to suppress on March 27, 1991. After an evidentiary hearing on April 15, Judge I. Sylvan Brown suppressed the marijuana, then granted the State's request to dismiss without prejudice so it could appeal. The Arizona Court of Appeals reversed on May 19, 1992. The Arizona Supreme Court, January 13, 1994, vacated the intermediate opinion and sustained suppression. There is no conviction to reinstate, no acquittal, and no factual finding that Evans was innocent or guilty of the possession charge. Review concerns the suppression judgment and ensuing proceedings.

The petitioner's July 1994 merits brief, page 10, expressly treats the Fourth Amendment violation as conceded and distinguishes it from the remedy. Its account on page 2 also acknowledges that the officer would not have arrested Evans merely for driving with a suspended license. The earlier alternative-arrest argument cannot silently replace the conceded premise at this merits event. The petitioner's submitted merits argument seeks an exception for objectively reasonable reliance where court personnel caused the mistake. Its question presented is broader, but its assertion that court personnel caused this error remains disputed. No position taken in an unexamined amicus filing is attributed to that amicus or to either party.

## Primary record facts, with disputed propositions separated

The warrant issued December 13, 1990, following a failure to appear on December 12. Evans appeared on December 19, and the justice court quashed it and released him on his own recognizance. The date discrepancy in the State's lower appellate narrative, which describes December 12 as issuance, is resolved by the actual testimony and petitioner's merits brief: December 12 was the missed appearance; December 13 was issuance. The computer still listed the warrant when Evans was arrested January 5, 1991. The quashing-to-arrest interval was seventeen days. The dates do not establish a lawful administrative grace period.

**The arrest and search.** Officer Bryan Thomas Sargent saw Evans drive the wrong way on a one-way street outside the Phoenix police station around 6:30 p.m. Evans was cooperative, lacked a driver's license, and said his license had been suspended. The patrol-car computer confirmed the suspension and reported an outstanding misdemeanor warrant. Sargent advised Evans that he was under arrest on the warrant. During handcuffing, Evans's clenched hand opened and a hand-rolled cigarette fell. The officers suspected marijuana; a vehicle search incident to the arrest found a bag of marijuana beneath the passenger seat. The officer's separate telephone inquiry to the I-Bureau came **after the arrest**, and produced the same report that the warrant remained active. It was not a second pre-arrest validation. The State's earlier appellate descriptions reversing that sequence are contradicted by the transcript, Evans's answering brief at JA64, and the petitioner's eventual merits brief at page 2. A repeated police-side answer also does not independently establish contact with the issuing court's current file. [JA15–25.]

**Court-side procedure and the missing notation.** Chief clerk Frances Crossman testified about her particular East Phoenix Number One Justice Court, where she had served for eight years, after four years in another court. Ordinarily one of three traffic clerks telephoned the Sheriff's warrant section when a warrant was quashed and recorded that call and its recipient in the court file. The clerks were to check their work before filing. Evans's file contained the quash notation but no notation that the Sheriff was notified. Crossman first described the call as not made. The judge pressed the distinction between failure to call and failure to write down a completed call; she acknowledged that she could not know which occurred. None of the three potential callers testified. Her proposed causal explanation about a substitute judge's different notation was met by a sustained speculation objection. That explanation is not a finding or admissible proof of why the system failed. [JA27–37; the rotated file reproduced at JA13 was also visually inspected.]

**Both portions of the frequency testimony.** At JA37, Crossman called these incidents rare and estimated perhaps one every three or four years **in her particular court**. In the same testimony she then reported that the file search following this incident found **three other cases**: her wording was that there were three others on the same day that it happened, and that the people were not all arrested. Both statements are evidence; neither may be omitted. Her estimate is not a measured statewide error rate. The record does not supply the denominator of warrants or quashed warrants, an independently verified time series, a statewide audit, or a finding that this was either an isolated error or a chronically defective system.

The State's June 1991 appellate brief at JA58 and its July 1994 merits brief at page 4 characterize the other three as warrants quashed on the same day as Evans's whose removals had not been communicated, with corresponding missing court-file notations; the merits brief additionally says the other entries remained in the computer. Those are the State's accounts of the testimony, not independent judicial findings. Evans's answering brief at JA64 disputes the inference of nonnotification: the absence of a notation does not prove that no call occurred. The testimony establishes three additional irregularities uncovered in the associated file review and permits competing inferences about reliability and causation. It does not prove three additional arrests, three separate error dates, or that all four failures were caused by a court clerk. The description of the three as same-day quashings is specifically attributed to the State's filings rather than expanded from the ambiguous testimony.

**Sheriff-side procedure.** Records clerk Emily Luna described recording a recall call's time, date, caller, court, personal identifiers and warrant information; pulling the active file; clearing the computer; and checking again that it had cleared. Recall records for December 18–20 contained no entry for the Evans call. Luna had no personal knowledge that a call had not been received and omitted from the record. Her account of ordinary procedure supported an inference, not certainty. A contemporaneous police inquiry directed to the still-active Sheriff file would have received an active-warrant answer. That fact does not identify the original failure. [JA38–45.]

**Response upon discovery.** Crossman testified that on January 7, after receiving papers showing the warrant arrest, she called the jail, explained the earlier release, and faxed a release order preserving the pretrial date. She also described the file review that disclosed the other three irregularities. The record supports those prompt responsive acts; it does not establish the precise time when the computer entry was actually deleted or a later successful audit of all corrections. The candidate phrase “immediate correction of Evans's entry” is replaced by this supported account. [JA30, 37.]

**What the trial judge found and did not find.** Judge Brown said the arresting officers did what the computer report called for and expressed disagreement with the controlling Arizona appellate precedent, Greene. He nevertheless applied that precedent because negligent state recordkeeping had produced the invalid arrest. He referred expressly to possible negligence in the justice court or Sheriff's Office, regarded that distinction as immaterial, and made no attribution finding. His comments about Sargent's conduct are not findings that the recordkeeping system was reasonably maintained, that the error was isolated, or that all involved law-enforcement personnel were blameless. The State repeatedly asserted court-clerk responsibility; that assertion was not adopted as a finding. [JA46–54.]

## Judgment below and state-law boundaries

The full majority and dissent in **State v. Evans, 177 Ariz. 201, 866 P.2d 869 (Jan. 13, 1994)** have been read. The majority refused the intermediate court's attribution inference and emphasized the trial judge's failure to make that finding. It nonetheless held that suppression was proper even assuming court-clerk responsibility: routine record maintenance is ministerial, not a judge's discretionary determination of probable cause, and suppression can create institutional incentives to maintain reliable arrest records. It treated an already-quashed warrant as different from the facial warrant later invalidated in Leon. It rejected the alternative traffic-arrest account on this record.

The majority also discussed **A.R.S. §13-3925(C)(1)**, concerning a reasonable judgmental mistake about facts that would constitute probable cause, and **§13-3925(C)(2)**, concerning a technical violation including a warrant later invalidated due to a good-faith mistake. It concluded this negligent recordkeeping fit neither description. Those are separate statutory categories. The failure of an exception to bar exclusion is not itself an affirmative command to suppress independently of federal law.

The dissent quoted **§13-3925(A)** as addressing exclusion because of a peace officer's conduct in obtaining evidence, regarded that provision as inapplicable to this court-employee theory, and invoked State v. Bolt's description of Arizona's state exclusionary rule as no broader than the federal rule. It would apply Leon or remand for attribution findings. The majority does not expressly announce an independently sufficient Arizona constitutional suppression ground. It relies substantially on federal doctrine and disagrees about Leon's reach. Citation of state cases and state statutory exceptions must be examined, but cannot be treated automatically as either a separate state mandate or a waiver of state judicial authority.

The entire event-date Arizona statute has not been independently retrieved. The reproduced primary lower opinion supports the specific quoted categories and the parties' disagreement. A construction of any additional exception, definition, remedy or state-law command would require the full applicable enactment; this packet supplies none by inference. The federal question can be adjudicated within these boundaries.

## Entering law and date-eligible individual materials

* **Section 1257(a)** authorizes certiorari review of qualifying final judgments of a State's highest court where a federal title, right, privilege or immunity is specially set up or claimed, among its enumerated grounds. Arizona's prosecution was dismissed without prejudice following suppression; the state appellate route has ended. The continuing effect of suppression on any prosecution supplies the concrete dispute, not an invented conviction. The authorized slice does not create a broader Cox-finality holding.
* **Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032, 1040–1042 (1983)** supplies the presumption for an opinion fairly appearing to rest primarily on or to be interwoven with federal law, where the adequacy and independence of another ground are not clear. A clearly expressed, independently sufficient state ground remains outside federal review. O'Connor wrote that rule; Stevens's separate writing, 1065–1072, objects to the presumption and emphasizes restraint when States afford greater protection. These are distinct legal positions, not an Evans lineup.
* **Actual Sochor, June 8, 1992:** Souter's Part II-A, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas among others, honors a clear adequate independent preservation ground despite alternative federal merits discussion. Stevens would have reached that different claim. The ruling does not make any state citation an independent basis.
* **Actual Middlebrooks, December 13, 1993:** O'Connor's opinion, joined by all then-participating Justices including Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, dismisses where the original opinion and subsequent express clarification established an independently sufficient state constitutional ground. It does not replace Long with a new universal presumption or decide every finality problem.
* **United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897 (July 5, 1984):** objective reliance on a neutral magistrate's subsequently invalidated warrant can make exclusion inappropriate; constitutional validity and exclusion remain separate. Suppression remains appropriate for an affidavit knowingly or recklessly misleading the magistrate, abandonment of the judicial role, an affidavit so lacking in probable-cause indicia that reliance is entirely unreasonable, or a facially deficient warrant on which the executing officer cannot reasonably rely. The standard concerns reasonably trained officers, and footnote 24 includes officers obtaining the warrant or supplying material information as well as those executing it. The case assumes proper execution within the warrant's scope. O'Connor joined. Stevens's full separate writing distinguishes technical form from absence of constitutional authority, stresses institutional deterrence, and criticizes unnecessary broad decisionmaking. His separate theory is not controlling law.
* **Illinois v. Krull, 480 U.S. 340 (March 9, 1987):** Scalia joined the objective-reliance extension to an apparently constitutional statute, with limits for legislative abandonment and an enactment whose invalidity a reasonable officer should recognize. Footnote 17 reserves good-faith action outside statutory scope and preserves remand questions; it identifies the relevant actor as important to deterrence. O'Connor's dissent, joined by Stevens, distinguishes particularized independent judicial decisions from broad law-enforcement-enabling action and rejects treating street-officer blamelessness as the entire inquiry. Krull does not itself classify this recording failure.
* **Actual Dickerson, June 7, 1993:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter joined suppression where accepted findings showed unauthorized manipulation, while accepting lawful tactile recognition within a protective frisk. Thomas joined the legal rule but joined Blackmun's separate request for findings under the correct rule. This supplies those Justices' actual treatment of findings and remedial scope, not a controlling computer-record exception. Ginsburg and Breyer did not participate and inherit no predecessor's position.

## Competing lawful approaches and narrower alternatives

**Court-employee objective-reliance approach:** Leon's deterrence analysis can extend to a court employee's negligent reporting when officers reasonably rely on the court's apparently current warrant information and exclusion would not materially influence the responsible nonpolice actor. It must confront the difference between adjudication and ministerial maintenance, the three other irregularities, and the unresolved source of this error. Its narrow disposition can reject the state court's rule that attribution never matters and remand to resolve attribution and any legally relevant reliability issue. It cannot deem the warrant valid or direct final admission on a finding the trial judge never made.

**Institutional-recordkeeping approach:** removal of a quashed warrant is part of the mechanism authorizing arrest, so deterrence may operate through the institutions maintaining that mechanism even when the arresting officer is blameless. The same ministerial duty may support suppression whether assigned to court or police employees; the three other irregularities bear on whether merely labeling the episode rare answers the institutional concern. This approach must confront Leon's separate remedial inquiry, its judicial-error rationale, the costs of suppression, the prompt corrective acts, and the absence of a measured record establishing systemic breakdown. It need not overturn Leon to distinguish this nonadjudicative failure.

**Threshold and bounded alternatives:** apply Long and reach the federal question if no independent ground is established; if independence is genuinely unresolved, seek clarification rather than adjudicate an advisory federal issue; if a sufficient independent state ground is established, dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. On the merits, remand for attribution or reliability findings is narrower than final admission or a rule covering every negligent database. No available path adjudicates police-generated or mixed errors categorically without examining that separate issue, requires an officer to telephone the court in every arrest, finds constitutional adequacy from computerization alone, supplies a universal negligence threshold, or awards damages.
