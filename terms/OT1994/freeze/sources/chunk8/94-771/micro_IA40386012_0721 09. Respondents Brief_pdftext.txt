Whether the court of appeals could have reasonably con-
cluded that the legal incidence of Oklahoma's motor fuel
tax falls upon the retailer of the fuel?

Whether, if the answer to the preceding question is "yes," '
and the retailer is a federally-recognized Indian tribe
conducting business in Indian country, principles of fed-
eral preemption or Indian sovereignty preclude imposi-
tion of the state tax directly on the tribe?

Whether the Treaty of September 27, 1830, to which the
Chickasaw Nation later became beneficiary by treaty,
precludes state taxation of the income of Chickasaw tribal
members who are employed by the government of the
Chickasaw Nation within Chickasaw Indian country, but
who reside outside such Indian country?

                            1. Respondent Chickasaw Nation is a federally-recog-
                     nized Indian tribe, which, as of the time of the arrival of the
                     first Europeans in what is now the southern United States in
                     the sixteenth century, occupied most of what is now western
                     Tennessee, and portions of western Kentucky, northern Ala-
                     bama and Mississippi.!' Arrett M. Gipson, THE CHICKASAwWS
                     4-6 (1970); James H. MAtone, THE CHIcKASAW Nation 29-5]
                     (1922). From the earliest recorded times, the Chickasaw
                     Nation practiced a form of democratic government. /d. at 38.

                            Having defended itself from French attacks in the mid-
                     eighteenth century, Gipson, supra, at 46-57, and attacks from
                     various powers during the Revolutionary War, id. at 72-78,
                     the Chickasaw Nation established friendly relations with the
                     United States, Treaty of Jan. 3, 1786, arts. II, IV, 7 Stat. 21,
                     21-22. By the dawn of the nineteenth century, the power and
                     sophistication of the Chickasaw, Choctaw, Cherokee, Creek,

      By the election of 1828, Calhoun had served for twelve
years as Secretary of War under President Monroe, and Vice
President during the presidency of John Quincy Adams. Dur-

Andrew Jackson as President of the United States in Novem-
ber of that year (Calhoun was re-elected Vice President),
Indian policy would shift to a more aggressive one favoring
immediate removal. /d. at 99.

                     SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

       1. Two of the three issues now properly before this
Court!° address the validity of Oklahoma's motor fuel excise
tax as applied to Indian-country retail fuel sales by the Chick-
asaw Nation. As to those issues, the Chickasaw Nation will
defend three straightforward propositions.

      First, this Court has long adhered to a per se rule forbid-
ding state taxation whose legal incidence is on Indian tribes
regarding tribal activities in Indian country absent congres-
sional consent. The Tax Commission struggles mightily to
establish that "per se rule" means "balancing test," see infra
at 33-36, 39, and that the legal incidence question is second-
ary to the alleged locus of the "economic burden," see Pet. Br.
12, of the tax. But this Court has expressly rejected the
former proposition, inter alia, in County of Yakima v. Yakima
Indian Nation, 502 U.S. 251, 267 (1992), and expressly
rejected the latter, inter alia, in Gurley v. Rhoden, 421 U.S.
200, 204 (1975).

      Second, the per se rule, which establishes a zone of
predictability in an area of law with a greater need for
certainty than most, should not now be abandoned by this
Court. In that conclusion, it is pleased to be supported by the
eleven large Indian-country states which have appeared as
amici in this case. Brief of States of South Dakota [et al.] in
Support of Petitioner at 1-2 [hereinafter "States' Br."]; cf.

A. The Motor Fuel Tax Is Authorized By The Hayden-
      Cartwright Act

      1. The Tribe and the United States are wrong in contending
(at Resp. Br. 15-18, U.S. Br. 20-21) that the Commission is
precluded from relying on the Hayden-Cartwright Act in this Court
because it did not advance that Act below or in its petition for
certiorari. The Commission's reliance on the Act is "not a new
claim * * *, but a new argument to support what has been [its]
consistent claim" (Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.,
No. 93-1525 (Feb. 21, 1995), slip op. 4): that the motor fuel tax
is not preempted by federal law. Use of the Act to establish that
proposition "is fairly embraced within the question set forth in the
petition for certiorari." Jd. at 5 (citation omitted). Insofar as is
relevant here, the first question presented in the petition is
"[w]hether principles of federal preemption * * * preclude a State
from imposing a tax on sales of motor fuel by an Indian tribe"
(Pet. i); far from presenting a distinct issue, the Hayden-Cartwright
Act is additional authority that helps to answer that question by
establishing that federal law does not preempt the motor fuel tax.

See Yee v. Escondido, 112 §. Ct. 1522, 1532 (1992); United States
Nationa! Bank of Oregon v. Independent Insurance Agents, 113 S.
                                               3

Ct. 2173, 2178 (1993).* We therefore are frankly mystified by the
Tribe's contention (at Br. 15) that a federal statute authorizing state
legislation that is "otherwise forbidden by federal law" has nothing
to do with an inquiry into whether that legislation is preempted by
federal law.'

      2. On the merits, the United States (at Br. 21) and the
Tribe's other amici contend principally that the Act is inapplicable
here because it does not in terms authorize state taxation of sales on
Indian reservations. This assertion is insupportable. Although we
certainly agree that statutory ambiguities should be resolved in the
Tribe's favor, that principle is simply beside the point where, as
here, the statute is wholly unambiguous.

      The Hayden-Cartwright Act provides that States may tax sales
of motor fuel that occur "on United States military or other

      Engaging in the sort of ad hominem attack that typifies its brief, the

in the courts below. While we believe it inappropriate to respond in kind,
we note that the Commission's thinking below is, for present purposes,
beside the point; the relevant issue is whether the applicability of the
Hayden-Cartwright Act is subsumed within the question presented in the
petition. On that, the Tribe's wholly conclusory discussion is, in the

Tribe's word (at Br. 19 n.17), "bizarre."
