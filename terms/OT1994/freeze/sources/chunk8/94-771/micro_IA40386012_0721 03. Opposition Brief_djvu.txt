SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Petitioner maintains that “[t]his case involves a ‘vex- 
ing’ and ‘intricate’ question . . . : how to ‘reconcile the 
plenary power of the States over residents living within 
their borders with the semi-autonomous status of Indians 
living on tribal reservations.’ ” Petition at 7. At the very 
highest level of generality, that may be true — but only to 
the extent that any case in which the relative boundaries 
of state and tribal jurisdiction are in issue raises such 
questions. Virtually all Indian-law cases do. But when 
viewed at the level of specificity appropriate to a decision 
by this Court on a Petition for Certiorari, the case 
involves nothing more than a decision whether the Tenth 
Circuit was correct in determining that the legal inci- 
dence of Oklahoma’s motor fuel tax is on the retailer of

Indians who work on tribal trust lands but live outside of

this case presents the issue whether state taxation of the 
Indian-country income of tribal members (whether or not

Williams v. Lee, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959).3 It does not.

taxation is precluded by application of the “preemption/ 
balancing” test, as applied in cases such as California v. 
Cabazon Band of Mission Indians, 480 U.S. 202, 216 (1987). It 
does not.4

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


I. THE QUESTIONS PETITIONER PRESENTS ARE 
PROFOUNDLY FACTBOUND, AND THEIR RESOLU- 
TION BY THIS COURT WOULD RESOLVE NO ISSUE 
OF NATIONAL IMPORTANCE. 


A. The First Two Questions Presented by Petitioner 
Rise or Fall With the Correctness of the Court of 
Appeals’ Conclusion Regarding the Legal Inci- 
dence of Oklahoma’s Motor Fuel Tax. 


Since Petitioner Tax Commission’s second Question 
Presented both logically and analytically precedes its 
first, Respondent will address that question at the outset. 
That question inquires “[w]Jhether the legal incidence of 
Oklahoma’s tax on motor fuel falls upon the ultimate 
purchaser of the fuel.” Petition at i. 

Excluding its “importer” and “special fuel” provi- 
sions, see Oxta. Stat. tit. 68, §§ 601-723 (1991 & Supp. 
1993), the core of Oklahoma’s fuel tax scheme consumes 
twenty-two pages of double-column print in the unanno- 
tated official version of OxtaHoma Statutes. Both the 
nature of the Question Presented, and the nature of Peti- 
tioner’s and Respondent’s countervailing arguments con- 
cerning the legal incidence of the tax, compare Petition at 
20-23 with infra at 16-21, reveal the profoundly factbound 
nature of the issue concerning the legal incidence of the 


5 


tax established by that complex and lengthy statutory 
scheme. 

Moreover, although the omission was directed to the 
attention of Justice Breyer (as well as Petitioner) when 
Petitioner unsuccessfully applied to this Court for a stay 
of the Court of Appeals’ mandate in this case, see 
Response to Application for Stay at 8 & n.6, Oklahoma Tax 
Commission v. Chickasaw Nation (No. A-227) (U.S. 1994), 
Petitioner again defaults on any attempt to direct this 
Court’s attention to the motor fuel tax schemes of other 
states substantially identical in material respect to Okla- 
homa’s. Although the burden is on Petitioner to demon- 
strate the certworthiness of this case, Respondent has 
researched the fuel tax schemes of the twelve states with 
the largest Indian populations and/or the greatest quan- 
tity of Indian country. That research reveals that none of 
the other states has a fuel tax scheme substantially similar 
to Oklahoma’s in material respect: each places the legal 
incidence of the tax either “upstream” of the retailer (on 
the distributor), or “downstream” of the retailer (on the 
ultimate consumer).°

All the above has focused on the factbound nature of 
Petitioner’s second Question Presented. Its first appar- 
ently seeks to have this Court revisit its long line of 
holdings establishing a per se rule forbidding direct state 
taxation of Indian tribes regarding their Indian country 
activities absent congressional authorization. While 
Respondent will later establish that neither this Court nor 
any court of appeals has ever tolerated such taxation, 
infra at 21-25, four observations should suffice for present 
purposes. First, that issue cannot be reached without 
review of the factbound incidence-of-the-fuel-tax ques- 
tion. Second, since as Petitioner itself notes, supra at 6

Oklahoma legislature may well resolve the entire prob- 
lem, this case presents no compelling justification for 
revisiting firmly established and century-old caselaw. 
Third, Congress has been aware of this Court's per se rule 
for a very long time, infra at 24, and this Court has long 
been reluctant to overrule its own caselaw in nonconstitu-

becoming unsatisfactory.© And fourth, while Petitioner
