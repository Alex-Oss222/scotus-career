PETITION 


1. In this class action, plaintiffs seek declaratory 
and injunctive relief and refunds of unlawfully exacted 
fees and taxes on behalf of themselves and all others 
similarly situated. Plaintiffs challenge the lawfulness of 


18 


68 O.S. (Supp. 1983) §607.1 and 47 O.S. (Supp. 1983) 
§22.5j(K), under which the defendant Oklahoma Tax 
Commission (“Commission”) has imposed “retaliatory” 
taxes and fees on motor carriers whose vehicles are regis- 
tered in other states. Because these taxes and fees are not 
imposed on motor carriers whose vehicles are registered 
in Oklahoma, plaintiffs contend that they discriminate 
against interstate commerce and otherwise violate plain- 
tiffs’ rights under the Commerce Clause, Article I, Section 
8, Clause 3, of the United States Constitution; the Privi- 
leges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, Section 2, 
Clause 1, of the United states Constitution; and Section 
1983 of Title 42 of the U.S. Code. 


PARTIES 


2. Plaintiff Private Truck Council of America, Inc. 
(PTCA), is a nonprofit business association incorporated 
under the laws of New York State. It has its principal 
place of business in Washington, D.C. PTCA is comprised 
of over 1,800 companies presently operating trucks and 
related equipment as a private carriers within the scope 
and in furtherance of their own manufacturing, process- 
ing or other businesses, and associated companies. A 
large number of PTCA’s member companies, operating 
thousands of vehicles in interstate commerce through the 
state of Oklahoma, register their vehicles in states against 
which Oklahoma retaliates under the authority contained 
in Sections 607.1 and 22.5j. As such, these companies have 
been or will be required to pay the retaliatory taxes and 
fees imposed by the Oklahoma Tax Commission.

3. Plaintiff Circle W. Transportation, Inc., a wholly-

is a Delaware corporation with its principal place of 
business in North Huntingdon Pennsylvania. It operates 
numerous vehicles, all of which are authorized to operate 
in the State of Oklahoma. Fifteen of those vehicles are 
registered in Pennsylvania and are therefore subject to 
the retaliatory taxes and fees imposed under Sections 
607.1 and 22.5). 


4. Plaintiff Roy E. Griffis is an unincorporated

(1983 Supp.) §22.5j(K), to adopt rules and regulations 
implementing the retaliatory taxes and fees authorized in 
Sections 607.1 and 22.5j. The Commission is also the state 
agency which assesses, collects, and enforces those retal- 
iatory taxes and fees.

THE CLASS 


8. Pursuant to 12 O.S. (1984 Supp.) §2023 of the 
Oklahoma Statutes, plaintiffs bring this action on behalf

of Oklahoma. Most of those are paid by all motor carriers 
which use the State’s roads, regardless of their state of

COUNT | 


Unlawful Burden On Interstate Commerce 


18. Plaintiffs repeat and reallege the allegations con- 
tained in paragraphs 1 through 17 as if fully set forth 
herein. 


19. The retaliatory taxes and fees imposed on plain- 
tiffs and the plaintiff class pursuant to Sections 607.1 and 
22.5) constitute an unlawful burden on interstate com- 
merce in violation of the Commerce Clause of Article I, 
Section 8, Clause 3 of the United States Constitution 
because they are imposed only on motor carriers regis- 
tered outside the State of Oklahoma, while no compara- 
ble tax or fee is imposed on carriers registered in the State 
of Oklahoma. As such, the state by its taxing scheme 
favors domestic enterprises over enterprises from other 
states. Oklahoma’s retaliatory taxes and fees further 
impose an unlawful burden on interstate commerce 
because they vary in amount depending on the particular 
state of vehicle registration. On its face, therefore, the tax 
discriminates against interstate commerce. Moreover, the 
amount of the tax does not bear a reasonable relationship 
to the services provided by the State in connection there- 
with. 


COUNT II 


Denial of Privileges and Immunities 


20. Plaintiffs repeat and reallege the allegations con- 
tained in paragraphs 1 through 17 as if fully set forth 
herein.

21. The retaliatory taxes imposed pursuant to Sec- 
tions 607.1 and 22.5j constitute a denial of the privileges 
and immunities of plaintiffs and the plaintiff class, whose 
vehicles are registered outside the State of Oklahoma, 
because it imposes a tax on them solely because of their 
residence in another state. No comparable tax is imposed 
on residents of Oklahoma. Accordingly, these taxes dis- 
criminate against non-residents in violation of the Privi- 
leges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, Section 2, 
Clause 1.

FIRST DEFENSE 


6. There are essentially three tiers or structures of 
tax applied by the states to the operation by motor car- 
riers of motor vehicles within, and for the use of high- 
ways within the states’ borders. The first two, applied by 
essentially all states, are: (a) vehicle licensing and regis- 
tration, and; (b) motor fuel taxes. These two tiers of taxes 
are, in those states which are members of the Interna- 
tional Registration Plan (I.R.P.), apportioned or prorated 
for registered motor carriers according to the percentage 
of the carrier’s total annual miles driven within the state. 
Oklahoma, Arkansas and Pennsylvania are each members 
of the I.R.P.; and each state imposes both of these forms 
of tax, and apportions and prorates the taxes according to 
the reciprocal policies of the I.R.P. 


7. In addition, there is a third tier or structure of tax 
enacted by some of the states, which is not apportioned 
or prorated; and which may take the form of fuel marker 
fees, mileage or highway use tax, weight-distance tax, 
axle tax or fees, trip fees, etc. The State of Arkansas 
imposes such a third-tier, non-apportioned tax, denomi- 
nated the Highway Use Equalization Tax, which is cod- 
ified in §75-817.3, Arkansas Statutes, a copy of which is 
attached as “Exhibit A” hereto and made a part hereof. 
Under the provisions of said statute, non-resident motor 
carriers operating in the State of Arkansas must pay a 
nonapportionable, flat fee of $175.00 per year; while resi- 
dent motor carriers, if not otherwise exempt or excluded 


37 


by statute, may pay either said flat fee or a lesser amount, 
at the resident motor carrier’s option, computed on such 
resident motor carrier’s mileage driven within the State 
of Arkansas. Similarly, the State of Pennsylvania has 
enacted a third-structure tax, denominated an Axle Tax, 
and codified in 75 Pa.C.S.A., §9901, et seq., a copy of 
which is attached hereto as “Exhibit B” and made a part 
hereof. By the provisions of the Pennsylvania axle tax, 
motor carriers, including carriers base-registered in the 
State of Oklahoma operating in Pennsylvania, must pay a 
tax of $36.00 per axle per year. 


8. The imposition of such third-structure taxes upon 
Oklahoma resident operators of such vehicles constitutes 
a barrier to the conducting of interstate business by such 
Oklahoma residents, not encountered in other states 
where, by reciprocal agreement or otherwise, such third- 
structure taxes are not imposed upon Oklahoma resi- 
dents. 


9. In order to equalize the burden placed upon such 
Oklahoma resident or base-registered motor carriers by 
said third-structure taxes, the Oklahoma Legislature 
enacted 47 O.S. Supp. 1982, §22.5j(K), and 68 O.S. supp. 
1982, §607.1. The purpose of the two Oklahoma enact- 
ments, as stated in the statutes and as reflected in House 
Concurrent Resolution No. 1001, a copy of which is 
attached hereto as “Exhibit C” and made a part hereof, 
was to remove the economic disadvantage imposed upon 
Oklahoma residents and base-registered carriers, by 
requiring the base-registered residents of those states 
such as Arkansas and Pennsylvania, who impose the 
third-structure taxes, to pay a comparable or “mirror tax” 


38 


when such carriers operate within the borders of this 
State. 


10. Pursuant to the Oklahoma Statutes in question, 
plaintiffs Circle W Transportation Inc. and Roy E. Griffis, 
have been assessed the taxes as ail» ;ed in paragraph 16 
of plaintiffs’ petition. 


11. Defendant denies that plaintiffs are unconstitu- 
tionally discriminated against; or that the impact upon 
and the cost incurred by plaintiffs as a result of the 
imposition of the Oklahoma tax, constitute an impermis- 
sible burden upon or discrimination against the plaintiffs.

I. THE TAX COMMISSION’S MOTION IS WITHOUT 
MERIT AND SHOULD BE DENIED 


The Tax Commission’s motion seeks to drop the Pri- 
vate Truck Council of America, Inc. (PTCA) from the case 
and to dismiss the action as to it on the ground that 
PTCA is not itself a taxpayer. The Commission’s conten- 
tion is without merit because this is a class action which 
seeks not only refunds of taxes paid but also a declaration 
that the challenged statutes are unconstitutional and an 
injunction against their future enforcement. The standing 
of an association like PTCA to seek such relief on behalf 
of its members is well settled in Oklahoma, in the federal 
courts and in most other states. See, e.g., Democratic Party 
of Oklahoma v. Estep, 652 P.2d 271 (Okla. 1982); Oklahoma 
Association of Municipal Attorneys v. State of Oklahoma, 577 
P.2d 1310 (Okla. 1978); Oklahoma State AFL-CIO v. State 
Board For Property and Casualty Rates, 463 P.2d 693 (Okla. 
1970), all sustaining suits by associations brought on 
behalf of their members. 


67 


In Hunt v. Washington Apple Advertising Commission, 
432 U.S. 333, 343 (1977), the Supreme Court of the United 
States articulated the criteria that are generally applied in 
both federal and state courts to determine the standing of 
associations: 


[W]e have, recognized that an association has 
standing to bring suit on behalf of its members 
when: (a) its members would otherwise have 
standing to sue in their own right; (b) the inter- 
ests it seeks to protect are germane to the orga- 
nization’s purpose; and (c) neither the claim 
asserted, nor the relief requested, requires the 
participation of individual members in the law- 
suit. 


There can be no doubt that PTCA satisfies these 
criteria with respect to the claim for declaratory and 
injunctive relief in this case. PTCA is a non-profit busi- 
ness association that was incorporated in 1939 under the 
laws of New York and that now has more than 1,800 
members that operate from a few to large fleets of motor 
vehicles in interstate commerce in furtherance of their 
primary, non-transportation businesses and that are thus 
exempt as private carriers from the jurisdiction of the 
Interstate Commerce Commission. Many of its members 
are subject to the challenged taxes because they have 
vehicles registered in states against which Oklahoma 
retaliates, and would thus have standing to sue in their 
own right. 


The interest of its members that PTCA seeks to pro- 
tect are plainly germane to its purpose. PTCA has func- 
tioned since 1939 to promote and protect the interests of 


68 


private carriers by a variety of means, including dissem- 
inating information and undertaking other educational 
activities on behalf of its members; presenting the views 
of private carriers on federal and state legislative pro- 
posals or regulatory issues; supporting efforts to promote 
uniform state laws affecting private carriers; and partici- 
pating when necessary in judicial proceedings affecting 
the interests of its members. Among other judicial pro- 
ceedings in which PTCA has participated on behalf of its 
members, it successfully prosecuted a class action chal- 
lenging the constitutionality of an Indiana property tax 
imposed on interstate motor carriers, which resulted in 
more than $18 million in refunds being paid to more than 
20,000 motor carriers. Private Truck Council of America Inc. 
v. Huie, 466 N.E.2d 485 (Ind. 1984). Furthermore, the 
instant action is one of seven similar and coordinated 
class action suits that PTCA together with some of its 
members and other carriers have brought against the 
seven states that have implemented retaliatory taxes simi- 
lar to Oklahoma’s.! 


Finally, neither the constitutional claims asserted nor 
the petition’s request for declaratory and injunctive relief 
requires the participation of individual members in the 
suit. See, e.g., Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 515 (1975). 
Because the complaint includes two individual motor 
carriers and because it is brought as a class action, it also 
seeks by way of relief refunds of all taxes that may have 
been paid by the time of the Court’s final judgment. That

POST HEARING BRIEF IN OPPOSITION TO 
DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS 


On June 21, 1985, this Court held oral argument on a 
Motion to Dismiss filed on April 19, 1985 by defendants 
Odie A. Nance, Robert L. Wadley and J. L. Merrill in their 
official capacities. At that hearing, counsel for defendants 
relied upon certain case law not cited in their Motion to 
Dismiss for the proposition that the plaintiffs are not 
entitled to relief under the Federal Civil Rights Act, 42 
U.S.C. §1983. They argued that neither the Commerce 
Clause nor the Privileges and Immunities Clause, on 
which this action is based, protect any “rights, privileges 
or immunities secured by the Constitution” within the 
meaning of §1983. It is to this argument that this brief is 
addressed. 


Defendants’ motion should be denied, first, because 
the arguments contained therein have already been 


84 


advanced and rejected by this Court. As discussed in 
Plaintiffs’ Memorandum in Opposition to Motion to Dis- 
miss (at pp. 1-3), this Court’s order of March 29, 1985 
rejected defendants’ attempts to have this suit dismissed 
as to them in their official capacities. Yet that is precisely 
what defendants attempt to do again here. 


Moreover, as a matter of substantive law, defendants’ 
contention is clearly incorrect. 49 U.S.C. §1983 offers 
wide-ranging protection against the deprivation of “any 
rights, privileges or immunities secured by the Constitu- 
tion...” (emphasis added), including those protected by 
the Commerce Clause and the Privileges and Immunities 
Clause. The Supreme Court of the United States in con- 
struing this section has said: 


[T]here can be no doubt that §1 of the Civil 
Rights Act [of 1871] was intended to provide a 
remedy to be broadly construed against all forms of 
official violation of federally protected rights. 


Monell v. New York City Department of Social Services, 436 
U.S. 658, 700-701 (1978) (emphasis added). The Court has 
further stated that §1983 embraces “all of the Constitution 
and laws of the United States,” Lynch v. Household Finance 
Corporation, 405 U.S. 538, 549 n..16 (1972) (emphasis 
added). Thus, it is clear that §1983 was intended to be 
broadly construed to protect all federal rights. 


In support of their position, the defendants cited an 
Eighth Circuit case which held that the Commerce Clause 
did not secure a right, privilege or immunity within the 
meaning of §1983. Consolidated Freightways Corp. of Del. v. 
Kassell, 730 F. 2d 1139 (8th Cir. 1984). However, the defen- 
dants failed to discuss two other cases, one of which was 


85 


affirmed by the United States Supreme Court, which have 
held that a violation of the Commerce Clause is indeed 
actionable under §1983. In Confederated Salish and Kootenai 
Tribes v. Moe, 392 F.Supp. 1297 (D. Mont. 1975), aff'd, 425 
U.S. 463 (1976), an Indian tribe and some of its members 
brought a class action contesting the constitutionality of 
Montana’s cigarette sales tax and dealer licensing statutes 
as applied to them because, among other things, it vio- 
lated the Commerce Clause. The court concluded the 
§1983 claim was proper, stating: 


Although §1983 was enacted primarily to 
enforce the Fourteenth Amendment, Lynch v. 
Household Finance Corp., 405 U.S. 538, 545, 92 
S.Ct. 1113, 31 L.Ed.2d 424 (1972), the Court held 
in Lynch that the phrase “rights, privileges, or 
immunities secured by the Constitution and 
laws” includes not only Fourteenth Amendment 
rights, but “[all of] the Constitution [and] laws 
of the United States”, noting its interpretation of 
similar language with respect to 18 U.S.C. §242 
in United States v. Price, 383 U.S. 787, 797, 86 
S.Ct. 1152, 16 L.Ed.2d 267 (1966). 404 U.S. at 549, 
n. 16. We therefore have no difficulty in holding 
that plaintiffs’ alleged violation of Commerce 
Clause rights is sufficient to state a claim under 
§1983. 


392 FSupp. at 1304-1305. Later, in Kennecott Corp. v. 
Smith, 637 F.2d 181 (3rd Cir. 1980), the Third Circuit held 
that §1983 encompasses Commerce Clause claims. The 
court stated: 


The present action is properly brought under 
§1983 because it seeks redress for deprivations 
of constitutional rights secured by the Commerce 
Clause and of federal statutory rights protected 


86 


by the Williams Act. See, Maine v. Thiboutot, __ 
U.S. __, 100 S.Ct. 2502, 65 L.Ed.2d 555 (1980). 


637 F.2d at 186 (emphasis added). 


The court in Consolidated Freightways based its hold- 
ing on the conclusion that the Commerce Clause does not 
protect or establish individual rights against government 
interference. That conclusion, however, is manifestly 
incorrect. While the underlying purpose of the Commerce 
Clause is to “create an area of free trade among the 
several states” McCleod v. J. E. Dilworth Co., 322 U.S. 327, 
330 (1944), this purpose is achieved by protecting individ- 
uals engaged: in commerce from discriminatory and 
excessive taxation and regulatory burdens imposed by 
state governments. 


Thus, the United States Supreme Court has often 
construed the Commerce Clause as protecting individual 
rights. In Boston Stock Exchange v. State Tax Commission, 
429 U.S. 318 (1977), the Court granted standing to several 
stock exchanges to sue on behalf of their members chal- 
lenging a New York tax which discriminated against out- 
of-state stock exchanges and their members. In discussing 
the exchanges’ standing to sue on behalf of their mem- 
bers, the Court clearly indicated that the Commerce 
Clause protects individual rights: 


The exchanges are asserting their right under the 
Commerce Clause to engage in interstate commerce 
free of discriminatory taxes on their business and 
they allege that the transfer tax indirectly 
infringes on that right. 


87 


420 U.S. at 320 n. 3 (emphasis added). In further discuss- 
ing the harm suffered by the individual members, the 
Court continued: 


The members therefore suffered an actual injury 
within the zone of interests protected by the Com- 
merce Clause and the exchanges satisfy the 
requirements for representational standing. 


Id. (emphasis added). Thus, the Commerce Clause pro- 
tects a specific zone of interests for individuals. Likewise, 
in Maryland v. Louisiana, the Court’s ultimate holding was 
that 


[T]he first use tax is unconstitutional under the 
Commerce Clause because it unfairly discrimi- 
nates against purchasers of gas moving through 
Louisiana in interstate commerce. 


451 U.S. at 760. It is significant that the disputed Louisi- 
ana tax was unconstitutional because it discriminated 
against purchasers of gas, not because it disrupted the 
federal-state balance with respect to the regulation of 
commerce. 


With respect to the Privileges and Immunities Clause, 
it would be anomalous indeed if §1983 did not protect 
against violations of that clause, since §1983 specifically 
refers to “rights, privileges or immunities secured by the 
Constitution.” (Emphasis added.) Moreover, as the plain- 
tiffs noted in their memorandum in support of their 
motion for summary judgment, the protections of the 
Commerce Clause and the Privileges and Immunities 
Clause are similar and frequently overlapping. Hicklin v. 
Orbeck, 437 U.S. 518, 531-532 (1978). Thus, for the same 
reasons described above with respect to the Commerce 


88 


Clause, violations of the Privileges and Immunities 
Clause should be actionable under §1983. 


The case cited by the defendants at oral argument, 
Spencer v. South Carolina Tax Commission, 316 S.E.2d 386 
(S.C. 1984), aff’d by an equally divided court, 53 U.S.L.W. 
4431 (1985), is inapposite. In that case, the trial court “did 
not address the §1983 claim” and the South Carolina 
Supreme Court did not squarely address the applicability 
of §1983 to a Privileges and Immunities Clause claim. 
There is no discussion whatever of the Privileges and 
Immunities Clause or why it should not be covered by 
§1983. Moreover, since the decision below was affirmed 
by an equally divided Supreme Court, the affirmance has 
no applicability beyond that specific case. Laird v. Tatum, 
409 U.S. 824 (1972) (per Rehnquist, J., on motion to 
recuse). 


In further support of their claim that the plaintiffs do 
not have an appropriate claim under §1983, and their 
related contention that plaintiffs are not entitled to costs 
and attorneys’ fees under 42 U.S.C. §1988, the defendants 
cited Brown v. Hornbeck, 458 A.2d 900 (Md. 1983) and 
Backus v. Chilivis, 224 S.E.2d 370 (Ga. 1976). These cases 
are both inapposite. 


In Brown v. Hornbeck, the court denied §1988 attor- 
neys’ fees after a case had settled because it found that 
the plaintiffs had no colorable Equal Protection claim 
which would be actionable under §1983. In this case, as 
has been shown, the plaintiffs have a valid §1983 claim. 


In Backus v. Chivilis, the court denied §1983 damages 
for a claim of unequal taxation because the state had 
statutorily established procedures for equalizing such 


89 


assessments. Because of the adequate state law remedy, 
the court held that the federal cause of action under §1983 
was precluded. However, the Backus holding has no 
applicability in this case. Here, although the defendants 
concede that the State’s tax refund statute, 68 O.S. (1981) 
§226, applies to afford refunds to the plaintiffs, they deny 
that costs and attorneys’ fees are available to the plain- 
tiffs under the common fund doctrine should they suc- 
ceed in obtaining refunds for the members of the class. 
As shown in plaintiffs’ memorandum in support of their 
motion for summary judgment, this is incorrect. How- 
ever, assuming arguendo that the defendants are correct, 
Backus would not apply in this case because, according to 
defendants’ theory of the law, the plaintiffs would not be 
able to obtain costs and attorneys’ fees under state law, 
and therefore would not have a complete and adequate 
remedy under state law. Attorneys’ fees obviously are a 
substantial part of the plaintiffs’ remedy. 


For all the foregoing reasons, in addition to the rea- 
sons set forth in plaintiffs’ initial Memorandum In Oppo- 
sition To Motion To Dismiss, defendants’ Motion To 
Dismiss should be denied.

SUPPLEMENTAL BRIEF IN SUPPORT 
OF DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO 
DISMISS COUNT III 


(Filed June 27, 1985) 


This action, brought pursuant to 68 O.S. 1981, §226, 
involves a challenge on constitutional grounds to Okla- 
homa’s reciprocal tax levied by 68 OS. Supp. 1982, §607.1 
and 47 O.S. Supp. 1982, §22.5(j)(K). In Count I of the 
Petition, Plaintiffs allege that the taxes imposed by said 
statutes constitute an unconstitutional burden on inter- 
state commerce. In Count II it is alleged that the taxes 
constitute a violation of the Privileges and Immunities 
Clause of the United States Constitution. In Count III 
Plaintiffs allege that by the imposition of these taxes as 
provided by statute, the Defendants “are therefore liable 
to Plaintiffs pursuant to 42 U.S.C. §1983.” The relief 
sought in this action is a declaration by this Court that 
said taxes are unconstitutional, and a refund of the taxes 
paid under said statute, both of which forms of relief are 


92 


available under §226. By inclusion of Count III, Plaintiffs 
also seek an award of attorneys fees, which are not pro- 
vided for by §226. 


Defendants Nance, Wadley, and Merrill, Commis- 
sioners of the Oklahoma Tax Commission, have moved 
the Court to dismiss Count III on the grounds that said 
Count Iil fails to state a claim upon which relief can be 
granted, for the reason that the relief sought in Count III, 
i.e., attorneys’ fees, is not an independent right of action 
cognizable under the Federal Civil Rights Act, 42 U.S.C. 
§1983. 


Upon oral argument of this motion, Plaintiffs’ coun- 
sel conceded that the Commerce Clause does not secure 
rights cognizable under §1983; but requested leave to 
rebut by supplemental brief the authorities cited by 
Defendant on the proposition that §1983 could not be 
used to circumvent the provisions of state law for the sole 
purpose of asserting a claim for attorneys fees. 


ARGUMENT AND AUTHORITIES 


Initially, Plaintiffs assert that the issue here in ques- 
tion, i.e., the applicability of 42 U.S.C. §1983 for the 
purposes of claiming attorneys’ fees under the attendant 
Section 1988, has been previously ruled upon by this 
Court. It has not. On March 29, 1985, this Court dismissed 
this action as against the individual Defendants Nance, 
Wadley, and Merrill, for the reason that no cause of action 
had been stated or claimed against them as individuals. 
The issue of whether §1983 may be used to support a 
claim for attorneys’ fees in derogation of state law was 


| 


iat 


93 


neither presented to nor ruled upon by this Court. Plain- 
tiffs’ assertion to the contrary is at best an attempt to 
confuse this issue; and at worst an attempt to mislead this 
Court. 


As to the question whether the Commerce Clause 
secures rights cognizable under 42 U.S.C. §1983, the 
Plaintiffs, having conceded Defendant's position in oral 
argument, now reassert this claim, relying heavily upon 
Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes v. Moe, 392 F. Supp. 
1297 (D. Mont. 1975), and Kennecott Corp. v. Smith, 637 
F.2d 181 (3rd Cir. 1980). However, none of the cases cited 
by Plaintiffs substantially discuss whether the Commerce 
Clause is a Constitutional provision which secures rights 
within the meaning of Section 1983; nor did those cases 
so hold. Further, Kootenai, which dealt with state taxation 
of cigarette sales by Indian tribes on tribal reservation 
land, was affirmed by the United States Supreme Court 
on the basis of the supremacy clause rather than the 
Commerce Clause. 425 U.S. 463, 96 S.Ct. 1634. The 
Supreme Court in discussing the jurisdictional issues pre- 
sented by that case, noted that the District Court had 
found jurisdiction over the individual Indian plaintiffs on 
the basis of 28 U.S.C. §1343(3), together with their allega- 
tion that the taxes deprived them of a right secured by 
the Commerce Clause, but went on to state “We need not 
decide this question, however, since all of the substantive 
issues raised on appeal can be reached by deciding the 
claims of the tribe alone, which did bring this action in 
the District court under Section 1362. Id., at 425 U.S. 475, 
n. 14. The only case cited to this Court after substantial 
research by both sides, and which deals directly and 
squarely with the issue before this Court, is Consolidated 


94 


Freightways Corporation of Delaware v. Kassel, 730 F.2d 1139 
(8th Cir. 1984). The same cases relied upon by plaintiffs in 
this case were effectively disposed of by the opinion and 
ruling in Consolidated Freightways. On October 1, 1984, 
certiorari was denied by the United States Supreme 
Court, __ U.S. ___, 105 S.Ct. 126; and that ruling has been 
followed in the-7th Circuit in Gould, Inc. v. Wisconsin 
Department of Industry, Labor and Human Relations, 750 
F.2d 608 (1984). The only federal courts dealing squarely 
with this issue having ruled that the Commerce Clause 
does not secure rights cognizable under Section 1983, this 
Court should do likewise. 


As to Defendants’ second proposition, the issue 
before this Court is not whether the Privileges and Immu- 
nities Clause, in the abstract, secures rights cognizable 
under 42 U.S.C. 1983. The question before this Court is 
whether Section 1983 may be invoked by plaintiffs for the 
sole purpose of claiming attorneys’ fees and circumvent- 
ing the remedies provided by state law. In oral argument 
and briefing on this point, it has been repeatedly asserted 
by Defendants, and never denied by Plaintiffs, that the 
sole reason for the inclusion of Count III in the petition 
was and is for the purpose of seeking attorneys’ fees. 
With the exception of attorneys’ fees, all the relief to 
which plaintiffs may be entitled in this case, and the 
procedure to obtain such relief, is provided for and gov- 
erned by 68 O.S. 1981, §226, under which this action was 
brought. By the inclusion of Count III, Plaintiffs are 
merely trying to establish an independent right of action 
to recover attorneys’ fees. In Brown v. Hornbeck, 458 A.2d 
900 (1983), the Maryland court denied just such a claim. 
Further, in Backus v. Chilivis, 236 Ga. 500, 224 S.E. 2d 370 


95 


(1976), the Georgia Supreme Court ruled that where tax- 
payers had a plain, speedy and efficient remedy under 
state law to challenge ad valorem tax assessments, the 
plaintiffs could not avail themselves of a claim under 
Section 1983 to circumvent the remedies provided by 
state law. Similarly, in Spencer v. South Carolina Tax Com- 
mission, 316 S.E. 2d 386 (S.C. 1984), affirmed March 27, 
1985, __ U.S. __, 53 L.W. 4431, the South Carolina 
Supreme Court, following the reasoning and ruling in 
Chapman v. Houston Welfare Rights Organizations, 441 U.S. 
600, 99 S.Ct. 1905 (1979), and the rulings in Backus, supra, 
and Brown, supra, held that Section 1983, being remedial, 
does not provide for any substantive rights; that state 
remedies for asserting rights may not be circumvented by 
invoking Section 1983; and that where it could reasonably — 
be inferred that the sole reason that taxpayers alleged 
Section 1983 in their suit challenging state tax statutes 
was to justify the allowance of counsel fees which were 
not provided for in the state law, the taxpayers would not 
be entitled to an award of attorneys fees under Section 
1988. 


Understandably, Plaintiffs do not agree with the cited 
cases, and would like this Court to ignore the rule of law 
announced therein. However, Plaintiffs cite no authority 
on this point, either in derogation of these rulings or in 
support of their position. Having been given the oppor- 
tunity to cite to this Court authority in support of their 
position or in rebuttal to the cases cited by Defendants, 
Plaintiffs have failed to do so. 


It should be noted that Oklahoma’s statutory pro- 
cedures for contesting the constitutional validity of this 
State’s tax statutes, and for obtaining refunds of taxes 


96 


found to be unconstitutional, including 68 O.S. 1981, 
§226, have met the test in the federal courts, and have 
been found to provide a plain, speedy, and adequate 
remedy at law. Bunte Candies, Inc. v. Cartwright, 508 F. 
Supp. 229 (D.C.W.D. Okl1.1981), Cities Service Gas Company 
v. Oklahoma Tax Commission, 656 F.2d 584 (10th Cir. 1981). 


CONCLUSION 


It is clear the Plaintiffs have totally failed in Count II] 
to state a claim for which relief can be granted; that 
Count III asserts no substantial rights cognizable under 
42 U.S.C. §1983; and that Count III was included for the 
sole purpose of attempting to claim attorneys’ fees in 
circumvention of the state statutes under which this 
action was brought. Therefore, Count III of the Petition 
should be dismissed.

STATEMENT OF FACTS 


This case has a long history. As set forth more fully in 
this Court’s opinion, appellants filed this suit in Decem- 
ber 1984 as a class action challenging the constitutionality 
of certain Oklahoma statutes that imposed retaliatory 
taxes and fees on out-of-state motor carriers operating in 
Oklahoma. Appellants filed a motion for summary judg- 
ment in February 1985.2 On February 6, 1987 District 
Judge John Amick issued a brief opinion upholding the 
challenged taxes on the merits and denying appellants’ 
motion. Appellants appealed to this Court. 


On June 28, 1990 this Court issued an opinion revers- 
ing Judge Amick. The Court held: “The questioned stat- 
utes are plainly discriminatory” and thus violated the 
Commerce Clause under the test set forth in Complete 
Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 271, 279 (1977). 806 
P.2d at 604. The Court stated: “Obviously . .. Oklahoma’s 
retaliatory taxes do not pass the third and fourth prongs 
[of the Complete Auto test].” Id.

ARGUMENT 


Appellants submit that there is no need at this time 
for this Court’s consideration of any issues, or the brief- 
ing of any issues to this Court. Instead, the Court should 
simply remand the case to the trial court for its consider- 
ation and resolution of the various issues remaining in 
dispute, inasmuch as the resolution of those issues will 
turn in large part on additional factual submissions. Any 
party dissatisfied with the district court’s resolution of 
any of those issues may thereafter appeal to this Court. 


The earlier decisions of this Court and the United 
States Supreme Court establish, appellants submit, the 
following propositions beyond dispute: 


1. Appellants and the plaintiff class are enti- 
tled to refunds plus interest for all taxes 
paid by them for tax periods after June 23, 
1987. 


2. Appellants are also entitled to recover rea- 
sonable attorneys’ fees and litigation 
expenses from the Tax Commission under 42 
U.S.C. § 1988. 


The latter proposition is clearly required by Dennis and 
other cases. It is now firmly established that plaintiffs 


114 


who are entitled to relief under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 are also 
entitled to recover their reasonable attorneys’ fees and 
litigation expenses from the state defendant whether or 
not the plaintiffs might also have been entitled to, or 
obtained substantive redress on, other grounds. See, e.g., 
Maher v. Gagne, 448 U.S. 122, 132 n. 15 (1980); Maine v. 
Thiboutot, 448 U.S. 1,10-11 (1980). It is also settled that a 
state may not avoid this obligation on any ground based 
on state law or immunity or asserted good faith. Howlett 
v. Rose, 110 S.Ct. 2430 (1990); Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 
693-700 (1978). 


The issues that have not been resolved by the deci- 
sions of this Court and the United States Supreme Court 
and which remain to be resolved are as follows: 


1. The precise procedures for refunding taxes 
and accrued interest to taxpayers, including 
the means of notifying taxpayers, requiring 
proofs of claims, the disposition of 
unrefundable amounts, etc. 


2. The amount of reasonable attorneys’ fees and 
litigation expenses that plaintiffs are entitled to 
be awarded from the Tax Commission under 
42 U.S.C. § 1988 and from the common fund 
under the common fund doctrine.° 


> A third unresolved issue concerns the entitlement of the 
plaintiff class to refunds of taxes for periods prior to June 23, 
1987. This Court rejected appellants’ claim for such refunds, but 
the Court’s decision was vacated by the United States Supreme 
Court. Although that Court’s order did not refer to the refund 
issue, the vacating of this Court’s entire decision appears to 
leave that issue open. In the interest of terminating this litiga- 
tion in some reasonable time frame, however, appellants have 
decided not to pursue that issue further.

With respect to the two issues that remain to be 
resolved, if the Court grants this Motion to Remand, 
appellants intend to promptly file with the district court a 
motion to approve a plan of distribution and a petition 
for an award of attorneys’ fees and litigation expenses. 
The petition for fees and expenses will include affidavits 
and time and expense records of the attorneys, together 
with a memorandum of points and authorities concerning 
the pertinent legal principles. Appellants will also ask the 
trial court to order notice to be issued to members of the 
plaintiff class of the substance of the distribution plan 
and the fee petition, and to schedule a hearing on those 
matters at which any interested class member can appear 
and be heard. 


CONCLUSION 


The Court should remand this case to the District 
Court for Oklahoma County to determine an appropriate 
procedure for refunding taxes and interest to the plaintiff 
class and to determine and award reasonable attorneys’ 
fees and litigation costs to appellants.

Dear Mr. Patterson: 


This letter is to draw the Court’s attention to and 
make a further request for expedited consideration of the 
above-captioned case, which has been languishing before 
the Court for two and one-half years awaiting a purely 
ministerial act of remand to the District Court. 


On June 28, 1991 the United States Supreme Court 
granted a writ of certiorari, vacated the judgment of this 
Court herein and remanded the case to this Court in light 
of the decision in Dennis v. Higgins, 498 U.S. __, 111 S.Ct. 
865 (1991). The effect of the U.S. Supreme Court’s deci- 
sion was to establish that plaintiffs are entitled to recover 
attorneys’ fees under 42 U.S.C. § 1988, but otherwise to 
leave in place this Court's decision granting the plaintiff 


120 


taxpayers refunds of the motor carrier taxes that were the 
subject of this action. 


Plaintiffs herein now seek to have the case remanded 
to the Oklahoma District Court solely for the purpose of 
petitioning for a distribution of those refunds and 
requesting an award of attorneys’ fees. There are no other 
issues to be considered by this Court or the District 
Court. 


Plaintiffs initially thought that this Court would 
automatically remand the case to the lower court. When 
that did not happen after one year from the Supreme 
Court’s decision, plaintiffs filed a motion for remand in 
this Court on July 20, 1992. The Oklahoma Tax Commis- 
sion did not file an opposition or any response what- 
soever to that motion. When the Court still did not act, I 
sent a letter to you dated October 8, 1992 asking for 
expedited consideration of the unopposed motion. 


Now, over fourteen months later, the Court still has 
not remanded the case to the District Court. The effect of 
this failure to act is to deny relief to the plaintiffs, as they 
are unable to obtain refunds of the taxes that this Court 
has declared unconstitutional. Again, the motion to 
remand is unopposed, and there are no legal issues for 
the Court to weigh. All we seek is the purely ministerial 
act of remanding the case to the trial court for purposes 
of carrying out the decisions of this Court and the U‘S. 
Supreme Court.
