      The Commerce Clause of the United States Constitu-

       Every person who, under color of any statute,

       State or Territory or the District of Columbia,

       the United States or other person within the

                     STATEMENT OF THE CASE

      Petitioners successfully challenged a state truck tax
as violating their rights under the Commerce Clause of
the United States Constitution. In the decision under
review, the Supreme Court of Oklahoma has, for the
second time, held that these petitioners are not entitled to
federal remedies under 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 1988 not-
withstanding this Court's action in vacating and remand-
ing the first decision denying such relief "for further
consideration in light of Dennis v. Higgins, 498 U.S. 439
(1991)." National Private Truck Council, Inc. v. Oklahoma Tax

Commission, 501 U.S. 1247 (1991).

      A. Proceedings Prior to this Court's Remand

     This case has a long and tortuous history. Over ten
years ago, on December 17, 1984 petitioners filed a class
action suit in Oklahoma state court challenging the con-
stitutionality of certain "retaliatory taxes" that the State
of Oklahoma imposed on motor carriers who operated in
Oklahoma trucks that were registered in certain other
states. J.A. 17. Petitioners filed the case in Oklahoma state
court because they assumed that the state courts would
provide them with a "plain, speedy and efficient remedy"
and because access to federal court for injunctive and
declaratory relief was barred by the Tax Injunctior Act,
28 U.S.C. § 1341, and for monetary remedies by the
Eleventh Amendment. More than ten years later, that
expectation has fallen somewhat short of fulfillment.

     The challenged taxes were not imposed on trucks
registered in Oklahoma. Pet. App. 27a-28a, 59a. The
 purpose of these taxes was to retaliate against states
 imposing so-called "third structure taxes,"3 which certain
 states have imposed on all motor carriers operating in
 those states, including Oklahoma-registered carriers, and
 to which the Oklahoma legislature objected. These stat-
 utes carried out this purpose by authorizing respondents
 to impose taxes on carriers whose vehicles are operated
 in Oklahoma but are registered in states that impose third

 structure taxes. These retaliatory taxes are equal in

 amount to the third structure tax imposed by the carriers'
 State of registration. By thus penalizing carriers from
 third-structure tax states, Oklahoma's legislature hoped
 to pressure the legislatures of those states to repeal their
 third-structure taxes or to exempt Oklahoma-based car-

 riers from them.

        3 A "third structure tax" is one that is imposed on motor

carriers in addition to the more traditional charges states have

levied on such carriers, which are registration fees and fuel

taxes (so-called "first structure" and "second structure" taxes).

Examples of third structure taxes include ton-mile taxes, which
are based on the weight of trucks and the mileage operated in

the taxing state, and axle taxes, which impose a flat charge

based on the number of axles on each vehicle. In American

Trucking Ass`ns v. Scheiner, 483 U.S. 266 (1987), this Court invali-

dated one such third structure tax, Pennsylvania's axle tax. The
Court noted that flat taxes like Pennsylvania's had prompted

Oklahoma and six other states to enact retaliatory taxes, and it
stated: "Such taxes can obviously divide and disrupt the market

for interstate transportation services." Id. at 285 (footnotes omit-
ted).

        B. This Court's Remand

      Petitioners filed a petition for a writ of certiorari to
review two aspects of the Oklahoma Supreme Court's
decision: its denial of refunds of taxes paid with respect
to the period before the Scheiner decision and its denial of
petitioners' claims under 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 1988.
Petitioners pointed out that the denial of their claims
under §§ 1983 and 1988 conflicted directly with this

Court's decision in Dennis v. Higgins. In Dennis, which

involved the challenge by NPTC and others to Nebraska's
retaliatory truck tax on the same Commerce Clause
ground asserted in this case, the Court held that the
taxpayer's Commerce Clause claims were cognizable
under § 1983. The Court stated that § 1983 "provides a
remedy, to be broadly construed, against all forms of

       C. The Decision Below

      After several unsuccessful attempts to resolve out-
standing issues with respondents, petitioners on July 20,
1992 filed a motion with the Oklahoma Supreme Court to
remand the case to permit the trial court to establish a
plan of distribution of tax refunds and accrued interest?
and to determine petitioners' attorneys fees and litigation
expenses. Respondents filed no opposition or other
response to that motion.

                     SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

      The holding below that state courts are not required
to apply and enforce claims arising under 42 U.S.C.
§§ 1983 and 1988 conflicts with the principle, long-estab-
lished in this Court's decisions, that state courts must
entertain causes of action arising under federal law. This
Court has consistently held that under the Supremacy
Clause state courts have an obligation to apply and
enforce federal law as the "Supreme Law of the Land." In
doing so, the Court has specifically rejected the argument
that a state court may refuse to hear a federal claim
because the rights or remedies involved conflict with
state policy. Moreover, state courts may not discriminate
against rights arising under federal laws.

      10 The court also held that petitioners' claim of entitlement
to attorney's fees under the state law common fund doctrine
was barred by its earlier opinion and the law of the case doc-
trine (Pet. App. 10-1la), even though its earlier opinion had
never addressed petitioners' entitlement to fees and expenses
under the common fund doctrine.
                                          13

      This basic principle that state courts must entertain
federal claims also applies to claims under 42 U.S.C.
§§ 1983 and 1988. In cases arising under § 1983, this Court
has held that a state court may not refuse to entertain a
federal claim absent a "valid excuse," and that an excuse
that is inconsistent with or violates federal law is not a
valid excuse. States may apply their own neutral pro-
cedural rules to federal claims, unless those rules are pre-
empted by federal law.

     The principle of federal court noninterference in state
tax matters underlying the Tax Injunction Act does not
constitute a valid excuse for a state court to refuse to hear
a federal claim involving state taxes. The Tax Injunction
Act limits the jurisdiction of federal courts, not state
courts. Furthermore, allowing state courts to hear § 1983
claims challenging state taxes implements the policies of
both § 1983 and the Tax Injunction Act. Under the Tax
Injunction Act, federal courts are precluded from hearing
state tax claims because those claims may be heard in state

court.

      Additionally, the comity consideration arising from
the Tax Injunction Act is not a neutral rule of state court
procedure. Nor is the desire of the court below for exact
uniformity in federal and state court jurisdiction. There
are many instances where federal courts cannot hear
claims cognizable in state courts because federal courts
are courts of limited jurisdiction and state courts are
normally courts of general jurisdiction.

      Finally, the reasons of the court below are not a valid
excuse to refuse to hear federal claims because those
reasons are inconsistent with the principles of the Civil
                                          14

Rights Attorney's Fees Act, which provides for attorney's
fees to successful plaintiffs in all actions enforcing rights
under § 1983. There is nothing in the language or legisla-
tive history of the Act to suggest that a state may refuse
to follow its provisions in state tax cases. This Court has
determined that attorney's fees under § 1988 are an inte-
gral part of the remedies necessary to obtain compliance
with § 1983, and are available whether such an action is
brought in federal or state court. The refusal of the court
below to hear petitioners' claims under §§ 1983 and 1988
denies them a remecy granted by Congress.

                                 ARGUMENT

I. STATE COURTS ARE REQUIRED TO ENTERTAIN
    CLAIMS UNDER 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 AND 1988 IN
    THE ABSENCE OF A "VALID EXCUSE" BASED ON
    NEUTRAL RULES OF COURT ADMINISTRATION.

     The Supreme Court of Oklahoma held that state
courts are not required to apply and enforce federal law,
namely 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 1988, in cases where federal
courts are precluded by-jurisdictional statutes from
applying and enforcing that law. That novel proposition
finds no support in any federal statute, and it conflicts
with fundamental principles of federalism well-estab-
lished by this Court's opinions.

     This Court has long made clear that, in our constitu-
tional system, state courts have an obligation under the

Supremacy Clause, Article IV, § 2, to apply and enforce

federal law as the "Supreme Law of the Land." This
obligation is concurrent with and independent of the
                           15

obligation of federal courts to enforce federal law. State
courts would have that obligation even if there were no
lower federal courts at all. Indeed, before 1875, federal
courts had no general federal-question jurisdiction and
very limited original jurisdiction of any kind. As the

Court observed in Palmore v. United States, 411 U.S. 389,

401 (1973): "Until that time, the state courts provided the
only forum for vindicating many important federal
claims."

       Even before the Constitution was ratified, Alexander

Hamilton, in urging its adoption, stated that it would

impose on state courts a concurrent and independent
obligation to apply and enforce federal law. In The Feder-
alist No. 82 (E. Bourne ed. 1947), at 132, he wrote: "[T]he
inference seems to be conclusive, that the State courts
would have a concurrent jurisdiction in all cases arising
under the laws of the Union, where it was not expressly
prohibited."

Although before the Civil War the scope of the oblig-

ation of state courts to apply federal law was sometimes       \

questioned (see Testa v. Katt, 330 U.S. 386, 390 (1947)), the

Court took occasion in 1876 to endorse Hamilton's view

unequivocally. In Claflin v. Houseman, 93 U.S. 130, 136-37

(1876), the Court stated:

The laws of the United States are laws in the
several States, and just as much binding on the
citizens and courts thereof as the State laws
are ... The two together form one system of
jurisprudence, which constitutes the law of the
land for the State; and the courts of the two
jurisdictions are not foreign to each other, nor to
be treated by each other as such, but as courts of
                                               16

      the same country, having jurisdiction partly dif-
      ferent and partly concurrent.

The Court went on to state that if Congress provides a
cause of action to a party, "there is no reason why it
should not be enforced, if not provided otherwise by
some act of Congress, by a proper action in a state court."
Id. at 137.

      The Court has reaffirmed this basic principle many
times in the intervening 120 years. For example, in Mon-
dou v. New York, N.H. & H. R. Co., 223 U.S. 1 (1912), this
Court held that when Congress creates a federal cause of
action and vests enforcement jurisdiction in federal
courts, that fact does not support the inference that Con-
gress intended to make federal court jurisdiction exclu-
sive. Nor does it permit state courts of general
jurisdiction to refuse to exercise jurisdiction over federal
causes of action. In Mondou, the Connecticut courts had
refused to enforce a claim under the Federal Employers'
Liability Act; it reasoned both that the Congress had
impliedly excluded a state court remedy, and that in any
event the substantive principles of the FELA were not in
accord with state policy. This Court concluded that Con-
gress had not excluded state court jurisdiction, and that
state courts had an obligation to enforce the federal right
regardless of state policy considerations:

      The suggestion that the act of Congress is not in
      harmony with the policy of the State, and there-
      fore that the courts of the state are free to
      decline jurisdiction, is quite inadmissable,
      because it presupposes what does not exist.
      When Congress, in the exertion of the power
      confided to it by the Constitution, adopted thet
                                            17

       act, it spoke for all the people and all the States,
       and thereby established a policy for all. That
       policy is as much the policy of Connecticut as if

         the act had emanated from its own legislature,

       and should be respected accordingly in the
       courts of the State.

 223 U.S. at 57. See also McKnett v. St. Louis & S.F. Ry., 292

 U.S. 230 (1934), where a state court declined jurisdiction

over a FELA claim because it arose under federal law. In
McKnett, this Court reversed on the ground that denial of
jurisdiction based "solely on the source of the law sought
to be enforced" violated the rule that a state court may

not "discriminate against rights arising under federal
laws." 292 U.S. at 233-34.

      In Testa v. Katt, the Court again applied this principle
to require state courts to enforce federal remedies that
were contrary to state policy. In Testa, the Rhode Island
courts refused to enforce a claim for treble damages for
selling an automobile above the ceiling price established
under the Emergency Price Control Act. The state court
refused to enforce the federal remedy because it was
"penal" and "foreign in the international sense" to the
state. This Court held that state courts "are not free to
refuse enforcement" of a federal right, including the tre-
ble damage remedy; the Court stated that "the obligation
of states to enforce these federal rights is not lessened by
reason of the form in which they are cast or the remedy
which they provide." 330 U.S. at 391. More recent cases
applying the same principle include Tafflin v. Levitt, 493

U.S. 455 (1990) (requiring state enforcement of the civil

RICO statute, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961-1968) and Yellow Freight
                                          18

System, Inc. v. Donnelly, 494 U.S. 820, 823 (1990) (requiring
state court enforcement of 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq.).

      This Court has also consistently applied this princi-
ple to hold that state courts are required to apply and
enforce federal rights and remedies under 42 U.S.C.
§§ 1983 and 1988. In Patsy v. Board of Regents of Florida,

457 U.S. 496, 506-507 (1982), for example, the Court noted

that the framers of the Civil Rights Act of 1871 intended
that suits under § 1983 could be brought in either federal
or state courts. The Court has also held that state courts
may not refuse to enforce federal claims under §§ 1983
and 1988 on the basis of state law policies or procedures
that are in conflict with the policies and purposes of the
federal statutes. Felder v. Casey, 487 U.S. 131 (1988) (reject-
ing defense based on state notice-of-claim and exhaus-

tion-of-remedies requirements); Martinez v. California, 444
U.S. 277 (1980) (rejecting defense based on state official
immunity statute).

     The Court summarized the governing principles as
they apply to § 1983 claims in Howlett v. Rose, 496 U.S. 356
(1990). In Howlett, the Court held that Florida courts
could not reject a plaintiff's § 1983 claim on the basis of a
sovereign immunity defense under state law. The Court
reaffirmed the fundamental principle that

      Federal law is enforceable in state courts not

      courts would otherwise be burdened or that
      state courts might provide a more convenient
      forum -- although both might well be true -- but
      because the Constitution and the laws passed
      pursuant to it are as much laws in the States as
      laws passed by the state legislature.

Il. THE FEDERAL TAX INJUNCTION ACT AND
       "PRINCIPLES OF COMITY AND FEDERALISM"
       PROVIDE NO VALID EXCUSE TO STATE
       COURTS TO REFUSE TO ENTERTAIN CLAIMS
       AND PROVIDE RELIEF UNDER 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983
       AND 1988.

      The Oklahoma Supreme Court explained its denial of
petitioners' claims under 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 1988 by
reasoning that the claims could not have been brought in
federal court under the Tax Injunction Act, 28 U.S.C.
§ 1341, and therefore principles of "comity and federal-
ism" allow the Oklahoma courts to refuse to hear these
claims in state court. Pet. App. 6a. There is no merit to the
reliance by the court below on the Tax Injunction Act and
principles of comity and federalism. Indeed, such reliance
to excuse state courts from entertaining and enforcing
federal rights and remedies in state tax cases turns that
Act and those principles on their heads.

     The sole purpose of the Tax Injunction Act was to
prevent the federal judiciary from interfering with state
tax procedures when state law provides adequate state
procedures for addressing the claims. The Act's language
says nothing about the jurisdiction of state courts, or
whether state courts should entertain federal causes of
action.

      Nor does the legislative history of the Tax Injunction
Act contain any indication of the slightest intent by Con-
gress to limit the authority or obligation of state courts to
enforce all federal rights in cases challenging state taxes
and tax practices. What that history indicates is that the
Congress wanted to preserve the ability of states to con-
tinue collecting tax revenues during litigation over the
                                          23

validity of state taxes, and to prevent federal courts from
interfering with those collections. The Tax Injunction Act
was in response to a perceived practice of out-of-state
corporate taxpayers qualifying for diversity jurisdiction
in federal court and then obtaining preliminary injunc-
tions of the tax collection while their cases were pending.
By enjoining state tax collection, federal courts imposed a
financial burden on state and local governments and
forced taxing authorities to accept reduced tax payments
in lieu of protracted litigation. See 2569 CONG. REC. 1415

(1937).

     Additionally, the Tax Injunction Act was intended to
resolve an anomaly whereby nonresident state taxpayers
received more favorable treatment in court than resi-
dents. Nonresident taxpayers could qualify for diversity
jurisdiction in federal courts to challenge state taxes
while resident taxpayers were required to resort to state
courts. Id. at 1416-1417. Under the laws of most states,
taxpayers challenging state taxes were obligated to pay
the tax first and then sue for a refund. See S. Rep. No.

1503, 75th Cong., Ist Sess. 2 (1937). Eliminating federal
court jurisdiction where state court remedies were ade-

quate provided equal treatment for residents and nonresi-
dents challenging state tax laws.

      Lastly, Congress passed the Tax Injunction Act to
establish state courts as the primary forum for challeng-
ing state taxes because most suits arose from state law,
and this Court was available to review any state court
decisions involving federal grounds for challenging state
taxes. 2569 CONG. REC. 1417 (1937).
                               24

      None of these congressional purposes justifies a state

court's refusal to entertain a federal claim. Although this

Court has reversed the decisions of federal courts assert-

ing jurisdiction in § 1983 cases when the state court

remedies were "plain, speedy and efficient," see Fair

Assessment in Real Estate Ass'n v. McNary, 454 U.S. 100
(1981); Rosewell v. LaSalle Nat'l Bank, 450 U.S. 503 (1981),
this Court has never indicated that a state court could
choose not to hear those same § 1983 claims because its

state law remedies were adequate. Indeed, the Court in

Fair Assessment specifically noted that "the Missouri

Supreme Court has expressly held that plaintiffs such as

petitioners may assert a § 1983 claim in state court." 454
U.S. at 116. Similarly, in Pennhurst State School and Hospi-
tal v. Halderman, 465 U.S. 89, 122 (1984), the Court

observed that, because of the limitations that the Tax
Injunction Act and the Eleventh Amendment impose on

federal courts in state tax matters, "[c]hallenges to the
validity of state tax systems under 42 U.S.C.
§ 1983 ... must be brought in state court."

      More important, in this case there is no concern of
federal court interference with state tax procedures

involving requests for preliminary injunctions, declara-
tory relief or damages. Petitioners brought their action in

state court, and the Oklahoma Supreme Court declared
the state taxes to be in violation of the federal Constitu-
tion and ordered refunds and interest. The federal courts
were not involved in this proceeding to any extent, and
thus the reasons that preclude federal court consideration

of a state tax claim under § 1983 are inapposite here.

As the Court held in Howlett v. Rose, 496 U.S. at 371, any

excuse that is "inconsistent with or violates federal law is
not a valid excuse." Specifically, the reasons given by the
court below are inconsistent with the requirements of the
Civil Rights Attorney's Fees Award Act of 1976, now
codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1988, and with Congress' basic

purpose in enacting that statute.

     The basic Congressional purpose in enacting § 1988
was to ensure that all persons who are required to resort
to legal action to vindicate their federal rights, when
those rights are infringed by persons acting under color

of state law, can recover the costs of those actions, includ-

ing reasonable attorneys' tees. As one commentator has
noted: "The adoption of a fee-shifting statute for § 1983
and other civil rights actions is part of a trend moving
away from the `American Rule' under which parties are
responsible for their own fees." 2 Steven H. Steinglass,

Section 1983 Litigation in State Courts § 22.4 (1994).

       In Maine v. Thiboutot, 448 U.S. 1, 11 (1980), this Court

held that "Congress viewed the fees authorized by § 1988
as `an integral part of the remedies necessary to obtain'
compliance with § 1983. S. Rep. No. 94-1011, p. 5 (1976)."
Congress enacted § 1988 in recognition of the fact that
parties who bring suit under § 1983 and prevail are
typically seeking to redress not only their own injuries
but also to vindicate important civil and constitutional
rights of many other people and the public at large. See
City of Riverside v. Rivera, 477 U.S. 561, 574 (1986). As the
House Report accompanying § 1988 noted, a person who
obtains injunctive relief under § 1983 " `does so not for
himself alone but also as a `private attorney general,'
                                            33

 vindicating a policy that Congress considered of the high-

  est importance." H.R. Rep. No. 1558, 94th Cong., 2d Sess.

 2 (1976) (quoting Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.,
 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968)).

       Section 1988 thus reflects Congress' awareness that
 the monetary damages resulting from state deprivations
 of federal rights will often not be large enough to make it
 feasible for persons, or even groups of persons, to bear
 the expense of litigation to vindicate those rights unless
 they and their attorneys can recover those expenses if
 they prevail. Accordingly, the ability of plaintiffs and
 their attorneys to recover litigation costs and attorneys'
 fees is often essential to the effective protection of their
 federal rights.

      These facts are no less true in the case of state tax
exactions that contravene federal rights. Because the

monetary damage resulting from unconstitutional taxa-
tion is often relatively small on an individual basis, com-

pared to the costs of litigation, the availability of
attorneys' fees can make the difference between protect-
ing the federal rights of taxpayers and allowing viola-
tions to go unredressed. See William L. Taylor, Section

1983 in State Court: A Remedy for Unconstitutional State
Taxation, 95 Yale L.J. 414, 417 (1985).

      In this case, for example, it is most unlikely that
petitioner National Private Truck Council, Inc. or any of
its individual member companies would have been will-
ing to undertake the costs, including attorney's fees, of
over ten years of litigation to secure the tax refunds that
