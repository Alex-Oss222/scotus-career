QUESTION PRESENTED 


Where a “plain, speedy and efficient remedy” is 
available under state law for Commerce Clause or other 
Constitutional or federal law challenges to state tax laws, 
and, as in this case, that state law remedy was successfully 
pursued, are the state courts, in view of the principles of 
federalism and comity, required by federal law to entertain 
a coextensive claim under 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 1988?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioners (plaintiffs below) initially brought this action 
in state district court pursuant to OKLA. STAT. tit. 68, § 
226 (1981) to recover taxes paid under protest, which taxes 
plaintiffs charged were violative of the Commerce Clause.’ 
In Count III of their state court petition, plaintiffs alleged 
that the actions of the defendants?’ in collecting the taxes 
imposed by the challenged statutes were “taken under 
color of state law,” and that defendants, Respondents here,

The trial court upheld the tax statutes’ constitutionality

On appeal, the Oklahoma Supreme Court reversed the

holding them violative of the Commerce Clause, and

Subsequent to the Oklahoma Supreme Court’s decision 
on June 28, 1990, this Court ruled in Dennis v. Higgins, 
498 U.S. 439, 111 S.Ct. 865 (1991), that suits for violations 
of the Commerce Clause may be brought under § 1983. As 
a result, on plaintiffs’ petition for certiorari, this Court 
vacated the Oklahoma Supreme Court’s opinion and 
remanded for further consideration in light of Dennis.° 


On remand, the Oklahoma court recognized this Court’s 
holding in Dennis, and acknowledged that, pursuant 
thereto, Commerce Clause violations do indeed provide a 
basis for the bringing of a § 1983 action. 879 P.2d at 138, 
140, Pet.App. la, 5a. The court, however, went on to 
examine the pertinent issue of this case, which was not 
involved in Dennis: Are the state courts required to 
entertain such actions where, as here, a “plain, speedy and 
efficient remedy” is available—and successful--under state 
law? 


The Oklahoma court first found—and Petitioners here 
do not challenge such conclusion—that Oklahoma law 
provides an adequate state remedy affording the relief the 
plaintiffs requested. 879 P.2d at 138, 139, Pet.App. 1a, 4a. 
Consequently, under the provisions of the Tax Injunction 
Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1341, a federal court would be prohibited 
from entertaining Petitioner-plaintiffs’ claim. Jd., at 138, 
Pet.App. 2a-3a. Thus that court, while acknowledging

Accordingly, the court below found that plaintiff-

did not compel a different result in this case. As the court 
said, “[aJlthough the reasons for so doing have changed,”

“reached the right result in declining to grant an attor-

As will be shown, that decision is consistent with 
applicable decisions of this Court, and does not conflict as 
to any federal question with the applicable decisions of the 
highest courts of other states. Further review by certiorari 
is unwarranted, and should be denied. 


REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


This case is about attorney fees—nothing else. Contrary 
to Petitioners’ claim, the Oklahoma Supreme Court in its 
opinion below did not rule, contrary to Dennis v. Higgins, 


6 


that suits for violation of the Commerce Clause could not 
be brought under § 1983. Rather, on remand, and as 
dictated by Dennis, that court specifically recognized that 
they could. Then, however, the court below examined the 
applicable decisions of this Court and those decisions of the 
state courts of last resort which have directly addressed 
the question, and held that state courts are not required to 
entertain § 1983 actions to challenge state tax laws where 
taxpayers have (and here utilized) a plain, speedy and 
efficient remedy under state law; and where Petitioners 
can and do receive fully adequate redress of their grievan- 
ces thereunder. 


Neither, as Petitioners claim, is the decision below 
contrary to Howlett v. Rose, 496 U.S. 356 (1990). Here, 
unlike Howlett, the state court did not attempt to deny to 
Petitioners the substantive relief protected by § 1983—the 
right to challenge the State’s tax laws and to obtain 
declaratory relief and refunds. Rather, that relief was 
granted—under state law. Petitioners’ complaint is that, 
while they received the full measure of relief under state 
law to which they are constitutionally entitled, they didn’t 
get the attorney fees they wanted. However, neither 
Dennis nor Howlett guarantees them the extra windfall 
they would like. 


Finally, Petitioners suggest that the states’ courts of 
last resort are “sharply divided” on the question. Not so. 
According to Petitioners, the “question” is only whether § 
1983 protects taxpayers’ rights to challenge state laws, and 
to obtain relief from those found to be unconstitutional or 
violative of federal law. It certainly does that. What 
Petitioners omit to acknowledge, however, and what the 
majority, if not all, state court decisions have held, is that 
where those rights are protected under state law, and that 
state law provides a remedy to redress taxpayers’ grievan- 


7 


ces satisfactorily and adequately under the Constitution, 
the state courts are not required to look elsewhere for 
another, or additional, remedy. 


The Petitioners have received all the relief to which 
they are entitled. The petition for a writ of certiorari 
should be denied. 


STATE LAW HAS PROVIDED PETITIONERS AN 
INDEPENDENT, CONSTITUTIONALLY COMPLETE, 
AND ADEQUATE REMEDY IN THIS CASE, AND 
THE STATE COURT IS NOT REQUIRED TO EN- 
TERTAIN A COEXTENSIVE FEDERAL LAW CLAIM 
WHERE STATE LAW PROVIDES THE REMEDY. 


l. The decision below does not conflict with either 
Dennis v. Higgins or Howlett v. Rose, neither of 
which dealt with the issue involved here. 


The Petitioners argue that the decision below conflicts 
with Dennis v. Higgins and with Howlett v. Rose. How- 
ever, as the court below correctly observed: 


Neither Dennis, nor Howlett, addressed the issue of 
whether a state court should decline to impose 
federal remedies when state remedies are adequate 
and a federal court would, therefore, be prohibited 
from enforcing such remedies under the Tax In- 
junction Act. 


879 P.2d at 140-141, Pet.App. at 7a. (Emphasis added.) 
In the footnote to this observation, the court also noted 
that the state law remedies provided here, a refund of the 
taxes wrongly exacted, interest on those amounts, and an 
injunction prohibiting the collection of such taxes in the 
future, fully satisfied due process requirements. Ibid. 


8 


As noted earlier, the Petitioners brought an action in 
state court under the provisions of OKLA. STAT. tit. 68, § 
226 (1981) for declaratory and injunctive relief to challenge 
the constitutionality of certain state tax laws, and to 
obtain a refund of those taxes. Although unsuccessful at 
the trial level, the Petitioners prevailed on appeal to the 
Oklahoma Supreme Court. Private Truck Council of 
America, Inc. v. Oklahoma Tax Com’n, 806 P.2d 598 (Okla. 
1990). In that decision, the Oklahoma Supreme Court 
declared the offending tax statutes unconstitutional 
because they violated the fundamental requirement of 
nondiscrimination against interstate commerce. Id.; see 
also, 879 P.2d 137, 138 (Okla. 1994), Pet.App. 2a—3a, same 
case on remand. The court further allowed a refund to 
Petitioners based upon the utilized state-law procedures, in 
accordance with this Court’s decisions in American 
Trucking Ass’ns, Inc. v. Scheiner,.483 U.S. 266 (1987), and 
American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc. v. Smith, 496 U.S. 167 
(1990). Private Truck Council, 879 P.2d at 139, Pet.App. 
3a. 


However, Petitioners, plaintiffs below, also sought 
attorney fees: under state law—which was denied—and 
under 42 U.S.C. § 1988. In its earlier opinion the state 
supreme court affirmed the dismissal of Petitioners’ §§ 
1983 and 1988 claim for attorney fees, giving as its reason 
its conclusion, under then extant law, that a Commerce 
Clause violation did not support a § 1983 claim. See, 
Private Truck, 879 P.2d at 139; Pet.App. 4a. It is that 
reasoning that was rejected in Dennis, not whether Peti- 
tioners are entitled to attorney fees. 


Commensurate with this Court’s remand order, the 
Oklahoma court asked the parties to brief, and considered, 
the following question: What is the effect of Dennis v. 
Higgins on Appellants’ coextensive state law claims, 


9 


including Appellants’ right to attorney’s fees in this case? 
Pet.App. 60a. [Emphasis added]. After such consideration, 
and for the reasons given in the opinion below, the court 
correctly found that Dennis has no effect; it neither 
mandates nor authorizes attorney fees in this case. 


It is important to note what Dennis decided—and what 
it did not. The only question presented or decided in 
Dennis was whether suits for violations of the Commerce 
Clause may be brought under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Dennis, 
498 U.S. at 440. While the Court held that such suits may 
be brought, the Court did not address, and did not decide, 
whether state courts are required to allow such suits, 
especially where an adequate remedy exists under state 
law, independent of and coextensive with the remedy 
provided by § 1983. State v. Quill Corp., 500 N.W. 2d 196 
(N.D. 1993), cert. denied, 114 S.Ct. 173 (1993). 


In Quill, which the court below noted was “virtually 
identical to the case at bar,” 879 P.2d at 140, Pet.App. at 
6a, the petitioner (Quill) had been successful before this 
Court in its resistance on Commerce Clause grounds to the 
State’s effort to require the collection of use taxes on 
interstate mail order sales. Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, 
112 S.Ct. 1904 (1992). On remand, Quill sought attorney 
fees under § 1983 and § 1988 for vindication of its consti- 
tutional rights, but acknowledged, as here, that the state 
statutes provided it full relief from the unconstitutionality 
of the involved tax acts. 


The Quill court, applying the reasoning of its earlier 
opinion in Linderkamp v. Bismark School District No. 1, 
397 N.W.2d 76 (N.D. 1986), observed that the retailer had 
no cognizable federal civil rights claim under § 1983 to 
enjoin the collection of the offensive taxes because state 
law provided a plain, speedy and efficient remedy, and the 
retailer was thus not entitled to recover attorney fees 


10 


under § 1988. Quill’s petition for certiorari in that case 
was denied. 114 S.Ct. 173 (1993). 


Petitioners here also rely on Howlett v. Rose, 496 U.S. 
356 (1990). However, as correctly observed in State v. 
Quill and by the court below, the issue in Howlett was 
entirely different, and directly opposite from the case at 
bar. In Howlett, this Court struck down a Florida decision 
that purported to extend a state sovereign immunity 
protection under state common law to an entity (school 
board) in a manner contrary to and directly at odds with 
§ 1983. In this way, the state court attempted completely 
to deny the right of action wich § 1983 protected. Here, 
however, the right of action and right to relief are pre- 
served and recognized under state law—not defeated. As 
the court below correctly observed, its decision “is perfectly 
consistent with the federal interest.” 879 P.2d at 141, 
Pet.App. at 8a. 


2. The decision below does not conflict as to any 
federal question with the applicable decisions of 
state courts of last resort regarding the question 
presented, but is consistent with them. 


Petitioners here argue that the courts of last resort of 
the various states are “sharply divided” on this question, 
and suggest a need for resolution. However, this is not a 
case of “exhaustion of remedies,” nor of jurisdiction to hear 
§ 1983 claims in state court. Nor, of course, is it an 
attempt to supplant the remedy of § 1983 with state law 
so as to afford less, constitutionally, than what § 1983 was 
designed to protect. 


To the contrary, where tax challenges are concerned, a 
majority of state courts have held that where an indepen- 
dent and adequate state remedy exists to redress alleged 


11 


constitutional violations, a § 1983 action or claim may not 
be maintained. The Supreme Court has not disagreed. As 
seen in State v. Quill Corp., supra, a § 1983 action was not 
cognizable in state court and taxpayers were not entitled 
to attorney’s fees under § 1988 because a full and ade- 
quate remedy was provided by state law. In Spencer v. 
South Carolina Tax Comm’n, 316 S.E.2d 386 (S.C. 1984), 
affirmed by an equally divided court, 471 U.S. 82 (1985), it 
was held that a taxpayer may not circumvent state 
remedies through § 1983. See also, Backus v. Chilivis, 224 
S.E.2d 370 (Ga. 1976) (§ 1983 not available where state 
law provides adequate remedy); Davis v. City of Elkhorn, 
393 N.W.2d 95 (Wis. App. 1986) (same); Johnston v. Gaston 
County, 323 S.E.2d 381 (N.C. App. 1984) (same); Linder- 
kamp v. Bismark School Dist. No. 1, 397 N.W.2d 76 (N.D. 
1986) (same); Stufflebaum v. Panethiere, 691 S.W.2d 271 
(Mo. banc 1985) (same); Vann v. DeKalb County Bd. of Tax 
Assesscrs, 367 S.E.2d 43 (Ga. App. 1988) (same); Ziska v. 
Water Pollution Control Authority, 490 A.2d 509 (Conn. 
1985) (same). 


These cases have followed the reasoning of the federal 
courts that, because of principles of comity and the pro- 
visions of the Tax Injunction Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1341, 
taxpayers may not challenge the validity of state tax 
systems through a § 1983 action in federal court, but 
“must seek protection of their federal rights by state rem- 
edies, provided of course that these remedies are plain, 
adequate, and complete.” Fair Assessment in Real Estate 
Ass’n v. McNary, 454 U.S. 100, 116 (1981) (emphasis 
supplied). See, Quill, 500 N.W.2d at 202. 


Although the Tax Injunction Act, by its terms, applies 
to federal courts and not to state courts, “it nonetheless 
points the way to the proper standard by which state tax 
challenges should be measured.” Ziska, supra, 490 A.2d at 


12 


513. Following that standard, “a majority of states that 
have considered the issue have held that a claim involving 
state taxes made pursuant to 42 U.S.C. § 1983 that would 
be barred in federal court because of the Tax Injunction 
Act should not be heard in a state court if an adequate 
remedy with respect to the matter is otherwise available.” 
L.L. Bean, Inc. v. Bracey, 817 S.W.2d 292, 296 (Tenn. 1991) 
(Emphasis added).° 


Although not a tax case, Oklahoma has adopted the 
same rule on similar reasoning—a § 1983 action may not 
be maintained for the deprivation of a constitutional right 
if adequate and meaningful State grounds for redress exist. 
Wilhelm v. Gray, 766 P.2d 1357, 1359 (Okla. 1988), cert. 
denied, 490 U.S. 1092 (1989). In Wilhelm, a man who had 
been arrested on Christmas Eve and jailed for several days 
on a recalled bench warrant, brought a § 1983 action 
against the court clerk for the clerk’s allegedly negligent 
failure to notify the sheriff's office that the warrant had 
been recalled. The court observed that the Oklahoma 
Political Subdivision Tort Claims Act, 51 O.S. Supp. 1987, 
§ 151 et seq., was “perfectly suited to providing redress for 
appellant’s grievance,” and could “fully compensate the 
appellant for the loss he has suffered.” Wilhelm, 766 P.2d 
at 1360-61. Thus, concluding that “it is irrelevant that the 
appellant may not recover as much under the State

Supreme Court declined to recognize § 1983 jurisdiction. 817 
S.W.2d, at 297. A year later, in Bloomingdale’s By Mail Ltd. v. 
Huddleston, 848 S.W.2d 52 (Tenn. 1992) the court distinguished 
L.L. Bean and concluded that that taxpayer was entitled to 
utilize § 1983 and § 1988 to avoid the state law limitation on 
the amount of attorney fees to be awarded. This latter decision 
is correctly characterized by the North Dakota Supreme Court 
as “inexplicable.” Quill, 500 N.W.2d at 200 n2. 


13 


remedy as he could through § 1983,” Jd., at 1359, the court 
held that “§ 1983 is unavailable as a means of redress for 
the wrongs alleged in this case.” Id., at 1361.’ 


Oklahoma’s remedies under OKLA. STAT. tit. 68, § 226 
for challenging the constitutionality of state tax systems 
are “perfectly suited to providing redress” in this case. In 
its original opinion herein the court characterized § 226 as 
an “all-in-one legal remedy,” authorizing declaratory and 
injunctive relief, abatement of the challenged taxes, and a 
refund. 806 P.2d at 606. Likewise, the federal courts have 
reviewed Oklahoma’s remedies, including § 226, and have 
declared them fully adequate for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 
1341, thereby precluding federal court jurisdiction. Cities 
Service Gas Co. v. Oklahoma Tax Comm’n, 656 F.2d 584, 
587-88 (10th Cir.), cert. denied, 454 U.S. 1124, 102 S.Ct. 
972 (1981); Brooks v. Nance, 801 F.2d 1237, 1240 (10th Cir. 
1986). 


By the reasoning of these decisions it is clear that 
Petitioners here were not and are not entitled to maintain 
an action under § 1983 in this case, and are not entitled to 
attorney fees under § 1988. The majority of state courts 
that have considered the question do not disagree. Dennis 
does not state or imply to the contrary. As observed in 
Quill, the majority in Dennis never discusses whether the 
state court had subject matter jurisdiction or whether 
there was a full and complete remedy at state law, and 
there can be no “implied holding” read into Dennis that 
prevents the court from refusing to entertain Petitioners’ 


’ Wilhelm is distinguishable from an earlier case, Wilborn 
uv. City of Tulsa, 721 P.2d 803 (Okla. 1986), in which the remedy 
provided by § 1983 was allowed to be maintained where the 
remedies of the Tort Claims Act were unavailable because 
barred by the statute of limitations. 


14 


§ 1983 claim on these grounds. Quill, 500 N.W.2d at 202- 
203. 


Although given and affirmed for a different reason, the 
dismissal of appellants’ § 1983 claim and denial of their 
request for attorney’s fees were correct, and should not be 
disturbed. Cf., Atlantic Richfield Co. v. State, 659 P.2d 
930, 934, n.10 (Okla. 1983); Matter of Estate of Bartlett, 680 
P.2d 369, 374 (Okla. 1984). 


As in Spencer v. South Carolina Tax Comm’n, supra, it 
is clear that the sole reason Petitioners in this case alleged 
§ 1983 was to justify the allowance of an attorney fee 
which was not allowable under state law. As the court 
below said, however, “[n]o case . . . requires an attorneys’ 
fee to be granted to satisfy due process requirements.” 879 
P.2d at 141 n.3, Pet.App. at 7a n.3. This attempt to 
circumvent state remedies by invoking § 1983 may not be 
permitted. Spencer, 316 S.E. 2d at 388-89. 


CONCLUSION 


The decision below is correct, and is not in conflict with 
the applicable decisions of this Court or those of the state 
courts of last resort that have addressed the question 
actually presented. The Petition for a writ of certiorari 
should be denied.

APPENDIX 
OKLA. STAT. tit. 68, § 226 (1981): 


(a) In addition to the right of appeal to the Supreme 
Court provided for in the preceding section, a right of 
action is hereby created to afford a remedy to any taxpayer 
aggrieved by the provisions of this article or of any other 
state tax law, or who resists the collection of or the 
enforcement of the rules or regulations of the Tax Commis- 
sion relating to the collection of any state tax. 


(b) Within thirty (30) days from the date of mailing to 
the taxpayer of the order, ruling or finding of the Tax 
Commission, any such taxpayer shall pay the tax to the 
Tax Commission, and at the time of making such payment 
shall give notice to the Tax Commission of his intention to 
file suit for recovery of such tax. If the taxpayer prevails 
the Tax Commission shall, by cash voucher drawn by the 
Tax Commission upon its official depository clearing 
account or special refund reserve account with the State 
Treasurer, refund to the taxpayer the amount of tax 
determined not to be due pursuant to the final judgment 
of the court having jurisdiction, together with interest on 
such amount at the rate of three percent (3%) per annum 
from the date of payment by the taxpayer to the date of 
the court’s final order. The refunds paid shall be payable 
as provided in Section 225(d). 


(c) This section shall afford a legal remedy and right 
of action in any state or federal court having jurisdiction 
of the parties and the subject matter. It shall be construed 
to provide a legal remedy in the state or federal courts by 
action at law in cases where the taxes complained of are 
claimed to be an unlawful burden on interstate commerce, 
or the collection thereof violative of any Congressional Act 


2a 


or provision of the Federal Constitution, or in cases where 
jurisdiction is vested in any of the Courts of the United 
States. In all actions brought hereunder service of process 
upon the Chairman of the Tax Commission shall be 
sufficient service, and the Tax Commission shall be the 
sole, necessary and proper party defendant in any such 
suit, and the State Treasurer shall not be a necessary or 
proper party thereto. 


(d) Upon request of any taxpayer and upon proper 
showing that the principle of law involved in the as- 
sessment of any tax is already pending before the courts 
for judicial determination, the taxpayer, upon agreement 
to abide by the decision of the court, may pay the tax so 
assessed under protest, but need not file a suit.
