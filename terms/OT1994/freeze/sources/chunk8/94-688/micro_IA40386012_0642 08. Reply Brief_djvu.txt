I. CONTRARY TO RESPONDENTS’ ASSERTION, 
THE TAX INJUNCTION ACT, 28 U.S.C. § 1341, 
DOES NOT PERMIT STATE COURTS TO RE- 
FUSE TO APPLY 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 AND 1988 IN 
STATE TAX SUITS. 


Respondents Oklahoma Tax Commission et al. rely en- 
tirely on the Tax Injunction Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1341, in support 
of the Oklahoma Supreme Court’s decision in this case. They 
argue that the Act, as well as cases construing it, such as Fair 
Assessment in Real Estate Ass’n v. McNary, 454 U.S. 100 
(1981), Rosewell v. La Salle Nat’l Bank, 450 U.S. 503 (1981) 
and California v. Grace Brethren Church, 457 U.S. 393 (1982), 
permit state courts to refuse to apply and enforce federal reme- 
dies under 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 1988 in suits challenging state 


2 


taxes on federal constitutional grounds when “a plain, speedy 
and efficient remedy” for the constitutional violation may be had 
in the courts of the state. Resp. Br. at 8-18.!--. 


Respondents, however, conspicuously fail to note that 
§ 1341 by its terms only applies to, and limits the authority of, 
federal courts to entertain suits challenging state taxes when 
there is plain, speedy and efficient remedy in the state courts. 
Similarly, Fair Assessment, Rosewell and Grace Brethren 
Church all concerned actions brought in federal courts, and the 
only issue in those cases concerned the authority of the federal 
courts to entertain the actions. 


Respondents have pointed to nothing in the language or 
legislative history of the Tax Injunction Act or in the cases 
construing it that remotely suggests that it was intended to have 
any effect on the authority or obligations of state courts to 
enforce federal rights and remedies, including those under 
§§ 1983 and 1988. Nor have respondents discussed Howlett v. 
Rose, 496 U.S. 356 (1990) or any of the other cases cited by 
petitioners establishing the fundamental obligation of state 
courts under our constitutional system to apply and enforce 
federal laws.?

Il. THERE IS NO SUPPORT FOR AMICI NCSL’S 
CLAIM THAT § 1983 WAS NOT INTENDED TO 
APPLY TO CHALLENGES TO STATE TAXES 


Unlike respondents and the Oklahoma Supreme Court, 
amici NCSL does not rely on the Tax Injunction Act, but argues 
instead that Congress never intended § 1983 to provide a reme- 
dial scheme for state taxpayers. NCSL Br. at 6-22. The Con- 
gress that enacted § 1983, NCSL maintains, “never considered 
the impact of subjecting [state tax systems] to Section 1983 
suits.” Jd. at 11. In further support of its arguments, NCSL 
asserts that requiring state courts to apply and enforce § 1983 in 
state tax cases would “significantly disrupt state tax administra- 
tion.” Id. at 8. 


These arguments are without merit on several counts. 


1. It is an elementary principle that the starting point for 
determining the meaning and application of a statute is the 
language of the statute itself. Landreth Timber Co. v. Landreth, 
471 U.S. 681, 685 (1985). Here NCSL appears to acknowledge 
that the “literal” language of § 1983 applies to cases challenging 
state taxes (NCSL Br. at 17, 18), and it points to nothing in the 
legislative history of the statute indicating that Congress in- 
tended it not to apply to such cases. Yet it contends that the 
Court should construe § 1983 as not applying to such cases 
simply because, NCSL asserts, Congress never affirmatively 


4 


considered such an application. It asserts: “[T]he Forty-Second 
Congress never considered the question and thus did not intend 
for Section 1983 to provide a remedial scheme to challenge state 
taxation... .” Id. at 19.3 


Petitioners are aware of no decision of this Court standing 
for the extraordinary proposition that a statute should be con- 
strued not to apply to a particular matter because Congress 
assertedly did not consider that specific application. As this 
Court has noted, it is commonplace for statutes to have particular 
applications that were not specifically considered. See, e.g., 
Unexcelled Chemical Corp. v. United States, 345 U.S. 59, 64 
(1953). See generally, Norman J. Singer, Sutherland Stat. 
Constr. § 49.02 (4th ed. 1984) (collecting cases). If Congress 
wishes to limit the application of broadly worded statutes, the 
Court has properly required Congress to signify that intent in 
the language, or at least by very clear legislative history to that 
effect. See, e.g., INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432, 
n.12 (1987); United States v. James, 478 U.S. 597, 606 (1986); 
Consumer Product Safety Com. v. GTE Sylvania, Inc., 447 U.S. 
102, 108 (1980); Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill, 437 U.S. 
153 (1978). 


Gn the other hand, NCSL’s approach to statutory construc- 
tion, if adopted, would cause great mischief. However clear any 
statute might appear on its face, its actual application to particu- 
lar matters would be uncertain and would depend on the exist- 
ence of evidence that Congress specifically contemplated that 
application. 


Furthermore, NCSL’s effort to carve one species of action 
out of § 1983’s coverage is sharply at odds with this Court’s 
consistent interpretation of that statute, both in general and 
specifically in connection with state tax matters. As the Court 
noted in Golden State Transit Corp. v. City of Los Angeles, 493

U.S. 103, 105 (1989), it has “repeatedly held that the coverage 
of [§ 1983] must be broadly construed.” Similarly, in Monell v. 
Dept. of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658, 700-701 (1978), the 
Court stated that “there can be no doubt that [§ 1983] was 
intended to provided a remedy, to be broadly construed, against 
all forms of official violation of federally protected rights.” 
(emphasis supplied.) In accordance with that principle, the 
Court has consistently rejected efforts to limit the scope of 
matters to which § 1983 applies. See, e.g , Maine v. Thiboutot, 
448 U.S. 1 (1980); Lynch v. Household Finance Corp., 405 U.S. 
538 (1972). 


Moreover, contrary to NCSL’s view, this Court has spe- 
cifically held or stated on several occasions that § 1983 does, in 
fact, apply to state tax matters. In McNary, the Court specifi- 
cally noted that the Missouri Supreme Court recognized § 1983 
claims in its courts. 454 U.S. at 116. Subsequently, in 
Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman, 465 U.S. 
89 (1984), the Court observed that “[c]hallenges to the validity 
of state tax systems under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 . .. must be brought 
in state court.” Jd. at 122. Finally, in Dennis v. Higgins, 498 
U.S. 439 (1991), the Court squarely held that a claim based on 
an unconstitutional state tax may be brought under § 1983. 


2. NCSL is also wrong in contending that applying 
§ 1983 to actions challenging state taxes would have significant 
adverse consequences beyond the recovery of attorney’s fees. 
Specifically, NCSL claims that applying § 1983 in such cases 
would significantly disrupt state tax administration by overrid- 
ing state procedures and by permitting injunctive and declara- 
tory relief in cases in which such relief would not be available 
at state law. NCSL Br. at 8-18. 


This claim is not correct. First, it should be noted that the 
Oklahoma Supreme Court did not indicate that any of the 
consequences hypothesized by NCSL were of concern to it or 
were a basis of its opinion. Moreover, none of them are at issue 


6 


in this case. Petitioners complied fully with state procedures in 
this case. As the court below noted in its first opinion, petition- 
ers paid the challenged taxes before filing suit, gave notice of 
their intent to file suit challenging the taxes and served their 
complaint on the Chairman of the Tax Commission and the 
Oklahoma Attorney General, all as required by state law. Pet. 
App. 40a. 


Furthermore, contrary to NCSL, plaintiffs who file a 
§ 1983 claim in state courts are subject to state procedural 
requirements; the only exception is when such requirements 
discriminate against federal claims or impair the substantive 
remedies required by fe -ral law. In general, state courts may 
use their own procedural rules in adjudicating federally created 
claims, (Friedenthal, Kane, & Miller, Civil Procedure, § 4.8 (2d 
ed. 1993)), and this same principle applies in the context of state 
enforcement of § 1983. See Felder v. Casey, 487 U.S. 131 
(1988) (“No one disputes the general and unassailable proposi- 
tion . . . that States may establish the rules of procedure govern- 
ing litigation in their own courts.”) Where, however, the state 
procedural requirement “so interferes with and frustrates the 
substantive right Congress created, under the Supremacy 
Clause, it must yield to the federal interest.” Jd. at 151. 


Although NCSL asserts that applying § 1983 in state tax 
cases would prevent states from applying exhaustion-of-reme- 
dies requirements, notice-of-claim requirements and tax-spe- 
cific statutes of limitations, that is not necessarily the case. Nor 
is it an issue in this case. While NCSL has cited decisions of 
this Court precluding state courts from applying those require- 
ments and special statutes of limitation in other types of § 1983 
actions (NCSL Br. at 9-10), those decisions would not foreclose 
the Court from deciding that such requirements may be imposed 
in tax cases if it were persuaded that they did not impair the 
vindication of the federal remedies mandated by § 1983 and 
were warranted by the state’s special interests in tax matters. 


7 


Even if those requirements could not be imposed in tax 
cases, however, there is no basis for concluding that such a result 
would “significantly disrupt state tax administration,” as NCSL 
claims. As we noted in our opening brief, many state courts 
have enforced § 1983 claims in cases challenging state taxes on 
federal grounds (Pet. Br. at 21, n. 12 (collecting cases)), and 
none of them has indicated that doing so has significantly 
disrupted their tax administration.4 


The specter of punitive damages against state tax officials, 
which NCSL also raises (NCSL Br. at 9), is plainly a bogeyman. 
Damages of any kind under § 1983 against state officials, 
including tax officials, are only available where the official has 
been acting in bad faith. See, e.g., Swanson v. Powers, 937 F.2d 
965 (4th Cir. 1991), cert. denied, 502 U.S. 1031 (1992). Peti- 
tioners in this case did not assert any claim for damages against 
the individual respondents. In any event, a legitimate state tax 
system does not depend for its proper administration on the 
ability of its officials to act in bad faith disregard of the federal 
rights of taxpayers.. 


Nor is there any merit in the similar argument of amici 
States of New Jersey et al. that allowing persons to challenge 
State tax laws under § 1983 will encourage groundless suits 
against state officials. (New Jersey Br. at 14.) Groundless

There is no merit to this argument. The argument could be
