2. Respondents’ argument that the decision below does 
not conflict with this Court’s decision in Dennis v. Higgins, 498 
U.S. 439 (1991) is astonishing. Respondents state: 


While the Court [in Dennis] held that such suits 
[challenging state taxes under the Commerce Clause] 
may be brought [in state courts under § 1983], the 
Court did not address, and did not decide, whether 
state courts are required to allow such suits, particu- 
larly where an adequate remedy exists under state law, 
independent of and coextensive with the remedy pro- 
vided by § 1983. 


Br. in Opp. at 9 (emphasis in original). The Court’s holding in 
Dennis that persons may bring Commerce Clause challenges to 
state taxes in state courts under § 1983 would certainly appear 
to require state courts to allow such suits. If state courts could 
refuse to allow such suits, it is difficult to see what is left of the 
Court’s holding that persons may bring such suits. In any event, 
whether the Court’s decision in Dennis is as limited as respon- 
dents claim is itself an important question warranting review. 


3. Respondents’ argument that the decision below does 
not conflict with Howlett v. Rose, 496 U.S. 356 (1990) is also 
incorrect. Respondents argue that in Howlett Florida courts had 
applied state law “to completely deny the right of action which 
§ 1983 protected”, whereas the courts below have applied state 
law to uphold petitioners’ Commerce Clause claims and to grant 
them the relief they sought. Br. in Opp. at 10. In fact, as this 
Court noted (496 U.S. at 360, n. 3), the Florida court decisions

in Howlett did not decide whether Howlett had a substantive 
right to relief under state law for the actions complained of. 
Indeed, this Court noted that the Florida court in which Howlett 
had filed suit “exercises jurisdiction over [state law] tort claims 
by private citizens against state entities (including school 
boards), of the size and type of petitioner’s claim here, and it 
can enter judgment against them.” 496 U.S. at 378. What the 
Florida court of appeals had held in Howlett was that state law 
barred Howlett’s federal claim for relief under § 1983, and it 
was that holding that this Court found to be in error. The holding 
of the Oklahoma Supreme Court denying petitioner’s federal 
claim for relief under § 1983 is in error for the same reasons. 


4. More generally, respondents echo the view of the 
Oklahoma Supreme Court that it is inappropriate to award relief 
under § 1983 and attorneys’ fees under § 1988 in cases where 
the substantive relief on the underlying claims was available and 
awarded under state law. Br. in Opp. at 12-13. This Court has 
rejected that argument in many cases. As the Court stated in 
Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167, 183 (1961): “It is no answer that 
the State has a law which if enforced would give relief. The 
federal remedy is supplementary to the State remedy, and the 
latter need not be first sought and refused before the federal one 
is invoked.” See also Patsy v. Board of Regents of Florida, 457 
U.S. 496 (1982). Furthermore, the Court has held that if § 1983 
would have been an appropriate basis for relief, the plaintiff is 
entitled to attorneys’ fees under § 1988 even if relief was actually 
awarded on another ground. Maher v. Gagne, 448 U.S. 122, 
132 n. 15 (1980). 


5. There is also no merit to respondents’ characterization 
of an award of attorneys’ fees under § 1988 as an “extra 
windfall” (Br. in Opp. at 6) and their effort to denigrate the 
importance of the issue by saying that the case is only about 
attorneys’ fees (id. at 5). Section 1988 reflects Congress’s 
determination that when a state entity violates a citizen’s federal

rights under color of state law and the citizen is required to take 
legal action to vindicate those rights, the responsible state entity 
will be liable for the full extent of the costs that its actions impose 
upon the citizen, including the costs of litigation. Recovery of 
those costs is in no sense a windfall. As this Court has also 
recognized in many cases, § 1988 represents an important 
Congressional policy that is “ ’an integral part of the remedies 
necessary to obtain’ compliance with § 1983.” Maine v. Thi- 
boutot, 448 U.S. 1, 11 (1980)(citation omitted).2 


Furthermore, the decision below presents fundamental 
questions about the obligations of state courts to enforce federal 
law that go far beyond the immediate question of attorneys’ fees 
and §§ 1983 and 1988. Whether the court below correctly 
answered those questions warrants this Court’s review.
