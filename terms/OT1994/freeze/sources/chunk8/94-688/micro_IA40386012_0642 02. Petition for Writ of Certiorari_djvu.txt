STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


In the decision under review, the Supreme Court of Okla- 
homa has, for the second time, held that petitioners, who suc- 
cessfully challenged a state truck tax as violating their nghts 
under the Commerce Clause of the United States Constitution, 
are not entitled to federal remedies under 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 
1988 notwithstanding this Court’s action in vacating and re- 
manding the first decision denying such relief “for further 
consideration in light of Dennis v. Higgins, 498 U.S. 439 
(1991).” National Private Truck Council, Inc., et al. v. Okla- 
homa Tax Commission, et al., 501 U.S. 1247 (1991). 


A. Proceedings Before this Court’s Remand 


This case has had a long and tortuous history. On Decem- 
ber 17, 1984, petitioners filed a class action suit in Oklahoma 
state court challenging the constitutionality of certain “retali- 
atory taxes” that the State of Oklahoma imposed on motor 
carriers who-operated trucks in Oklahoma that were registered 
in certain other states. These taxes were not imposed on trucks 
registered in Oklahoma. (App. 27a-28a, 59a.) The purpose of 
these taxes was to retaliate against states imposing so-called 
“third structure taxes,“2 which certain states have imposed on 


taxes, which are based on the weight of trucks and the mileage operated in 
the taxing state, and axle taxes, which impose a flat charge based on the 
number of axles on each vehicle. In American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc. v. 
Scheiner, 483 US. 266 (1987), this Court invalidated one such third structure 
tax, Pennsylvania’s axle tax. The Court noted that flat taxes like Pennsylva- 
nia’s had prompted Oklahoma and six other states to enact retaliatory 'axes, 


4 


all motor carriers operating in those states, including Oklahoma 
registered carriers, and to which the Oklahoma legislature ob- 
jected. These statutes carried out this purpose by authorizing 
respondents to impose taxes on carriers registered in those states 
that operate in Oklahoma which taxes are equal in amount to the 
third structure tax imposed by the carriers’ state of registration. 
By thus penalizing carriers from third-structure tax states, Okla- 
homa’s legislature hoped to pressure the legislatures of those 
states to repeal their third-structure taxes or to exempt Okla- 
homa-based carriers from them. 


Respondents implemented these statutes in 1983 by impos- 
ing retaliatory taxes on carriers with trucks operating in Okla- 
homa that were registered in 25 states.4

Petitioners’ complaint alleged that Oklahoma’s retaliatory 
tax statutes discriminated on their face against interstate com-

Clause and the Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, 
Section 2 of the United States Constitution. It also alleged that 
the respondents were therefore liable to petitioner under 42 
U.S.C. § 1983. The complaint sought declaratory and injunctive 
relief, refunds of taxes paid and attorneys’ fees under state law 
and 42 U.S.C. § 1988. Upon filing the complaint, petitioners 
moved for a preliminary injunction or, alternatively, for an order 
requiring the tax collections to be held in escrow pending the 
outcome of the suit. 


Although the trial court acknowledged that the taxes were 
probably unconstitutional, it denied petitioners’ motion for a 
preliminary injunction against their collection, or alternatively, 
for an order requiring them to be held in escrow because it felt 
that the merits of the action could be promptly decided. (App. 
54a-55a, 58a.) Two years later, another judge of the same court 
finally ruled on petitioners’ summary judgment motion, denying 
it and upholding the constitutionality of the taxes. (App. 48a- 
53a.)

More than three years later, on June 28, 1990, that court issued

under the test set forth in Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 
430 U.S. 274, 279 (1977). (Ud. at 34a-35a.) The court stated:

B. This Court’s Remand 


Petitioners filed a petition for a writ of certiorari to review 
two aspects of the Oklahoma Supreme Court’s decision: its 
denial of refunds of taxes paid with respect to the period before 
the Scheiner decision and its denial of petitioners’ claims under 
42 US.C. §§ 1983 and 1988. Petitioners pointed out that the 
denial of their claims under §§ 1983 and 1988 conflicted directly 
with this Court’s decision in Dennis v. Higgins. In Dennis, 
which involved the challenge by NPTC and others to Nebraska's 
retaliatory truck tax on the same Commerce Clause ground 
asserted in this case, the Court held that the taxpayer's Com- 
merce Clause claims were cognizable under § 1983. The Court 
stated that § 1983 “provides a remedy, to be broadly construed, 
against all forms of official violation of federally protected 
rights.” 498 U.S. at 445. With respect specifically to the 
challenge to Nebraska’s truck taxes, the Court held: “We con- 
clude that the Supreme Court of Nebraska erred in holding that 
petitioner’s Commerce Clause claim could not be brought under 
42 U.S.C. § 1983.” Id. at 451. 


On July 31, 1991 this Court issued an order in which it 
summarily vacated the judgment of the Oklahoma Supreme 
Court and remanded the case to that court “for further reconsid- 
eration in light of Dennis v. Higgins, 498 U.S. 439 (1991).” S501 
U.S. 1247.

C. The Decision Below 


After several unsuccessful attempts to resolve outstanding 
issues with respondents, petitioners on July 20, 1992 filed a 
motion with the Oklahoma Supreme Court to remand the case 
to permit the trial court to establish a plan of distribution of tax 
refunds and accrued interest and to determine petitioners’ attor- 
neys’ fees and litigation expenses. Respondents filed no oppo- 
sition or other response to that motion. 


On February 24, 1994 the Oklahoma Supreme Court sua 
sponte issued an order directing the parties to file briefs address- 
ing various issues related to petitioners’ entitlement to attorneys’ 
fees. (App. G, infra.) After receipt of briefs from the parties, 
the court issued a decision holding that “plaintiffs are not 
entitled to relief under §§ 1983 and 1988.” (App. 6a.) 


The court below concluded that “principles of comity and 
federalism” supported its view that petitioners are not “entitled 
to assert federally created remedies under §§ 1983 and 1988 in 
this state court action.” (App. 6a.) The court reasoned that the 
policies of the Tax Injunction Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1341, which 
prohibits federal courts from enjoining state taxes where “a 
plain, speedy and efficient remedy may be had in the courts of 
such State,” authorize state courts to refuse to provide federal 
remedies established by §§ 1983 and 1988 to litigants challeng- 
ing State taxes in State courts. (App. 6a-9a.) The court also relied 
on a decision of the North Dakota Supreme Court in State v. 
Quill, 500 N.W.2d 196 (N.D. 1993), cert. denied, ___ U.S. __, 
114 S.Ct. 173 (1993) to the same effect. 


The court also asserted that its decision was not contrary 
to this Court’s decision in Dennis v. Higgins, because, it said, 
this Court in Dennis had not addressed the effect of the Tax 
Injunction Act on the availability of remedies under §§ 1983 and 
1988 in state tax cases (although it noted that the dissenting 
opinion in Dennis had cited the Tax Injunction Act). (App. 7a.) 


9 


For similar reasons the court rejected petitioners’ reliance on 
Howlett v. Rose, 496 U.S. 356 (1990). (App. 8a.) 


Accordingly, the court reinstated its earlier judgment with- 
out change. (App. 11a.) 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I. THE DECISION BELOW PRESENTS A 
RECURRING AND IMPORTANT ISSUE ON 
WHICH STATE SUPREME COURTS ARE 
SHARPLY DIVIDED 


In the decision below the Oklahoma Supreme Court held 
that state courts need not accord litigants remedies established 
by federal statutes in cases where federal courts lack jurisdiction 
to provide such remedies. That decision presents very funda- 
mental and far reaching questions regarding the obligations of 
state courts to enforce federal law under our constitutional 
system. 


In the context of this case — whether state courts must 
enforce rights and remedies under 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983 and 1988 
in cases challenging state taxes on federal grounds — the 
question is an extraordinarily important and recurring one. As 
this Court’s reports alone reflect, cases challenging state taxes 
on federal grounds are legion, and claims for relief under §§ 
1983 and 1988 may be presented in every such case. Inasmuch 
as federal courts are precluded by the Tax Injunction Act, 28 
U.S.C. § 1341, from providing injunctive or declaratory relief 
from state taxes that contravene federal constitutional or statu- 
tory requirements “where a plain, speedy and efficient remedy 
may be had in the courts of such state,” the effect of the decision

Such a result would take away the ability of many persons 
effectively to enforce their federal rights under the Commerce 
Clause or other constitutional or statutory provisions when they 
are violated by state taxes. When Congress enacted § 1988 in 
1976, it recognized that the ability of plaintiffs to recover 
litigation costs and attorneys’ fees is often essential to the 
effective protection of their constitutional rights. In Maine v. 
Thiboutot, 448 U.S. 1, 11 (1980), this Court noted that “Con- 
gress viewed the fees authorized by § 1988 as ‘an integral part 
of the remedies necessary to obtain’ compliance with § 1983.” 
(citation omitted). See also Smith v. Robinson, 468 U.S. 992, 
1006 (1984).10 


Furthermore, Congress enacted § 1988 in recognition of 
the fact that parties who bring suit under § 1983 and prevail are 
typically seeking to redress not only their own injuries but also 
to vindicate important civil and constitutional rights of many 
other people and the public at large. See City of Riverside v. 
Rivera, 477 U.S. 561, 574 (1986). As the House Report accom- 
panying § 1988 noted, a person who obtains injunctive relief 
under § 1983 “ does so not for himself alone but also as a ‘private 
attomey general,’ vindicating a policy that Congress considered

Section 1988 is no less important in vindicating the public 
and national interests in the free flow of interstate commerce, 
and the policies underlying § 1988 are fully applicable to 
Commerce Clause tax cases like this one. In many cases, 
including this one, a state tax that violates the Commerce Clause 
or other federal constitutional or statutory strictures will impose 
a cost on persons and companies that is substantially less, in the 
case of even the largest companies, than the expense of bringing 
a lawsuit to challenge it. If, as the court below held in this case, 
attorneys’ fees are not recoverable under state law, the prospect 
of recovering litigation and attorneys’ fees under § 1988 may be 
the only practical means of bringing suit to invalidate and enjoin 
the tax.!! 


Accordingly, whether the Oklahoma Supreme Court cor- 
rectly held that the remedies Congress established in 8§ 1983 
and 1988 are not available in cases challenging state taxes would 
be an important question warranting this Court’s review even if 
there were no conflict on that issue among the courts of the 
states. State courts, however, are sharply divided on that issue.

supra, the Connecticut Supreme Court in Ziska v. Water Pollu-

Even though I would reach the same result reached by the 
Court today, the flaw in the majority’s rationale must be dis- 
cussed. Following State v. Quill, 500 N.W.2d 196 (N.D. 1993), 
cert. denied 114 S.Ct. 173, the majority opinion holds that our 
state courts should decline to grant relief under § 1983 to a 
taxpayer aggrieved by a tax statute that violates the Commerce 
Clause because the federal courts are prohibited from granting 
such relief under 28 U.S.C. § 1341 . Section 1341, the Tax 
Injunction Act of 1937, provides that the federal district courts 
“shall not enjoin, suspend or restrain the assessment, levy or 


collection of any tax under State law where a plain, speedy and 


22a 


efficient remedy may be had in the courts of such State.” The 
federal Tax Injunction Act appears to defer to state remedies 
similarly to McKesson. But, neither the majority nor the Quill 
court recognize the procedural/substantive demarcation. The 
“plain, speedy and efficient” remedy in state court requirement 
in § 1341 is a procedural requirement. Rosewell v. LaSalle 
National Bank, 450 U.S. 503, 101 S.Ct. 1221, 67 L.Ed.2d 464 
(1981). The “meaningful relief’ requirement of the Due Process 
Clause as pronounced in McKesson is a substantive require- 
ment. Oklahoma’s taxpayer remedies satisfy the minimum pro- 
cedural requirements of § 1341. Cities Service Gas Co. v. 
Oklahoma Tax Commission, 656 F.2d 584 ( 1 Oth Cir. 1981 ) 
cert. denied, 454 U.S. 1124, following Rosewell. However, it 
does not necessarily follow that those same remedies are mean- 
ingful under the Due Process Clause. Hence, Quill may be no 
more than a primrose path.

MEMORANDUM OPINION 


The plaintiffs, Private Truck Council of America, Inc., a 
nonprofit New York corporation, Circle W. Transportation, a 
Delaware corporation, and Roy E. Griffis, an unincorporated 
motor Carrier registered in Arkansas, contend that Oklahoma 
Statutes 68 O.S.A. § 607.1 and 47 O.S.A. § 22.5 j (k) (now 47 
O.S.A. §1120 (K) ) are unconstitutional as to them and the 
members of the class they represent, and they ask for declaratory 
and injunctive relief, for a refund of all taxes collected under 
these statutes and for attorneys fees. 


In Count I of the petition, plaintiffs contend that these taxes 
are in violation of the Commerce Clause (Article I, Section 8, 
Clause 3 of the United States Constitution); in Count II they 
assert that these taxes violated the Privileges and Immunities 
Clause (Article IV, Section 2). 


In Count II of their petition, plaintiffs seek damages under 
42 U.S.C. §1983 for violation of their constitutional rights, and 
for attorneys’ fees under 42 U.S.C. § 1988. 


49a 


The statutes under attack impose taxes called “mirror” or 
retaliatory taxes and they are sometimes called “third structure” 
or “third tier” taxes. The effect of these statutes is to tax motor 
carriers which operate in Oklahoma but are base-registered in 
another State in the same manner and to the same extent that 
Oklahoma based carriers are taxed in that other State. 


The plaintiffs have moved for summary judgment in their 
favor on the ground that there is no issue of material fact in issue, 
and that they are entitled to judgment as a matter of law. 


I have previously held that Count III should be dismissed 
insofar as all corporate plaintiffs are concemed, for the reason 
that the privileges and immunities clause is not applicable to 
corporations. Western & Southern Life Ins. Co. v. State Bd. of 
Equalization of Calif., 451 US. 648, 101 S. Ct. 2070, 68 L. Ed 
2d 514 (1981). 


Defendants have moved to dismiss Count III on the ground 
that the commerce clause does not secure rights cognizable 
under 42 U.S.C. §1983. Though there is some authority argu- 
able to the contrary, the most persuasive authority on this point, 
in my opinion is the case of Consolidated Freightways Corp. of 
Del. v. Kassel, 730 F.2d 1139 (3rd Cir. 1984). In that case it was 
squarely held that although the appellant was successful in its 
assertion that an Iowa statute was in violation of the Commerce 
Clause, this did not establish a deprivation of any right, privi- 
lege, or immunity secured by the United States Constitution 
within the meaning of 42 U.S.C. §1983. It follows that Count 
III should also be dismissed insofar as all corporate plaintiffs 
are concerned. 


Thus, the questions left to be decided are (1) whether or 
not the taxes violate the Commerce Clause; (2) whether or not 
such taxes violate the Privileges and Immunities Clause insofar 
as the individual members of the Plaintiffs’ class are concerned, 
and (3) If so, whether or not the individual plaintiffs are entitled 
to damages and attorney fees. 


50a 


A tax analogous, in some respects, to the Oklahoma tax 
under attack here was a tax enacted by California against non- 
resident insurance companies. The constitutionality of that tax 
was considered in Western, see supra. 


That case clearly held that such taxes are not a violation of 
the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment. It is not 
authority on the question of the validity of the tax under the 
Commerce Clause for the reason that Congress has given the 
State the power to regulate the insurance business; nor is it 
authonty on the validity of the tax under the Privileges and 
Immunities Clause for the reason that the plaintiff in that case 
was acorporation, and that clause in inapplicable to corpora- 
tions, Western, supra at 655. 


The facet of that case which would seem to be helpful here 
is the view of the purpose and effect of such taxes taken by the 
court. Justice Brennan, in writing the opinion of the Court, 
quoted the California Court of Appeals in saying: 


“(T]he purpose of the retaliatory tax is to put pressure 
oa the several states to impose the same tax burden on 
all insurance companies, foreign or domestic, and 
thereby encourage the doing of interstate business.” 


and at sage 672: 


“But whether in fact the provision will accomplish its 
objectives is not the question: The Equal Protection 
Cause is satisfied if we conclude that the California 
lezislature rationally could have believed that the 
realiatory tax would promote its objective.” 


The Court concluded that it could not say that the California 
legislaure’s conclusion was irrational or even unreasonable. 


The parameters of decision under the Commerce Clause 
are set ‘orth in Complete Auto Transitt, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 
274, 97S.Ct. 1076, 51 L.Ed. 326 (1977) . It was there held that 
the practical effect, not the forrnal language of the tax statute 


S5la 


should be considered by the courts, and that the tax should be 
sustained if: 


1. It has a substantial nexus with the Taxing State; 

2. It is fairly apportioned; 

3. It does not discriminate against interstate commerce, and 
4. It is fairly related to the services provided by the State. 


In that case the appellant, a Michigan corporation, hauled 
automobiles which came by rail to Jackson, Mississippi to other 
points within the State. These automobiles had been shipped into 
Mississippi from assembly plants outside the State. The tax was 
five percent of the gross income of the business within the State. 
This tax was upheld as meeting all four of the criteria. 


In Maryland v. Louisiana, 451 U.S. 725, 101 S.Ct. 2114, 
68 L.Ed. 576 (1981), a so-called “first use” tax imposed by the 
State of Louisiana on natural gas coming from the Outer Conti- 
nental Shelf and passing through Louisiana to other States was 
held to violate the Commerce Clause since residents of Louisi- 
ana were largely immune from this tax. The Court said that this 
tax provided direct commercial advantage to local business. The 
Supreme Judicial Court of Maine has recently considered the 
question of the validity of a law in that State which imposed a 
reciprocal or retaliatory tax, and concluded that such a tax is a 
constitutionally impermissible burden on interstate commerce. 
Private Truck Council of America, Inc. et al. v. Secretary of 
State, 503 A.2d 214 (1986). In that case, the Maine Court simply 
held that the taxing statute was facially discriminatory because 
it levies a tax on certain foreign registered trucks which it does 
not levy against Maine-registered trucks. The Maine Court 
refused to accord any precedential value to a New Jersey case 
upholding such a tax. In that case the Supreme Court dismissed 
the appeal for want of a federal question. Three members of the 
Supreme Court would have noted probable jurisdiction and set 
the case for oral argument B & L Motor Freight, Inc. v. 


52a 


Heymann, 293 A.2d711, aff'd without opin., 311 A.2d 184, cert. 
denied, 317 A.2d 707 (N.J. 1974), app’! dislm’d, 419 U.S. 1042 
(1974). 


The opinion of the New Jersey Court was in a lower court, 
which very clearly and explicitly discussed the commerce clause 
at length, and concluded that the New Jersey statute did not 
violate the commerce clause. : 


The Maine Court consigned the action of the United States 
Supreme Court to a footnote, stating that such a summary 
disposition may mean only that the issues were not there refined 
with sufficient clarity, precision and certainty desirable before 
the Supreme Court undertakes review. : 


What would seem more probable since the United States 
Supreme Court has in the past upheld these reciprocal taxes was 
that the matter was no longer open to question. The New Jersey 
Court said id. at 715: 


Reciprocal (not retaliatory) features of state laws im- 
posing taxes on motor vehicles came before the court 
in Bode v. Barrett, 344 U.S. 583, 73 S.Ct. 468, 97 
L.Ed. 567 (1953) and in Storaasli v. Minnesota, 283 
U.S. 57, 51 S.Ct. 354, 75 L.Ed. 839 (1931). In Bode 
the appellants were motor truck operators engaged in 
interstate as well as intrastate commerce. They at- 
tacked the constitutionality of an Illinois law impos- 
ing a tax for the use of that state’s highways and 
measuring the tax exclusively by the gross weight of 
the vehicle. The law was held valid and among the 
arguments rejected by the court was this: That the 
Statute should be held unconstitutional because it 
required [Illinois residents to pay the tax and at the 
same time exempted non-residents if the states of their 
residence rciprocated by granting like exemptions to 
Illinois residents. Storassli was cited as controlling. 


53a 


and further, id. at 716: 


State legislation which has the effect of regulating 
interstate commerce is sometimes valid, sometimes 
invalid. If the regulation takes the form of a reciprocal 
(retaliatory) Statute, the use of that form will not make 
unconstitutional a state requirement which would oth- 
erwise be valid for a foreign corporation engaged in 
interstate commerce. This view of existing law is 
supported by In re Insurance Tax Cases., 160 Kan. 
300, 161 P.2d 726 (1945), aff'd wtihout opinion 
Prudential Ins. Co. of America v. Hobbs, 328 US. 
822, 66 S.Ct. 1360, 90 L.Ed. 1602 (1946). That deci- 
sion followed the general rule and sustained insurance 
laws having a retaliatory feature. 


It would seem that the view taken by the New Jersey Court 
is better reasoned and more in accord with the views of the 
United States Supreme Court. 


It is the actual effect on interstate commerce that we must 
consider, not the form of the statute. It seems to me that the 
purpose of the reciprocal laws is to motivate all states to abandon 
excessive taxes on out-of-state truckers, and I am not willing to 
say that such laws are either an undue burden on interstate 


commerce or that they violate the privileges and immunities 
clause. 


It is therefore the order and judgment of this court that the 
plaintiffs motion for summary judgment be overruled.
