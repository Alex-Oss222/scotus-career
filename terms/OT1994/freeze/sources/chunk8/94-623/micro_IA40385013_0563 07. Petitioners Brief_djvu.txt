STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


1. Section 3(8) of the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 46 
U.S.C. 1303(8), provides: 


Any clause, covenant, or agreement in a contract of car- 
riage relieving the carrier or the ship from liability for 
loss or damage to or in connection with the goods, aris- 
ing from negligence, fault, or failure in the duties and 
obligations provided in this section, or lessening such 
liability otherwise than as provided in this chapter, shall 
be null and void and of no effect. 


2. Section 2 of the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. 2, 
provides in pertinent part: 


A written provision in any maritime transaction * * * to 
settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out 
of such contract * * * shall be valid, irrevocable, and 
enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in 
equity for the revocation of any contract. 


STATEMENT 


A. Statutory Framework 
1. Carriers’ rights and obligations under COGSA 


The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act (“COGSA”), 46 U.S.C. 
1300 et. seg., was enacted in 1936 “to define by law the rights 
and liabilities of water carriers and shippers in foreign com- 
merce.” See Uniform Ocean Bills of Lading—Hague Rules, 
H.R. Rep. No. 2218, 74th Cong., 2d Sess. 1 (1936). Modeled 
on the International Convention for the Unification of Certain 
Rules of Law Relating to Bills of Lading (the “Hague Rules”), 
COGSA prescribes both the rights and responsibilities of 
common carriers (the companies that own and operate the 
ocean-going vessels that carry cargo for hire), and the rights 
and responsibilities of shippers and consignees (the compa- 
nies that own the cargo shipped on board the carriers’ ves- 
sels). 


The statute establishes a carefully-crafted balance of rights 
and duties. On the one hand, COGSA imposes on the carrier the 
nondelegable obligation “to exercise due diligence to * * * 
{mJake the ship seaworthy” before and at the beginning of the 
voyage (46 U.S.C. 1303(1)), and to “properly and carefully 
load, handle, stow, carry, keep, care for, and discharge the 
goods carried” (46 U.S.C. 1303(2)). On the other hand, the 
statute immunizes the carrier from liability for losses result- 
ing from numerous causes (46 U.S.C. 1304(2)(a) through (q)), 
including, for example, the “[a]ct, neglect or default of the 
master, mariner, pilot, or the servants of the carrier in the nav- 
igation or in the management of the ship” (46 U.S.C. 
1304(2)(a)), or “[f]ire, unless caused by the actual fault or 
privity of the carrier” (46 U.S.C. 1304(2)(b)). The statute also 
limits a carrier’s liability to $500 per package or customary 
freight unit unless the cargo owner declares the value of the 
cargo in advance. 46 U.S.C. 1304(5). 


2. The regulation of bills of lading under COGSA 


COGSA also regulates “bills of lading”—the documents 
that evidence the transportation contract between the carrier 
and the cargo owner. A bill of lading is typically a single- 
page form document prepared by the carrier, which the car- 
rier’s agent issues to the shipper at the port of loading. See, 
e.g., J.A. 46-56.' It acknowledges the carrier’s receipt of the 
cargo for transportation to a designated port of discharge; it 
is the contract of carriage between the carrier and the shipper 
(and subsequent holders of the bill of lading); and it is often 
a negotiable (i.e., transferable) document of title issued to 
order, which the shipper negotiates to its buyer incident to the 
sale of the goods. The buyer or its consignee returns the bill 
of lading to the carrier or its agents at the port of discharge 
and takes delivery of the goods from the carrier. The reverse 
side of the bill of lading typically contains numerous boiler- 
plate terms imposed unilaterally by the carrier. J.A. 49-56. 


By its terms, COGSA applies to “[e]very bill of lading or 
similar document of title which is evidence of a contract for the 
carriage of goods by sea to or from ports of the United States, 
in foreign trade * * *.” 46 U.S.C. 1300. Because the bills of 
lading drafted by carriers had historically contained various 
clauses designed to insulate the carriers from any liability for 
cargo damage—in conflict with the goals of COGSA—the 
statute sought to make bills of lading simple and uniform,’

loss or damage to or in connection with the goods, aris-

obligations provided in this section, or lessening such 
liability otherwise than as provided in this chapter, shall 
be null and void and of no effect. 


B. The Present Controversy 


1. Petitioner, a Spanish insurance company, is the subro- 
gated cargo underwriter of Bacchus Associates, a New York 
partnership and a wholesale distributor of fruit in New Eng- 
land.’ In February 1991, Bacchus contracted with a Moroccan 
supplier, Galaxie Negoce S.A. (“Galaxie”), to buy a shipload 
of Moroccan oranges, and chartered the M/V Sky Reefer, a 
refrigerated cargo ship, to transport the fruit from Morocco to 
New England. The Sky Reefer is owned by the respondent 
Maritima, a Panamanian corporation that is affiliated with 
certain Japanese shipping companies that chartered, operated, 
and managed the vessel.‘ 


, Petitioner issued to Bacchus a policy of marine cargo insurance 


that insured Bacchus against loss or damage to its goods. Bacchus sub- 
mitted a claim for the damaged fruit in question to petitioner under that 
insurance policy and petitioner became subrogated to Bacchus’ rights 
against respondents when it paid the major part of Bacchus’ claim (in the 
sum of $733,442.90). J.A. 13 99; 404 10. 


* Respondent Maritima, the vessel owner, initially time-chartered 


the Sky Reefer on a 3-year basis to Honma Senpaku Co., Ltd. of Tokyo 
(J.A. 57-62); Honma in turn chartered the vessel to Gyogyo Kaisha, Ltd.

manage the vessel; and Nichiro Corporation in turn chartered the vessel

At the time the oranges were placed on board the Sky 
Reefer, local agents acting for the vessel’s manager, Nichiro 
Corporation (“Nichiro”), a Japanese corporation based in 
Tokyo, issued a bill of lading to Galaxie, the shipper, stating

(2) Any dispute arising from this Bill of Lading shall be 
referred to arbitration in Tokyo by the Tokyo Mar- 
itime Arbitration Commission (TOMAC) of the 
Japan Shipping Exchange Inc., in accordance with 
the Rules of TOMAC and any amendments thereto, 
and the award by the arbitrators shall be final and 
binding on both parties.

INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Congress enacted section 3(8) of COGSA “[iJn anticipation 
of persistent efforts by carriers, who are the drafters of ocean 
bills of lading, to limit or eliminate their own duties and 
responsibilities under [(COGSA] * * *.” Encyclopaedia Bri- 
tannica, Inc. v. SS Hong Kong Producer, 422 F.2d 7, 12 (2d 
Cir. 1969), cert. denied, 397 U.S. 964 (1970). The foreign 
arbitration clause is but the latest in a long line of bill-of-lad- 
ing clauses having “the strong likelihood of not only lessen- 
ing the liabilities of the carrier but also stripping the shipper 
of all the protection afforded it by COGSA.” /d. at 13. Under 
the sweeping command of section 3(8) of COGSA—which 
forbids “any” clause that “relieves” or even “lessens” a car- 
rier’s COGSA liabilities—a foreign arbitration clause is “null 
and void and of no effect.” And the court of appeals erred in 
supposing that section 2 of the Federal Arbitration Act 


requires the enforcement of a foreign arbitration clause that 
is otherwise prohibited by COGSA. 


A. 1. The text of section 3(8) is stark and emphatic: “/aJny 
clause, covenant, or agreement in a contract of carriage 
relieving the carrier from liability * * * or lessening such 
liability * * * shall be null and void and of no effect.” Nearly 
100 years ago, this Court—construing a nearly identical pre- 
cursor to COGSA—declared foreign law clauses in ocean 
bills of lading null and void. Knott v. Botany Worsted Mills, 
179 U.S. 69 (1900). Following that lead, courts have unani- 
mously held that section 3(8) of COGSA invalidates foreign 
forum selection clauses, which require the cargo owner to sue 
the carrier in a remote foreign country, often under color of 
foreign law. A foreign arbitration clause, “a specialized kind 
of forum-selection clause,” Scherk v. Alberto-Culver Co., 417 
U.S. 506, 519 (1974), is equally invalid and unenforceable 
under section 3(8) of COGSA, especially when (as here) cou- 
pled with a prohibited foreign law clause. 


2. Foreign arbitration clauses also run afoul of the policies 
of COGSA. Congress enacted COGSA to “ensure uniformity 
in the basic rights and responsibilities arising out of bills of 
lading,” Mitsui & Co., Ltd. v. American Export Lines, Inc., 
636 F.2d 807, 815 (2d Cir. 1981), in order to “enhance the 
currency and negotiability of ocean bills of lading,” Portland 
Fish Co. v. States Steamship Co., 510 F.2d 628, 632 (9th Cir. 
1974), on the premise that “uniformity and simplification of 
bills of lading will be of immense value to shippers * * *; to 
underwriters * * *; and to bankers who extend credit upon 
the bills of lading.” H.R. Rep. No. 2218, 74th Cong., 2d Sess. 
7 (1936). A foreign arbitration clause frustrates those pur- 
poses by making the liabilities of ocean carriers substantially 
less uniform and far more uncertain. 


B. The court of appeals acknowledged that “absent the 
FAA, COGSA might operate to nullify foreign arbitration 
clauses in bills of lading.” Pet. App. 10a n.5. It concluded, 


10 


however, that COGSA and the FAA are in “conflict” and that 
“canons of statutory interpretation” were therefore required 
to decide between them. /d. at 1la. That was plainly mistaken. 
“The courts are not at liberty to pick and choose among con- 
gressional enactments, and when two statutes are capable cf 
co-existence, it is the duty of the courts, absent a clearly 
expressed congressional intention to the contrary, to regard 
each as effective.” Morton v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, 551 
(1974). In our view, the two statutes are easily reconciled. 


1. Certainly the terms of the statutes are compatible. Under 
the saving clause of section 2 of the FAA, arbitration agree- 
ments are enforceable “save upon such grounds as exist at law 
or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” A bill-of-lad- 
ing clause explicitly rendered “null and void” by section 3(8) 
of COGSA is necessarily revocable at law under section 2 of 
the FAA. In effect, the FAA does not remove foreign arbi- 
tration clauses in bills of lading “from the grasp of COGSA,” 
as the court of appeals held (Pet. App. 10a); rather, COGSA, 
by virtue of the saving clause of section 2 of the FAA, 
removes foreign arbitration clauses “from the grasp” of the 
FAA. 


2. The »olicies of the two statutes are reconcilable as well. 
Federal arbitration policy is implicated only when a party has 
voluntarily “agree[d] to arbitrate a statutory claim.” Mitsu- 
bishi Motors Corp v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc., 473 U.S. 
614, 628 (1985). But a cargo owner virtually never “agrees” 
to the terms in a bill of lading, since they are invariably 
imposed, unilaterally, by the carrier. Moreover, federal arbi- 
tration policy has always taken account of competing federal 
statutory policies, and in this case the FAA can comfortably 
accommodate the policies of COGSA. 


3. But even if the two statutes conflict, the court of appeals 
selected the wrong winner. COGSA, not the FAA, is the more 
specific statute. And COGSA therefore takes precedence even 
if the FAA were the later enacted statute—a postulate which

itself is highly doubtful given the sharply limited purpose of 
the enactment of the FAA as positive law in 1947.

A FOREIGN ARBITRATION CLAUSE IN A MARITIME 
BILL OF LADING IS INVALID UNDER SECTION 3(8) 
OF THE CARRIAGE OF GOODS BY SEA ACT 


A. Section 3(8) of COGSA Prohibits Foreign Arbitration 
Clauses in Maritime Bills of Lading 


The indisputable premise of COGSA is that bills of lading 
for the carriage of goods by sea—drafted by ocean carriers to 
suit their exclusive interests—require special treatment under 
the law. For that reason, section 3(8) of COGSA invalidates 
any “clause, covenant or agreement” in a bill of lading that 
either “relieves” or “lessens” a carrier's COGSA liabilities. In 
light of that stark command, the courts have uniformly held 
that foreign forum selection clauses—which force cargo own- 
ers to litigate their claims in a foreign court, typically under 
color of foreign law—effectively lessen the carrier’s COGSA 
liability and are therefore null and void under section 3(8). 


A foreign arbitration clause, particularly when (as here) 
tied to a foreign law clause, has precisely the same prohibited 
purpose and effect. It forces the cargo owner, without consent, 
to present its claim at great and often prohibitive expense in 
a remote foreign jurisdiction, in arbitral proceedings not of its 
choosing, with no guarantee that the foreign arbitrators would 
apply controlling COGSA law. Foreign arbitration clauses 
also thwart the primary purpose of COGSA—to make bill-of- 
lading claims readily and uniformly enforceable—while at the 
same time depriving American cargo owners and insurers of 
their statutory right to enforce their cargo claims in the 
United States. Like foreign forum selection clauses, foreign 
arbitration clauses are therefore prohibited by section 3(8).

b. COGSA, the specific statute, takes priority

Relying on the canon of interpretation that “a later enacted 
statute generally limits the scope of an earlier statute if the

that “the FAA must be given priority over COGSA in light of

But in doing so the court of appeals erred by ignoring a 
weightier canon: the “cardinal principle of construction that 
repeals by implication are not favored.” United States v. Bor- 
den Co., 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939). As this Court has repeat- 
edly made clear, “a later statute will not be held to have 
implicitly repealed an earlier one unless there is a clear 
repugnancy between the two.” United States v. Fausto, 484 
U.S. 439, 452-53 (1988). Absent such a “clear repugnancy”— 
and here, for the reasons noted above, the statutes are readily 
reconciled—a later-enacted statute effects a repeal only when 
an intent to repeal is “clear and manifest.” United States v. 
Borden Co., 308 U.S. at 198. There is no evidence—much 
less “clear and manifest” evidence—that Congress intended 
the FAA to repeal COGSA in the context of maritime bills of 
lading.**
