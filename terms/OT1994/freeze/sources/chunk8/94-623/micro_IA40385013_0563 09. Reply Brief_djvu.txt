PRELIMINARY STATEMENT 


Respondents defend the court of appeals’ decision in this 
case by embracing all of its principal errors. Like the court 
below, respondents assume that COGSA and the FAA are in 
“conflict,” and they therefore invoke maxims of statutory 
construction to select a winner. Like the court below, respon- 
dents cast their lot with the FAA because, in their view, it is 
more “specific” and was later enacted. And most fundamen- 
tally, respondents, like the court below, ignore the text and 
policies of COGSA, which cannot be squared with a foreign

arbitration clause—particularly one, as in this case, that 
requires the application of foreign law, not COGSA. 


ARGUMENT 


A. Foreign Arbitration Clauses Lessen The Carrier’s 
Liability In Violation Of Section 3(8) Of COGSA 


1. In one respect, respondents depart from the court of 
appeals’ decision. Unlike the court of appeals—which con- 
ceded that “absent the FAA, COGSA might operate to nullify 
foreign arbitration clauses in bills of lading” (Pet. App. 10a 
n.5)—respondents contend (Resp. Br. 35-39) that section 3(8) 
does not cover foreign arbitration clauses at all. In their view, 
“the ‘lessening’ language of section 3(8) refers specifically to 
the carrier’s ‘liability’ under COGSA and should be read to 
apply specifically to, and only to, those liabilities described 
in the other sections of COGSA” (Resp. Br. 37). In particular, 
respondents add, “(t]he lessening provision of section 3(8) 
Should be construed to apply to * * * [duties] such as the 
duty to load, stow and handle the cargo properly and care- 
fully, and to preclude the enforcement of a bill-of-lading pro- 


vision which, by its terms, limits those responsibilities and 
liabilities” (id. 39). 


That is true, but irrelevant. We fully agree that section 3(8) 
applies to those “responsibilities and liabilities * * * which 
are specified plainly in COGSA” (Resp. Br. 39); after all, that 
is exactly what the statute says. See 46 U.S.C. 1303(8). The 
question, however, is not what kinds of liabilities “lessening” 
applies to; the question is what “lessening” means, and how 
broadly it should be construed. On that question, respondents 
venture no view at all. Apparently, respondents would limit 
the term “lessening” only to clauses that “relieve,” “shorten,” 
or “reduce” the substantive obligations imposed on carriers in 
sections 3 and 4 of COGSA (Resp. Br. 37-38). But “lessen- 
ing” sweeps much more broadly, including any means by 
which an object is made “less in size, quantity, scope, 


amount, etc.” The Oxford English Dictionary (1971). Thus, to 
“lessen” liability encompasses any provision by which lia- 
bility is made more difficult or costly to establish—including, 
as here, by coupling enormous transaction costs with the 
application of foreign law.' 


2. That is certainly the view this Court took in Knott v. 
Botany Worsted Mills, 179 U.S. 69 (1900). As we explained 
in our opening brief (at 13-14), the Court in Knott, applying 
the nearly identical language in section | of the Harter Act, 
46 U.S.C. 190, the precursor to section 3(8), invalidated a 
bill-of-lading clause that purported to require the application 
of foreign law to bill-of-lading claims. The Court held that 
the foreign-law clause was inconsistent with the Act, which 
prohibited any bill-of-lading clause that “relieved” a carrier 
from liability.2 The lesson of Knott is clear: liability is 
“relieved” (and, thus, necessarily is “lessened”) by any bill- 
of-lading clause that displaces (or threatens to displace) the 
application of COGSA rules. 


Respondents overlook this aspect of Knott, treating the case 
as if it “simply held that provisions in a bill of lading which 
would permit the carrier to avoid liability for its own negli- 
gence contravened a specific provision in the Harter Act” 
(Resp. Br. 38). But that is not all the case held: in addition to 
invalidating the exculpatory negligence clause, the Court 
explicitly struck down the foreign-law clause. If respondents 
were correct that the words “relieving” and “lessening” in

B. The FAA Does Not Conflict With COGSA 


Admittedly, even if section 3(8) of COGSA would other- 
wise prohibit foreign arbitration clauses, Congress could 
supersede that provision by enacting another, inconsistent 
statute that clearly requires that such clauses be enforced. 
Respondents believe that section 2 of the FAA does just that. 
As they put it themselves, “[rjespondents’ basic argument in 
the present case is that the FAA controls with respect to the 
enforceability of the arbitration clause contained in the Bill 
of Lading, and therefore COGSA does not apply to that 
clause” (Resp. Br. at 36 n.27). Like the court below, respon- 
dents regard the two statutes as conflicting, and they therefore 
rely on interpretive principles to resolve that perceived con- 
flict. See id. at 15-19. 


But respondents do not dispute the cardinal rule that “[t}he 
courts are not at liberty to pick and choose among congres- 
sional enactments, and when two statutes are capable of co- 
existence, it is the duty of the courts, absent a clearly 
expressed congressional intention to the contrary, to regard 
each as effective.” Morton v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, 551 
(1974). As we explained in our opening brief (at 25-33), both 
the text and policies of the FAA easily accommodate the man- 
date of COGSA; in this setting, COGSA controls. 


% Instead of addressing these basic policy concerns, respondents 
invent an entirely new and different “policy” for COGSA—“harmoniz- 
ing trade among the nations of the world” (Resp. Br. 26). Needless to say, 
having devised this policy for COGSA, respondents conclude that 
COGSA and the FAA serve the same purposes. But Congress could not 
have enacted COGSA in general, and 46 U.S.C. 1300, 1303(8) and 1312 
in particular, with the actual intent (as amici also postulate) that COGSA 
not prohibit foreign forum-and-law clauses that would, at a stroke, ren- 
der COGSA itself entirely toothless. AVC Nederland B.V. v. Atrium Inv. 
Partnership, 740 F.2d 148, 160 (2d Cir. 1984) (“[a] foreign exception to 
COGSA would swallow the whole”).

1. As a textual matter, we explained (at 26), the “saving 
clause” in section 2 of the FAA expressly provides for the 
enforceability of arbitration clauses “save upon such grounds

One such “ground” for revocation is where an arbitration 
clause violates federal statutory law. Because a foreign arbi- 
tration clause in an ocean bill of lading violates COGSA, it is 
therefore revocable “at law or in equity” and subject to the 
saving-clause exception in section 2.

controlling statute” (ibid.). But that contention is completely 
circular: the FAA is not the “controlling statute” if (1) foreign 
arbitration clauses, like forum selection clauses, violate sec- 
tion 3(8) of COGSA, and (2) the violation of a federal statute 
(like COGSA) is a ground for revoking a contract at law or in 
equity. The court of appeals assumed the first point (Pet. App. 
10a n.5), and respondents concede the second point. If, as we 
have argued, foreign arbitration clauses are prohibited by sec- 
tion 3(8) of COGSA, then they are necessarily excepted from 
coverage under the FAA by virtue of the saving clause in sec- 
tion 2 of the FAA.'®
