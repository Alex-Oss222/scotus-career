STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


1. Section 3(8) of the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 46 
U.S.C. 1303(8), provides: 


Any clause, covenant, or agreement in a contract of 
carriage relieving the carrier or the ship from 
liability for loss or damage to or in connection with 
the goods, arising from negligence, fault, or failure 
in the duties or obligations provided in this section, 
or lessening such liability otherwise than as provided 
in this chapter, shall be null and void and of no 
effect. 


2. Section 2 of the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. 
2, provides in pertinent part; 


A written provision in any maritime transaction 
* * * to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter 
arising out of such contract * * * shall be valid, 
irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such 
grounds as exist at law or in equity for the 
revocation of any contract. 


STATEMENT 
A. Statutory Framework 


The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act (“COGSA”), 46 
U.S.C. § 1300 et. seg., was enacted in 1936 “to define by 
law the rights and liabilities of water carriers and shippers in 
foreign commerce.” See Uniform Ocean Bills of Lading — 
Hague Rules, H.R. Rep. No. 2218, 74th Cong., 2d Sess. 1 
(1936). Modeled on the International Convention for the 


3 


Verification of Certain Rules of Law Relating to Bills of 
Lading (the “Hague Rules”), COGSA prescribes both the 
rights and responsibilities of common carriers (the companies 
that own and operate the ocean-going vessels that carry cargo 
for hire), and the rights and responsibilities of shippers and 
consignees (the companies that own the cargo shipped on 
board the carriers’ vessels). 


The statute establishes a carefully-crafted balance of 
rights and duties. On the one hand, COGSA imposes on the 
carrier the nondelegable obligation “to exercise due diligence 
to * * * [mJake the vessel seaworthy” before and at the 
beginning of the voyage (46 U.S.C. 1303(1)), and to 
“properly and carefully load, handle, stow, carry, keep, care 
for, and discharge the goods carried” (46 U.S.C. 1303(2)). 
On the other hand, the statute immunizes the carrier from 
liability for losses resulting from numerous causes (46 U.S.C. 
1304(2)(a) through (q)), including, for example, the “[aJct, 
neglect or default of the master, mariner, pilot, or the 
servants of the carrier in the navigation or in the management 
of the ship” (46 U.S.C. 1304(2)(a)), or “[flire, unless caused 
by the actual fault or privity of the carrier” (46 U.S.C. 
1304(2)(b)). The statute also limits a carrier’s liability to 
$500 per package or customary freight unit unless the cargo 
owner declares the value of the cargo in advance. 46 U.S.C. 
1304(5).

Significantly, the court of appeals “recognize[d] * * * 
that absent the FAA, COGSA might operate to nullify foreign 
arbitration clauses in bills of lading” (App., infra, 10a n.5). 
The court surmised, however, that COGSA and the FAA 
were in “conflict” (id. at 11a), and it therefore applied “two 
canons of statutory interpretation” (ibid.) to resolve the 
conflict it perceived. First, the court noted, “a later enacted 
Statute generally limits the scope of an earlier statute if the 
two laws conflict”; under that principle, “the FAA must be 
given priority over COGSA in light of the FAA’s reenactment 
in 1947, eleven years after COGSA was passed.” Id. at 1la- 
12a. Second, the court stated, “where two statutes conflict, 
*™™ the specific statute ordinarily controls the general”; and 
under that principle, the FAA must again take precedence, 
since it “specifically validates arbitration clauses contained in 
maritime bills of lading,” whereas Section 3(8) of COGSA 
“makes no reference to arbitration, or for that matter, forum 
selection clauses.” Jd. at 12a. In light of these statutory 
maxims — coupled with “the strong federal policy favoring 
arbitration” (ibid.) — the court held that “the FAA alone 
governs the validity of arbitration clauses, both foreign and 
domestic, and consequently removes them from the grasp of 
COGSA” (id. at 10a).

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


The court of appeals has decided an important and 
recurring question of federal maritime law. Expressly 
rejecting the contrary decision of the Eleventh Circuit in State 
Establishmem for Agricultural Product Trading v. M/V 
Wesermunde, 838 F.2d 1576, cert. denied, 488 U.S. 916 
(1988), the court of appeals held that a foreign arbitration 
clause in a bill of lading must be enforced under the Federal 
Arbitration Act, notwithstanding Section 3(8) of COGSA. 
Applying two “canons of statutory interpretation” (App., 
infra, lla), the court held that Section 2 of the FAA 
“removes” foreign arbitration clauses “from the grasp of 
COGSA.” 


The court of appeals’ decision creates an untenable circuit 
conflict, depriving maritime businesses of the uniformity that 
is sO essential to their commercial relations. The decision 
below is also deeply flawed. It rests on a faulty construction 
of both the FAA and COGSA. It unjustifiably abandons 
settled case law under Section 3(8). And if left undisturbed, 
it will undermine the fundamental purpose of COGSA: to 
promote “(t]he uniformity and simplification of bills of 
lading” in order to “relieve[]” shippers “of the necessity of 
closely examining all bills of lading to determine the 
exceptions contained therein to ascertain their rights and 
responsibilities.” Uniform Ocean Bills of Lading — Hague 
Rules, H.R. Rep. No. 2218, 74th Cong., 2d Sess. 7 (1936). 
Further review is warranted.

B. The Decision Below Is Wrong 


The decision of the court of appeals is flawed in three 
fundamental respects. First, the court created an indefensible 
dichotomy between foreign forum selection clauses (which the 
court conceded are invalid under COGSA) and foreign 
arbitration clauses (which the court upheld), insisting on a 
distinction without a difference. Second and relatedly, by 
sustaining foreign arbitration clauses the court effectively 
gutted the basic protections afforded by COGSA. Finally, the 
court of appeals’ rationale for its decision — that COGSA and 
the FAA are irreconcilably in “conflict” (App., infra, 11a), 
and that “canons of statutory interpretation” are therefore 
required to select a winner — is entirely mistaken. 


1. As noted above (supra page 10), the court of appeals 
itself conceded (App., infra, 6a (emphasis added)) that “§ 
3(8) has been consistently used by federal courts to invalidate 
forum selection clauses in bills of lading governed by 
COGSA.” The rationale is plain: “‘It is entirely unrealistic 
to look to an obligation to sue overseas as not “lessening” the 
liability of the carrier.’” Conklin & Garrett, Lid. v. M/V 
Finnrose, 826 F.2d 1441, 1444 (Sth Cir. 1987) (quoting G. 
Gilmore & C. Black, The Law of Admiralty, 146 (1975)). By 
compelling a buyer, like petitioner, to litigate its rights in a 
foreign courthouse, typically under foreign law, and

statute is void and cannot be enforced.” Burck v. Taylor,

BACKGROUND 


Plaintiff-appellant Bacchus Associates is a wholesale fruit 
distributor in the Northeast United States. Bacchus was the 
owner of a shipment of oranges travelling from Agadir, 
Morocco to New Bedford, Massachusetts, in February 1991 
aboard the SKY REEFER,' a vessel owned by M.H. 
Maritima, S.A. Maritima had time-chartered the vessel to 
Honma Senpaku Co., Ltd., who in tum time-chartered it to 
Nichiro Corp. Bacchus entered into a voyage charter with 
Nichiro for the February 1991 voyage. 


The oranges were shipped under a bill of lading issued in 
Morocco by Nichiro. The bill of lading constitutes the 
contract of carriage between Bacchus and Maritima. En route 
to New Bedford, numerous boxes of oranges were crushed. 
Bacchus filed an action in the United States District Court for 
the District of Massachusetts, in rem against the SKY 
REEFER, and in personam against Maritima, seeking to

COGSA was passed in 1936 as the American enactment 
of the Hague Rules, and was part of an international effort to 
achieve uniformity and simplicity in bills of lading used in 
foreign trade. Union Ins. Soc’y of Canton, Lid. v. S.S. 
Elikon, 642 F.2d 721, 723 (4th Cir. 1981). COGSA was also 
intended to reduce uncertainty concerning the responsibilities 
and liabilities of carriers, responsibilities and rights of 
shippers, and liabilities of insurers. State Establishment for 
Agric. Prod. Trading v. M/V Wesermunde, 838 F.2d 1576, 
1580 (llth Cir.), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 916 (1988) 
("Wesermunde"); S.S. Elikon, 642 F.2d at 723; see generally 
Grant Gilmore & Charles L. Black, The Law of Admiralty 
§ 3-25 at 145 (2d ed. 1975).

"courts must be on guard for artifices in which the ancient 
suspicion of arbitration might reappear"). 


This court finds the analysis in Ecuadorian Reefer to be 
persuasive, particularly in light of the fact that the FAA, 
which was reenacted after the promulgation of COGSA, 
specifically refers to bills of lading in defining the types of 
agreements which might contain valid and irrevocable 
arbitration clauses. 9 U.S.C. §§ 1,2; seeIndussa_ v. 
Ranborg 377 F.2d 200, 204 n.4 (2d Cir. 1967) (Friendly, J.) 
(noting that FAA was reenacted after COGSA, and therefore 
prevails in the event of conflicting provisions). Given the 
strong endorsement by Congress and the President of 
arbitration in maritime transactions, there is no basis for 
finding that requiring the parties to honor the arbitration 
clause in the bill of lading and submit to arbitration in Tokyo 
would impermissibly lessen the liability of defendants, in 
contravention of COGSA. 


The court is also not persuaded by plaintiffs’ argument 
that the arbitration is unenforceable because it constitutes a

the district court in Ecuadorian Reefer did not adequately 
consider § 2 of the FAA which provides that a court may 
decline to enforce an arbitration clause, "upon such grounds 
as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any 
contract." 9 U.S.C. § 2. While this court does not reject the 
general proposition that an adhesional arbitration clause would 
be unenforceable under the FAA, plaintiffs have not here 
demonstrated that the arbitration clause found in the Bill of 
Lading constitutes such an adhesional term.

28 U.S.C. § 1292(b) establishes three conditions for 
certification of an interlocutory appeal. The order must 
involve a question of law (1) which is "controlling;" (2) as to 
which there is substantial ground for difference of opinion; 
and (3) whose immediate determination "may materially 
advance the ultimate termination of the litigation." 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1292(b). 


In this case, this Order should be regarded as 
“controlling” because it requires that the dispute be referred 
to another tribunal for decision. Furthermore, the principle 
point of contention between the parties, namely whether 
enforcement of the arbitration clause at issue violates 
COGSA, is a question about which courts have disagreed. 
Compare Ecuadorian Reefer, 754 F. Supp. 229; Kaystone 
Chemical v. Bow-Sun, No. 88-5859, 1989 U.S. Dist. LEXIS

On April 19, 1993, this court issued a Memorandum and 
Order ("Memorandum & Order") staying litigation of this 
matter pending arbitration, pursuant to 9 U.S.C. § 3. The 
court rejected Plaintiffs’ contention, made in opposition to 
Defendants’ motion to stay, that the arbitration clause 
contained in the Bill of Lading governing this dispute was 
unenforceable. Specifically, this court held that the 
arbitration clause at issue was not void as violative of the 
United States Carriage of Goods by Sea Act ("COGSA"), 46 
U.S.C. § 1300 et seq. Memorandum & Order at 4-6. 


In their Surreply Memorandum in Opposition to 
Defendant’s Motion to Compel Arbitration, Plaintiffs 
requested that any order staying litigation be certified for 
immediate interlocutory appeal, pursuant to 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1292(b). This request was not made by motion, and 


28a 


Defendants had not been afforded an opportunity to respond 
as of the time of this court’s ruling. Largely for that reason, 
the court denied Plaintiffs’ request for certification, but did so 
without prejudice to reconsideration upon a notion timely 
filed. Memorandum & Order at 11-14. Plaintiffs 
subsequently filed a motion for reconsideration, accompanied 
by memoranda, to which Defendants have responded. Upon 
consideration of these filings, and further reflection on the 
motion, the court will certify for immediate appeal the 
controlling question of whether COGSA precludes 
enforcement of the arbitration clause at issue in this case. 


As was previously noted on page 12 of the Memorandum 
& Order, whether the arbitration clause violates COGSA is a 
"controlling question of law" under § 1292(b). Furthermore, 
it is a question as to which there is substantial ground for 
difference of opinion. Memorandum & Order at 12. In fact, 
this court’s ruling on the issue ran contrary to the decision by 
the United States Court of appeals for the Eleventh Circuit in 
State Establishment for Agricultural Product Trading v. M/V 
Wesermunde, 838 F.2d 1576 (11th Cir. 1988). 


The court is aware of the strong public policy against 
interlocutory appeals of orders enforcing arbitration clauses. 
See 9 U.S.C. § 16(b); USM Corp. v. GKN Fasteners, Ltd., 
574 F.2d 17, 20 (1st Cir. 1978); Corion Corn. v. Chen, 964 
F.2d 55, 59 (1st Cir. 1992). The court is also mindful that 
interlocutory certification generally “should be used sparingly 
and only in exceptional circumstances." McGillicuddy v. 
Clements, 746 F.2d 76, 76 n.1 (1st Cir. 1984). 


However, the precise issue that this court certifies for 
interlocutory appeal, whether foreign arbitration clauses in 
bills of lading are void under § 1303(8) of COGSA, is a 
question of statutory interpretation which is not fact-specific, 
and is particularly well-suited to immediate appellate review. 
It involves a serious legal issue, on which courts have 
disagreed, which doubtless will arise in future cases. See 


29a 


S.A. Mineracao Da Tinidade-Samitri v. Utah International, 
Inc., 579 F. Supp. 1049, 1050 (S.D.N.Y. 1984), aff'd, 745 
F.2d 190 (2d Cir. 1984) (certifying for appeal order 
compelling arbitration, where controlling issue was not fact- 
specific, would arise repeatedly, and where decision on 
appeal would help clarify murky area of the law). A decision 
by the Court of Appeals in this case will provide more 
certainty to, and therefore less delay in, the process of 
resolving maritime shipping disputes. Because the issue on 
certification is narrowly tailored to the COGSA question, 
certification here will not invite certification motions by 
parties seeking to forestall arbitration in future cases not 
involving COGSA. For these reasons, certification here is 
not inconsistent with the strong federal policies of honoring 
arbitration clauses and promoting speedy resolution of 
commercial disputes. 


Furthermore, the court now agrees with plaintiffs that 
immediate appeal may materially advance the ultimate 
termination of this litigation. If the Court of Appeals finds 
that COGSA prohibits enforcement of the arbitration clause 
in this case, the parties will be spared the expense of 
arbitration in Japan, which, given the location of the Plaintiffs 
and several potential witnesses, could prove considerably 
costly. See Banner Industries v. Central States, Southeast 
and Southwest Areas Pension Fund, 663 F. Supp. 1290, 1291 
(N.D. Ill. 1987), aff'd, 875 F.2d 1285 (7th Cir. 1987) 
(certifying decision referring dispute to arbitration); 
Trinadade-Samitri, 579 F. Supp. at 1051. Finally, 
Defendants have not demonstrated that the costs associated 
with immediate appeal would be unduly prejudicial, as 
compared with the expense and potential waste associated 
with pursuing arbitration in Japan, which may later be 
invalidated. 

Accordingly, this court hereby amends its Memorandum 
and Order dated April 19, 1993 and certifies for immediate 
appeal to the Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, pursuant 


30a 


to 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b), the question whether 46 U.S.C. 
1303(8) nullifies an arbitration clause contained in a bill of 
lading governed by COGSA.
