STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


1. Section 1 of the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 1, 
provides, in pertinent part: 


“Maritime transactions”, as herein defined, means 
charter parties, bills of lading of water carriers, . . . or 
any other matters in foreign commerce which, if the sub- 
ject of controversy, would be embraced within admiralty 
jurisdiction; “commerce”, as herein defined, means com- 
merce among the several States or with foreign nations 


2. Section 2 of the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 2, 
provides, in pertinent part: 


A written provision in any maritime transaction or a 
contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to 
settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out 
of such contract or transaction ... shall be valid, 
irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as 
exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any 
contract. 


3. Section 3(8) of the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 46 
U.S.C. § 1303(8), provides, in pertinent part: 


Any clause, covenant, or agreement in a contract of 
carriage relieving the carrier or the ship from liability 
for loss or damage to or in connection with the goods, 
arising from negligence, fault, or failure in the duties and 
obligations provided in this section, or lessening such lia- 
bility otherwise than as provided in this chapter, shall 
be null and void and of no effect.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Statutory Framework 


Arbitration has existed in the United States as a mechanism 
for dispute resolution since colonial times. Domke on Com- 
mercial Arbitration § 2:04 (rev. ed. Wilner 1990). American 
courts, however, importing the long-standing concern of 
English courts that arbitration would deprive them of jurisdic- 
tion, refused to enforce agreements to arbitrate. See Allied- 
Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson, 115 S. Ct. 834 (1995); Dean 
Witter Reynolds Inc. v. Byrd, 470 U.S. 213, 220 n.6 (1985). 
Even after 1889, when England enacted its Arbitration Act, 
which deemed agreements to arbitrate valid and enforceable, 
courts in this country stubbornly persisted in their refusal to 
enforce agreements to arbitrate. Domke § 2:04. 


In 1925, Congress enacted the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 
U.S.C. § 1 et seq. (“FAA”), to reverse the previous “centuries 
of judicial hostility to arbitration agreements.” Scherk v. 
Alberto-Culver Co., 417 U.S. 506, 510 (1974). Congress 
sought primarily to ensure judicial enforcement of arbitration 
agreements between private parties, and secondarily to pro- 
vide a means for avoiding the expense and delay of litigation. 
Dean Witter, 470 U.S. at 219-20. The FAA, by proclaiming 
agreements to arbitrate “valid, irrevocable, and enforceable,” 
9 U.S.C. § 2, gives arbitration agreements the same validity 
and enforceability enjoyed by every other type of contract. 
Gilmer v. Interstate/Johnson Lane Corp., 500 U.S. 20, 24 
(1991); Scherk, 417 U.S. at 511. “Section 2 is a congressional 
declaration of a liberal federal policy favoring arbitration 
agreements . . . .” Moses H. Cone Memorial Hosp. v. Mer- 
cury Constr. Corp., 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983). The clarity and 
force of this “liberal federal policy” require that agreements 
to arbitrate be “rigorously enforce[d].” Dean Witter, 470 U.S. 
at 221.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. The Federal Arbitration Act Governs The Enforce- 
ment Of Arbitration Clauses In Maritime Bills Of 
Lading. 


The First Circuit was correct when it decided that the Fed- 
eral Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 1 et seg. (“FAA”), governs 
the enforceability of the arbitration clause contained in this 
maritime Bill of Lading. A straightforward reading of the 
plain language of the FAA can lead to no other conclusion. 
The statute applies specifically to “[m]aritime transactions,” 


* If petitioner and Bacchus had proceeded to pursue their rights under

6 


which are defined to include “bills of lading of water car- 
riers,” 9 U.S.C. § 1, and states plainly that “[a] written provi- 
sion in any maritime transaction ... to settle by arbitration 
a controversy thereafter arising out of such ... transaction 
... Shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable... .” 9 
U.S.C. § 2. 


The decisions of this Court construing and applying the 
FAA and repeated expressions of the will of Congress support 
enforcement of this arbitration clause. This Court rigorously 
enforces the FAA, see, e.g. Dean Witter Reynolds Inc. v. 
Byrd, 470 U.S. 213, 221 (1985), consistent with the “liberal 
federal policy favoring arbitration . . . .” Moses H. Cone 
Memorial Hosp. v. Mercury Constr. Corp., 460 U.S. 1, 24 
(1983). In the context of international trade, it is particularly 
important to insist that parties honor their contracts to arbi- 
trate. See Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler- 
Plymouth, Inc., 473 U.S. 614, 629 (1985). By enacting, in 
1970, legislation implementing the Recognition and Enforce- 
ment of Foreign Arbitral Awards Convention, 9 U.S.C. § 201 
et seq., Congress made it clear (if reasonable doubt ever 
existed) that the strong federal policy favoring arbitration 
applies with particular force where commerce among nations 
is involved. 


Although the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 46 U.S.C. § 
1300 et seg. (“COGSA”), addresses maritime bills of lading, 
COGSA does not govern the arbitration clause at issue in this 
case because, as the First Circuit ruled, the FAA removes such 
clauses from COGSA’s grasp. Thus, petitioner’s contention 
that foreign arbitration results in a lessening of the carrier's 
liability in contravention of COGSA, 46 U.S.C. § 1303(8), 
even if it were true, is irrelevant. 


Two fundamental rules of statutory construction mandate 
this result. First, a specific statute such as the FAA prevails 
over a general statute such as COGSA in the event of a con- 


7 


flict. Radzanower v. Touche Ross & Co., 426 U.S. 148, 153 
(1976). The FAA is more specific because it addresses precisely 
the type of clause in question, while COGSA addresses bills 
of lading generally. Second, later enactments of Congress are 
presumed to prevail over earlier ones. See, e.g., Watt v. 
Alaska, 451 U.S. 259, 266 (1981). COGSA was enacted in 
1936, while the FAA, originally enacted in 1925, was 
reenacted as positive law in 1947, thus making it the con- 
trolling statute. Indussa Corp. v. S.S. Ranborg, 377 F.2d 200, 
204 n.4 (2d Cir. 1967). 


2. The Arbitration Clause Is Enforceable Under The 
FAA. 


As the holder of the Bill of Lading, petitioner is bound by 
its terms. Petitioner may not avoid the arbitration clause on 
the ground of adhesion. Arbitration agreements are to be en- 
forced according to the basic principles of ordinary contract 
law, Fisser v. International Bank, 282 F.2d 231, 233 (2d Cir. 
1960), and provisions in an adhesionary contract are valid and 
enforceable in the absence of fraud, undue oppression or un- 
conscionability. See, e.g., Finkle and Ross v. A.G. Becker 
Paribas, Inc., 622 F.Supp. 1505, 1512 (S.D.N.Y. 1985). Such 
grounds have not been alleged, and do not exist, in the present 
case. 


The savings clause of the FAA does not allow petitioner to 
evade arbitration. That clause provides that agreements to 
arbitrate are enforceable “save upon such grounds as exist at 
law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” 9 U.S.C. 
§ 2. A party may avoid an arbitration provision under the 
savings clause when that provision has been brought about 
by fraud or overwhelming economic power, or where Con- 
gress has clearly expressed its intent to preclude waiver of a 
judicial forum for the vindication of other federal statutory 
rights. Mitsubishi, 473 U.S. at 627. Petitioner has not alleged 
fraud or overwhelming economic power, and there is no indi- 


8 


cation in either the text or the legislative history of COGSA 
that Congress intended COGSA claims to be nonarbitrable. 
Indeed, petitioner concedes that domestic arbitration of 
COGSA claims is permissible. Because the FAA provides no 
basis for distinguishing between arbitration clauses on the 
basis of the location of the forum of the proceedings, claims 
which are arbitrable domestically are also arbitrable abroad. 


3. Foreign Arbitration Clauses Are Distinguishable 
From Foreign Jurisdiction Clauses. 


Petitioner incorrectly equates foreign arbitration clauses 
with foreign jurisdiction clauses and argues that foreign 
arbitration clauses should be held to be unenforceable for the 
same reasons that courts have refused to enforce foreign juris- 
diction clauses. Petitioner's argument is flawed in two 
respects. First, Congress, by enacting the FAA, has declared 
that arbitration clauses in maritime bills of lading are “valid, 
irrevocable, and enforceable.” 9 U.S.C. §§ 1, 2. Congress has 
not, however, enacted a corresponding federal statute 
requiring enforcement of foreign jurisdiction clauses. 


Second, petitioner's argument ignores the fundamental 
differences between arbitration and litigation. The courts that 
have held foreign jurisdiction clauses unenforceable under 
COGSA have done so, in part, on the grounds that such 
clauses constitute an actual or a potential lessening of the car- 
rier’s liability by requiring the presentation of a cargo claim 
in a foreign forum with no assurance that the judge there will 
apply COGSA “in the same way as would an American 
tribunal subject to the uniform control of the Supreme 
Court . . . .” Indussa Corp. v. S.S. Ranborg, 377 F.2d 200, 
204 (2d Cir. 1967). Since the parties to an arbitration 
proceeding — whether it is conducted in the United States 
or in a foreign country — also lack assurance that the 
arbitrators will apply the law as would an American court, 
application of the reasoning in the foreign jurisdiction clause 


9 


cases to arbitration clauses in maritime bills of lading would 
lead to the conclusion that all such clauses are invalid. Such 
a result flies in the face of the explicit language of the FAA, 
which provides that arbitration clauses in maritime bills of 
lading are valid, irrevocable and enforceable. Indeed, peti- 
tioner concedes that arbitration, as such, does not violate 
COGSA. The case at bar involves an arbitration clause, and 
petitioner’s reliance on cases involving foreign jurisdiction 
clauses is misplaced. 


4. The Reference To Japanese Law In The Bill Of 
Lading Does Not Render The Arbitration Clause 
Unenforceable. 


The Bill of Lading provides for the application of Japanese 
law. J.A. 49 at §3(1). It also, however, provides for the appli- 
cation of COGSA in certain circumstances. J.A. 56 at $19. 
The enforceability of the arbitration provision should not turn 
on which of these laws will be applied by the arbitrators. The 
Japanese Hague Rules and COGSA are substantively the same 
in all relevant respects. The two statutes are national codifica- 
tions of the international Hague Rules, and both reflect the 
same legal principles regarding the rights and liabilities of 
shippers and carriers set forth in that international agreement. 
Thus, reliance by the arbitrators on one of these statutes 
rather than the other will not result in the application of a 
different legal standard. 


5. The Wesermunde Decision Is Wrong. 


In State Establishment for Agric. Prod. Trading v. M/V 
Wesermunde, 838 F.2d 1576 (llth Cir.), cert. denied, 488 
U.S. 916 (1988), on which petitioner relies, the court ruled 
that a provision in a maritime bill of lading to which COGSA 
applies by its own force requiring “arbitration in a foreign 
country that has no connection with either the performance 

. or the making of the bill of lading contract ... would 


10 


conflict with COGSA’s general purpose of not allowing car- 
riers to lessen their risk” of liability and would, as a result, 
be “void and utterly without effect.” 838 F.2d at 1581 
(emphasis in original). The decision is incorrect. The opinion 
in Wesermunde contains no meaningful analysis of the FAA, 
the application of the FAA to foreign arbitration clauses, or 
the method by which conflicts between the FAA and COGSA 
should be resolved. The court proceeded on the incorrect 
assumption that COGSA is controlling. The decision is wrong 
because the FAA controls, and it, not COGSA, establishes the 
legal standards governing the enforceability of arbitration 
clauses in maritime bills of lading. The Wesermunde court's 
requirement that there be a “connection” between the foreign 
arbitration forum and the making or performance of the bill 
of lading contract is not supported by or consistent with the 
plain language of the FAA. 


6. The Arbitration Clause In Issue Does Not “Lessen” 
The Carrier's Liability. 


Petitioner asserts that the arbitration clause in the Bill of 
Lading violates section 3(8) of COGSA because enforcement 
of the clause would result in a “lessening” of the carrier’s lia- 
bility. Petitioner relies on Indussa Corp. v. S.S. Ranborg, 377 
F.2d 200 (2d Cir. 1967), to establish the meaning of the word 
“lessening” as it is used in COGSA. The court in Indussa, 
however, construed the term too broadly. The “lessening” lan- 
guage of section 3(8) refers specifically to the carrier’s “lia- 
bility” under COGSA, and it should be read to apply to, and 
only to, those liabilities and responsibilities described in other 
sections of COGSA. Such a reading gives full effect to the les- 
sening provision and prohibits, for example, provisions in bills 
of lading which purport to relieve a carrier of its statutory 
duty to load, stow and care for the cargo properly and care- 
fully, or to relieve a carrier of its duty to exercise due diligence 
to make the vessel seaworthy. Construed in this manner, the 
lessening provision of section 3(8) of COGSA does not prohibit

B. FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF STATUTORY 
CONSTRUCTION ESTABLISH THAT THE FAA, 
NOT THE CARRIACE OF GOODS BY SEA ACT, 
CONTROLS. 


Petitioner claims that the arbitration clause in the Bill of 
Lading is void because it violates section 3(8) of the Carriage 
of Goods by Sea Act, 46 U.S.C. § 1300 et seg. (*“COGSA”), 
in that it allegedly results in a “lessening” of the carrier's lia- 
bility under COGSA. Although COGSA applies by its own 
force to the Bill of Lading, the FAA alone is controlling with 
respect to the arbitration clause which the Bill of Lading con- 
tains. Two significant rules of statutory construction lead 
directly to this conclusion. 


1. The FAA Is More Specific Than COGSA With 
Respect To Arbitration Clauses In Maritime Bills of 
Lading, And Therefore It Controls. 


A primary rule of statutory construction is that a more 
specific statute prevails over a general one. Radzanower v. 


16 


Touche Ross & Co., 426 U.S. 148, 153 (1976); Sutherland 
Stat. Constr. § 51.05 (5th ed. 1992). In the event of a statutory 
conflict, “[w]here there is no clear intention otherwise, a 
specific statute will not be controlled or nullified by a general 
one, regardless of the priority of enactment.” Morton v. Man- 


cari, 417 U.S. 535, 550-51 (1974). 


In this case, the FAA is the more specific statute. It 
addresses precisely the type of clause in issue: an arbitration 
clause in a maritime bill of lading. To be sure, COGSA applies 
to a more narrow group of contracts, i.e., “[e]very bill of 
lading or similar document of title which is evidence of a con- 
tract for the carriage of goods by sea to or from ports of the 
United States, in foreign trade . . . .” 46 U.S.C. § 1300. But 
COGSA, as reflected in 46 U.S.C. § 1303(8), purports to cover 
“{a]ny clause, covenant, or agreement” contained in those 
contracts. (Emphasis added.) The FAA, while it applies to a 
broader range of contracts than COGSA, addresses only one 
type of provision in those contracts, i.e., arbitration provi- 
sions, and it makes that type of provision “valid, irrevocable, 
and enforceable.” 9 U.S.C. § 2. The FAA focuses directly on 
arbitration clauses in maritime bills of lading, while COGSA 
covers a multitude of aspects of bills of lading. 


2. The FAA, As The Later-Enacted Statute, Controls. 


It is also a fundamental principle of statutory construction 
that later enactments of Congress are presumed to prevail over 
earlier ones. See, e.g., Watt v. Alaska, 451 U.S. 259, 266 
(1981). The FAA was originally enacted in 1925. COGSA was 
enacted in 1936. The FAA was reenacted as positive law in 
1947. On the strength of this chronology, the First Circuit in 
the present case, and the Second Circuit, have concluded that 
the FAA, as the later-enacted statute, governs. Indussa Corp. 
v. S.S. Ranborg, 377 F.2d 200, 204 n.4 (2d Cir. 1967). 


Petitioner relies on Indussa, supra, in support of its assertion

question of arbitration clauses in bills of lading which require 
this to be held abroad.” Jd. at 204 n.4 (emphasis added). 
Judge Friendly went on to note that “if there be any incon-

3. The Savings Clause Contained In 9 U.S.C. § 2 
Offers Petitioner No Grounds For Circumventing 
The Arbitration Clause. 


The savings clause of section 2 of the FAA allows a party 
to escape its agreement to arbitrate “upon such grounds as 
exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” 
9 U.S.C. § 2. This clause permits avoidance of an arbitration 
agreement where that agreement is brought about by fraud 
or overwhelming economic power, or where Congress has 
clearly expressed its intent to preclude waiver of a judicial 
forum for the vindication of other federal statutory rights. 
Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc., 
473 U.S. 614, 627 (1985); see also Finkle and Ross v. A.G. 
Becker Paribas, Inc., 622 F.Supp. 1505, 1512 (S.D.N.Y. 1985) 
(showing of unfairness, oppression or unconscionability neces- 
sary to make arbitration agreement unenforceable). 


A party must be held to its agreement to arbitrate “unless 
Congress itself has evinced an intention to preclude a waiver 
of judicial remedies for the statutory rights at issue.” Mit- 
subishi, 473 U.S. at 628. If Congress had such an intention, 
it must be deduced from the text and legislative history of the 
relevant statute. Jd. The burden is on the party opposing 
arbitration to demonstrate that Congress intended to preclude 
arbitration, or that arbitration conflicts with the underlying 
purpose of the other statute. Rodriguez de Quijas v. 
Shearson/Am. Express Inc., 490 U.S. 477, 483 (1989); 
Shearson/Am. Express Inc. v. McMahon, 482 U.S. 220, 226-27 
(1987).

The bill of lading for the shipment incorporated by reference 
both the terms of the charter party and COGSA. Id. at 1580. 


The defendants in Wesermunde moved for a stay of the 
action pending arbitration, and the district court allowed the 
motion. Id. at 1578-79. The court of appeals reversed. Id. at 
1583. In the section of the opinion most relevant to the issue 
presently under review, the court of appeals concluded that 
a provision in a maritime bill of lading to which COGSA 
applies by its own force requiring “arbitration in a foreign

conflict with COGSA’s general purpose of not allowing car-

was correct in rejecting it. 


The opinion in Wesermunde contains no meaningful anal- 
ysis of the FAA, the application of the FAA to foreign arbitra- 
tion clauses, the conflict between the FAA and COGSA, or 
the rules of statutory construction which should be applied 
to resolve the conflict. Instead, the court proceeds on the 
incorrect assumption that COGSA is controlling with respect 
to foreign arbitration clauses in maritime bills of lading. Id. 
at 1580-1581. As discussed supra at Sections A and B, this fun- 
damental premise is incorrect. The FAA applies to foreign 
arbitration clauses in maritime bills of lading, and it removes 
such clauses from the grasp of COGSA. 


Reliance on the wrong statute led the court in Wesermunde 
to apply the wrong legal standard. It considered the crucial

issue to be whether the arbitration clause in issue constituted 
a lessening of the carrier's liability under COGSA.” It asked 
the wrong question and, by doing so, reached the wrong 
result. |

standards to be applied in determining the enforceability of 
arbitration clauses in maritime bills of lading. The Weser-

enforceability of an arbitration clause, 838 F.2d at 1581, finds

the FAA. The FAA provides that arbitration agreements iu

hibits, for example, provisions in bills of lading which purport 
to relieve a carrier of its statutory duty to exercise due dili- 
gence to make the vessel seaworthy, 46 U.S.C. § 1303(1), ‘or 
to load, stow and discharge the cargo properly and carefully. 
46 U.S.C. § 1303(2). Such a construction of the lessening pro- 
vision also prohibits the enforcement of bill of lading pro- 


38 


visions purporting to shorten the period of time within which 
suit must be brought to less than one year, 46 U.S.C. 
§ 1303/6), or to reduce the carrier's liability to less than $500 
per package, 46 U.S.C. § 1304(5). This is the common-sense 
meaning of the lessening prohibition contained in section 3(8). 


In Indussa, the court construed the word “lessening” to 
apply to issues beyond the liabilities of the carrier and the 
vessel as described in the statute. In doing so, the court relied 
on this Court's decision in Knott v. Botany Worsted Mills, 179 
U.S. 69 (1900). Indussa, 377 F.2d at 203. Knott, however, 
does not support the court's expansive reading of the lessening 
provision of COGSA. In Knott, this Court held that the 
Harter Act nullified a bill of lading provision which purported 
to exempt the carrier from liability for its own negligence in 
respect to the stowage of cargo. The bill of lading in issue in 
Knott contained a provision providing that the contract was 
governed by the law of the vessel's flag state, England. The 
Court indicated that the exculpatory clause in the bill of 
lading would be enforceable under the laws of England. 
Knott, 179 U.S. at 71-72. In these circumstances, the Court 
held that the bill of lading clause and the provision requiring 
the application of the law of the flag state were both nullified 
by the Harter Act, which specifically prohibited clauses which 
relieved the carrier of liability for damage arising from its own 
negligence in the loading, stowage and care of the cargo. Id. 
at 77. 


Insofar as the court in Indussa relied on Knott as support 
for a bright-line rule precluding the enforcement of all foreign 
jurisdiction clauses in maritime bills of lading, that reliance 
was misplaced. The Court in Knott simply held that provi- 
sions in a bill of lading which would permit the carrier to 
avoid liability for its own negligence contravened a specific 
provision in the Harter Act. The decision in Knott is entirely 
consistent with the more narrow construction of the lessening 
provision proposed by respondent in the present action. That 


39 


construction of section 3(8) of COGSA would require that an 
exculpatory provision such as the one in issue in Knott be held 
to be unenforceable because it would result in a lessening of 
the carrier’s liability by relieving it of the duty expressly stated 
in COGSA to load, stow, and care for the cargo “properly 
and carefully.” 46 U.S.C. § 1303(2).%° 


The lessening provision of section 3(8) should be construed 
to apply to those responsibilities and liabilities of the carrier 
and the vessel which are specified plainly in COGSA, such 
as the duty to load, stow and handle the cargo properly and 
carefully, and to preclude the enforcement of a bill of lading 
provision which, by its terms, limits those responsibilities and 
liabilities. Construed in this manner, the lessening provision 
of COGSA does not prohibit the enforcement of the arbitra- 
tion clause in the Bill of Lading. 


** In Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. v. Shute, 499 U.S. 585 (1991), this Court 
upheld the validity of a forum-selection clause in a cruise line's passenger 
ticket requiring that suits against the carrier be brought in Florida. Shute, 
499 U.S. at 595. The Court rejected the argument that the forum-selection 
clause was unenforceable because it violated 46 U.S.C. App. § 183c, which 
prohibits the owners of passenger vessels from inserting into the contract 
of carriage any provision which would “lessen . . . the right of any claimant 
to a trial by court of competent jurisdiction on the question of uability for 

. . [personal] injury... .” Id. at 595-596. Respondents filed suit in 
Washington. Noting that a court in Florida would be a “court of competent 
jurisdiction” within the meaning of the statute, the Court concluded that 
the forum-selection clause did “not take away respondents’ right to ‘a trial 
by [a] court of competent jurisdiction’ and thereby contravene the explicit 
proscription of § 183c.” Id. at 596. The Court also rejected the argument 
that enforcement of the forum-selection clause would cause the passengers 
“unreasonable hardship in asserting their rights and therefore violat{e] Con- 
gress’ intended goal in enacting § 183c." Id. Although Shute is distinguish- 
able from the present case in that it did not involve COGSA and the clause 
in issue provided for a domestic judicial forum, the Court's straightforward 
approach to the construction and application of the lessening provision of 
the statute under review is consistent with that proposed by respondent in 
the present case.
