QUESTION PRESENTED 


Under Section 9(a)(1) of the Endangered Species Act 
of 1973, 16 U.S.C. § 1538(a)(1), it is unlawful for any 
person to “take” an endangered fish or wildlife species. 
The Act defines “take” as meaning “to harass, harm, 
pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect, 
or to attempt to engage in any such conduct.” 16 U.S.C. 
§ 1532(19). The question presented is: 


Whether the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s regulation 
defining “harm” was correctly held invalid by the court 
below to the extent that it prohibits ordinary land uses 
which are not directed against protected wildlife but 
which may inadvertently injure such wildlife?

2. After the “harm” regulation was used to impose 
restrictions on Respondents’ timber harvesting, Respond- 
ents filed this suit to allege that the “harm” regulation’s 
application to ordinary land uses violated the ESA. See 
Complaint at 94 4-14, 17-27. This ultra vires challenge 
—the first such challenge ever brought—was rejected in 
the district court (Pet. App. 73a) and initially in the 
court of appeals in a conclusory 2-1 per curiam opinion 
(Pet. App. 46a). 


Judge Sentelle dissented from the initial per curiam 
opinion based on a straightforward reading of the Act's 
“take” definition; he would have found the “harm” regu- 
lation adopted by the FWS exceeded the agency’s powers 
under the Act. Pet. App. 68a. Judge Williams, in a 
separate opinion, indicated his agreement that the “harm” 
regulation could not be squared with the language of the 
1973 ESA, but he believed that the 1982 ESA amend- 
ments could be read to impliedly ratify the rule. Pet. 
App. 67a. He therefore joined with Judge Mikva (Pet. 
App. 59a) to uphold the regulation. 


3. Sweet Home’s petition for rehearing was directed in 
part at the legislative ratification theory which Judge 
Williams initially had found persuasive. Judge Williams, 
on rehearing, agreed with Sweet Home’s position and 
authored the March 11, 1994 majority opinion. Pet. 
App. la-24a (17 F.3d 1463). Relying on the Act’s defi- 
nition of “take,” its overall structure, and its legislative 
history, the court concluded that the “harm” regulation 
is invalid in “embrac[ing] habitat modifications.” Pet. 
App. 24a. 


Since “harm” can have narrow and broad meanings, 
the court looked to the way the term was used in the Act. 
This common sense approach is encapsulated in the 
noscitur a sociis principle—that indefinite words in a list 
(such as “harm” in the list of words comprising the ESA

definition of “take”) derive meaning from neighboring 
words. Pet. App. 3a-6a. The court noted that each of 
the other terms in the Act’s definition of “take” involve 
“a substantially direct application of force, which the 
Service concept of forbidden habitat modification alto- 
gether lacks.” Pet. App. 5a-6a.* Thus, the court found 
that, when “harm” is read in its ESA context, FWS’s 
interpretation of that term is impermissibly broad. 


The court then considered the relationship among the 
ESA’s different sections. It noted, in particular, that the 
Act “pursues its conservation measures through three 
basic mechanisms:” (1) a federal land acquisition pro- 
gram under § 5 (16 U.S.C. § 1534); (2) the imposition, 
under §7 (id. § 1536), of strict obligations on federal 
agencies to avoid adverse impacts on endangered species; 
and (3) § 9’s prohibition (id. § 1538) on the taking of 
endangered species by anybody. Pet. App. 7a. It con- 
cluded that the structure of the ESA is rational and non- 
conflicting only if § 7 “imposes very broad burdens on 
the [federal government], including the avoidance of ad- 
verse habitat modifications,” while § 9’s “take” and other 
prohibitions place on “every person . . . [only] relatively 
narrow” burdens. Pet. App. 7a-8a.

which imposes duties on a “Federal agency” to not “jeop-

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


The Petition to review this statutory interpretation case 
should be denied. In the course of three separate visits 
to the court below and review by all of its judges, this 
case has been thoroughly considered and thoughtfully 
decided. Although Petitioners argue that the decision is 
wrong (Pet. at 12-21), an allegedly incorrect result does 
not warrant review unless the decision below “has so far 
departed from the accepted and usual course of judicial 
proceedings” that it calls out for this Court’s supervisory 


8 


review. Rule 10.1(a). This case does not meet the 
exacting standard.” 


1. Petitioners’ lead argument—that this is a “facial” 
challenge to the “harm” regulation, and that Sweet Home 
failed to meet its alleged burden of showing the absence 
of any circumstances under which the regulation would 
be valid (Pet. at 12-13)— is a red herring. 


Notably, the importance FWS assigns to the “facial” 
nature of the challenge escaped the attention of any of 
the judges below, whether in the majority or the dissent. 
FWS did not present to the district court, the original 
panel, or the rehearing or en banc panels, the authority 
it now emphasizes as the appropriate standard for review- 
ing a “facial challenge.” It is therefore difficult to see 
how this undiscussed issue could be a proper focal point 
for a grant of certiorari. 


Moreover, the judges below did not have to wrestle 
with the conceptual difficulties raised by the “facial chal- 
lenge” argument because the argument leads nowhere. 
FWS adopted a “harm” definition that prohibits, indeed 
criminalizes, actions which unintentionally injure listed 
wildlife. The court below held that such a definition 
sweeps too broadly because it leaves out an essential ele- 
ment of the type of conduct meant to be prohibited by 
ESA § 9—an action which is directed at listed wildlife. 
Thus, the court’s decision can only be read as invalidating 
the “harm” regulation to the extent that it encompasses 
ordinary land use actions which are taken for a legitimate

species.” Pet. at 25. We do not doubt that it is an ad- 
ministratively convenient tool, but it is not the tool that 
Congress, balancing all the relevant and competing factors, 
ultimately chose. As this Court observed only recently, 
“[p]olicy considerations cannot override our interpretation 
of the text and structure of the Act”—the issue for the 
courts “is not whether imposing private civil liability ... 
is good policy but whether [it] is covered by the statute.” 
Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate. Bank of Den- 
ver, U.S. -—, 114 S.Ct. 1439, 1448, 1453-54 
(1994). 


In enacting the ESA, Congress chose not to protect 
habitat through the § 9 “take” prohibition, which applies 
to “any person.” It fully recognized the importance of 
protecting habitat, but simply chose other mechanisms for 
accomplishing that objective. It did so through § 7, which 
requires federal agencies to use their authorities (over all 
federal lands, and all private lands where any federal 
agency action—e.g., funding and permitting—is necessary 
to make use of those lands) to prohibit or condition any 
habitat-affecting actions that might jeopardize listed species 
or result in the destruction or adverse modification of their 
“critical habitat,” as identified by rulemaking, and through 
§ 5, which authorizes the federal acquisition of land and 
water resources in order to protect listed species. By 
choosing these mechanisms, Congress achieved its objec- 
tive of providing for habitat protection without unneces- 
sarily intruding on private property rights or imposing a 
disproportionate share of the cost of securing a “public 
good” upon individual private citizens. See Pet. App. 
6a-9a. 


Those tools are doubtless less convenient and more ex- 
pensive—for FWS—than simply continuing to apply the 
“harm” regulation to constrain citizens from making ordi- 
nary uses of their lands. But they are the only tools 
Congress provided.
