QUESTION PRESENTED 


Section 9(a)(1) of the Endangered Species Act of 
1973, 16 U.S.C. § 1538(a)(1), makes it unlawful for 
any person to “take” an endangered fish or wildlife 
species. The Act defines “take” to mean “to harass, 
harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or 
collect, or to attempt to engage in any such conduct.” 
Id. § 1532(19). By regulation, the U.S. Fish and Wild- 
life Service has defined “harm” in a way that focuses 
exclusively on the ultimate effect that any action has on 
a wildlife species; by its terms, it extends to ordinary 
land use activities (“habitat modifications”), like those 
engaged in by Respondents here, neither directed at wild- 
life nor directly injuring wildlife. 50 C.F.R. § 17.3. The 
question presented is: 


Whether the Fish and Wildlife Service’s regulatory 
definition of “harm” represents a permissible expan- 
sion of liability under Endangered Species Act 


§ 9(a) (1)?

STATEMENT 


Congress did not intend the prohibition against “take” 
of protected wildlife, set forth in ESA § 9(a)(1), to 
encompass ordinary land use activities that are not di- 
rected at wildlife and do not directly injure protected 
wildlife. 


The Statute. The ESA protects against two significant 
sources of species extinction: (1) the depletion of needed 
habitat, and (2) human predation. Tennessee Valley 
Authority v. Hill, 437 U.S. 153, 179 (1978) (“TVA”). 
‘The ESA targets the first source in §§ 5 and 7, and the 
second in §9. 16 U.S.C. §§ 1534, 1536, and 1538. 


ESA § 9(a)(1) prohibits commerce in or the taking 
of an endangered wildlife species.” Jd. § 1538(a)(1). 
The provision specifically at issue here makes it “unlaw- 
ful for any person subject to the jurisdiction of the United 
States to . . . take any such species”. Id. § 1538(a) 
(1)(B). “Take” is defined in the Act. It means “to 
harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, cap- 
ture, or collect, or to attempt to engage in any such 
conduct.” Id. § 1532(19).

“ESA §11 sets civil and criminal penalties for violations of the 
Act. An individual who has “knowingly” taken a listed species can 
be imprisoned for up to one year and fined up to $100,000 for 
each member taken. 16 U.S.C. § 1540(b); 18 U.S.C. §§ 3559, 3571. 
The lower courts have held that the “knowingly” standard of 
§ 1540(b) requires only a general intent to perform an act, not 
a specific intent to violate the ESA. F.g., United States v. Nguyen, 
916 F.2d 1016 (5th Cir. 1990). In addition, ESA § 11 authorizes 
governmental and citizen suits to enjoin violations. 16 U.S.C. 
§ 1540(e) (6), (2) (1). 

Although ESA §9(a)(1) refers to endangered wildlife species, 
FWS has adopted a regulation extending the “take” prohibition 
to most threatened wildlife species. 50 C.F.R. §17.31(a). ESA 
§$9(a)(1)(G) makes it unlawful for any person to “violate any 
[ESA] regulation.” 16 U.S.C. § 1538(a) (1) (G). 


3 


The Regulation. FWS has elaborated upon the statu- 
tory definition by adopting a regulation that in turn de- 
fines “harm”: 


Harm in the definition of “take” in the Act means 
an act which actually kills or injures wildlife. Such 
act may include significant habitat modification or 
degradation where it actually kills or injures wild- 
life by significantly impairing essential behavioral 
patterns, including breeding, feeding or sheltering. 


50 C.F.R. §17.3. That regulatory definition sweeps 
within the “take” prohibition a host of activities that are 
not directed at wildlife and not ordinarily thought of as 
the taking of wildlife. Specifically, it includes “habitat 
modifications” that cause or contribute to some injury to 
a listed species* generally, for example by withdrawing 
some beneficial habitat from the species and thus “impair- 
ing essential behavioral patterns, including breeding, feed- 
ing or sheltering.” The agency definition achieves this 
result by relying on the ultimate effect of an action; the 
nature of the action itself is not important. Thus, the 
regulation does not require that the prohibited action be 
directed at or against wildlife. Nor does it require any 
direct injury to some identifiable creature.*

The Proceedings Below. Respondents are directly af- 
fected by the agency’s “harm” regulation. They brought 
this action under ESA § 11(g) and the Administrative 
Procedure Act (“APA”) to challenge it. See J.A. 17-26. 
As the district court summarized (Pet. App. 76a): 


Plaintiffs in this action are small landowners, small 
logging companies, and families allegedly dependent 
on the forest products industry in the Pacific North- 
west and in the Southeast. In order to enforce the 
regulations at 50 C.F.R. § 17.3 and § 17.31 (a) 
and to avoid “harm”-type takings of the northern 
spotted owl and other threatened wildlife species, 
the Fish and Wildlife Service (“FWS”) has placed 
restrictions on timber harvesting. Plaintiffs claim 
that these restrictions have forced them to lay off 
employees, limited their income from trust lands, 
reduced the timber supply, and placed some of the 
plaintiffs in the position of being unable to support 
their families. They bring this action challenging the 
“harm” definition at 50 C.F.R. § 17.3 as contrary 
to the ESA. 


The district court sustained the regulation. Pet. App. 
77a-83a. The court of appeals reversed,* holding the 
“harm” regulation invalid in its effort to “embrace habitat 
modifications.” Pet. App. 24a. The court concluded that 
the agency’s definition was inconsistent with the statutory 
(and ordinary) meaning of “take,” with the overall struc- 
ture of the Act, and with its legislative history. Pet. App. 
3a-10a.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The prohibition against “take” of listed wildlife set 
forth in ESA §9, 16 U.S.C. § 1538, cannot fairly be 
read to bar ordinary uses of land. Yet FWS has seized 
on the elastic word “harm,” one of ten verbs within the 
statutory definition of “take,” to expand the reach of 
ESA §9 in just that way. FWS defines “harm” to forbid 
any “habitat modification” if its ultimate effect is to injure 
a listed species, even by reducing the reproductive success 
of a species member. See note 4, supra. By looking ex- 
Clusively to ultimate effect, and disregarding the character 
of the conduct sought to be prohibited, FWS’s regulation 
reaches many normal activities that are not directed at 
wildlife and cause no concrete injury to listed wildlife. 
That effort is irreconcilable with the Act as written. 


The words Congress used to describe what it sought to 
prohibit reveal adherence to the traditional concept of a 
wildlife “take.” They reflect Congress’ desire to stop all 
forms of human predation on protected species. As en- 
acted by Congress, the “take” prohibition evokes purpose- 
ful efforts to injure or capture wildlife: the direct applica- 
tion of force to, or physical intrusions on, specific crea- 
tures; direct and concrete injury to identifiable animals; 
and actions which a specifically acting human does to a 
specific creature. Whatever elasticity “harm” may have 
in other contexts, its use as one of ten descriptors of 
wildlife “take” will not allow it to reach “habitat modifi- 
cations” that unintentionally deprive listed wildlife of 
some environmental benefit. The expansion of ESA § 9 
to reach and potentially criminalize ordinary actions of 
Ordinary citizens cannot be hung on so “small a_ nail” 
(Pet. App. 96a) as the unelaborated and unhighlighted 
use of “harm,” a word added to nine existing words in 
the reported definition of “take” by “technical amend- 
ment” on the Senate floor. 


Petitioners are correct that Congress was concerned 
with the need to preserve vital habitat for endangered 


8 


species. The structure of the Act confirms, however, that 
Congress addressed that concern through other provisions. 
Those provisions, which impose special duties on the 
federal government, would be rendered meaningless or 
inconsequential if Petitioners’ view of “harm” were ac- 
cepted. The intent of the Congress that enacted ESA § 9 
cannot be found in amendments that subsequent Con- 
gresses did not adopt or made to other provisions of the 
Act. Nor can Petitioners’ policy arguments overcome the 
text and structure of the Act.

C. The Structure of the Act Further Undermines 
FWS’s “Harm” Regulation 


1. The basic structure of the 1973 ESA belies Peti- 
tioners’ theory of “harm.” It is difficult to believe that 
Congress extended the reach of the ESA to ordinary 
land uses by adding the word “harm,” without elabora- 
tion, to nine other verbs, in the course of defining a 
“take” prohibition that, on its face and in context, seemed 
to prohibit predatory actions directed at listed wildlife. 


If Congress had intended to reach land use under 
ESA § 9, it would have addressed the matter forthrightly. 
If Congress had meant “harm” to have such significance, 
it would have made the operative term in the statute 
“harm” instead of “take’—or found some other way 
to highlight ESA § 9’s intended breadth. See, e.g., note 
20, supra. Instead, Congress cast the section more nar- 
rowly, as a prohibition on “take,” and quietly placed 
the word “harm” in a list alongside nine other terms in 
the definitional section.

437 U.S. at 179. The absence of any reference to habitat

the Act.** The statutory structure shows that Congress
