STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case turns on its facts. The relevant facts, which 
inform the holding on review in this Court — that the state 
anti-discrimination statute does apply, and constitu- 
tionally can apply, to the Parade — concern two important 
aspects of Massachusetts history and tradition. 


One is Massachusetts’ long history, dating from the 
civil war period, of very broad statutory anti-discrimina- 
tion protection, extending to virtually every area of pub- 
lic life. In 1865, three years before the adoption of the 
Fourteenth Amendment, the Commonwealth enacted a 
statute providing that “no distinction, discrimination or 
restriction on account of color or race shall be lawful in 
any licensed inn, in any public place of amusement, pub- 
lic conveyance or public meeting in this common- 
wealth.” 1865 Mass. Acts 277 (emphasis added); see 
Bryant v. Rich’s Grill, 103 N.E. 925 (Mass. 1914). The 
Massachusetts Legislature has step by step expanded 
statutory protection beyond racial discrimination, as the 
Commonwealth’s sense of the public interest has 
matured, and now proscribes discrimination on the basis 
of religion (1950 Mass. Acts 479, § 3), national origin 
(1963 Mass. Acts 613, § 5), sex (1971 Mass. Acts 418, § 2), 
deafness and blindness (1975 Mass. Acts 338, § 2), physi- 
cal or mental disability (1979 Mass. Acts 595, § 2), and 


sexual orientation (1989 Mass. Acts 516, § 15).1 See Mass. 
Gen. L. ch. 272, §§ 92A, 98 (1994).? 


It is not, and could not be, disputed that the Com- 
monwealth’s public accommodations statute, applied by 
the Massachusetts courts in this case, is a statute of 
general applicability. The statute was enacted under the 
Commonwealth’s police power and is broadly targeted at 
discrimination. It is not specially aimed or targeted at 
speech, although on occasion it has been, as it was here, 
applied to conduct that is claimed to have expressive 
aspects. 


The second facet of Massachusetts tradition involved 
in this case is the unique civic celebration that takes place 
in Boston each year on Evacuation Day, the day the 
British sailed away from Boston on March 17, 1776 after 
General Washington’s first victory in the War of Indepen- 
dence. March 17, of course, also happens to be St. 
Patrick’s Day. (Cert. Pet. App. at B8.)3 The rich history

The Parade was once formally under the City of 
Boston’s management. (Cert. Pet. App. at B8-B9.) After 
World War II, Mayor James Michael Curley granted a 
group of returning veterans, Petitioners here (hereafter 
“the Council”) “authority to organize and conduct the 
Parade.” (Id. at B9.) Whatever its titular sponsorship, the 
Parade has without interruption been, in fact, an open 
recreational event for all the people of Boston who seek to 
celebrate both our freedom as Americans and the many 
strands of Irish heritage that underlie so much of Massa- 
chusetts’ modern history. Parades are not all alike, and 
the Boston Evacuation Day/St. Patrick’s Day parade can- 
not be understood without attention to its provenance 
and history. Whatever expressive elements it is claimed to 
have, which the state courts addressed in detail, it is 
beyond dispute that the Boston Parade differs in impor- 
tant ways from such social protests or political events as 
civil rights marches, labor demonstrations, or protest 
marches. 


A. The Proceedings Below. 


Litigation in this case began in March, 1992, when 
GLIB, a group of Irish-American gay men, lesbians, bisex- 
uals and their supporters, filed a complaint in state court 
alleging, inter alia, that the City of Boston and the Coun- 
cil, acting under color of law, had deprived GLIB of its 


First and Fourteenth Amendment rights by refusing to 
permit GLIB to register and march in the 1992 “City of 
Boston Evacuation Day Parade.” (Record, Paper No. 1, 
Complaint, March 10, 1992.) The trial court issued a 
restraining order requiring GLIB’s inclusion in the Evac- 
uation Day Parade, limiting the number of marchers to 25 
and imposing other conditions related to the safety of 
GLIB’s members and also addressing the Council’s fears 
that GLIB would be disruptive. (Record, Paper No. 3, 
March 11, 1992; Cert. Pet. App. at B26 n.28.) No appeal 
was taken, and GLIB participated in the 1992 event in an 
orderly fashion. (Cert. Pet. App. at B3, 26 & n.29.) 


In February 1993, after GLIB’s request for a registra- 
tion form for the 1993 Parade was refused by the Council, 
GLIB filed an amended complaint (J.A. at A5 et seq.), 
repeating its 1992 allegations of deprivation of federal 
and state constitutional rights under color of law, and 
adding a count alleging that the Council, aided and abet- 
ted by the City of Boston, had unlawfully discriminated 
against GLIB and its members on the basis of sexual 
orientation in violation of Massachusetts General Laws, 
chapter 272, sections 92A and 98. After the hearing, on 
February 22, 1993, the Superior Court allowed GLIB’s 
motion for a preliminary injunction, once again ordering 
GLIB’s inclusion in the Parade, on essentially the same 
terms and conditions as in 1992. (J.A. at A2.) 


In November 1993, the trial court conducted a four- 
day bench trial to determine whether GLIB was entitled 
to permanent, as opposed to preliminary, injunctive 
relief. In the course of trial, the trial judge heard 12 
witnesses and received 113 exhibits. (Cert. Pet. App. at 
B2.) After hearing, the trial judge denied GLIB’s claims 


against the City (Id. at B22), but concluded that the Coun- 
cil had violated GLIB’s right “to be free from sexual- 
orientation discrimination in a place of public accom- 
modation, resort, or amusement” in violation of the Mas- 
sachusetts public accommodations law. (Cert. Pet. App. at 
B28.) The court rejected the Council’s claim that the First 
Amendment barred application of the state law to its 
conduct. (Id. at B25.) To remedy the violation of state law, 
the superior court decreed that “the defendant Veterans 
Council is permanently enjoined from preventing plain- 
tiff GLIB from participating in the St. Patrick’s Day/ 
Evacuation Day Parade because of the group members’ 
sexual orientation.” (Jd. at B28.) At GLIB’s request, the 
court rescinded the special limiting conditions that had 
been imposed on GLIB by the 1992 and 1993 court orders, 
ordering, instead, that “the defendant Veterans Council 
shall allow GLIB to participate in the Parade in the same 
manner and under the same terms and conditions appli- 
cable to all other participants in the Parade.” (Id. at B29.) 
The Supreme Judicial Court affirmed that judgment, one 
justice dissenting. (Id. at C15.) 


B. The Judgment Below. 


The trial court’s judgment in GLIB’s favor rested on 
three fact-based determinations: (1) that the Council had 
unlawfully discriminated against GLIB’s members on the 
basis of their sexual orientation (Cert. Pet. App. at B4); (2) 
that the event described at trial, as the Council had 
administered it, was a place of public accommodation 
within the meaning of the public accommodations statute 
(Cert. Pet. App. at B9); and (3) that the Council was not 
entitled to an exemption from the anti-discrimination law

2. The determination that the parade is a place 
of public accommodation 


The Council contended in the courts below that the 
anti-discrimination statute does not apply to its conduct 
of the Parade because the Parade is not a place of “public 
accommodation . . . or amusement” within the meaning 
of the statute. (Cert. Pet. App. at B6.) The trial judge 
rejected this on the basis of abundant evidence that the 
Council had shown no selectivity in the admission of 
groups to the Parade. (Id. at B6.) Not only did the Council 
allow a diverse array of commercial, political, theatrical, 
charitable, religious, athletic, and other groups to march, 
but it generally made no inquiry into “the specific views” 
of participating groups. (Id. at B7-8.) Moreover, some 
groups simply showed up and marched in the Parade 
without submitting an application, and others marched 
because they made a financial contribution in lieu of 
filling out an application. (Id. at B8.)7

protected under the public accommodations statute. (Cert. Pet. 
App. at B7.) The Supreme Judicial Court recognized that the 
Veterans Council had excluded a few other groups as well, a 
Right to Life group and a truck carrying anti-homosexual signs 
among them. The Supreme Judicial Court found “no legal! rele- 
vance of this fact to the findings of the judge or to the conclusion 
we reach.” (Id. at C8 n.10.)

The trial judge also took note of the specific charac- 
ter of the Boston Parade as a “public celebration of long- 
standing.” (Cert. Pet. App. at B8.) In particular, the court 
noted that the Parade celebrates a public holiday, as well 
as St. Patrick’s Day. The Parade was formerly adminis- 
tered by the City directly, in conjunction with a citizens 
association. It was only in 1947 that Mayor James Michael 
Curley conferred on the Council, composed of veterans 
returning victorious from World War II, the honor of 
organizing and conducting the Parade. Since then, the 
Council has been the exclusive holder of the permit to 
administer the Parade. (Jd. at B8-9; Tr. at 302.) 


The Governor of the Commonwealth issues a procla- 
mation each year recognizing the historical significance 
of Evacuation Day, and public schools and buildings close 
for the day in Suffolk County. (Cert. Pet. App. at B8, 
n.10.) 


The Parade draws as many as 20,000 participants and 
up to 1,000,000 spectators (id. at B9), and it is “the largest 
New England event for Irish-Americans, their friends, 
and well-wishers.” (Id.) 


Based on these factual findings, and the plain mean- 
ing of the public accommodations statute,® as construed 
in prior instances, the Supreme Judicial Court affirmed 
the trial court’s ruling that the Parade falls within the 
scope of the public accommodations law. (Id. at C11-12.)

3. The determination that the parade is not 
entitled to a first amendment exemption 


The Council contended at trial and on appeal in state 
court that the Parade “is a form of speech protected by 
the First Amendment via the Fourteenth Amendment, 
and that [the Council’s] speech will be effectively diluted 
or distorted if they are required to include GLIB in the 
Parade.” (Cert. Pet. App. at B23.) 


Invoking this Court’s precedents,’ the trial court con- 
sidered the Council’s First Amendment claim, but ulti- 
mately rejected it. (Id. at B23-24.) Based on the factual

within the limits that the Constitution tolerates.

II. THE STATE ANTI-DISCRIMINATION LAW

IV. THE FINDINGS AND CONCLUSIONS OF THE

As we have demonstrated, the Council’s challenge to 
the statute, on its face and as applied, fails on the law and

The courts below appreciated the fact-sensitive

this Court makes an “independent examination of the

Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 284-286 (1964)).27 


Yet the precise nature of this Court’s appellate review 
of fact-finding is a complex matter. The findings of state 
courts, reached after thorough litigation at trial, are not to 
be ignored entirely, but rather are entitled to respect. The 
measure of that respect is a function of three variables: (1) 
the character of the relevant factual issue (that is, 
whether the question is one of historical fact in the sense 
of a description of the primary elements of an historical 
event, or, instead, calls for some inference from the his- 
torical facts); (2) the character of the finder of fact below 
(that is, whether the trier of fact was a judge or a jury); 
and (3) the extent to which the relevant finding depends 
on credibility choices by the trier of fact.

A. The Crucial Finding Below Was One of Histori- 
cal Fact. 


The most important disputed issue at trial was 
whether the Council selected participants according to 
any standard at all or, instead, opened the event to the 
public generally. That issue turned on what the individ- 
ual administering the event, Mr. Hurley, understood his 
task to be — that is, what was in his mind at the time he 
approved other participants, but barred GLIB. The con- 
tent of Hurley’s mind at that juncture was plainly a 
matter of historical fact. Hernandez v. New York, 500 U.S. 
352, 367 (1990) (holding that a state court finding of 
discriminatory intent is a factual determination entitled 
to deference even if it relates to a constitutional issue); 
Bose Corp., 466 U.S. at 498; Pullman-Standard v. Swint, 456 
U.S. 273, 287-88 (1982) (holding that discriminatory intent 
under Title VII is question of pure fact reviewable only 
for clear error). 


In close cases, this Court has typically characterized 
questions as factual or legal in a way that achieves the 


42 


most appropriate forum allocation: if it appears that an 
issue is best left to the jury or the trial judge, then it 
usually is considered to be one of fact; if, however, an 
issue is better determined by an appellate court, it is 
typically considered to be one of law or a mixed question. 
Cf. Miller v. Fenton, 474 U.S. 104, 113-15 (1985). In this 
instance, the nature of what Hurley was thinking and 
doing, and, therefore, the nature of the event he was 
administering, is the kind of factual issue that is best left 
to the trier of fact, who actually observed Hurley on the 
witness stand. 


In sharp contrast to conventional analysis, the Coun- 
cil asserts that this Court must disregard the trial court’s 
finding on this vital point and substitute its own de novo 
assessment of Hurley’s intentions from the cold record. 
The obscenity and libel cases the Council cites for such a 
questionable approach involve questions of an entirely 
different order. Matters such as whether material is “pat- 
ently offensive” or whether a speaker acts with “actual 
malice” are not issues of primary, descriptive, historical 
fact at all. Those questions cannot be resolved solely by 
investigating historical events and assessing the cred- 
ibility of witnesses, but rather demand substantial judi- 
cial judgment. The more judgment a question is 
understood to require, and the less simple historical 
investigation, the more suitable the question is for con- 
sideration on appeal. 


Moreover, lower courts administering the federal law 
of obscenity and libel require some consistent under- 
standing of what counts as “patently offensive” material 
or personal attacks with “actual malice” and can get that 


43 


guidance only if this Court reviews lower court judg- 
ments and uses its appellate opinions to elaborate those 
issues. Only in that way can this Court achieve some 
rough uniformity in a series of cases in which more or 
less the same issue is presented in similar circumstances. 


When, however, the question in a case is the “subjec- 
tive state of mind” that a “particular” person had at a 
“particular” time, then the issue is clearly one of descrip- 
tive, historical fact that is best left to the trial judge who 
saw the relevant witness testify. Bose Corp., 466 U.S. at 518 
n.1 (Rehnquist, C.J., dissenting). An issue of this kind 
does not arise routinely in a category of cases, but, 
instead, is presented only in an isolated lawsuit and will 
not reappear somewhere else. The justifications for more 
extensive appellate review in the obscenity and libel con- 
texts have no purchase here, any more than they would in 
a fact-bound negligence case.

determined by an experienced trial judge, who set out the 
evidence and explained his evaluation. This Court is not, 
then, invited to rely on factual findings that may have 
been subsumed within a jury’s general, unexplained ver- 
dict. The Chief Justice has explained that independent 
review of fact-finding is justified primarily in cases in 
which the facts were found by a jury, rather than by a 
trial judge. Bose Corp., 466 U.S. at 518 n.2 (Rehnquist, C.J., 
dissenting) (joined by O’Connor, J.).

D. The Supreme Judicial Court Properly Sustained 
the Superior Court’s Findings. 


The Council charges that the Supreme Judicial Court 
below failed to undertake its constitutional obligation to 
review the entire record to ensure that the trial court’s 
determinations, including its determinations of fact, 
properly respected the Council’s First Amendment rights. 


To upset the Supreme Judicial Court’s judgment on 
this score, this Court must fasten on that court’s properly 
appreciative references to the work of the trial judge; 
attach significance to the state court’s failure to articulate 
the “independent review” doctrine explicitly; and ignore 
the state court’s statements that it did, indeed, examine 
the entire record. (Cert. Pet. App. at C6, C7 n.9, C8 n.10, 
C11, C13-14 n.18; C14, C15.) This Court reviews state 
court judgments, not state court opinions. So long as it 
appears from a fair reading of the whole of such an 
opinion that the state court has not mistaken its duty and 
has, instead, actually applied the appropriate constitu- 
tional standard, its judgment should be sustained. Accord 
Harte-Hanks Communications, 491 U.S. at 667-68. 


The Council also contends that the Supreme Judicial 
Court reached the wrong result on the merits of the 
central factual issue. Simply put, the Council insists that 
the Supreme Judicial Court should have substituted its 
judgment about what Hurley was thinking and doing at 
the critical time for the different assessment made by the 
trial judge, who saw Hurley on the witness stand. To do 
that, the Supreme Judicial Court would have had to set 
aside considerations that routinely call for appellate def- 
erence to trial court findings: this issue is one of primary,

trial judge, not a jury; and, perhaps most important, the

such thing, much less that this Court should do so now, at 
a stage even further removed from the “feel” of the trial.

CONCLUSION 


The task in this case, as in all such highly fact-driven 
cases, is to examine the record below and to determine 
whether the courts of the Commonwealth committed 
reversible error in their appraisal of the evidence regard- 
ing this singular event and the legal implications that 
follow. This task is not aided by the invocation of formal- 
istic labels, and the courts below resisted any attempt to 
resolve the case in that fashion. Instead, they looked 
closely at the facts. What they found was that the Coun- 
cil, dishonoring its long history as the faithful steward of 
a splendid public event, is attempting to use the First 
Amendment as a ruse for evading the Commonwealth's 
anti-discrimination laws and, into the bargain, drowning 
out other legitimate voices. The state courts reached this 
conclusion after laboring over this case, as it unfolded
