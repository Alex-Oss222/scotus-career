SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


The Florida Bar's 30-day ban on written communications 
concerning “an action for personal injury or wrongful death or 
otherwise relating] to an accident or disaster,” violates the First 
Amendment. Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Association, 486 U.S. 466 
(1988), held that direct mail lawyer advertising is 
constitutionally protected commercial speech. The speech at 
issue in this case — truthful, non-misleading letters which fully 
conform to The Florida Bar's extensive rules on content, style 


and delivery — is squarely under Shapero’s protective umbrella. 


The Florida Bar offers two justifications for its 30-day ban. 
The first, never raised below, is that the 30-day prohibition serves 
“a substantial interest in maintait ng the professionalism of the 
bar and public confidence in ti administration of justice.” Pet. 
Brief, p. 7. The second is an interest “in protecting the personal 
privacy and tranquility of its citizens from crass commercial 


intrusion by attorneys upon their personal grief in times of 
trauma.” /d. at 8. Although concerns for professionalism underlie 
all regulations of professional conduct, the Bar's tardy invocation 
of the “administration of justice” as ics primary argument in this 
Court is procedurally improper, as well as substantively flawed. 
With that caveat, respondents address both state interests now 


The burden is upon the Bar to prove that (1) its interests are 
substantial; (2) the 30-day ban “advances these interests in a 
direct and material way;” and (3) “the extent of the restriction on 
protected speech is in reasonable proportion to the interests 
served.” Edenfield u Fane, 113 S. Ct. 1792, 1798 (1993) (citing 
the commercial speech test of Central Hudson Gas & Elec. Corp. 
v. Public Serv, Comm'n, 447 U.S. 557, 564 (1980)). The Bar has 
not carried that burden in this case.

Nothing in this record supports The Florida Bar's suggestion 
that professionalism, public confidence in the administration of 
justice, and personal privacy are directly, materially and 
reasonably served by the 30-day ban on attorney direct mail 
related to personal injury. Both the district court and the court of 
appeals concluded that the 30-day rule violates the First 
Amendment. McHenry u The Florida Bar, 808 F. Supp. 1543 
(M.D. Fla. 1992), aff'd, 21 F.3d 1038 (11th Cir, 1994); Pet. Cert. 
App. 18-29, 30-46. Apparently recognizing that Shapero and its 
progenitors and Central Hudson and its progeny condemn the 
Bar's rule, the Bar has asked the Court to “revisit the Bates lv 
State Bar of Arizona, 433 U.S. 350 (1977)] premise and broaden 
the discretion of the States in the regulation of attorney 
commercial solicitation.” Pet. Brief, p. 34. The Court should 
decline the invitation to scrap its precedents and to allow the 
States to create their own lawyer advertising First Amendment 


jurisprudence. 


The Florida Bar and its amici do not offer principled bases 
for their attack upon Bates and upon established commercial 
speech doctrine. In briefs filled with emotion and hyperbole, 
references to unpublished, self-serving studies which dehors the 
record, argumentative analogies which fail the test of the 
legitimate leeways of precedent, and repeated disregard of the 
precise issues presented by the sole form of communication in 
this case, the Bar and its amici seek to exploit judicial antipathies 
to lawyer advertising. Reason, precedent, and stare decisis must 
guide First Amendment analysis. The decision below should be 
affirmed because the Bar's 30-day ban does not pass the tests of 
Central Hudson and Shapero, and because the Bar's States 
rights” argument fails to credit the value of First Amendment 
doctrinal stability.

B. Emotion and Hyperbole Are No Substitute for Reasoned

The Bar charges that Bates “thrust aside centuries of Anglo-

History does not support the hyperbole of the Bar or its 
amici. Our profession’s English roots evolved from “[a]n 
intimate fraternity of rich young men [who] lived, ate, and 
learned the law together [and who] Iblecause they were wealthy 
and did not need to earn a living, . . viewed law practice as a 
public service instead of a trade. Economic competition did 
not exist in barristers’ legal or social interactions, and the 
profession assumed a certain cordial dignity.” Katherine Laroe, 
Much Ado About Barratry: State Regulation Of Attorneys’ 
Targeted Direct-Mail Solicitation, 25 St. Mary’s L.J. 1513, 1519 
(1994) [hereinafter Laroe] (footnotes omitted). In colonial 
America, the residue of these class distinctions led to the 
perception that bar associations were “the exclusive and secret 
trade unions of a privileged class [and] were deemed to be 
undemocratic and un-American.” Louise Hill, Solicitation by 


29 


Lawyers: Piercing the First Amendment Veil, 42 Me. L. Rev. 369,

formalized by the Alabama State Bar Association’s adoption of 
“the first formal Code of Ethics for the legal profession in 1887.” 
Id. at 379-381 (footnotes omitted). 


The cataclysmic reactions to Bates are belied both by the 
vintage of the laws prohibiting solicitation and by the ability of a 
democratic society and its judicial system to have survived the 
evolution of the legal profession from a small fraternity of men 
from England’s first families to a profession comprised of men 
and women from diverse backgrounds who practice law and 
serve justice in a world far different from the thirteenth through 
nineteenth centuries. Attitudes which prevailed “as a principle of

RULE 4-7.4 DIRECT CONTACT WITH 
PROSPECTIVE CLIENTS 


(a) Solicitation. A lawyer shall not solicit professional 
employment from a prospective client with whom the lawyer has 
no family or prior professional relationship, in person or 
otherwise, when a significant motive for the lawyer’s doing so is 
the lawyer’s pecuniary gain. A lawyer shall not permit employees 
or agents of the lawyer to solicit in the lawyer 's behalf. A lawyer 
shall not enter into an agreement for, charge, or collect a fee for 
professional employment obtained in violation of this rule. The 
term “solicit” includes contact in person, by telephone, telegraph, 
or facsimile, or by other communication directed to a specific 
recipient and includes any written form of communication 
directed to a specific recipient and not meeting the requirements 
of subdivision (b) of this rule. 


(b) Written Communication. 


(1) A lawyer shall not send, or knowingly permit to be sent, 
on the lawyer’s behalf or on behalf of the lawyer’s firm or partner,

(A) the written communication concerns an action 
for personal injury or wrongful death or otherwise relates to 
an accident or disaster involving the person to whom the 
communication is addressed or a relative of that person, 
unless the accident or disaster occurred more than 30 days 
prior to the mailing of the communication;

CHAPTER 15. REVIEW OF LAWYER 
ADVERTISEMENTS AND SOLICITATIONS 


15.1 GENERALLY 
RULE 15-1.1 PURPOSE 


The Florida Bar, as an official arm of the Supreme Court of 
Florida, is charged with the duty of enforcing the rules governing 
lawyer advertising and solicitation and with assisting members of 
The Florida Bar to advertise their services in a manner beneficial 
to both the public and the legal profession. The board of 
governors, pursuant to the authority vested in it under rule 2-8.3, 
shall create a standing committee on advertising to advise 
members of The Florida Bar on permissible advertising and 
solicitation practices. It shall be the duty of the committee to 
administer the advertising evaluation program set forth in rule 4- 


7.5. 


Added Dec. 20, 1990, effective Jan. 1,1991 (571 So.2d 451). 
Amended July 23,1992, effective Jan. 1,1993 (605 So.2d 252). 


15-2. STANDING COMMITTEE ON ADVERTISING 
RULE 15-2.1 MEMBERSHIPAND TERMS 
The standing committee on advertising shall consist of 4 


members of The Florida Bar and 3 nonlawyers representing the 
public. Members of the committee shall be appointed by the 


2la 


Appendix A 


president-elect of The Florida Bar, as provided in rule 2-8.1. The 
president-elect shall designate the chair and vice-chair, with the 
advice and consent of the board of governors. Members of the 
committee shall serve staggered 3-year terms. No member may 
serve more than 2 consecutive terms. A quorum shall consist of a 
majority of the members. 


Added Dec. 21, 1990, effective Jan. 1,1991 (571 So.2d 451). 
Amended July 23,1992, effective Jan. 1,1993 (605 So.2d 252). 


RULE 15-2.2 FUNCTIONS 


It shall be the task of the committee to evaluate all 
advertisements filed with the committee for compliance with the 
rules governing advertising and solicitation and to provide 
written advisory opinions concerning compliance to the 
respective filers, to develop a handbook on advertising for the 
guidance of and dissemination to members of The Florida Bar, 
and to recommend to the board of governors from time to time 
such amendments to the Rules of Professional Conduct as the 
committee may deem advisable. 


Added Dec. 21, 1990, effective Jan. 1,1991 (571 So.2d 451).

The nonlawyer public members of the standing committee

associated with attendance at meetings of the committee.
