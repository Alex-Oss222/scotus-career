ARGUMENT 
I 


THE COURT SHOULD RETURN TO THE STATES A 
REASONABLE RANGE OF AUTHORITY TO DETER- 
MINE WHETHER ATTORNEY ADVERTISING 
ADVERSELY AFFECTS THE ADMINISTRATION OF 
JUSTICE, AND TO REGULATE IT ACCORDINGLY. 


In its initial brief on the merits, The Florida Bar 
urged the Court to revisit the line of cases following Bates 
and to return to the States a reasonable degree of author- 
ity to determine whether attorney advertising adversely 
affects the administration of justice. The Respondent 
complains that the request to revisit the Bates cases intro- 
duced a new issue that was not included in the Questions 
Presented for Review in the Petition for Writ of Cer- 
tiorari. Rule 24 of the Rules of the Supreme Court of the 
United States provides that the phrasing of the questions 
presented for review in a Brief on the Merits need not be 
identical with the questions set forth in the Petition for 
Writ of Certiorari, as long as the brief on the merits does 
not raise additional questions or change the substance of 
the questions already presented. The Florida Bar’s brief 
on the merits is in full compliance with Rule 24. The 
substance of the Bar’s argument presented under Point I 
of its brief on the merits was thoroughly laid out under 
Point I in the Petition for Writ of Certiorari. 


The Respondents and amici argue that the record is 
devoid of any evidence that attorney advertising has had 
an adverse effect upon the administration of justice, and 
they present data indicating that there has been a rela- 
tively small number of lawyer disciplinary cases involv- 
ing advertising. The arguments smack of the very 


sophistry which the Respondent decries. The decisions 
following Bates, and their interpretation by lower federal 
courts, have severely narrowed the scope of permissible 
state regulation so that the States are now effectively 
limited to merely investigating and regulating deception, 
and nothing more. Even the regulation of deceptive 
advertising has been heavily burdened by the Court so 
that States must either show actual deception in a given 
case or must leap a high hurdle to establish that a given 
type of advertising is inherently misleading. Given the 
narrow range of permissible State action, it is not surpris- 
ing that there are relatively few attorney disciplinary 
cases involving advertising. That fact alone does not 
establish that the nature and scope of attorney advertis- 
ing which has developed since Bates is not adversely 
affecting the administration of justice. 


It is also understandable that the record does not 
include conclusive proof of such adverse effect. As illus- 
trated in The Florida Bar’s initial brief, the decisions 
following Bates have held, as a matter of law, that States 
do not have a sufficient interest to step beyond the role of 
investigating deception in attorney advertising. As a 
result, States have not had the occasion or incentive to 
produce a record showing that other aspects of such 
advertising have an adverse impact upon substantial 
State interests. What The Florida Bar and its supporting 
amici urge is that the philosophical basis upon which 
State authority has been so severely restricted is 
unjustified by First Amendment law in general, and 
inconsistent with the application of commercial regula- 
tory law in contexts other than attorney advertising.

Both the Respondent and amicus Institute for Access 
argue that the institutional speech cases cited by The 
Florida Bar are inapplicable to the case at bar.! The 
Respondent states that nothing in the institutional speech 
cases indicated that the Court intended that they be 
applied to the citizenry at large or to regulated profes-

applied. As was discussed at some length in the Bar’s 
initial Brief on the Merits, lawyers, in the conduct of their 
professional activities, are not judged as citizens at large, 
but as officers of the court entrusted with the administra- 
tion of an essential element of a fundamental constitu- 
tional function. This Court recognized in Edenfield v. Fane, 
113 S.Ct. 1792, 123 L.Ed.2d 543 (1993), that the application 
of First Amendment principles to professional advertis- 
ing is not necessarily the same for lawyers as for other 
professionals.

to the operation of their institutions.” Brief of Respondents, 
p. 36. Amicus Institute for Access argues that the institu- 
tional speech decisions are distinguishable because they 
involve speech which occurred on property owned by the

Those cases have held that government owned property

Krishna Consciousness, Inc. v. Lee, 112 S.Ct. 2701, 120

concerned with the nature of the property upon which

speech on the basic mission of the institution. Thus, in 
Hazelwood, supra, the Court focused upon speech 
expressed in “a school-sponsored publication, theatrical 
production, or other vehicle of student expression” hav-

both with the effect upon the education of the students

“imprimatur” of the State. The Court’s reasoning in 
Hazelwood would have applied equally whether the 
expression took place in a play or publication produced 
on the school campus or anywhere else. The same is true 
of the reasoning in Rankin, supra, Hudson, supra, and 
Goldman, supra. The State’s ability to impose reasonable 
regulations upon the speech of its employees, prisoners 
and military personnel in order to foster the successful 
pursuit of basic institutional missions remains the same 
whether that mission is taking place on government 
owned or controlled property or anywhere else. 


This Court has recognized that a lawyer’s profes- 
sional advertising is entitled to First Amendment protec- 
tion primarily because of its role in making the public 
aware of the availability of legal services and thereby 
increasing access to our system of justice. The State’s 
interest in the effective administration of justice is surely 
as substantial as its interest in providing education, law 
enforcement, national defense and efficient public 
employment. Nevertheless, the Bates cases have failed to 
include consideration of the potential impact of attorney 
advertising upon the mission of administering justice as a 
factor in the overall equation.?

A. The challenged rules meet the requirements for 
valid commercial regulations because they are nar- 
rowly tailored to serve substantial governmental 
interests. 


The Florida Bar argued in its initial brief that, while it 
was respectfully urging this Court to revisit the Bates line 
of cases, it was unnecessary to do so in order to reverse 
the decision in the case at bar. The Bar argued that the 
State has a substantial interest in preserving reasonable 
standards of personal privacy and tranquility in the com- 
munity. In support of its position, the Bar cited Members 
of City Counsel v. Taxpayers for Vincent, 466 U.S. 806, 104 
S.Ct. 2118, 80 L.Ed.2d 772 (1984) [allowing regulation of 
political signs on public property in the interest of aes- 
thetic values]; Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 69 S.Ct. 448

(1949) [allowing regulation of sound trucks in the inter-

Schultz, 487 U.S. 474, 108 S.Ct. 2495, 101 L.Ed.2d 420

private residence in the interest of privacy] and Ward v. 
Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 78, 109 S.Ct. 2746, 2754, 105 
L.Ed.2d 661 (1989) [upholding regulation of music ampli-

The Respondent and amici do not dispute the fact 
that the State has a substantial interest in maintaining 
reasonable standards of personal privacy and tranquility. 
Instead, they argue that the challenged Florida regulation 
does not directly and proportionately serve such interest. 
In support of its argument, the Respondent states that 
grief is not the gravamen of the challenged rule since the 
rule applies to any accident, and asserts that it is the 
Bar’s burden to prove that direct mail letters to accident 
victims “harms them and harms the administration of 
justice.” Brief of Respondents, pp. 13, 14. Amicus Media 
Institute misstates the Bar’s position to be that the State 
has a substantial interest “in keeping a particular mes- 
sage from certain adults because Petitioner believes they 
cannot handle the message.” Brief of the Media Institute, 
et al, p. 9. The essence of these arguments is that, in order 
to sustain its rule, Florida must prove that every personal 
injury victim who receives a direct solicitation letter is in 
a state of grief and will be personally offended or other- 
wise injured by such letter. 


In the first place, the Bar is not asserting that the 
State’s interest is in protecting each individual from being 
harmed by the receipt of such letters. The State’s interest 
is in maintaining a reasonable community standard of 


14 


privacy and personal tranquility. This was precisely the 
interest that justified regulations in Taxpayers for Vincent, 
Kovaks, Frisby, and Ward. The Respondent attempts to 
distinguish those cases by noting that the sound trucks 
and loudspeakers emitted “loud and raucous noises,” 
that the sign ordinance was designed to “preserve urban 
aesthetics,” and that the Court emphasized the “offensive 
and disturbing nature of the form of communication” 
when it permitted regulation of residential picketing. 
Brief of Respondent, p. 19. In these respects, however, the 
cited cases are actually indistinguishable from the case at 
bar. 


As with the residential picketing, Florida’s rule is 
directed at the “offensive and disturbing nature of the 
form of communication.” In Kovaks, Taxpayers, and Ward 
the States did not seek to prove, and obviously could not 
practicably have proved, that each person who viewed 
the political signs or heard the loudspeakers or music 
was offended or otherwise adversely affected by them. 
What those cases approved was State enforcement of 
reasonable community standards of privacy, tranquility, 
and aesthetics. Similarly, in the case at bar, the State 
cannot practicably formulate any rule that will restrict 
direct solicitation letters only as to those accident victims 
who are grieving and who would find such letters offen- 
sive. In addition, any more subjective rule would leave 
both the advertising lawyer and the disciplinary arm of 
the Bar in confusion as to when a letter became a viola- 
tion in any given instance.

tral Hudson holding that a commercial regulation shall be 
“no more extensive than necessary” to serve the State’s

least restrictive means but, as we have put it in 
the other contexts discussed above, a means 
narrowly tailored to achieve the desired objec- 
tive. Within those bounds we leave it to govern- 
mental decisionmakers to judge what manner of 
regulation may best be employed. 


[emphasis supplied] The 30-day rule adopted by the Flor- 
ida Supreme Court represents a reasonable accommoda- 
tion which serves the State’s interest without unduly 
burdening the lawyer.

B. The challenged rules are valid time, place or man- 
ner restrictions. 


The Respondent and amicus Media Institute argue 
that the challenged rules are not content-neutral because 
they are limited in their application to letters which 
solicit legal business from persons who were victims of, 
or are relatives of victims of, personal injury or wrongful 
death. This Court has not held that content-neutrality is 
destroyed simply because reference must incidentally be 
made to the content in order to determine whether or not 
the class which is regulated is implicated. In Ward v. Rock 
Against Racism, supra at S.Ct. 2754, the Court specifically 
stated: 


A regulation that serves purposes unrelated to 
the content of expression is deemed neutral, 
even if it has an incidental effect on some 
speakers or messages but not others. [citation 
omitted] Government regulation of expressive 
activity is content-neutral so long as it is “justi- 
fied without reference to the content of the regu- 
lated speech.” 


[emphasis by Court] In Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 
475 U.S. 41, 106 S.Ct. 925, 89 L.Ed.2d 29 (1986), the Court 
upheld an ordinance which restricted the showing of 
sexually explicit motion pictures to limited geographical 
areas. The ordinance necessitated review of a film’s con- 
tent in order to determine whether it contained sexually 
explicit material and thus subjected it to the ordinance. 
The Court upheld the ordinance because it was “aimed 
not at the content of the films shown at ‘adult motion 
picture theatres,’ but rather at the secondary effects of such 
theatres on the surrounding community.” Id. at U.S. 47. 


17 


[emphasis by Court] The Court found that the City Coun- 
sel’s “predominate concerns” were with the secondary 
effects of adult theatres, and not with the content of 
adult-films themselves. The fact that the City Counsel 
might also have been motivated by other factors which, 
by themselves, would not have passed constitutional 
muster was held to be irrelevant. 


The challenged Florida rule serves two separate state 
interests, each of which is justified without reference to 
the content. First, as noted above, the State seeks to 
preserve a reasonable standard of personal privacy and 
tranquility. on behalf of its citizens. It is not concerned 
with the message contained within the letters, which an 
attorney is free to deliver after the thirty-day period or at 
any time by other advertising media, but with the mode 
of delivery and the nature of the recipients. Second, as in 
Renton, the State is concerned about the secondary effects 
of such letters upon the public’s perception of the legal 
profession and our system of justice. 


The real argument that Respondent and amici is mak- 
ing, albeit couched in the terminology of content-neu- 
trality, is that the challenged regulation is under-inclusive 
by failing to include all classes that they perceive to be in 
equal circumstances. This Court has firmly rejected the 
argument that an under-inclusive regulation is constitu- 
tionally suspect. Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, 348 
U.S. 483, 75 S.Ct. 461 (1955); McDonald v. Board of Election 
Comm'rs, 394 U.S. 802, 89 S.Ct. 1404 (1969). Indeed, the 
under-inclusive argument was also advanced and 
rejected in Renton where Court stated: 


18 


That Renton chose first to address the potential 
problems created by one particular kind of adult 
business in no way suggests that the City has 
“singled out” adult theatres for disciminatory 
treatment. [Id. at U.S. 53]. 


The challenged Florida rule meets each of the elements of 
a valid time, place or manner restriction and should be 
upheld on that basis alone if no other.
