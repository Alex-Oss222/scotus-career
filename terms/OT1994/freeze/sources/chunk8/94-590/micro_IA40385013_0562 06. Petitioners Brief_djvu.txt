STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case arises from the adoption and implementation 
by Vernonia School District 47J (the “District”) of the 
Student Athlete Drug Policy (the “Policy”) allowing the 
District to conduct urinalysis testing of its elementary 
and high school student athletes without individualized 
suspicion. The District did not allow James Acton, a 
seventh grade student at the District’s Washington Grade 
School, to participate in interscholastic football when he 
and his parents declined to sign the Policy's consent form 
required of all interscholastic athletic participants. The 
parents, as guardians ad litem for James Acton (the 
“Actons”), initiated this action against the District seck- 
ing injunctive and declaratory relief from enforcement of 


the Policy. 


A. Record Evidence 


1. The Community of Vernonia and the Role of Inter- 
scholastic Athletics. 


Vernonia, Oregon is a small, isolated logging com- 
munity of approximately 3,000 residents. (J.A. 19; R. 
102). The District employs 42 faculty members and 
consists of one high school and three grade schools with 
total enrollment of approximately 690 students. (PI.Ex. 
5 at 5-6). Because of its size and location, the town has 
few recreational opportunities or organized entertainment 
programs for elementary and high school students. (J.A. 
19-20; R. 102). As a result, community life in general 
centers around the schools, with interscholastic athletics 
playing a dominant role. (J.A. 20; R. 101-102). Given 
the importance of athletics, student athletes are admired 
within the District’s schools and the community. (J.A. 
20, 37). 


The District offers football, basketball, track. cross 
country and volleyball starting at the seventh grade level, 
and wrestling and golf starting at the ninth grade level. 
(J.A. 43, 64; PLEx. 5 at 6). Between 60 to 65 percent 
of high school students and approximately 75 percent of 
elementary students participate in interscholastic athletics. 
(J.A. 20, 37). 


Participation in interscholastic athletic programs is vol-

4. The District’s Attempts to Reduce Drug Use Prior to 
Adopting and Implementing the Policy. 


The District attempted to deter drug and alcohol use 
through education. Special classes and presentations were 
held on the effects and addictive nature of drugs. (J.A. 
25, 49, 63: R. 61). The District also spent several thou- 
sands of dollars to employ well-known speakers to ad- 
dress students on the dangers of drug use. (J.A. 25-26). 
The Vernonia City Council, after being approached by 
the District, hired a policeman for two weeks to patrol 
areas near the high school to deter drug use. (J.A. 22, 
25, 34-35). 


These efforts failed to achieve any significant impact 
on the disruptive atmospshere in the District’s schools. 
(J.A. 25-26, 34-35, 49-50, 65-66). For example, the 
arrests of the five athletes referred to above occurred the 
day after the presentation of a student play directed at 
the dangers of drug use. (J.A. 49-50, 63-64). By 1989, 
District officials considered adopting a drug testing policy. 
(J.A. 26-27; R. 31-32). According to a District princi- 
pal, such a policy was the only alternative other than 
“massive expulsions.” (J.A. 26-27; R. 32). 


The District investigated possible drug testing programs. 
(J.A. 26-27; R. 30-32). The District had previously re- 
fused to adopt any drug testing program, choosing instead 
to exhaust other alternatives. (J.A. 27). The District 
studied testing programs of other school districts and 
obtained legal opinions regarding such programs. (J.A. 
58-59; R. 33-35). The District administration recom- 
mended implementation of the Policy after a parent “input 
night” was held on the topic and the parents in attendance 
unanimously approved the Policy. (R. 32-33). The 
School Board approved the Policy for implementation in 
the fall of 1989. (R. 35-36).

5. The District’s Drug Testing Policy. 


The purpose of the Policy is non-punitive. (J.A. 7, 
27-28). It was designed as a preventative program to 
keep athletes from using drugs, to protect their health and 
safety, and to provide drug users with assistance pro- 
grams. (J.A. 7, 27-28, 32). An earlier version of the 
Policy covered all students who participated in extracur- 
ricular activities, but the Policy involved in this case was 
limited to interscholastic athletics to assure its legality. 
(J.A. 29-30). 


The Policy provides for testing by urinalysis for am- 
phetamines, marijuana and cocaine. (PI.Ex. 5 at 17; 
Def.Ex. 109). The Policy does not test for steroids or 
alcohol. (J.A. 85-86). Other drugs, such as LSD, are 
only tested for at the request of the District because of 
the high costs of testing for such drugs. (PI.Ex. 5 at 17).” 
The identity of a student, however, does not determine 
the drugs tested for. (PI.Ex. 5 at 17). To avoid sanc- 
tions for a “positive test,” students must identify any 
prescription medications they are taking by providing a 
copy of the prescription or a doctor’s authorization. (J.A. 
8). 


All students who participate in interscholastic athletics 
are tested at the beginning of the athletic season. (J.A. 
8, 30-31). The student and his or her parents are re-

the student chooses to participate in interscholastic sports. 
(J.A. 10-11, 16-17; R. 8, Pl.Ex. 5 at 25-26). During the 
athletic season, athletes are tested randomly. (J.A. 8, 
30-21; PILEx. 5 at 20). Once a week, the names of all 
athietes competing during the particular season are placed 
in a “pool.” (J.A. 30-31; PI.Ex. 5 at 20). A student 
draws from the “pool” the names of ten percent of the 
athletes, who are then tested. Two adult witnesses observe 
the selection. (J.A. 30-31). The student drawing the 
names is unaware of who is selected. Jd. The students

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The District’s drug testing policy (the “Policy”), re- 
quiring students who choose to participate in interscholas- 
tic athletics to submit to suspicionless urinalysis, does 
not violate the Fourth Amendment. The special na- 
ture of the school setting has led this Court to hold 
that certain constitutional rights must be reduced for 
students. See, e.g., Goss v. Lopez, 419 U.S. 565, 582 


15 


(1975) (due process); Ingraham v. Wright, 430 US. 
651, 681-682 (1977) (Eighth Amendment). In particu- 
lar, while students have Fourth Amendment rights in the 
school setting, those rights are diminished at least to the 
extent that schools may conduct reasonable searches of 
students without a warrant or probable cause. New Jersey 
v. T.L.0., 469 U.S. 325, 340-341 (1985). The issue 
presented in this case is whether drug testing of student 
athletes can be reasonable under the Fourth Amendment 
in the absence of individualized suspicion. 


In other cases the Court has found, as it did in the 
school setting, that neither a warrant nor probable cause 
is necessary. See, e.g., O'Connor v. Ortega, 480 U.S. 
709, 722, 725 (public employees); United States v. 
Martinez-Fuerte, 428 U.S. 543, 546 (1976) (border 
checkpoints). In some of those cases the Court has gone 
on to hold that searches were reasonable despite the ab- 
sence of individualized suspicion. See, e.g., Skinner v. 
Railway Labor Exec. Assn., 489 U.S. 602, 633 (1989); 
National Treasury Employees Union v. Von Raab, 489 
U.S. 656, 679 (1989); Martinez at 559-562; New York 
v. Burger, 482 U.S. 691, 700-701 (1987). In those cases, 
the Court has balanced: (1) the governmental interest 
in the search; (2) the intrusion on reasonable expecta- 
tions of privacy; (3) the control on government dis- 
cretion in conducting the search; and (4) the effective- 
ness of the search. See, e.g., Martinez, 428 U.S. at 559; 
Burger, 482 U.S. at 702. Because the school setting 
requires deference to the judgment cf school officials and 
protection of the educational environment, Ingraham vy. 
Wright, 430 U.S. 651, 681-682 (1977), this flexible 
standard should also be applied in the school setting and 
individualized suspicion should not be an essential ele- 
ment of reasonableness for a school search. 


Balancing these four factors in this case reveals that the 
Policy was reasonable and, therefore, constitutional. First, 
the District had a compelling interest in protecting the 


16 


safety of its student athletes and the educational atmos- 
phere of its schools from a pervasive drug problem. Be- 
fore implementing the Policy the District attempted to 
stem the drug problem with education and counseling but 
achieved no discernible reduction in the problem. Second, 
the Policy adequately restricted or eliminated governmen- 
tal discretion because it mandated truly random testing 
and followed an unvarying protocol. Third, the Policy 
was only a minimal intrusion on the student athletes’ rea- 
sonable expectations of privacy because students, and es- 
pecially athletes, are already subject to substantial regula- 
tion in the school environment, some of which—like the 
Policy—are reasonable and necessary safety regulations. 
Interscholastic sports involve substantial additional intru- 
sions on a student’s privacy. Finally, the decision to play 
sports is entirely voluntary—if a student does not want 
to be subject to the Policy he or she can opt not to play 
sports. 


The Policy was effective in achieving its purpose. In 
addition to detecting drug use, drug testing has deterred 
drug use and thus has met the District's objectives of 
reducing drug use by student athletes, and, through their 
example, the entire school population. After the Policy 
was implemented there was a substantial improvement in 
school order and discipline.

2. The District should not be subject to a “com-

Both the district court and court of appeals applied 
Skinner and Von Raab to this case and held that those 
cases require a “compelling interest” as a prerequisite to 
upholding a search in the absence of individwalized 
suspicion. 23 F.3d at 1826 (Pet. App. A at 58a); 796 
F.Supp. at 1360-1361 (Pet. App. B at 102a-103a). Any 
standard set in Skinner and Von Raab does not control 
this case because those cases were not decided in the 
school context. The unique nature of the school setting 
calls for “easing” of Fourth Amendment requirements, 
“flexibility” and “common sense” in applying those re- 
quirements, and reasonable deference to the judgment of 
school administrators. 7.L.0., 469 U.S. at 340, 342- 
343. The governmental purpose in the school setting, 
therefore, need not be as strong as in other settings. 


‘Moreover, Skinner and Von Raab do not establish a 
“compelling” governmental interest standard for suspicion- 
less searches. Although this Court found the interests of 
the employers in Skinner and Von Raab to be “compel- 
ling,” it did not state that a “compelling” interest was re- 
quired to justify a suspicionless search. 489 U.S. at 633; 
489 U.S. at 679. There are several references to lesser 
interests throughout those opinions. The Court referred 
to the requisite interest as “important,” * “substantial,” ” 
and “valid.” * 


Skinner and Von Raab, like the other suspicionless 
search cases, require a baiancing process. The central 
question is whether the governmental interest outweighs 
the privacy interest. The required level of governmental 
interest naturally varies with the extent of the privacy 
instrusion. Requiring a specific level of governmental

3. The District had a compelling interest in solving 
the drug problem in its schools. 


Even if this Court were to hold that Skinner and Von 
Raab govern in the school setting, and that those cases 
require a “compelling” governmental interest as a pre- 
requisite to a suspicionless school search, the District 
meets that standard here. The state has a “compelling 
interest” in educating and training its young people. 
T.L.O., 469 U.S. at 350 (Powell, J., concurring). “Edu- 
cation ‘is perhaps the most important function’ of govern- 
ment, Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 493, 
and the government has a heightened obligation to safe- 
guard students whom it compels to attend school.” 
T.L.O., 469 U.S. at 353 (Blackmun, J., concurring). 


In contrast to Von Raab, where there was no evidence 
of actual drug use, 489 U.S. at 660, there is substantial 
and convincing evidence in this record of a drug problem 
which threatened safety and discipline. The question will 
undoubtedly arise whether the problems of a small rural 
school district can begin to rival the evidence in Skinner 
of drug abuse on a national level by railroad personnel 
or the risk in Von Raab that our nation’s borders may be 
open to illegal drug trafficking. The answer is that drug 
abuse such as that the District sought to address is every 
bit as significant as the problems in Skinner and Von 
Raab. 


In Von Raab, this Court was influenced by the fact 
that: “The Customs Service is our nation’s first line of 
defense against one of the greatest problems affecting the 
health and welfare of our population.” 489 U.S. at 668. 
Certainly the next line of defense must be preventing drug 
use by children, when they are most vulnerable to the 


34 


physical and psychological effects of drugs. Indeed, it is 
difficult to make a qualitative distinction in terms of drug 
abuse prevention between the importance of keeping drugs 
out of this country and the importance of deterring our 
children from abusing drugs at a time when they are most 
vulnerable to addiction. (R. 43; Def.Ex. 106 at 30-32, 
42). 


The court of appeals interpreted “compelling” govern- 
mental interest as requiring the risk of “some additional 
horrible event, some terrible threat to society that can 
flow from the presence of drugs—some shooting, some 
explosion, some crash of train, truck or aircraft, or some 
breach of top secret national security.” 23 F.3d at 1526 
(Pet. App. A at 58a). This is far too narrow an inter- 
pretation. It incorrectly assumes that dramatic events 
which result in immediate physical injury are more serious 
than less tangible, but arguably far more significant, long- 
term injuries, such as those which result when schools 
fail in their educational mission. In fact, the drug problem 
in the Vernonia schools and drug use by chiidren generally 
is a “horrible event” which does pose a “terrible threat 
to society.” It has immediate effects on the students, 
teachers, parents and community involved. More impor- 
tantly, to the extent it inevitably interferes with the teach- 
ers’ ability to instill knowledge and the values essential to 
citizenship, it will have even more profound, long-term 
consequences on society. A myopic view of what is suffi- 
ciently “compelling” to justify drug testing which focuses 
on near-term, physical injuries ignores these crucial facts. 


Our public school officials are also responsible for the 
safety of their students. By requiring the threat of some 
serious physical injury caused by a catastrophic event, the 
circuit court’s holding could either prevent schools from 
demonstrating a “compelling interest” altogether, or, at 
least, prevent schools from dealing with serious threats 
to safety until a traumatic injury or death occurs. By 
then, of course, it is too late, and the school district is 


35 


potentially subject to liability for failure to take reason- 
able steps to prevent a known risk of harm. School offi- 
cials should be allowed to take reasonable and necessary 
steps to prevent serious injuries before they occur. 


The court of appeals’ narrow definition of “compelling” 
would make random drug testing—widely acknowledged 
to be an important tool in detecting and deterring drug 
use—available only to a select few. In Skinner, this Court 
relied on evidence that drug testing is sometimes the only 
effective means of detecting and deterring drug use. 489 
U.S. at 628 (J.A. 67). In many cases only those with 
the ability to drug test will be able to deal effectively with 
drug abuse. A holding that would make what is in some 
cases the only tool to deal with drug abuse available to 
prevent shootings and train wrecks but not available to 
solve a serious drug problem in a school would be wholly 
unreasonable. It would also be very difficult to square 
such a holding with this Court’s long-standing commit- 
ment to making it possible for teachers and administrators 
to maintain safety, discipline and order in the classroom.

relatively little experience with the drug may look quite 
impaired from a single dose, but their tolerance to the 
behavioral effects grows with more exposure to the drug

A requirement of individualized suspicion in this context 
would largely eliminate any hope of reliably identifying 
drug use by student athletes.

7. The Policy involved a minimal intrusion on the 
students’ privacy. 


An individual’s expectation of privacy varies according 
to several factors, including the context in which a search 
takes place, e.g., whether the individual is at home, at 
work, at school, in a car or in jail. Expectations of 
privacy are inherently diminished by a past history of 
significant governmental regulation, and the individual’s 
voluntary participation in the activity which gives rise 
to the search. The intrusion on the students’ privacy 
caused by the Policy was minimal. 


a. The school setting results in diminished expec- 
tations of privacy. 


James Acton’s expectations of privacy are diminished 
because he was a student in a middle school. While stu- 
dents certainly have constitutional rights, those rights are 
often reduced in the school setting. As Justice Powell 
noted in his concurrence in 7.L.O., “[i]t is simply un- 
realistic to think that students have the same subjective 


expectation of privacy as the population generally.” 469 
US. at 348. 


The process of educating young people is inherently 
invasive because of the unique relationship of students 
and teachers. Teachers do far more than instill factual 
knowledge. They are also responsible for discipline, safety, 
and the inculcation of values which are essential to citizen- 
ship, including respect for authority and the law. They 


40 


act as friend, authority figure, mentor, protector, moral 
leader, detective, prosecutor, judge and sometimes even 
jailer. “Of necessity, teachers have a degree of familiarity 
with, and authority over, their students that is unparalleled 
except perhaps in the relationship between parent and 
child.” 469 U.S. at 348 (Powell, J., concurring). 


A teacher simply cannot do his or her job without 
constantly and pervasively intruding into privacy interests 
into which an employer, for instance, would never con- 
sider intruding. “[T]he preservation of order and a proper 
educational environment requires close supervision of 
schoolchildren as well as the enforcement of rules against 
conduct that would be perfectly permissible if undertaken 
by an adult.” 7.L.0., 469 U.S. at 339. While many 
middle school students would enjoy adult-level privacy 
from intrusions by their teachers, no student realistically 
expects such privacy and no teacier could adequately 
educate students if such privacy were given.”

E. The District’s Drug Test Policy Satisfies the New 
Jersey v. T.L.0. “Reasonableness” Test Because It 
Was Reasonably Related in Scope to the Circum- 
stances Which Justified the Interference in the First 
Place. 


The second prong of the reasonableness test in New 
Jersey v. T.L.O. requires that the search be reasonably 
related in scope to the circumstances which justified the 
interference in the first place. This requires a determina- 
tion of whether “the measures adopted are reasonably 
related to the objectives of the search and not excessively 
intrusive in light of the age and sex of the student and 
the nature of the infraction.” 469 U.S. at 342 (footnote 
omitted). In this case the Policy was reasonably related 
to the goal of deterring drug use and preventing athletic 
injuries and was not excessively intrusive.

and thereby protect them from physical injury. (J.A. 7,
