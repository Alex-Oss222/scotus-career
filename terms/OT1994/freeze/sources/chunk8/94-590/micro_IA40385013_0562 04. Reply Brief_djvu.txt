Introduction 

The central issue presented to this 
Court is whether the Ninth Circuit erred 
in applying a "compelling interest" test 
to the Petitioner's drug-testing policy. 
This Court should grant certiorari be- 
cause there is a conflict between the 
Ninth Circuit's opinion and the Seventh 
Circuit, which applied a “substantial 
interest" test and upheld random drug 
testing under "very similar" circumstanc- 
es. 23 F.3d 1514, 1527 (Pet. App. at 
62a). Moreover, this Court should grant 
certiorari to resolve the unanswered 
question in New Jersey v. TLO, 469 U.S. 
325 (1985), concerning the appropriate 
standard for searches in a school setting 
in the absence of individualized suspi- 


cion. 


ss t 

Contrary to Respondents' contention, 
the Petition contains no misstatements of 
fact. As noted in the Petition, the 
facts were taken primarily from the find- 
ings of fact made by the district court. 
(Pet. at 4, n. 1). The specific facts 
Respondents contest are supported direct- 
ly by findings of that court or the Ninth 
Circuit. The Ninth Circuit expressly 
found no clear error in the district 
court's findings. 23 F.3d at 1519 (Pet. 
App. A at 20a). 

In NCAA v. Board of Regents of Uni- 
versity of Oklahoma, 468 U.S. 85 (1985), 
this Court held: 

The Court of Appeals rejected 

petitioner's position that it should 

set aside many of the District 

Court's findings as clearly errone- 

ous. In accord with our usual prac- 


tice, we must now accord great 
weight to a finding of fact which 


3 


has been made by a district court 
and approved by a court of appeals. 


468 U.S. at 98, n. 15. That is exactly 
what happened here. Respondents argued 
to the Ninth Circuit that the district 
court relied on inadmissible hearsay and 
that there was no evidence of an actual 
drug problem in the Vernonia Schools. 
The Ninth Circuit rejected those argu- 
ments. 23 F.3d at 1519 (Pet. App. A at 
18a-20a). In an attempt to muddy the wa- 
ters to avoid certiorari, Respondents 
once again raise questions about the 


facts which have long since been settled.' 


Respondents say it is "curious" 


that Petitioner relies on facts found in 
the district court opinion since that 
opinion was reversed. (Resp. at 4, n. 
1). That is irrelevant. The Ninth Cir- 
cuit reversed the district court's hold- 
ing on the law. It accepted the district 
court's findings of fact. See, United 
States v. O'Donnell, 303 U.S. 501, 508 
(1938) (although the circuit court re- 
versed the district court's holding, this 
Court refused to reexamine the evidence 


Risk of an Advisory Opinion Exists. 


Respondents claim that this case was 
decided solely on state law grounds 
(Resp. at 21) and that a decision by this 
Court might be an advisory opinion (Resp. 
at 22). Although stated as separate ar- 
guments, Respondents' positions are based 
on the assertion that the Ninth Circuit's 
decision rests solely on Article I, Sec- 
tion 9 of the Oregon Constitution. 
Respondent's arguments in this regard are 
flawed in two respects. 

First, the Ninth Circuit's conclu- 
sion that the Policy violates the Oregon 
Constitution is premised exclusively on 
its conclusion that it violates the 


Fourth Amendment. The majority specifi- 


where the two lower courts made concur- 
rent findings). 


5 
cally stated that: “in the specific area 
of urine testing - the core issue in this 
case - we will unabashedly refer to fed- 
eral law and decide this case based on 
the Fourth Amendment." 23 F.3d at 1519 
(Pet. App. A at 17a-18a). Likewise, 
Judge Reinhardt noted that "there is no 
way to ‘avoid' a federal constitutional 
holding in this case" and that "our deci- 
sion is based almost exclusively on the 
application of federal law." 23 F.3d at 
1527 (Pet. App. A at 66a). In fact, Judge 
Reinhardt went so far as to say: "Any 
attempt to limit our decision to Article 
I, Section 9 would be sheer flim- 
flammery". 23 F.3d at 1519 (Pet. App. A 
at 69a). Thus, this case raises an inm- 
portant federal constitutional question 
which is appropriate for this Court to 


review. 


6 

Second, an opinion by this Court 
would not be an impermissible advisory 
opinion because, to the extent the Ninth 
Circuit's decision is based on the Oregon 
Constitution, that decision was dictated 
by the court's interpretation of the 
Fourth Amendment. Thus, an opinion by 
this Court that the Ninth Circuit erred 
in interpreting the Fourth Amendment 
would render the Ninth Circuit's judgment 
invalid and, hence, would not constitute 
an advisory opinion. 

Respondents maintain that such an 
opinion would nonetheless be advisory by 
asserting the purely speculative argument 
that the Oregon Constitution theoretical- 
ly could later be interpreted differently 
from the federal constitution. (Resp. at 
23-24). This Court, however, rejected 


such an argument in the analogous case of 


7 
Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983). 
In granting review in Long this Court 
stated: 


Even if we accept that the Michigan 
Constitution has been interpreted to 
provide independent protection for 
certain rights also secured under 
the Fourth Amendment, it fairly ap- 
pears in this case that the Michigan 
Supreme Court rested its decision 
primarily on federal law. 


463 U.S. at 1044. 

Similarly, in Zacchini v. Scripps- 
Howard Broadcasting Co., 433 U.S. 562 
(1977), this Court held: 


Even if the judgment in favor of 
respondent must nevertheless be un- 
derstood as ultimately resting on 
[state] law, it appears that at the 
very least the [state] court felt 
compelled by what it understood to 
be federal constitutional consider- 
ations to construe and apply its own 
law in the manner it did. In this 
event, we have jurisdiction and 
should decide the federal issue; for 
if the state court erred in its un- 
derstanding of our cases, and of the 
First and Fourteenth Amendments, we 
should so declare, leaving the state 
court free to decide the privilege 
issue solely as a matter of [state] 


8 
law. ... If the [state supreme 
court] ‘held as it did because it 
felt under compulsion of federal law 
as enunciated by this Court so to 
hold, it should be relieved of that 
compulsion. It should be freed to 
decide . .. these suits according 
to its own local law.’ 
433 U.S. at 568 (citations omitted). 
Thus, this Court has held in 
Long, Zacchini and Delaware v. Prouse, 
440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979), that review 
should be granted to decide federal is- 
sues, even where a question exists as to 
whether a lower court decision rested 
exclusively on federal grounds, and even 
where a different state constitutional 
ground might later be asserted. In the 
instant case, no question exists that the 
Ninth Circuit's decision at a minimum 
“rested . . . primarily on federal law." 
Long, 462 U.S. at 1044. Indeed, Respon- 


dents concede this in asserting that 


"(tjhe Court of Appeals concluded that 


9 

the District's drug testing policy vio- 
lates the Fourth Amendment and, there- 
fore, that it must also violate Article 
I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitu- 
tion... ." (Resp. at 22-23). (Empha- 
Sis added). There is no question that 
this Court has jurisdiction and should 


grand review. 


3. An Important Conflict Exists Among 


Respondents claim there is no con- 
flict among the circuits on the issue 
presented for review. (Resp. at 28). As 
the Ninth Circuit found, there is a clear 
conflict between the Seventh's decision 
in Schaill by Kross v. Tippecanoe County 
Sch. Corp., 864 F.2d 1309 (7th Cir. 
1988) reh'g denied (1989) and its own 
decision. 23 F.3d at 1527 (Pet. App. A 


at 62a-63a). Respondents nonetheless ar- 


10 
gue that Skinner v. Railway Labor 
Executives' Ass'n., 489 U.S. 602 (1989) 
and National Treasury Employees Union v. 
Von Raab, 489 U.S. 656 (1989) essentially 
overrule the Seventh Circuit's decision 
in Schaill. Respondents argue: 
There is no reason to believe that 
the Schaill court, writing today, 
post-Skinner and -Von Raab, and ap- 
plying a compelling-interest test 
rather than a substantial-interest 
test, would reach a different con- 
Clusion. (Resp. at 35). 
To the contrary, there is no reason to 
believe that the Schaill court, writing 
today, would apply a compelling-interest 
test rather than a substantial-interest 
test in the school setting. Skinner and 
Von Raab do not address the constitution- 
al standard applicable to random drug 
testing in the school setting. Skinner 


and Von Raab only establish the standard 


for such tests in the workplace. Fur- 


11 
ther, Skinner and Von Raab cannot be read 
to overrule New Jersey v. TLO, which held 
that the school setting requires an "eas- 
ing" of Fourth Amendment restrictions and 
a “degree of flexibility" in the school 
setting. 469 U.S. at 339-340. 

Thus, it is not surprising that the 
Seventh Circuit has cited Schaill since 
this Court's holdings in Skinner and Von 
Raab without casting any doubt on its va- 
lidity. In fact, the Seventh Circuit, 
sitting en banc, said Schaill was based 
"on the idea that the Fourth Amendment 
applies with diminished force in the 
schools." Dimeo v. Griffin, 943 F.2d 
679, 685 (7th Cir. 1991) (Pet at 26, n. 
12). The Seventh Circuit thereby recog- 
nized that a lessor standard applies in 
schools than in the workplace context of 


Skinner and Von Raab. 


Respondents incorrectly assume that 
the issues raised by the Petition would 
only have bearing on random drug testing 
in schools. (Resp. at 37). As noted in 
the Petition, Petitioner is asking this 
Court to decide the constitutional param- 
eters of suspicionless searches of any 
type in the school setting. (Pet. at 
28). This Court expressly left this is- 
sue unanswered in TLO and should now de- 


cide it.’

Respondents concede that school 
children can be searched without individ- 
ualized suspicion of wrongdoing in light 
of Skinner and Von Raab. (Resp. at 44). 
However, Respondents incorrectly assert 
that courts should apply a compelling 
interest test to random searches in pub- 
lic schools. (Resp. at 43-44). As noted 
above in Section 3, neither Skinner nor 
Von Raab established standards for 
searches in schools. (Supra. at 10). 
Further, they do not discuss or elucidate 
the effect of the TLO holding that Fourth

the school setting. In the school set- 
ting, TLO clearly requires some standard 
less than the compelling interest stan-
