## Other governing law

### Issue 1: Religious Freedom Restoration Act

- **Other Governing Law:** Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993, Pub. L. 103-141, §§ 3, 5, 6, 107 Stat. 1488-89. Government may not substantially burden a person’s exercise of religion even through a rule of general applicability unless government demonstrates that application of the burden to that person furthers a compelling governmental interest and is the least restrictive means of furthering that interest. “Demonstrates” assigns government the burdens of going forward and persuasion. The Act applies to state and local government and to implementation of laws adopted before or after its enactment, and authorizes appropriate judicial relief.
- **Other Authority Used:** Landgraf v. ### RFRA enacted text and chronology facts

RFRA §3(a) forbids government substantially burdening a person's religious exercise even through a generally applicable rule, subject to §3(b). Under §3(b), government may impose the burden only if it demonstrates that application **to the person** furthers a compelling governmental interest and is the least restrictive means. Section 5(3) defines demonstrates as both producing evidence and persuasion. Section 3(c) permits a claim or defense and appropriate relief, with ordinary Article III standing. Section 5(1) includes State subdivisions. Section 5(4)'s original definition is exercise under the First Amendment; do not import later amendments.

Section 6(a) applies to all federal/state law and its implementation, statutory or otherwise, adopted before or after enactment. Section 6(b) requires later federal statutes to exclude RFRA expressly by reference if that is intended; §6(c) does not authorize burdens on belief. Section 7 leaves the Establishment Clause unaffected and says permissible grants of funding/benefits/exemptions do not violate RFRA, with denial excluded from the specified meaning of granting. ## Other governing law

### Issue 1: Merits

- **Other Governing Law:** U.S. Const. amend. XIV; McKesson Corp. v. Division of Alcoholic Beverages and Tobacco; Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation.
- **Other Authority Used:** McKesson requires a clear and certain remedy; Harper makes the governing federal rule applicable to cases still open on direct review.

### Validation additions controlling the neutral framing

The state judgment and State's characterization of its prepayment procedures are disputed legal premises, not facts this Court must adopt. The 1993 formal judgment affirmed in part and reversed in part; the operative issue here is denial of backward-looking relief for Reich's 1985–1988 eligible-year claim, not a new decision on the conceded substantive tax defect.

The statute expressly promises refunds of any and all taxes or fees determined erroneously or illegally assessed and collected, whether voluntarily or involuntarily paid. The state court's exclusion of taxes assessed under a law later invalidated and its demand-at-payment/return rule are later judicial constructions, not limitations quoted in the statute. The separate question is whether the State's available procedure and the apparent refund route supplied constitutionally adequate notice and opportunity; no finding of taxpayer bad faith or contemporaneous knowledge is made.

Reich's supported path invokes McKesson's clear-and-certain remedial requirement and the statutory refund language against withdrawal after payment. Georgia's strongest path invokes an adequate prepayment opportunity as sufficient due process and the state court's authority to construe its own statute. Its claimed APA, declaratory and appeal alternatives must be tested with actual bond, stay and constitutional-adjudication limits, rather than the word ample. The source records those disputes and supports a legal ruling/remand; it does not establish that an administrative officer could decide constitutionality or that a permissive stay was automatic.

Harper's June 18, 1993 direct-review rule makes the applicable constitutional rule operative; its separate remedy holding preserves lawful refunds, actual nondiscriminatory retroactive assessments or a combination when constitutionally sufficient. Barker does not fix the refund amount. A reversal may require a clear and certain lawful backward-looking remedy without granting an uncalculated refund or reopening final cases; affirmance requires a supported determination that the available procedures satisfy the constitutional obligation notwithstanding the refund language and its timing. Any terminal decision depending on an unverified limitation, protest requirement or prepayment-stay mechanism requires the exact historical code and relevant state constructions.

The 1992 state opinion quoted §48-2-35(a): “A taxpayer shall be refunded any and all taxes or fees which are determined to have been erroneously or illegally assessed and collected from him under the laws of this state, whether paid voluntarily or involuntarily.” It construed the statute to cover erroneous assessment under a valid law, excluding a law later held invalid, and announced a demand-at-payment-or-return rule. Those are **state judicial constructions**, distinct from the quoted enactment. The 1993 opinion retained denial on its conclusion that prepayment review was ample. Its formal disposition was affirmed in part and reversed in part; do not flatten that into an unqualified affirmance of every trial component.

The state majority identified declaratory/injunctive relief, APA hearing under §§50-13-12/19/20 and direct superior-court appeal under §48-2-59. It noted that Reich had not appealed dismissal of his own declaratory action. Carley's dissent, joined by Sears-Collins, disputed constitutional adequacy: §48-2-59(c)'s security condition; inability of administrative officials to adjudicate constitutionality; §50-13-12(c)'s **may** suspend/postpone wording; and absence of automatic collection stays in general declaratory proceedings.
