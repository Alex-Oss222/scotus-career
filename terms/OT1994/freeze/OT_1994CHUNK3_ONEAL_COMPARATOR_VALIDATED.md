## Historical disposition and opinion structure

The Sixth Circuit judgment, O'Neal v. Morris, 3 F.3d 143, was vacated and remanded, 6–3. Breyer wrote the Court opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Thomas dissented, joined by Rehnquist and Scalia. There was no separate concurrence. Historical authorship does not determine an assignment in this timeline. ## Historical premises and rule

The historical Court treated Brecht as having adopted Kotteakos's substantial-and-injurious-effect standard for habeas constitutional trial error. It held that a reviewing judge left in grave doubt after examining the record must treat the error as harmful. Grave doubt meant virtual equipoise about the error's effect. The Court deliberately framed the inquiry as judicial evaluation of the completed record rather than a burden governing evidence presentation.

The majority relied on Kotteakos's complete formulation, Chapman's treatment of constitutional error, Stevens's historical Brecht concurrence, the risk of unconstitutional custody, and administrative continuity. It rejected the inference that the civil form of habeas or §2254(a) required the prisoner to bear the risk of equipoise. It did not find that the Sixth Circuit was actually in grave doubt, finally adjudicate the assumed instructional violation, or itself conduct the ultimate harmlessness application. The remand required further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

Thomas would require the prisoner to establish a harmful constitutional or federal violation as a cause of custody under §§2241(c)(3) and 2254(a). He distinguished direct review and the harmless-error statute/rules from collateral review, invoked finality and comity, and understood historical Brecht's actual-prejudice language to put the risk of nonpersuasion on the prisoner. He also observed that the Sixth Circuit had not expressly declared equipoise. Scalia joined that dissent. The dissent did not deny the government's direct-review harmlessness burden or claim that innocence was the substantive harmlessness test.
