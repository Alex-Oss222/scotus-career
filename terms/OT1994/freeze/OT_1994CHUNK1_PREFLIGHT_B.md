## 5. Church of Scientology Flag Service Organization v. Clearwater

### Source identity and lower judgment

- [Eleventh Circuit, 2 F.3d 1514, September 30, 1993](https://static.case.law/f3d/2/html/1514-01.html), No. 91-3988, Dubina for Anderson, Dubina and Clark; local `clearwater.html/.txt`.
- [District court, 756 F.Supp.1498 (1991)](https://static.case.law/f-supp/756/html/1498-01.html); local `clearwater_district.html/.txt`.
- [RFRA, Pub. L.103-141, 107 Stat.1488–1490, approved November 16, 1993](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-107/pdf/STATUTE-107-Pg1488.pdf); local `rfra.pdf/.txt`.

The appellate judgment affirmed limited identification disclosures and contemporaneous written disclosure of a voluntarily offered refund policy; reversed the disclosure/recordkeeping provisions and mandatory sixty-day refund provision as applied to churches; reversed summary judgment for the City on discriminatory purpose and vacated denial of the Church's summary-judgment motion on that issue; remanded for purpose and, conditionally, severability proceedings. The court did not finally find the City's predominant purpose. It evaluated conflicting evidence under the summary-judgment standard, including public officials' statements, hearing arrangements and changes requested by other denominations. Bona fide religious status was established for this litigation by an unappealed district ruling. The Court need not conduct a truth-of-religion inquiry to restate that procedural premise.

The Eleventh Circuit's rationale included Establishment Clause entanglement and an independently articulated strict-scrutiny analysis. Its conception of which laws fall outside Smith was part of its reasoning, not a new Supreme Court holding. Its §1983/standing discussion is distinct from merits. The district court relied on neutral anti-fraud regulation, the private-statement option, continued solicitation pending review, court enforcement of subpoenas, and earlier disclosure regimes sustained in tax/labor cases.

### Ordinance details that must remain distinct

The challenged enactment is Ordinance 3479-84, not its repealed 1983 predecessor. The text quotations below come from judicial reproductions of that enactment. Neither a current municipal code nor a complete original ordinance image was obtained.

1. Section 100.01(2) includes requests for donations and sales with an express or implied representation that proceeds will serve a charitable purpose; it expressly excludes an offer of membership. A solicitation is complete when communicated to a person then within the city. Do not replace this with a finding that every sale necessarily has that representation.
2. Under §100.02(3), information concerning member solicitations **may** be omitted under conditions. The Eleventh Circuit construed the permission as covering §100.03(1)(d)–(i), not the entire registration requirement. The organization must identify the use of the option, prepare the annual sworn private statement of omitted information, retain necessary underlying records for three years, and make the statement/records reasonably available to every member. These are conditions on the option, not a requirement that the organization select it. The district opinion's quoted §100.02(3) should be used to verify the complete terms before drafting any comprehensive ordinance account.
3. Public registration information includes organizational identity/form, tax-exemption determinations, purposes/use, people controlling solicitation/disbursement, periods/methods, estimated expenses, other Florida cities, criminal-history information and explanations for unavailable data. The appellate prose has inconsistent subsection citations for criminal histories: the initial quotation identifies **(m)**, while later shorthand lists omit it or mislabel it. Do not silently resolve the letter inconsistency by inventing text. Its operative holding names **(a), (b), (c), (j)** as surviving identification disclosures; match any mandate to actual text and the preserved question.
4. Section 100.03(8) adds retrospective sums collected, expenses, bank placement and actual/proposed use. Section 100.04 concerns retained supporting records; §100.05 separately attaches criminal consequences. The private-statement option does not eliminate all recordkeeping.
5. Upon registration denial solicitation can continue while the clerk pursues judicial review. Section 100.03(4) permits the court, upon concluding disclosure creates a unique or special hardship, to dispense with the specific information/explanation. It is not a mandatory exemption. The appellate court expressly did not decide a challenge to this hardship discretion or to the procedural safeguards because those challenges were not made.
6. Section 100.05(1)(f), as quoted at 2 F.3d1523: “promising any person that the proceeds of a solicitation of funds will be refunded upon request, and thereafter wilfully failing within 60 days to make a refund that has been requested in writing.” The 1984 amendment removed the earlier reasonable-time limit for requesting a refund. It does not compel adoption of a refund promise in the first instance. One later appellate discussion mislabels this provision; use **(f)**.
7. Section 100.05(1)(g), quoted by both courts: “promising any person that refunds of the proceeds of the solicitation of funds will be made upon request without providing such person, at the time such representation is made, with a written statement of the terms and conditions upon which refunds are made; provided, however, that any statement made in good faith at the time is not prohibited by this section.” The good-faith proviso is part of this provision. The provision supplies no power to dictate a refund policy's substance. The appellate court relied on evidence of undisclosed procedural obstacles and waivers in upholding this disclosure, while reserving circumstances in which a civil court could construe or enforce church policy.
8. Section 100.06(1), district quotation at 756 F.Supp.1530: the city attorney **shall investigate** alleged violations **only after ten individuals file separate bona fide written, sworn or affirmed complaints**, setting out facts demonstrating one or more §100.05 prohibited acts and injury to each complaining party from the act(s). Subsection (2) gives subpoena, document-production and oath powers in such an investigation. Subsection (3) says the attorney **shall institute** prosecution if the investigation demonstrates probable cause. The appellate court's suggestion that bona-fides screening retains discretion is a judicial characterization, not replacement statutory language. The City's proposition that subpoenas are not self-executing is a legal argument accepted below; it is not quoted as a separate ordinance clause.
9. The appellate court expressly did not review §100.05(1)(c)'s general anti-fraud prohibition. Other unaddressed provisions remained subject to purpose and severability. All offenses were described as carrying up to $1,000 and/or six months. The whole ordinance's purpose and severability remained for remand.

### RFRA enacted text and chronology facts

RFRA §3(a) forbids government substantially burdening a person's religious exercise even through a generally applicable rule, subject to §3(b). Under §3(b), government may impose the burden only if it demonstrates that application **to the person** furthers a compelling governmental interest and is the least restrictive means. Section 5(3) defines demonstrates as both producing evidence and persuasion. Section 3(c) permits a claim or defense and appropriate relief, with ordinary Article III standing. Section 5(1) includes State subdivisions. Section 5(4)'s original definition is exercise under the First Amendment; do not import later amendments.

Section 6(a) applies to all federal/state law and its implementation, statutory or otherwise, adopted before or after enactment. Section 6(b) requires later federal statutes to exclude RFRA expressly by reference if that is intended; §6(c) does not authorize burdens on belief. Section 7 leaves the Establishment Clause unaffected and says permissible grants of funding/benefits/exemptions do not violate RFRA, with denial excluded from the specified meaning of granting. No delayed effective-date provision appears. These are already-effective statutory inputs expressly identified by the supplied prospective-relief claim. The law is not a new OT1994 enactment. The tracker search reported by control found no RFRA entry; that absence alone does not establish that an earlier judgment changed or needs correction. Kiryas Joel postdates enactment but concerns Establishment Clause questions expressly left unaffected by §7. No previous adjudication is reassessed here.

## 6. Federal Election Commission v. NRA Political Victory Fund

The district court imposed a civil penalty equal to investigation/prosecution costs and enjoined similar transfers. ## 7. Reich v. Collins

[Georgia Supreme Court, 263 Ga.602, 437 S.E.2d320, December 2, 1993](https://static.case.law/ga/263/html/0602-01.html), local `reich.html/.txt`; [262 Ga.625, 422 S.E.2d846, November 19, 1992, reconsideration denied December 17](https://static.case.law/ga/262/html/0625-01.html), local `reich_1992.html/.txt`. The earlier procedural Supreme Court history in the raw 1993 source is omitted from this handoff; use the supplied established merits posture.

Reich is a retired Army colonel. The State taxed his federal military retirement while exempting state retirement, then repealed the exemption following Davis. He sought refund under OCGA §48-2-35; the Department denied it. The trial court accepted Davis's substantive application but denied retroactive relief. The State did not appeal the substantive constitutional ruling. Reich conceded eligible tax years would be 1985–1988 if a refund were due.

The 1992 state opinion quoted §48-2-35(a): “A taxpayer shall be refunded any and all taxes or fees which are determined to have been erroneously or illegally assessed and collected from him under the laws of this state, whether paid voluntarily or involuntarily.” It construed the statute to cover erroneous assessment under a valid law, excluding a law later held invalid, and announced a demand-at-payment-or-return rule. Those are **state judicial constructions**, distinct from the quoted enactment. The 1993 opinion retained denial on its conclusion that prepayment review was ample. Its formal disposition was affirmed in part and reversed in part; do not flatten that into an unqualified affirmance of every trial component.

The state majority identified declaratory/injunctive relief, APA hearing under §§50-13-12/19/20 and direct superior-court appeal under §48-2-59. It noted that Reich had not appealed dismissal of his own declaratory action. Carley's dissent, joined by Sears-Collins, disputed constitutional adequacy: §48-2-59(c)'s security condition; inability of administrative officials to adjudicate constitutionality; §50-13-12(c)'s **may** suspend/postpone wording; and absence of automatic collection stays in general declaratory proceedings. Whether these remedies were meaningful is disputed judicial analysis, not an enacted finding.

[McKesson, 496 U.S.18 (1990)](https://static.case.law/us/496/html/0018-01.html), local `mckesson.html/.txt`, Brennan for a unanimous Court, thus joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. Relevant text at 36–45 and n.21: adequate prepayment review can suffice; when coercive collection requires pay-first proceedings, a clear/certain postpayment remedy is required; a discriminatory tax may be cured by appropriate refund, actual lawful retroactive assessments or combination, not a merely hypothetical nondiscriminatory scheme. Prospective protest/limitation conditions may protect fiscal planning. This opinion does not decide Georgia's particular statute.

Actual simulated Harper, June 18, 1993: Standards “Federal rules applied to parties govern still-open direct review” is based on Thomas's six-Justice proposition; “Backward-looking relief for unconstitutional taxes” on the broader eight-Justice remedial portion. Public `OT_1992CHUNK9.md` preserves Kennedy's Davis-specific concurrence, Scalia's judicial-declaration rationale and O'Connor's prospectivity dissent. Do not convert their disagreement over the general retroactivity rule into disagreement on every distinct remedy question. Simulated Barker preserves refund questions for further proceedings and provides no automatic fixed refund.

## 8. Brown v. Gardner

[Federal Circuit, 5 F.3d1456, September 13, 1993](https://static.case.law/f3d/5/html/1456-01.html), No.92-7025, Archer for Archer/Michel/Lourie, local `gardner.html/.txt`. It affirmed the Veterans Court's invalidation of §3.358(c)(3) and remand for eligibility redetermination. The underlying medical causation remains Gardner's claim, not an appellate factual finding or an award.

[38 U.S.C.§1151, 1994 edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title38/pdf/USCODE-1994-title38-partII-chap11-subchapVI-sec1151.pdf), local `38usc1151.pdf/.txt`, was last amended/renumbered in 1991 in the source notes. It requires additional disability/death from injury/aggravation resulting from listed VA hospitalization, medical/surgical treatment, chapter31 vocational rehabilitation or examination, not the veteran's own willful misconduct, compensated as if service-connected. Its second sentence separately coordinates benefits with a qualifying FTCA judgment/settlement/compromise through withholding until the stated aggregate equals the recovery. There is no asserted FTCA payment in this case; do not turn the coordination provision into a fault element or omit it from a purported complete statutory quotation.

The Federal Circuit reproduces the challenged 1992 regulation: compensation not payable for contemplated or foreseeable after-results of approved care properly administered, however remote, absent additional disability/death proximately resulting from carelessness, negligence, lack of proper skill, error in judgment or similar fault; an exception permits compensation for an accident, defined as an unforeseen, untoward event, causing additional disability/death proximately resulting from hospitalization or care. Separate §3.358(c)(1) requires actual causal connection, not coincidence; §3.358(b)(2) concerns continuance/natural progress. The lower court's mandate did not adjudicate Gardner's ultimate causal entitlement.

The Secretary's arguments preserved in the full opinion: undefined causal language gives interpretive space; legislative hearings discussed negligent treatment/accidents; long administration and 1934 reenactment support the regulation; no-fault compensation costs more. The Federal Circuit's grounds: causation and fault are distinct; Congress expressly addressed claimant willful misconduct without adding provider fault; legislative statements also encompassed unsuccessful properly performed procedures; definitive administrative fault requirement appeared after reenactment; judicial review was largely unavailable before 1988; agency regulations implement rather than amend statutes. These are source descriptions, not adopted Supreme Court conclusions.
