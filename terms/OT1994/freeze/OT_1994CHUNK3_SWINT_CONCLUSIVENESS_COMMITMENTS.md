## Two paths and minimum shared conclusion

The strongest argument for treating the order as conclusive is that summary judgment and reconsideration were denied, the county remained a defendant, and identifying a policymaker is a legal question that could be reviewed immediately without waiting for jury findings. On that description, the immediate consequence of having to continue litigating is fixed despite the possibility that interlocutory rulings can generally be revised.

The stronger application of the mandatory condition distinguishes an actual express reservation from the abstract revisability of every interlocutory order. This judge expressly characterized the sheriff's status as unresolved, invited further persuasion on both sides, and promised a later legal ruling before the jury received the case. The county seeks review of that very attribution question. A conclusive denial of the pending motions does not conclusively determine a question the same order expressly leaves for later decision. Calling the question legal identifies who will decide it, not whether it has already been decided. Continuing litigation burdens cannot substitute for a missing cumulative requirement.

The minimum rule all eight associates support is therefore: **where the district court expressly leaves the disputed county policymaker issue open for further presentation and a later legal ruling before submission to the jury, its summary-judgment and reconsideration orders do not conclusively determine that issue for Cohen review. That failure independently defeats collateral-order jurisdiction over the county's challenge to that issue.** This is not a categorical rule that every denial of summary judgment lacks conclusiveness, nor a ruling on all orders containing tentative language. A genuinely conclusive ruling would require its own assessment of the remaining collateral-order requirements; satisfying this one condition would not itself establish appealability.
