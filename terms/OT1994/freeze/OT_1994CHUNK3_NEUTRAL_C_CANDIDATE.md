## 33. Mastrobuono v. Shearson Lehman Hutton, Inc., No. 94-18

### Event, record and lower judgment

Scheduled merits event March 6, 1995; argument January 10, 1995 under the supplied header. Review concerns whether the general New York governing-law term, read with the arbitration term, excludes punitive damages. Seventh Circuit judgment: 20 F.3d 713 (March 30, 1994), No. 93-1581, affirming the Northern District of Illinois's partial vacatur, 812 F. Supp. 845 (1993). The lower docket is not the Supreme Court docket.

The Illinois customers opened their brokerage account in October 1985. Shearson's Houston representative serviced it; Shearson's principal place of business was New York. The federal action alleged federal securities violations and state statutory and tort claims arising from unauthorized trading, churning and margin exposure. The arbitration hearings occurred in Chicago on August 11–12 and September 29, 1992. The arbitrators awarded $115,274 commissions and $44,053 margin interest, totaling $159,327 compensatory damages, plus $400,000 punitive damages. The compensatory portion was paid and is not the contested award. The district court vacated the punitive portion under FAA §10(a)(4), denied confirmation and denied the customers' alternative requests for a trial on punitive amount or punitive claims. No new misconduct finding or allocation of the punitive award among substantive claims is established here.

Preservation is not blank. Shearson raised lack of punitive authority on the last hearing day after proofs closed; the panel accepted the memorandum and allowed a response. The Seventh Circuit rejected the customers' waiver objection because they had an opportunity to respond and showed no prejudice. It also held their distinct argument that a predispute punitive waiver violates Texas and Illinois public policy forfeited because it had not been raised in district court. Neither point is silently converted into a new Supreme Court question. The court rejected the attempted use of New York conflicts rules to send the case back to Illinois and found the headquarters connection sufficient under Illinois conflicts rules. The admitted question is contract/arbitral scope; a disposition depending on independently overturning a forfeiture or conflicts holding requires its own reachability showing.

### Operative contract and remedial provisions

Paragraph 13, reproduced at petition appendix 44, provides that the agreement binds successors, heirs, executors, administrators and assigns and is governed by New York law. It then provides:

> Unless unenforceable due to federal or state law, any controversy arising out of or relating to [the petitioners'] accounts, to transactions with you, your officers, directors, agents and/or employees for me or to this agreement or the breach thereof, shall be settled by arbitration in accordance with the rules then in effect, of the National Association of Securities Dealers, Inc. or the Boards of Directors of the New York Stock Exchange, Inc. and/or the American Stock Exchange Inc. as I may elect.

If the customer does not elect by registered mail to Shearson's main office within five days after demand, Shearson may elect. Judgment on an award may be entered in a court with jurisdiction. The agreement excludes future federal-securities disputes only to the extent the law determines that the customer cannot be compelled to arbitrate them. This is an operative qualification, not a general exclusion of all securities claims. No clause expressly naming punitive damages appears in paragraph 13.

FAA §2 gives covered written maritime or commerce arbitration provisions and written submissions validity, irrevocability and enforceability, subject to grounds at law or equity for revoking any contract. Section 4 requires arbitration in the manner the agreement provides, not arbitration beyond agreement. Section 10(a)(4) permits vacatur where arbitrators exceeded their powers or so imperfectly executed them that no mutual, final and definite award on the submitted matter was made. The lower judgment rests on the first alternative; ordinary legal error alone is not its stated ground. Section 9's confirmation procedure and §10 review do not create a freestanding entitlement to punitive recovery for every underlying claim.

The lower opinion reproduces NASD Code §41(e): the award must contain a summary of the damages and other relief awarded. Its Arbitrator's Manual states that arbitrators can consider punitive damages as a remedy. NASD Rules of Fair Practice §21(f)(4) forbids a condition limiting arbitrators' ability to make an award. Distinguish the Code's required award contents from a substantive grant of every remedy, and the explanatory Manual from the contractually incorporated rules. The relevant lower opinion and filings support those provisions; a general NASD rulemaking challenge is not presented. Garrity v. Lyle Stuart, Inc., 40 N.Y.2d 354, 353 N.E.2d 793 (1976), reserves punitive sanctions to courts as a New York rule even where private arbitrators otherwise decide the dispute. New York substantive law's allowance of court-awarded punitive damages and its allocation of punitive authority to courts are different propositions.

### Entering law and supported paths

Allied-Bruce, effective January 18, 1995, retains §2's substantive state-court reach and applies actual interstate commerce rather than parties' contemplation. It preserves generally applicable defenses, assent, scope and actual contractual choices of state arbitration rules. Its Court opinion is Breyer's, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg as well as Stone-Zsela. Scalia and Thomas's published opposition concerned the FAA's command to state courts. This matter arose in federal district court, so that objection does not automatically decide it. No current commerce controversy is established by the lower record. The current Wolens contract holding also preserves actual voluntary undertakings while forbidding an external-policy enlargement; its different airline-preemption provision supplies analogy, not an arbitration holding.

Volt, 489 U.S. 468 (1989), enforces an actual contractual selection of state arbitration rules and does not create arbitration beyond consent. Its majority also preserves the federal instruction to resolve scope ambiguities in favor of arbitration; it accepted the state court's construction of the particular contract before it. Stevens, Scalia and Kennedy joined that majority; O'Connor did not participate. No join is invented for Justices not then seated. Mitsubishi, 473 U.S. 614, supplies generous construction of consent and the distinction between agreeing to an arbitral forum and surrendering substantive rights; its international antitrust setting does not by itself settle this contract.

The customers' supported routes are (a) read the whole agreement, broad controversy clause and NASD selection as encompassing punitive relief, with general state governing law supplying substantive liabilities rather than a special arbitral disability; or (b) if the choice term imports Garrity as an externally imposed arbitral restriction rather than genuine contractual choice, confront FAA preemption. Their fallback request for a court trial must confront the all-controversies arbitration promise, the lack of a separate punitive-only cause of action below, and whether any substantive claim remains open. Their stronger rule is not an automatic punitive award in every arbitration: agreement and the underlying substantive basis still matter.

Shearson's supported route reads general New York law as a condition on all arbitration, with the institutional rules procedural and subordinate. Volt then protects the bargain and §10(a)(4) permits vacatur. Its strongest answer to the federal-policy argument is that a policy enforcing agreements cannot rewrite the selected remedial restrictions; its vulnerability is whether a general governing-law clause really selects the forum-specific Garrity rule in light of the rest of the agreement. The Seventh Circuit treated punitive-remedy availability as distinct from arbitrable-issue scope and found the same limit regardless of which permitted institution the customers selected.

A contract construction allowing the award can remove the particular §10(a)(4) ground and permit appropriate confirmation proceedings without deciding broad preemption. A construction excluding it can sustain partial vacatur; rejection of the alternative trial requires the actual procedural and claim boundaries rather than an invented release of all tort rights. No path reopens the paid compensatory award or decides due-process excessiveness of the punitive amount on this packet.

## 34. Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen, No. 93-1935

### Event and corrected lower posture

Scheduled March 6, 1995; argument January 17, 1995 under the supplied header. The lower report is Schoonejongen v. Curtiss-Wright Corp., 18 F.3d 1034. Its heading gives March 3, 1994 and denial of rehearing March 14, 1994; the opinion explains that the earlier December 28, 1993 opinion was vacated and a revised panel opinion issued. Do not describe March 3 as merely an amendment to a later original decision.

CW's Wood-Ridge plant closed in late 1983. The class consists of non-UAW retirees whose medical benefits were terminated or denied around December 1, 1983. CW sponsored, administered and funded the welfare plan. The 1976 written Constitution reserved amendment and termination powers. A 1983 SPD said coverage for retirees and dependents would cease when the facility from which they retired ceased operations. Distribution of SPDs to retirees was disputed. The district court after a bench trial found a reserved power to amend, found the plant-closure clause a new term rather than a clarification, invalidated it for failure of an amendment procedure, and entered benefits relief exceeding $2 million. It rejected vested lifetime benefits. The Third Circuit affirmed both the company's appeal and retirees' cross-appeal.

The panel majority distinguished a power reservation from a procedure identifying who may exercise it. Its remedy enforced the old written plan until a valid amendment; it expressly limited its holding to sponsor-favorable amendments later challenged by beneficiaries. It did not permanently extinguish amendment power: formal board action could add a procedure and a later compliant amendment could change benefits. It rejected the company's supposed one-step termination/new-plan theory because the record did not show consideration or actual treatment of a whole-plan termination, and the old plan continued in substance.

Judge Roth's different view is recorded in footnote 3 of the panel opinion, not a separately published concurrence to be invented. She treated company action as action by its board or authorized actors under incorporation-state law; CW is a Delaware corporation. She nevertheless regarded this amendment as ineffective because the board or another empowered actor had not ratified it. The majority noted that the record did not show board adoption; it did not decide the full corporate-authority inquiry under Roth's construction.

### Text and distinct legal questions

ERISA §402(b)(3), 29 U.S.C. §1102(b)(3), requires every plan to “provide a procedure for amending such plan, and for identifying the persons who have authority to amend the plan.” Section 3(9), §1002(9), includes a corporation in “person,” along with an individual, partnership, joint venture, mutual company, joint-stock company, trust, estate, unincorporated organization, association or employee organization. Section 402(a)(1) requires a written instrument with one or more named fiduciaries controlling and managing operation and administration; those administrative duties are not automatically the sponsor's amendment power.

The plan Constitution states: “the Company reserves the right at any time and from time to time to modify or amend, in whole or in part, any or all of the provisions of the Plan.” It separately reserves termination for any reason at any time. The 1983 SPD plant-closing term is a separate proposed amendment, not proof of its authorized adoption. Delaware §141(a), as reproduced in the lower opinion, places corporate business and affairs under the board's management or direction except as otherwise supplied by that chapter or the certificate. That exception remains; no corporate charter, bylaw or delegation is supplied here as a finding.

Section 1022 requires an understandable, sufficiently accurate and comprehensive summary and identification of actual circumstances causing loss or denial of benefits. Section 1024 supplies disclosure and access obligations, including instruments under which the plan is established or operated. Neither statute automatically inserts hypothetical future benefit changes into the current SPD. Under §502(a)(1)(B), a participant or beneficiary may recover benefits due under the plan, enforce plan rights or clarify future rights; §502(a)(3)'s separate appropriate-equitable-relief category cannot be conflated with the benefits action. The complete selected statutory appendix has been read; its later amendments to unrelated enforcement provisions do not retroactively establish a 1983 duty.

### Supported paths and current-law limits

The company argues that naming a corporate person and reserving unilateral amendment supplies both the procedure and identification, with corporate law specifying its actors. A conclusion that this satisfies the statute removes the categorical procedure ground, but does not find actual approval. The retirees argue that naming the beneficiary of a reserved power tells employees neither the amendment process nor its authorized human decisionmakers. A conclusion that the clause fails requires a separate remedy analysis: enforcement of an unamended plan differs from inventing new benefits for a notice violation. A narrower path can accept corporate identification yet preserve or return the actual-authorization ground. An affirmance on that alternative needs an adequate record and the correct corporate-law standard; Roth's footnote is not a Court finding.

Mertens, effective June 1, 1993, limits §502(a)(3) to traditionally equitable categories on its reviewed nonfiduciary compensation claim; it does not prohibit a §502(a)(1)(B) benefits judgment under unchanged plan terms. Scalia authored, with Kennedy, Souter and Thomas among the majority; Stevens and O'Connor joined White's contrary trust-remedy dissent. Those positions support attention to the actual statutory remedy and do not predetermine procedural sufficiency. John Hancock, effective December 13, 1993, separates plan-asset status, fiduciary function, breach and remedy; Ginsburg authored with Stevens, Scalia and Souter among the joining Justices. It supplies no categorical fiduciary prohibition on a sponsor changing welfare benefits. Milwaukee Brewery, effective February 21, 1995, is Breyer's unanimous opinion separating statutory computational assumptions from payment, default and remedy rules; all current Justices joined that text-specific approach, which supplies no amendment-procedure holding. Welfare benefits lack ERISA's pension-vesting mandate, but a contractual commitment may still matter; the rejected lifetime-benefit cross-appeal is not automatically reopened by this review.

A reversal on facial procedural sufficiency supports remand for actual authority, approval and any legally effective ratification; it does not itself terminate all retirees' claims or order repayment of benefits. An affirmance on noncompliance or an adequate independent ground preserves only the actual benefits judgment and its boundaries, not permanent lifetime vesting. No disclosure damages, new fiduciary breach, or final ratification finding is supplied.

## 35. Shalala v. Guernsey Memorial Hospital, No. 93-1251

### Event, claims and posture

Scheduled March 6, 1995; argument October 31, 1994. Sixth Circuit, 996 F.2d 830 (June 18, 1993), No. 92-3563, reversed the Southern District of Ohio, 796 F. Supp. 283 (1992), on advance refunding and remanded for summary judgment for the hospital on that issue. It affirmed the Secretary on a separate debt-service-fund interest-offset issue. That separate affirmance is expressly outside the petition and supplied question; do not reverse it as part of an undifferentiated judgment.

In 1985 the nonprofit Cambridge, Ohio acute-care hospital refinanced 1972 and 1982 mortgage-revenue bonds. Treasury obligations purchased with refinancing proceeds were placed in an irrevocable escrow for old bondholders. The old liens were released and the hospital's old legal obligations defeased, though payments to bondholders continued according to the escrow arrangement. Net accounting loss was $672,581; the Medicare share claimed immediately was approximately $314,000. The dispute is timing of allowable costs, not whether all reasonable advance-refunding costs are categorically excluded. The reported total includes unamortized earlier issue costs and a funded call premium; an accounting loss is not itself a fresh cash expenditure of that whole amount.

The intermediary applied PRM §233; the PRRB allowed immediate recognition after a hearing; the HCFA Administrator reversed and gave the Secretary's final decision under §405.1875. Judicial review follows §1395oo(f). The district court granted the Secretary summary judgment. The Sixth Circuit found the manual inconsistent with operative accrual-accounting regulations and therefore a substantive amendment adopted without APA notice and comment. It acknowledged that amortization could be rational and within authority if lawfully promulgated. This is not an appellate finding that GAAP is independently commanded by the Medicare statute.

The unrelated offset involved $24,874 interest earned in 1985 in the new debt-service interest account and approximately $12,000 less reimbursement. It is background identifying the excluded component, not an open claim here.

### Statute, regulations and manual in their own terms

Section 1395x(v)(1)(A) defines reasonable cost as actual incurred cost excluding unnecessary cost in efficient delivery of needed services, to be determined under regulations specifying methods and included items. It directs consideration of national/prepayment accounting principles; “consider” is not mandatory adoption of GAAP. The methods may differ by circumstances and use estimates, limits, per-unit measures or charges where reasonable. Direct and indirect costs must be accounted for so that Medicare does not bear non-Medicare costs and the reverse; suitable corrective adjustments address aggregate overpayment or underpayment within lawful methods. Good Samaritan supplies the current construction of the latter requirement.

42 C.F.R. §413.20(a), headed financial data and reports, requires sufficient financial records and statistical data to determine payable costs; standardized definitions, accounting, statistics and reporting practices widely accepted in hospital and related fields “are followed.” Changes in those practices and systems “will not be required” to determine payable costs. The final sentence says Medicare cost determination essentially uses basic accounts as usually maintained to reach equitable and proper beneficiary payment. Section 413.24(a) requires adequate cost data from financial and statistical records verifiable by qualified auditors, based on an approved cost-finding method and accrual accounting. Section 413.24(b)(2) reports revenue when earned and expense when incurred, regardless of collection or payment. The present dispute is whether those directions incorporate GAAP reimbursement timing, or require the reporting base while separate cost-allocation rules govern payment.

Sections 413.9 and 413.153 concern reasonable patient-care costs and necessary and proper interest; they do not expressly name this particular defeasance-loss treatment. The Secretary's actual order invokes matching the cost to beneficiary services over the periods benefited and variable Medicare utilization, not merely a lawyer's subsequent policy preference. Her reply alternatively defends this individual adjudication as a rational application of existing regulations even apart from §233's status. That route must still fit the actual order and existing text under Chenery; a court cannot cure a conflicting regulation by inventing a different agency finding.

PRM §233 is reproduced completely at petition appendix 85a–89a. Its provisions are materially more qualified than “all loss is amortized”:

- Section 233.1 covers advance refunding through new debt; it excludes recall before maturity without issuing new debt, referring that situation to §215. It applies to refundings initiated on or after July 1, 1983. Initiation means provider action or official debt-issuer action clearly intending the refunding; board authorization, engaging an underwriter or applying to the issuing authority are examples, not mandatory cumulative elements.
- Section 233.2 distinguishes new refunding debt, replaced refunded debt, an advance-refunding transaction, a defeasance provision and legal satisfaction under that provision. Legal satisfaction need not mean immediate retirement of the old bonds.
- Section 233.3 applies when debt incurred for necessary patient care is defeased or repurchased through advance refunding. New debt issue costs amortize from incurrence to the new debt's scheduled maturity. Redemption and miscellaneous expenses, and annual authority/trustee fees, are allowed as paid or accrued subject to the manual's §2305 qualification. Call premiums/penalties are allowed in the periods old holders receive principal, prorated for serial bonds by the proportional principal payments. Unamortized old discounts/premiums and issue costs amortize from new issuance to old holders' principal receipt, with serial-bond proration. Interest on both debts is allowed annually as paid or accrued; new discount/premium amortization adjusts interest; investment income from new proceeds offsets interest whether earned directly or through a trust. Only individual expense elements are reimbursable under this policy. Its overall effect spreads the refunding gain or loss rather than recognizing the accounting total immediately.
- Section 233.4 retains necessary-and-proper and reasonable-cost limits. Available sinking funds count in necessary borrowing; existing funded depreciation counts where excess borrowing buys depreciable assets. Ordinarily aggregate net allowable refinancing costs cannot exceed costs allowable without refinancing. Compelling factors such as necessary cash flow or removal of a restrictive borrowing covenant may justify excess costs to the intermediary's satisfaction. If not shown, the annual cap is the costs that would have been incurred without refunding. Neither that exception nor §2305's separate limitation is established as independently disputed in this case.
- Section 233.5 permits qualifying debts and proceeds in equity-capital calculations, but excludes debt and related assets to the extent interest is disallowed under §233.4. No separate equity-capital award is before the Court.

The Manual foreword, reproduced in the Secretary's merits appendix 1a–3a, expressly says the manual lacks the effect of regulations, supplies guidelines and policies, and uses GAAP for cost situations not otherwise covered. A method among alternatives must be used consistently, with approved prospective rather than retrospective changes. These are source descriptions, not a conclusion that the Manual can displace regulations. The APA §553(b)(A) exemption for interpretive rules and general policy statements is distinct from a binding substantive change. HHS's undertaking not to invoke the benefits exception remains as reflected in Lincoln; no §553(a)(2) shortcut is assumed. Respondent's merits brief footnote 15, as specifically acknowledged in the Secretary's reply footnote 4, excludes the post-1987 §1395hh rulemaking provision from this preexisting 1983 manual dispute. Do not import its later rulemaking standard backward.

## 36. Ambassador Books & Video, Inc. v. City of Little Rock, No. 93-1886

### Admitted event and corrected factual posture

This is the admitted added matter; No. 93-1886 is the supplied Supreme Court simulation docket, separate from Eighth Circuit No. 93-1509. Scheduled argument January 10, 1995 and decision March 20, 1995 are the supplied scheduling premises. The judgment under review is 20 F.3d 858 (April 1, 1994), submitted November 10, 1993. It affirmed dismissal of the complaint and denial of a preliminary injunction **after a district-court bench trial**. Delete the contradictory sentence “This was not judgment after a bench trial.” No historical Supreme Court merits disposition exists to supply law or resolve this case.

The plaintiffs are Ambassador Books & Video and Allan Dunlap doing business as United Arcade; each operates an adult bookstore and arcade. They remained at prohibited locations after the three-year transition and sued after notice of enforcement. Both were within 750 feet of a church or residentially zoned property. They sought to enjoin the ordinance under the First and Fourteenth Amendments, inverse-condemnation principles and the Bill of Attainder Clause. Do not invent a damages amount or final parcel valuation.

Little Rock began work in 1985 after citizen complaints. A city attorney's early handwritten direction to staff expressed a wish to close the businesses. The subsequent legal research recognized that outright suppression was impermissible; the city attorney and planning staff developed zoning controls, studied other cities' reports concerning crime and property values, and presented those materials to the Planning Commission and Board of Directors. Public hearings occurred. The city considered 1,000 feet too restrictive and 500 feet inadequate, choosing 750 feet. These are reported development facts, not a Supreme Court endorsement of the evidence.

The district court **expressed the view that the predominant purpose was to hinder or eliminate adult businesses because of content**, while nevertheless upholding the ordinance under circuit law as a secondary-effects measure. The Eighth Circuit rejected that premise's legal/factual sufficiency. It relied on the enacted purpose and the record: the attorney was not an enacting legislator, changed his approach after research, and testified against a shutdown purpose; the city list of seven affected establishments and vice-squad distribution of notices did not establish punishment or censorship; lack of local adverse-effects data did not itself invalidate reliance on other cities. The seed must not recharacterize the district court as affirmatively finding a predominant secondary-effects purpose. The proper treatment of the trial court's intent statement is a live issue, not settled by calling it either a conclusive fact or legally irrelevant.

### Ordinance terms and alternatives evidence

Section 17-214 bars covered businesses within 750 feet of religious facilities; public or private elementary, secondary or postsecondary schools; residential-zone boundaries or single- or multiple-family uses; public parks; medical facilities; National Register properties or local historic districts; and another sexually oriented business. Section 17-212(9) covers adult arcades, bookstores, video stores, cabarets, motion-picture theaters and theaters, with further definitions in §17-212. Their detailed definitional thresholds are not reproduced in the reported opinion; plaintiffs' inclusion was established, and no new facial challenge to unquoted definitions is supplied.

Section 17-211 states land-use purposes of health, safety, welfare and avoiding continued concentration, while disavowing restriction of communicative content or adult market access except as otherwise restricted by law. Disavowal is evidence of stated purpose, not an exemption from constitutional review. Section 17-215(a) gives existing businesses three years and permits a reasonable extension when necessary to prevent undue financial hardship. It does not promise automatic extension, and the lower report does not establish an application or denial by these plaintiffs.

The district court found 97 available relocation sites and 6.75 percent of the areas in which such businesses could locate available under the ordinance; the Eighth Circuit expressly held those findings not clearly erroneous. Do **not** transform the denominator into 6.75 percent of the city's entire territory. United Arcade actually sought and obtained a conditional use permit for an alternative area. The plaintiffs argued relocation cost made sites commercially unusable; the appellate court rejected cost alone as establishing denial of a reasonable avenue. The record supplied here does not identify all parcels, lot dimensions, property-owner willingness, suitability constraints, rents or sale prices. It establishes neither universal commercial feasibility nor a sham count. The real distinction is opportunity to operate versus guaranteed affordable or preferred premises.

### Entering law and issue-specific paths

Renton, 475 U.S. 41 (1986), addresses adult-theater zoning directed at secondary effects: substantial governmental interest and reasonable alternative avenues; it permits reliance on another city's relevant evidence without first experiencing the harms locally and does not guarantee a commercially preferable site. Young, 427 U.S. 50 (1976), supports land-use regulation of concentrations but has distinct rationales; do not treat all of its discussion as a unified lesser-value-speech rule. O'Brien, 391 U.S. 367 (1968), cautions against invalidation solely from illicit motives attributed to individual participants. Those rules do not make every content-defined law neutral merely because its preamble claims secondary effects.

Turner, effective June 27, 1994, requires classification by what a law selects and why; benign motive alone does not cure content selection. Its neutral intermediate test asks substantial nonsuppressive interest and no substantially excessive speech burden, not least-restrictive means. Legislative predictions receive substantial deference with independent assurance of reasonable inference from substantial evidence; no single controlling remand-sufficiency theory arose from its fractured application. Its fact-intensive cable rules do not displace Renton's specific zoning holding. Standards and Tests: **Cable-carriage rules and review of legislative predictions**, present operation June 27, 1994. **Residential signs and preservation of a distinctive speech medium**, effective June 13, 1994, Ladue, protects against near-elimination of a distinctive home-sign channel; relocating an operating bookstore differs from prohibiting a medium, but actual usable opportunities remain relevant.

The city's First Amendment path applies Renton to the enacted objective and reported studies, retains the 97-site finding under the proper review standard and rejects a right to remain at each existing location. The businesses' strongest path uses the trial court's contrary purpose statement, the coverage of all known businesses and burdens of displacement to contest genuine neutrality or the evidentiary and alternative-site showing. Its limitation is that a staff remark and a relocation requirement do not by themselves prove legislative suppression; lack of local studies and high relocation cost alone are not Renton defects. A remand path must identify a genuinely missing constitutional inquiry or incorrectly reviewed material finding, not presume that Turner already found Little Rock's record inadequate.

The existing-business challenge is distinct. The Eighth Circuit applied Euclid's rule against arbitrary and unreasonable land use unrelated to health, safety or welfare, rejected a categorical prospective-only requirement, and relied on three years plus potential hardship extension. A reasonable transition may support validity, but three years is not a constitutional safe harbor in every case. A claimant-specific inability to recoup, extension denial or confiscatory application needs actual evidence. The First Amendment inquiry is not replaced by bare land-use rationality.

Takings: Penn Central's economic impact, reasonable investment-backed expectations and governmental character remain operative. The local Lucas holding of June 29, 1992 requires an established loss of all economically beneficial use, subject to substantiated background property/nuisance law; no complete loss is established simply because a particular adult business must relocate. Concrete Pipe, June 14, 1993, preserves contextual assessment and rejects a numerical-loss rule. Neither valid police-power purpose nor an amortization label categorically excludes compensation. This report supplies no physical appropriation, easement dedication or total parcel-value finding; do not apply Dolan's individualized dedication test to ordinary locational zoning. A separate takings remand or compensation disposition needs identification of the property interest, actual impact, procedural availability and relief within the admitted claim. No modern takings ripeness rule is imported.

Attainder: the governing question under Article I, §10 is legislative punishment of specified or readily ascertainable persons without trial, not identification alone. Nixon v. Administrator of General Services, 433 U.S. 425 (1977), supplies historical, functional and purpose considerations for punishment and permits genuine nonpunitive regulation. The lower court found the ordinance nonpunitive because it sought protection from secondary effects rather than punishment for prior activity. The plaintiffs may challenge that actual purpose/operation; the seven-business list alone does not establish all elements. Rejecting or remanding the speech claim does not automatically decide punishment.
