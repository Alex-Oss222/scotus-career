## 1. United States v. Shabani, No. 93-981 — November 1, 1994

### Issue, disposition, and operative ground

All eight non-Stone Justices reach the admitted statutory question and would **reverse the Ninth Circuit's overt-act ruling and remand for the unresolved appellate issues**. Section 846 does not require proof of an overt act. It requires the intentional agreement to commit the prohibited narcotics offense; it does not punish unexpressed thought, mere presence, or awareness alone. The common-law meaning of conspiracy, the omission of an overt-act clause, and Congress's express inclusion of such a clause in §371 resolve the construction without residual ambiguity. Neither indictment dismissal, acquittal, nor unconditional reinstatement of all aspects of the conviction follows from this legal ruling.

Nash v. United States, 229 U.S. 373, 378 (1913), supplies the common-law agreement baseline and the significance of statutory omission; Singer v. United States, 323 U.S. 338, 340 (1945), supplies a second statute-specific example. Current Felix distinguishes the agreement from its substantive execution, but does not itself interpret §846. The Ninth Circuit, 993 F.2d 1419, expressly reserved sufficiency and other claims and recognized the textual weakness of its own required element. Its intracircuit obligation does not bind this Court.

### Competing path and remedies

The best affirmative case for an overt act combines the circuit's settled practice, criminal-law resistance to punishment of intention alone, and lenity. Its decisive difficulty is that an intentional agreement is itself an external criminal act in the common-law conspiracy sense; Nash and Singer distinguish it from the additional overt act that some statutes demand. Congress's use of express language in §371 gives a direct textual comparison. All eight therefore reject that added statutory element, without denying lenity's force when ambiguity remains or deciding a constitutional question unnecessary to this appeal. The narrower common ground is §846 alone, with knowing agreement preserved.

**Remedy and limits:** Reverse the appellate judgment to the extent it requires the omitted instruction and remand for the appellate court's reserved issues. The allegation and proof of the cocaine agreement remain necessary. The Court does not decide the witnesses' credibility, ultimate evidentiary sufficiency, harmlessness under an element it rejects, the remaining appellate objections, or acquittal. No material source blocker prevents this bounded disposition.

## 2. U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership, No. 93-714 — November 8, 1994

### Components and proposed common rule

All eight recognize **residual authority to consider disposition of the judgment**, would **deny vacatur on the admitted consensual-mootness showing**, and would **dismiss the moot Supreme Court proceeding**. They do not decide the bankruptcy merits. Section 2106 permits a just disposition of a judgment lawfully brought before the Court; Walling and Munsingwear demonstrate that inability to decide the merits does not eliminate that distinct authority. Kokkonen concerns a new enforcement controversy after dismissal, not disposition of this already pending review.

Their common equitable ground is bounded: a party's voluntary settlement and resulting loss of appellate review do not, without a further sufficient equitable reason, justify erasing the judgment it sought to review. Relief remains discretionary; exceptional circumstances are not categorically excluded. No special circumstance is established by the supplied record, and the Court need not invent one. This is an assessment of the showing available, not a finding about every undisclosed settlement term. The preserved appellate judgment is not affirmed on its merits, and its bankruptcy reasoning does not become Supreme Court law.

## 3. Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., No. 93-1197, including Walsh — November 14, 1994

### Reach and distinct questions

All eight reach the admitted arm-of-State question, a legal premise of the dismissal. Hess's failure to contest status in the district court is preserved, but no appellate forfeiture ruling or new Supreme Court forfeiture disposition is inferred without the relevant submissions. All distinguish arm status from consent, conditions on consent, FELA coverage, congressional abrogation, and the underlying negligence claims. Feeney assumed arm status; Hilton preserves FELA's state-court reach without reviving constructive waiver in federal court; Puerto Rico Aqueduct establishes immediate appeal, not actual immunity.

The independent status commitments are: **Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: PATH is not an arm entitled to this immunity; reverse/remand. O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas: PATH has arm status; retain that legal premise and remand for any necessary separate consent/posture questions rather than invent Walsh-specific facts.** These are distinct disposition commitments; the latter three do not join a reversal declaring PATH nonimmune.

### Common record and alternative grounds

The Authority is a separately organized interstate entity with self-supporting operating finances and a reserve fund. Governors appoint commissioners and possess veto authority; legislation limits the Authority's projects and spending. Those control facts are substantial, not ignored. Article XV requires equal annual appropriations for salaries, offices and other administrative expenses only while operating revenues are inadequate, in amounts recommended by the Authority and approved by both governors, and obligates each State only up to $100,000 in any year. It does not promise payment of every judgment. PBA's inference of a theoretical treasury impact is a judicial interpretation, not the text of an unlimited state guarantee.

## 4. United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., No. 93-723 — November 29, 1994

### Components and independent commitments

All eight reach statutory construction and distinguish factual knowledge from knowledge of illegality. **Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer** would construe §§2252(a)(1) and (a)(2) to require knowledge of the performer's minority and the depiction's sexually explicit character, reject the facial invalidation resting on the absence of those elements, and reverse/remand for the remaining appellate issues and application of the proper standard. **Scalia and Thomas** would reject that construction as exceeding the enacted placement of “knowingly,” and affirm the reversal of convictions on the facial defect in the provisions as read without the necessary content-related fault. They would not hold that a federal court may simply replace that enacted arrangement with a judicial recklessness element.

The statutory-knowledge votes do not decide that the Constitution invariably requires actual knowledge rather than recklessness in every child-pornography statute. Their statutory construction avoids that larger issue. The contrary two distinguish the constitutional need for some adequate content-related culpability from the exact level another valid enactment might choose; they do not treat Osborne as holding that actual knowledge is invariably the constitutional minimum.

### Statute, current law, and strongest opposing paths

Sections 2252(a)(1) and (a)(2) place “knowingly” before specified handling acts and describe the prohibited depictions through separate conditions: production involving a minor in sexually explicit conduct, and a depiction of that conduct. Subsection (a)(2) separately includes receiving/distributing with the prescribed mail or commerce connection and knowingly reproducing for distribution. A minor is under eighteen. Section 2256's conduct definition covers actual or simulated intercourse, bestiality, masturbation, sadistic or masochistic abuse, or lascivious genital/pubic exhibition. No later digital-image category is supplied. The required handling conduct, content facts, actual minority, jurisdictional connection, and knowledge of illegality remain distinct questions.
