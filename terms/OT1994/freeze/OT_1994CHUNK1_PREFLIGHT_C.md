## 9. Nebraska Department of Revenue v. Loewenstein

**Primary sources:** [Loewenstein v. State, 244 Neb. 82, 504 N.W.2d 800 (August 27, 1993)](https://static.case.law/neb/244/html/0082-01.html), No. S-91-255; [31 U.S.C. §3124, 1994 edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title31/html/USCODE-1994-title31-subtitleIII-chap31-subchapII-sec3124.htm); [Rockford Life Insurance Co. v. Illinois Department of Revenue, 482 U.S. 182 (1987)](https://static.case.law/us/482/html/0182-01.html). Local full texts: `sources/preflight_c/loewenstein.txt`, `usc3124.txt`, `rockford.txt`.

**Record and scope additions.** The appeal concerns Revenue Ruling 22-85-1 only. The district court also invalidated Ruling 22-87-4, but the Department did not appeal that portion. The action was filed in May 1988 under Nebraska's Administrative Procedure Act; the parties amended the pleadings jointly to add 22-85-1. State review was de novo on the stipulated record. The trusts are two regulated investment companies holding only federal securities, directly or through repos. The second stipulation states actual delivery through the Federal Reserve book-entry system, no separate promissory note, a contemporaneous agreement to resell at the original price plus negotiated interest, and a repurchase price typically below the securities' market value. The negotiated repo interest is below the yield on the securities. Do not replace these facts with a categorical statement that no securities were delivered: that is wording in the revenue ruling, whereas the stipulation says delivery occurred.

The Nebraska majority found ownership classification nondispositive and relied on the statutory protection against direct or indirect consideration of federal obligations and testimony concerning federal financing markets. The two experts differed: Sternlight described repos as purchases and sales important to securities-market financing, while acknowledging similarity to collateralized loans; Brown described their economic substance as secured loans. The Caporale dissent, joined by retired Justice Grant, characterized the fixed return, repurchase/resale duties, substitution right, and allocation of market risk as indicating a private loan. Preserve the distinction between stipulated transactional facts, expert characterization, and the appellate court's legal conclusion.

**Exact federal text.** Section 3124(a) exempts stocks and obligations of the United States Government from state or local taxation and applies to each form of tax requiring the obligation, its interest, or both to be considered in computing the tax, **except** (1) a nondiscriminatory corporate franchise tax or another corporate nonproperty tax instead of a franchise tax, and (2) an estate or inheritance tax. Neither exception describes the individual income tax presented. Subsection (b) sends the specified interest, dividends, earnings, other income, and disposition gains/losses to the Internal Revenue Code; it also contains the pre-March 1, 1941 FHA contractual protection and territorial/District exclusions. Do not use subsection (b) to erase subsection (a)'s separate state-tax rule. The official revision notes establish that removal of “directly or indirectly” was as surplus, and the only listed 1986 amendment changed the Internal Revenue Code reference in subsection (b). The lower judgment quotes the applicable Nebraska subtraction for federally exempt interest/dividend distributions, §77-2716(1)(a), read with §77-2715(2).

**Authority additions.** Rockford is Stevens's unanimous opinion. Its actual subject is privately issued GNMA mortgage-backed certificates with a contingent federal guarantee, not repos. It preserves Smith v. Davis's distinction between a qualifying direct, certain federal obligation and a private obligation backed by federal credit. It rejects tax immunity merely because a private instrument assists a federal program; the constitutional borrowing-power inquiry requires more than a remote or conjectural burden. Stevens, O'Connor, and Scalia were members of that Court; Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer were not. Do not attribute its join to a later appointee. The Nebraska majority relies on Memphis Bank, 459 U.S. 392, American Bank, 463 U.S. 855, and First National Bank of Atlanta v. Bartow County, 470 U.S. 583; Rockford explains the distinct instrument question and cautions against enlarging tax immunity beyond its source. Those cases' generalized policy language does not itself classify these stipulated repos.

**Material limits.** No missing finding presently prevents analysis of the confined §3124 question on the stipulated transaction. A decision about differently structured repos, the unappealed revenue ruling, or a new tax calculation would require the actual instrument or additional record. The lower report is the source for the stipulations; the executed agreements themselves were not independently retrieved.

## 10. In re Baby K

**Primary sources:** [In re Baby K, 16 F.3d 590 (4th Cir. February 10, 1994)](https://static.case.law/f3d/16/html/0590-01.html), including Sprouse's dissent and all ten footnotes; [corroborating report text](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/016/16.F3d.590.93-1924.93-1923.93-1899.html); [42 U.S.C. §1395dd, official 1994 edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title42/html/USCODE-1994-title42-chap6-subchapXVIII_2-partC-sec1395dd.htm); [Gatewood v. Washington Healthcare Corp., 933 F.2d 1037 (D.C. Cir. May 28, 1991)](https://static.case.law/f2d/933/html/1037-01.html). Local full texts: `baby_k.txt`, `baby_k_cap.txt`, `usc1395dd.txt`, `gatewood.txt`. The unusual chapter/subchapter labels in the govinfo URL are its historical granule metadata; the page itself prints §1395dd.

**Record additions.** Baby K was born at the hospital in October 1992, transferred to a nursing home in November when acute hospital services were no longer needed, and had returned three times for breathing assistance by the appellate record. Her mother opposes the requested declaration; father and guardian ad litem support the hospital. The complaint followed the second readmission. The hospital conceded that absence of immediate attention during respiratory distress would cause serious impairment and that ventilation was needed to prevent deterioration. Do not turn its judgment that treatment is inappropriate for anencephaly into a finding that ventilation cannot stabilize the respiratory episode.

The hospital presented four statutory arguments: uniform treatment of similarly situated anencephalic infants suffices; Congress did not require treatment exceeding the prevailing medical standard; physician objections under Virginia law mean the required staff are unavailable; and the definition of stabilization limits the duty to an intended transfer. The Fourth Circuit rejected them, differentiating uniform screening from the stabilization obligation. It also stated an alternative response to the uniformity argument: respiratory distress is the immediate condition, and the hospital did not claim it would refuse respiratory support to a nonanencephalic infant with apnea or bradypnea. Sprouse's dissent accepted the screening/stabilization distinction but treated this infant's recurrent respiratory events as integral to a continuing terminal condition outside the statute's anti-dumping reach. His rationale was expressly case-specific; do not enlarge it to every terminally ill patient.

**Statutory terms and qualifications that a complete analysis must retain.**

- Section (a) requires a requested screening of any person coming to the emergency department, within that department's capability including routinely available ancillary services. Section (b)(1) separately applies when a person comes to a hospital and the hospital determines that an emergency medical condition exists. It requires, within available staff and facilities, the examination and treatment necessary to stabilize **or** transfer in accordance with subsection (c).
- Section (e)(1)(A) uses acute symptoms sufficiently severe, including severe pain, that without immediate attention serious jeopardy to health, serious impairment of bodily functions, or serious dysfunction of a bodily organ or part could reasonably be expected. Section (e)(3) defines stabilization in terms of no likely material deterioration, within reasonable medical probability, resulting from or during transfer. Transfer includes discharge, but excludes departure after declaration of death or without hospital-affiliated permission. The labor branch is separate and is not the factual branch presented here.
- Section (b)(2) deems the treatment requirement met after offered examination/treatment, informed risks and benefits, refusal by the individual or representative, and reasonable steps to secure written informed refusal. Section (b)(3) similarly concerns an offered transfer **in accordance with subsection (c)**, informed risks/benefits, refusal, and reasonable steps to secure written informed refusal. A mother's objection alone is not a finding that the hospital made a compliant transfer offer.
- Under (c)(1), transfer of an unstabilized patient requires an informed written request by the individual or legally responsible person, **or** a physician's signed benefits-over-risks certification. If no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a qualified medical person may sign after consulting a physician who makes the determination and later countersigns. The certification includes a summary of risks and benefits. In every branch the transfer must be appropriate under (c)(2): available risk-minimizing treatment; receiving space, qualified staff, and acceptance; transmission of available emergency-related records and the consent/certification; qualified transport personnel/equipment and needed life support; and applicable additional Secretary requirements.
- The appellate record says the mother objected, no benefits-over-risks certification had been obtained, and no qualified facility agreed to accept the child. These are separate failures. A future compliant transfer remains an alternative; treatment at this hospital is not statutorily required regardless of later availability of such a transfer.
- Section (f) preserves state/local requirements except to the extent of **direct conflict**. Section (g) separately bars a participating hospital with specialized capabilities or facilities from refusing an appropriate transfer requiring them **if it has capacity**. Footnote 6 identifies uncertainty whether nursing-home movement was discharge/presentation or transfer and relies on (g), with the hospital's admitted pediatric intensive-care/ventilator capability, for the latter situation.
- Section (d)(1)(B) subjects responsible physicians to administrative penalties, subject to the distinct (d)(1)(C) on-call-physician transfer protection. Section (d)(2)(A)'s private action is against the participating hospital, for personal harm directly resulting from a violation, state-law personal-injury damages and appropriate equitable relief; a two-year limitation applies. Do not turn physician administrative liability into a private damages action against physicians. The hospital is seeking prospective declaratory relief here; no damages finding is presented.

**Virginia and unreached grounds.** The reported 1993 version of Va. Code §54.1-2990 says nothing in the Health Care Decisions Act requires treatment a physician considers medically or ethically inappropriate. Footnote 10 states that the Act covers adult advance directives and adult surrogate decisions, not infant treatment decisions. Thus both applicability and direct conflict are conditional. The court did not hold that all Virginia medical law was displaced. Its footnote 2 reports that the district court found ADA/Rehabilitation Act discrimination but declined to decide CAPTA or Virginia-law obligations. The appellate affirmance rested on EMTALA and did not reach those additional statutes. A reversal of the EMTALA ground would not itself decide those unreviewed grounds or necessarily entitle the hospital to its entire requested declaration.

**Justice-authority addition.** Gatewood was authored by Harry Edwards and joined by Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Clarence Thomas. It gives both Justices a directly relevant pre-divergence EMTALA position: plain “any individual” language protects insured patients despite the anti-dumping history; the statute does not federalize ordinary diagnostic malpractice; screening uniformity is measured by the hospital's ordinary procedures. Gatewood expressly reserved stabilization/transfer scope because no emergency was diagnosed. Its footnote 1 distinguishes hospital civil liability from physician liability. This source supports attention to the exact duty triggered and cannot be converted into a prior Baby K position. Suter's simulated holdings separately distinguish an enforceable right, cause of action, and relief; they do not replace this statute's express duties and enforcement text.

**Path-dependent source needs.** The published appellate record is sufficient to identify the statutory construction controversy and the supported future-transfer conditions. Obtain the district court's operative declaration and the relevant full lower record before selecting relief that adjudicates the ADA/Rehabilitation Act, CAPTA, parental/guardian authority, or treatment unrelated to the presented emergencies. This handoff does not supply those decisions. The 1994 amendment note concerns technical administration of (d)(3); it does not identify a changed stabilization or preemption rule. No independent current-term statutory event is asserted here.

## 11. Plakas v. Drinski

**Primary sources:** [Plakas v. Drinski, 19 F.3d 1143 (7th Cir. March 21, 1994)](https://static.case.law/f3d/19/html/1143-01.html); [corroborating full report](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/019/19.F3d.1143.93-1431.html); [Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989)](https://static.case.law/us/490/html/0386-01.html); [Tennessee v. Garner, 471 U.S. 1 (1985)](https://static.case.law/us/471/html/0001-01.html). Local texts: `plakas.txt`, `plakas_cap.txt`, `graham.txt`, `garner.txt`, read in full.

**Chronology, evidence, and scope.** The encounter was **February 2, 1991**, before divergence. Any qualified-immunity notice inquiry must use law available then; the 1994 decision date supplies no later notice to the officer. The Seventh Circuit reviewed summary judgment for Drinski individually/officially and Newton County. All direct shooting witnesses were police officers; Perras was neither a defendant nor a county employee. The court said fatal-force summary judgment requires critical examination of forensic evidence, original reports/statements, and expert material, but found no supported contradiction here. Its treatment of the tree/sapling, body location, and witnesses' credibility must not be replaced by an invented trial finding. The photograph itself was not in the appellate record; witnesses had described it at depositions. The body had been moved before the paramedic saw its position. Missing ballistic/forensic facts were not filled by inference.

The nonlethal chemical was **CS repellant on Perras's belt**, not established as pepper spray carried by Drinski. Perras said distance and risk to other officers prevented its use. Canine services had been offered roughly ten minutes before the shooting; the record does not establish an attack dog standing beside Drinski. The officers tried to obtain surrender for about fifteen to thirty minutes. The appellate account describes the charge beginning at twelve to fifteen feet, retreat ending involuntarily, and the shot when Plakas was roughly two arms' lengths away. These are part of the summary-judgment account, not independent facts found by this Court.

The estate disputed that Plakas charged and alternatively contended available methods should have been used. It did not argue officers should leave and arrest him another day. A prior-beating theory was not pleaded; the panel additionally found no supported inference of a beating by Koby and noted Koby was absent from the shooting. It distinguished **Gilmere v. City of Atlanta, 774 F.2d 1495 (11th Cir. 1985)** while expressly neither approving nor disapproving its rule concerning an officer's own unjustified violence causing retaliatory danger. An expert mentioned Drinski's alleged invitation to strike somewhere other than the head, but the estate did not contend the invitation immediately preceded or caused the charge. Do not silently decide a developed provocation claim that the source says was not made.

**County component.** The panel addressed a distinct contention that the county created the dangerous circumstances by failing to provide equipment or training, even if Drinski's choice among available options was reasonable. It found no established constitutional equipment-list duty and no developed argument supporting one, while preserving the difference between adequate constitutional training and training to use every available device. The packet's simple no-officer-violation formulation is incomplete as a description of the reasoning below. Monell/Canton attribution, a federal deprivation, and whether a county's own conduct supplies a legally cognizable deprivation must remain separate. A personal qualified-immunity defense is not municipal immunity.

**Surviving and simulated authority.** Graham requires objective reasonableness under the complete contemporaneous circumstances: crime severity, immediate threat, active resistance/flight; viewpoint of a reasonable officer at the scene; allowance for tense, uncertain, rapid decisions; no intent-based malice requirement and no hindsight test. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy joined that opinion. Garner's majority, joined by Stevens, prohibits shooting an unarmed nondangerous fleeing suspect merely to prevent escape and allows deadly force against a serious threat under its stated necessity/warning conditions. O'Connor's dissent stressed on-scene uncertainty, serious crime, and historically accepted police practice; it supplies an individual authority, not a controlling exception to Garner. Plakas's immediate self-defense account differs factually from Garner's nonthreatening escape. Neither writing adopts a categorical equipment list or resolves all possible earlier unconstitutional police conduct.

Hunter's simulated holding requires the supported nonmovant account and complete contemporaneous record at summary judgment; Elder requires all relevant law on the preserved legal immunity issue and forbids retroactive notice. Leatherman governs pleading, not evidence sufficiency. Collins separates municipal attribution from the underlying deprivation. No case-specific Breyer, Souter, Thomas, or Ginsburg deadly-force position is inferred merely from succession or from those general rules; any material individual departure needs a relevant date-eligible writing.

**Path-dependent needs.** The published record permits assessment of the stated legal alternatives and the panel's evidence treatment. If a model would make its judgment depend on a different physical reconstruction, the missing photograph, ballistic evidence, or a causally developed provocation theory, it must retrieve the actual record or preserve that limitation. A qualified-immunity disposition requires preserved-defense verification and the February 1991 notice baseline; this handoff supplies neither an automatic immunity judgment nor a mandatory merits-first sequence.

## 12. ICC v. Transcon Lines

**Primary lower sources:** [9 F.3d 64 (Ninth Circuit October 26, 1993)](https://static.case.law/f3d/9/html/0064-01.html); [990 F.2d 1503, amended April 5, 1993](https://static.case.law/f2d/990/html/1503-01.html). Full text corroboration at Public.Resource uses `/F3/009/9.F3d.64.92-55036.html` and `/F2/990/990.F2d.1503.92-55036.html`. These judicial sources reproduce the operative historical statutory/regulatory provisions. Their recitation of other Court actions supplies no new in-world action; this handoff relies only on the lower courts' own findings, reasoning, and judgments.

**Record and procedural scope additions.** The trustee pursued unpaid original freight charges as well as additional loss-of-discount claims. Transcon filed a bureau rate, coded discount tariff TCON 625, and rules tariff conditioning discounts on payment within ninety calendar days after shipment. The regulatory authorized credit period is a distinct period with a different trigger; do not merge it into that ninety-day tariff condition. The original suit alleged both collection contrary to credit rules and charges above Transcon's own filed tariffs. The earlier appellate judgment directed an injunction against collections based on alleged unlawfulness of the coded/individual discount tariffs, while sustaining collection of bureau rates on overdue accounts. The October 1993 opinion addressed the latter credit-regulation issue. Do not reopen or erase the earlier, distinct coded-rate component simply by disposing of the reviewed credit-rule question.

The parties cross-moved for summary judgment conceding no material factual dispute at that stage. The lower report states that original bills lacked the notice and that the trustee had not complied with individual/revised-bill requirements; it does not establish the amount or legal status of every individual collection claim. The court found enforcement jurisdiction over the estate, rejecting the original theory that bankruptcy ended regulation. The court did not invalidate the regulations generally: it concluded that the filed-rate doctrine prevented this collection injunction, leaving other enforcement measures available. The October 1993 reasoning considered the Commission's express-enforcement reading of Reiter alternatively and still gave collection priority.

**Exact material regulatory qualifications.** The historical text is reproduced in full in footnote 1 of 990 F.2d 1503. Section 1320.2 begins the credit period the day after presentation of the freight bill; the default is fifteen days, including weekends/holidays, and a published alternative cannot exceed thirty days. Service charges for authorized extension and charges after expiry are distinct. Section 1320.2(g)(1) permits reasonable, certain collection-expense liquidated damages by either a specified dollar/percentage charge or loss of an identified discount after a stated payment date no earlier than expiration of authorized credit. Under (g)(2), damages and conditions must be clearly described; operate without unlawful prejudice/unjust discrimination; apply only to original, separate, independent bills, **not aggregate balance-due claims on past shipments** by a bankruptcy trustee or another collector; not apply to clear clerical/ministerial errors such as nonreceipt, a lost payment check, or misaddressing; not duplicate collection fees independently imposed by a bill of lading for the same transportation; and arise only after authorized credit expires with a revised bill or notice issued **within ninety days after that expiration**. Subsection (g)(3) separately permits a contract bill-of-lading collection-charge alternative outside the tariff. Section 1320.3(c) requires bill/accompanying notice of late-payment penalties, time limits, collection/service charges and discount terms, including notice that untimely payment may incur tariff penalties. Do not infer which additional exceptions apply to an individual debt.

The lower report quotes §10743(a)'s payment-before-possession rule and (b)(1)'s express allowance of credit under Commission regulations governing payment and preventing discrimination. It also quotes §10761(a)'s filed-tariff requirement and prohibition of differing compensation, and §11702(a)(4)'s civil-enforcement power, with its exception for an action under a provision governing rate reasonableness/discrimination. Section 10762(a)(1)'s motor-common-carrier tariff filing requirement is reproduced in Maislin. This is the pre-reform shipment regime; new relief under the Negotiated Rates Act or Trucking Industry Regulatory Reform Act is outside the supplied question and cannot be adjudicated without its actual effective/transition provisions.

**Date-eligible authority additions.** [Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Commercial Metals Co., 456 U.S. 336 (1982)](https://static.case.law/us/456/html/0336-01.html), read in full, rejects the consignor's implied affirmative defense for a credit violation where it remained liable under the bill of lading, and separately recognizes Commission enforcement tools including injunctions. It distinguishes detrimental-reliance double-payment cases and does not decide a presented agency collection injunction. Stevens and O'Connor joined the unanimous opinion; do not assign joins to later appointees.

[Maislin Industries v. Primary Steel, 497 U.S. 116 (1990)](https://static.case.law/us/497/html/0116-01.html), read in full, rejects the negotiated-rate unreasonable-practice policy while retaining the separate unreasonable-rate remedy and the possibility of statutorily grounded agency remedies. O'Connor and Kennedy joined the majority; Scalia joined and separately explained his textual reconciliation of rate commands; Stevens dissented on statutory context, Commission discretion, and deregulation. These writings supply actual individual approaches but no predetermined Transcon position.

[Delta Traffic Service v. Transtop, 902 F.2d 101 (First Circuit 1990)](https://static.case.law/f2d/902/html/0101-01.html), authored by Breyer and read in full, is especially useful with its **July 19, 1990 modification**: following Maislin, the court withdrew Section B on unreasonable negotiated-rate collection practices, preserving only Section A's referral of the rate-reasonableness issue. The withdrawn rationale is not controlling law. Breyer's preserved distinction between statutory rate review and ordinary collection supplies relevant published experience; do not cite the withdrawn portion as an available exception to Maislin.

The simulated Reiter holding separately recognizes statutory counterclaims, same-transaction recoupment, agency primary jurisdiction, and discretionary separate judgment; it does not decide this credit injunction. Security Services/Kmart distinguishes an ineffective incorporated tariff component from equitable avoidance of an effective tariff. Its Souter majority and Thomas/Ginsburg dissent require proposition-level attention; the dissent is not a general view that all Commission enforcement fails. MCI limits authority to the actual delegation without repealing Chevron or forbidding consequential regulation. ABF distinguishes delegated remedies from judicially created categorical forfeiture; it is an analogy, not transportation-specific authorization.

**Material source limit.** The categorical legal bar may be assessed on the published record. An operative injunction identifying particular bills requires their actual notice, timing, aggregation, and exception facts, or a remand that leaves those applications to the lower court. Do not create a universal discharge of the estate's freight debts. No exact simulated grant wording was supplied; the admitted operational question governs the reviewed credit-rule component.
