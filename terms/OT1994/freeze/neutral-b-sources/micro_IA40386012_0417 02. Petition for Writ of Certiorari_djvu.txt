STATUTE INVOLVED 


28 U.S.C. § 1291 provides in relevant part: “The courts of 
appeals * * * shall have jurisdiction of appeals from all final 
decisions of the district courts of the United States * * *.”

STATEMENT 


Petitioners are law enforcement officers who were sued under 
42 U.S.C. § 1983 for the allegedly excessive use of force. They 
sought summary judgment on good faith immunity grounds, 
asserting that their actions did not violate clearly established law 
because there was no evidence that their conduct was inconsistent 
with any legal standard. The district court denied the motion. The 
court of appeals then held that it lacked jurisdiction to entertain 
petitioners’ interlocutory appeal. This holding is squarely 
inconsistent with the decisions of other Circuits, is in tension with 
the rulings of this Court, and threatens to frustrate the purposes of 
the qualified immunity doctrine. Further review therefore is in 
order. 


1. On November 6, 1990, John Heidelmeier, an officer of 
the Villa Park, Illinois, police department, responded to a report of 
an apparently intoxicated man in or near an intersection. When 
Heidelmeier arrived at the scene he found plaintiff beating his head 
or forehead against the door of a building. Although some 
subsequent facts are in dispute, it is clear that an altercation ensued 
and that Heidelmeier attempted to arrest and handcuff respondent. 
Four more Villa Park police officers (the three petitioners here and 
Genaro Esposito) soon arrived and assisted Heidelmeier in 
handcuffing respondent. App., supra, 5a. After arriving at the

ribs. Ibid.

being dragged up the stairs of the police station. The state trial

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


This case presents a recurring issue of considerable practical 
importance. The law enforcement petitioners in this case moved 
for summary judgment, asserting that they were entitled to qualified 
immunity because plaintiff failed to establish that their actions had 
violated clearly established law -- or, indeed, that their actions had 
violated any law at all. The Seventh Circuit’s decision that the 
district court’s denial of such a motion may not be challenged in an 
interlocutory appeal is irreconcilable with the express contrary 
holdings of at least five other Circuits. It also is in clear tension 
with this Court’s repeated recognition that “[e]ven if the plaintiff s 
complaint adequately alleges the commission of acts that violated 
clearly established law, the defendant is entitled to summary 
judgment if discovery fails to uncover evidence sufficient to create 
a genuine issue as to whether the defendant in fact committed those 
acts.” Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511, 526 (1985). Because 
that decision threatens to impose substantial additional burdens on 
public officials by precluding “the dismissal of insubstantial suits 
without trial” (Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 814 (1982)) — 
a principal purpose of the qualified immunity doctrine — review by 
this Court plainly is warranted. 


A. The Decision Below Cannot be Reconciled With the 
Holdings of Other Courts of Appeals 


The court below held that “defendants may urge only legal 
issues” in interlocutory immunity appeals, opining that “[a] cry of 
‘we didn’t do it’ does not present a distinctly legal issue or seek 
protection from legal uncertainty, the genesis of doctrines of 
official immunity.” App., infra, la-2aa. The Seventh Circuit 
therefore is of the view that “interlocutory appeal to vindicate the 
right not to be tried is unavailable when there is no legal 
uncertainty,” reasoning that it is inappropriate for immunity claims 
to turn on the appellate court’s “comb{ing] through the factual 
record.” Elliott, 937 F.2d at 341. That precise conclusion, 


6 


however, has been expressly rejected by at least five courts of 
appeals — the First, Fourth, Eighth, Ninth and Tenth. And the 
District of Columbia Circuit has exercised appellate jurisdiction 
over interlocutory immunity appeals where the gist of the 
defendants’ argument was the factual one that they did not commit 
the constitutional tort. The decision below cannot be reconciled 
with these decisions.* 


1. a. In Unwinv. Campbell, 863 F.2d 124 (\st Cir. 988), 
the First Circuit addressed a state prisoner’s Section 1983 claim 
that the defendant police officers beat him while quelling a prison 
disturbance. See id. at 127. The district court had denied the 
defendants’ motion for summary judgment on qualified immunity 
grounds. On interlocutory appeal, the defendants’ “main arguments 
[were] that the undisputed facts gathered through discovery show 
that they could not have violated clearly established law because 
they had no contact, or only minimal contact, with [the plaintiff], 
or that any contact with him was no more than the result of their 
good faith efforts to quell a prison disturbance.” Jbid (emphasis 
added). The court of appeals thus posed “[tJhe question before us 
[as] whether, in light of th[e] [discovery] materials [before the 
district court], the district court erred in finding a genuine issue of 
material fact as to defendants’ entitlement to qualified immunity.” 
Id. at 128. 


In analyzing the case, then-Chief Judge Campbell, writing for 
the court, began by explaining that the constitutional law governing 
the defendants’ conduct was “clearly established” at the time of the 
events giving rise to the suit. 863 F.2d at 129. But the court 
nevertheless went on to hold that the exercise of appellate

“the qualified immunity right not to stand trial is just as effectively

declines to rule itself on the summary judgment motion.’” Jd. at 
132. The court therefore “conclude[d] that we have jurisdiction to 
review a district court’s denial of qualified immunity on grounds 
that a genuine issue of material fact exists as to the factual predicate 
of a qualified immunity claim.” Jbid. 


The court then proceeded to the merits, explaining that 
“[f]inding that we have jurisdiction to do so, we now address the 
question of whether, reading the pretrial record in the light most 
favorable to [plaintiff], a genuine issue of material fact exists as to 
whether defendants’ conduct violated [plaintiff s] clearly established

id. at 134-136), the court found that such an issue of fact existed 
as to several defendants because “all admit having physical contact 
with [plaintiff] during the time [plaintiff] was repeatedly beaten.” 
Id. at 134. But the court also held that two other defendants 
(Campbell and Furlone) were entitled to qualified immunity because 
“there is no evidence at all in the record showing that [Campbell] 
had any contact with [plaintiff]” and Furlone “likewise had no 
physical contact with [plaintiff].” Jd. at 133. “Because the 
undisputed facts show that Furlone and Campbell did not cause 
[plaintiff's] injuries, their conduct did not violate Clearly established 
law and thus they are entitled to qualified immunity.” /bid 
(emphasis added) (footnote omitted). 


Indeed, as to these latter two defendants the court specifically 
explained: 


This is a case where it has been shown not only that 
these two defendants did not violate law clearly 
established at the time of the event, but did not violate 
the law at all, then or now. It might be argued that 
defendants’ right to prevail “on the merits” forecloses 
their right to prevail on qualified immunity, thus barring 
the present interlocutory appeal. However, “the merits” 
and the issue of qualified immunity are inexorably 


8 


intertwined in this instance. To afford the two defendants 
immunity only if the law had been a little less favorable 
to them would seem ridiculous. 


Id. at 133 n.9 (emphasis in original). 


The First Circuit’s holding that interlocutory appellate 
jurisdiction exists to consider Section 1983 defendants’ claims of 
(as the court below put it) “we didn’t do it” was in no sense off- 
hand or ill-considered. To the contrary, the court rejected the 
dissenting view of then-Judge Breyer that “a defendant is not 
entitled to a ‘qualified immunity’ interlocutory appeal in respect to 
a pure fact-based ‘evidence sufficiency’ ruling.” 863 F.2d at 139 
(Breyer, J., dissenting). Judge Breyer explained — quite correctly 
— that there was “no real issue on appeal, in respect to the 
defendants Campbell and Furlone, other than the purely factual 
issue whether the evidence shows that they were present at the time 
plaintiff was hurt.” Jd. at 141. This, he believed, “is not an 
appropriate issue for us to resolve on an interlocutory appeal.” 
Ibid.*| The majority, of course, established the contrary view as 
the law in the First Circuit. 


Unwin therefore is indistinguishable from this case. In both, 
the plaintiff alleged that he was beaten by defendants. In both, the 
constitutional rule — beatings are impermissible — was clearly 
established at the time of the challenged conduct. In both, the 
defendants claimed that they were entitled to qualified immunity 
because, as a factual matter, they had no contact with the plaintiff. 
But in this case, the court held that it lacked appellate jurisdiction: 
in Unwin, the court held that it had jurisdiction and proceeded to 
address the merits of the defendants’ factual claims. 


b. The First Circuit has adhered to this approach 
consistently. In Caro v. Aponte-Roque, 878 F.2d 1 (ist Cir. 
1989), the plaintiff challenged an assertedly retaliatory discharge; 
claiming immunity, the defendant denied liability on the wholly 
factual ground that he lacked a retaliatory motive. Then-Judge 


* Then-Judge Breyer acknowledged, however, that “[t]he Supreme 
Court has not directly focused upon this question.” 863 F.2d at 137 
(discussing Mitchell); see id. at 138 (discussing Anderson).

Breyer, now writing for the court, noted that the law governing 
retaliatory discharges was settled and that “[t]he only issue in the 
case concerns motive.” Jd. at 2 (emphasis in original). And 
relying on Unwin, the court restated the holding that a “court 
Should examine evidence in respect to factual issues on 
interlocutory appeal of a denial of summary judgment.” Jbid. The 
court therefore explained that “[t]he single question that we must 
decide is whether the plaintiffs, in opposing the [defendant’s] 
motion for summary judgment, demonstrated the existence of a 
‘genuine’ and ‘material’ issue of fact warranting the district court’s 
proceeding to trial on the ‘qualified immunity’ question.” Ibid. 
After a careful review of the record (see id. at 3-4), the court held 
that a genuine issue of material fact did exist. 


Similarly, in Rogers v. Fair, 902 F.2d 140 (1st Cir. 1990), the 
plaintiff claimed that prison authorities discriminated on the basis 
of religion in denying him a furlough. The defendants’ motion for 
summary judgment on immunity grounds was denied. As the court 
described the case, 


Appellants ask us to reverse the denial of summary 
ju<gment because there is no evidence that their conduct 
could have violated the rights of the plaintiff even if those 
rights were clearly established. * * * The only question, 
therefore, is whether the plaintiff has presented sufficient 
facts to survive summary judgment on the issue of whether 
the religion of the sponsors was improperly considered in 
the denial of furlough. Because plaintiff, the nonmoving 
party, has failed to meet the normal summary judgment 
standard, we reverse and grant Summary judgment to the 
defendants. 


Id. at 142 (emphasis added). Relying on Unwin, the court noted 
that, assuming the governing constitutional law was settled, the 
plaintiff “must present more than mere [factual] allegations. He 
has not done so.” Jd. at 143. The court added that “[w]e have 
scoured the record on summary judgment and find no facts that 
[plaintiff] can use to support his allegations.” Jbid. The court 
therefore granted judgment for the defendants. 


Most recently, in Febus-Rodriguez v. Betancourt-Lebron, 14 
F.3d 87 (Ist Cir. 1994), the court reaffirmed that, when the 


10 


defendant appeals from the denial of a summary judgment motion 
asserting good faith immunity, “we must determine whether the 
plaintiffs have introduced sufficient evidence to create a genuine 
issue of material fact that [the defendants] violated [plaintiff's] 
constitutional rights.” Jd. at 91. The case involved a claim that a 
police superintendent and mayor failed adequately to supervise and 
train law enforcement officers; after a careful review of the record 
(see id. at 92-94), the court reversed and granted summary 
judgment because plaintiffs “failed to produce sufficient evidence 
to create a triable issue.” Jd. at 94. See id. at 95 (“the plaintiffs 
have not proffered any evidence that [the defendant] had actual or 
constructive knowledge of any training or supervisory 
deficiencies”). There was no suggestion that the controlling 
constitutional law was unclear. 


2. The Eighth Circuit reached an identical conclusion in 
Wright v. South Arkansas Regional Health Center, Inc., 800 F.2d 
199 (8th Cir. 1986). There, the plaintiff brought a Section 1983 
action claiming that he had been discharged in retaliation for the 
exercise of his First Amendment rights; “[dJefendant’s motion for 
summary judgment was based on the argument that there was no 
substantial evidence to support this theory.” Jd. at 202-203. 


Writing for the court, Judge Arnold noted that “there is no 
doubt that the right to be free from official retaliation for the 
exercise of First Amendment rights has been clear all along.” 800 
F.2d at 203. Judge Arnold accordingly explained that 


The question presented is thus both less clearly “legal,” 
as opposed to “factual,” than the issue in Mitchell, and 
less clearly separable from the merits, one of the 
requirements for immediate appealability under Cohen. 
We nevertheless hold that an interlocutory appeal is 
proper. If this is a step beyond Mitchell, it is a small 
one, and one fully consistent with the spirit and purpose 
of the Supreme Court in that opinion. It is important for 
public officials to be shielded from the burden of trial on 
insubstantial claims. Their right not to be forced to trial 
will be lost if an immediate appeal is not allowed. And 
the issue is, on close analysis, separable from the merits. 
To put it in terms of the present case, the issue is not 


a 
a4 


11 


whether defendant was improperly motivated in fact, but, 
rather, whether, when all the facts are viewed in the light 
most favorable to plaintiff, there is any genuine issue, 
triable to a jury, of improper motivation. There is a 
sense in which this sort of question is one of “law,” just 
as the question whether someone is entitled to a directed 
verdict is in some sense one of “law.” 


Id. at 203 (emphasis added). 


With this standard in mind, the court went on to review the 
record. It found that “[p]laintiff points to no evidence that [the 
defendant’s] motivation was unconstitutional.” 800 F.2d at 204. 
The court therefore concluded that “the record simply does not 
create a genuine issue of material fact.” Jd. at 205. It accordingly 
reversed the district court’s judgment and directed dismissal of the 
complaint. /bid. Again, that case is not distinguishable from this 
one: the controlling law was settled, and the only issue was a 
factual one (to paraphrase the Seventh Circuit’s words, the 
defendant’s claim of “I didn’t think it”). And again, the Eighth 
Circuit found jurisdiction proper; the Seventh Circuit did not. 


Like the First Circuit, the Eighth Circuit has consistently 
adhered to this approach. Thus in Tagstrom v. Enocksen, 857 F.2d 
502 (8th Cir. 1988), the plaintiff alleged that defendant exhibited 
deliberate indifference to the medical needs of a person injured in 
a high speed chase. The district court denied a motion to dismiss 
on immunity grounds, finding that there was “circumstantial 
evidence” of indifference. See id. at 503. The court of appeals 
reversed. Reviewing the discovery materials, the court found that 
“[t]here is nothing in the record to indicate that [the defendant] 
deprived [plaintiff] of any constitutional right” because defendant 
was, as a factual matter, “in no way deliberately indifferent to 
[plaintiff's] medical needs.” Jd. at 504, 503. The court therefore

The court went on to hold that appellate jurisdiction existed to

B. The Decision Below is in Conflict With This Court’s 
Decisions 

1. Given the inconsistency of the Seventh Circuit's approach 
with the clear weight of authority in the courts of appeals, it is 
hardly a surprise that the decision below also is in clear tension 
with this Court’s decisions. In fact, this Court has twice 
recognized that if, after discovery, the plaintiff has failed to 
uncover evidence that the defendant did what he was alleged in the 
complaint to have done, the defendant may obtain summary 
judgment on the basis of qualified immunity. In Mitchell, the 
Court held that “[e]ven if the plaintiff's complaint adequately 
alleges the commission of acts that violated clearly established law, 
the defendant is entitled to summary judgment if discovery fails to 
uncover evidence sufficient to create a genuine issue as to whether 
the defendant in fact committed those acts.” 472 U.S. at 526. 
Similarly, in Anderson, the Court stated that “if the actions [the 
defendant] claims he took are different from those the [plaintiffs] 
allege (and are actions that a reasonable officer could have believed 
lawful), then discovery may be necessary before [the defendant’s] 
motion for summary judgment on qualified immunity grounds can 
be resolved.” 483 U.S. at 646 n.6. The Court thus indicated that 
a Summary judgment motion would lie after discovery. 


Accordingly, qualified immunity protects the official whose 
actions did not violate clearly established law (see Harlow, 457 
U.S. at 818), whether the reason is that the law was in a state of 
flux or simply that the official did not commit the acts alleged by 


19 


the plaintiff. The necessary implication of this conclusion is that 
an appeal should be available when such a summary judgment 
motion is denied. After all, Mitchell and Anderson indicate that a 
defendant is entitled to summary judgment on qualified immunity 
grounds if the plaintiff fails to adduce evidence creating a genuine 
issue of fact on the question whether the defendant committed acts 
that violated clearly settled law. And “because ‘[t]he entitlement 
is an immunity from suit rather than a mere defense to liability’” 
(Hunter v. Bryant, 112 S. Ct. 534, 536 (1992), quoting Mitchell, 
472 U.S. at 526 (emphasis added by the Court)), the defendant 
should have an opportunity to test on appeal a wrongful denial of 
that entitlement by the district court. 


Any other conclusion, as the First Circuit noted in Unwin, will 
lead to perverse results. We know from Anderson that the 
immunity inquiry must look to the particular conduct engaged in by 
the defendant. See 483 U.S. at 641. A court therefore must 
determine whether what the defendant actually did violated clearly 
established law; as the Seventh Circuit itself acknowledged (see 
Elliott, 937 F.2d at 342), every Circuit has rejected the rule of 
Bonitz v. Fair, 804 F.2d 164 (1st Cir. 1986), overruled by Unwin, 
863 F.2d at 130-133, which had held that the courts of appeals 
could not look beyond the allegations of the complaint in reviewing 
the denial of a motion seeking summary judgment on immunity 
grounds. Instead, courts of appeals examine the entire summary 
judgment record. 


So, for example, if a plaintiff alleges that the defendant 
engaged in a particular act (say, that he conducted a warrantless 
search) and the defendant responds that he conducted a different act 
altogether (say, that he obtained a warrant before conducting the 
search), the court will have to determine whether the plaintiff's 
version is supported by the record before determining whether the 
defendant's conduct was inconsistent with clearly established law. 
If the plaintiff's version finds no support in the record, the court 
will have to determine whether the conduct as described by the 
defendant violated clearly settled law. And there is no doubt that 
appeal would lie if the legality of the defendant’s conduct had been 
unclear, because the question then would be whether the defendant 
had “acted in the shadow of legal uncertainty.” Elliott, 937 F.2d 
at 342. In that light, it makes no sense at all to deny the defendant 


a 


20 


an appeal because it was clear that his conduct was legal; “[t]o 
afford the * * * defendant[] immunity only if the law had been a 
little less favorable to [him] would seem ridiculous.” Unwin, 863 
F.2d at 133 n.9. 


2. The Seventh Circuit nevertheless appeared to believe that 
an appeal from a denial of a qualified immunity summary judgment 
motion premised on such a defense is improper because it presents 
no issue different from the merits. The court reasoned that “there 
is no separate ‘right not to be tried’ on the question whether the 
defendants did the deeds alleged; that is precisely the question for 
trial.” Elliott, 937 F.2d at 341 (emphasis in original). As this 
Court recognized in Mitchell, however, “a question of immunity is 
separate from the merits of the underlying action for purposes of 
the Cohen test even though a reviewing court must consider the 
plaintiff's factual allegations in resolving the immunity issue.” 472 
U.S. at 528-29. This is so because “it follows from the recognition 
that qualified immunity is in part an entitlement not to be forced to 
litigate the consequences of official conduct that a claim of 
immunity is conceptually distinct from the merits of the plaintiff's 
claim that his rights have been violated.” Jd. at 527-28. And as 
the Eighth Circuit explained, “the issue is, on close analysis, 
separable from the merits. * * * [T]he issue is not whether 
defendant [engaged in the acts alleged] in fact, but, rather, whether, 
when all the facts are viewed in the light most favorable to 
plaintiff, there is any genuine issue, triable to a jury, of improper 
[conduct].” Wright, 800 F.2d at 203. | 


For similar reasons, the Seventh Circuit was wrong in 
reasoning that a defense that the defendant did not commit the acts 
alleged by the plaintiff raises factual issues that do not qualify for 
interlocutory appeal under Mitchell’s holding that “a district court’s 
denial of a claim of qualified immunity, to the extent that it turns 
on an issue of law, is an appealable ‘final decision’ within the 
meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1291.” 472 U.S. at 530. When there is 
no genuine issue of material fact, the defendant’s entitlement to 
summary judgment is an issue of law under Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. 
Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986); Anderson 
v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986); Matsushita 
Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 
(1986). This principle applies to motions for summary judgment 


21 
based on qualified immunity just as much as it does to other 
summary judgment motions. See, e.g., Wright, 800 F.2d at 203: 
Martin, 812 F.2d at 1434. A properly supported qualified 
immunity summary judgment motion based on a defense-that the 
defendant did not commit the acts alleged by the plaintiff thus 
presents a “question of law.” Elder v. Holloway, 1148S. Ct. 1019. 
1023 (1994). 


3. The policies underlying this Court’s qualified immunity 
decisions would be thwarted by a rule precluding immediate appeal 
from the denial of a summary judgment motion, made after 
discovery, premised on the defense that the defendant did not 
commit the acts alleged by the plaintiff. One of the primary 
purposes of qualified immunity is to permit “the dismissal of 
insubstantial lawsuits without trial.” Harlow, 457 U.S. at 814. 
“The qualified immunity doctrine recognizes that officials can act 
without fear of harassing litigation * * * only if unjustified lawsuits 
are quickly terminated.” Davis v. Scherer, 468 U.S. 183, 195 
(1984). As a consequence, the Court “repeatedly ha[s] stressed the 
importance of resolving immunity questions at the earliest possible 
Stage in litigation.” Hunter, 112 S. Ct. at 536.’ 


Surely, as a matter of policy, an official who did nothing even 
arguably wrong is at least as deserving of the interlocutory appeal 
authorized by Mitchell as is an official whose conduct was 
arguably, but not clearly, unconstitutional at the time he acted. And 
where the plaintiff cannot adduce any evidence establishing that the 
official committed the acts alleged, a motion for summary judgment 
should be granted, or the appeal of its denial allowed, to further the 
policy served by Mitchell of sparing the official and the courts the 
time and expense of a trial, the result of which is a foregone 
conclusion. “[T]he purpose of qualified immunity is to remove 
most civil liability actions, except those where the official clearly 
broke the law, from the legal process well in advance of the 


” A governmental defendant's right not to stand trial is just as 
effectively lost if the court of appeals does not entertain an appeal from 
a legally erroneous denial of summary judgment, as here, as it is if the 
district court declines to rule on the summary judgment motion in the first 
place. Unwin, 863 F.2d at 132. 


22 


submission of facts to a jury.” Slattery v. Rizzo, 939 F.2d 213, 
216 (4th Cir. 1991) (Powell, J., sitting by designation) (citing 
Mitchell, 472 U.S. at 526). 


The contrary rule announced by the Seventh Circuit, if allowed 
to stand, would have significant practical consequences. Cases like 
Mitchell, in which the constitutionality of the defendant’s actions is 
genuinely unsettled at the time he acts, are relatively rare. More 
common are cases involving factually insupportable accusations by 
disgruntled plaintiffs, such as the allegations by respondent here. 
As this Court recognized in Anderson, Mitchell, Davis, and 
Harlow, such cases, if not expeditiously terminated, pose a 
substantial danger of chilling public officials in the legitimate 
exercise of their governmental duties. Yet it is appeals from 
precisely this class of cases that the court below has refused to 
entertain. This is a perverse result, for it forces the victims of the 
most insubstantial claims to go through trial. 


In addition, the decision below does more than add to the 
burdens of public officials; it also creates a complex rule that will, 
in practice, be extraordinarily difficult to apply. It often will be 
unclear whether the defendant genuinely “acted in the shadow of 
legal uncertainty” (Elliott, 937 F.2d at 342), and thus has a proper 
appeal, or merely “ma[d]Je * * * a stab at presenting a legal issue” 
(ibid.), and therefore does not. Since qualified immunity is a 
highly fact-specific inquiry (see Anderson, 483 U.S. at 641), and 
there is usually some dispute (whether material or not) about the 
facts, the parties and the court often will have to devote 
considerable effort to determining whether the defendant is really 
making an argument that he did not commit the acts alleged by the 
plaintiff. While this Court “has expressly rejected efforts to reduce 
the finality requirement of [28 U.S.C.] § 1291 to a case-by-case 
determination of whether a particular ruling should be subject to 
appeal” (Richardson-Merrell Inc. v. Koller, 472 U.S. 424, 439 
(1985)), such a determination will be required for qualified 
immunity appeals after this decision. 


In short, there is no basis in caselaw, logic, or public policy 
for the Seventh Circuit’s holding. The issue is a recurring and 
important one. Further review therefore is in order.

may be reviewed on appeal. When asked at oral argument if they 
could lose the factual dispute and still prevail, defendants’ lawyer 
answered no. In consequence, we lack appellate jurisdiction over 
the contention that the defendants did not commit or abet battery. 


The excessive force claim must be distinguished from the 
plaintiff's argument that the defendants lacked probable cause to 
arrest him. It is undisputed that an officer found plaintiff, 
apparently intoxicated, hitting his head against a building, and took 
him into custody. These facts imply disorderly conduct, which 
supports an arrest. Plaintiff contends that he was not drunk but 
was suffering from an insulin reaction, and that the police should 
have figured this out. The magistrate judge, presiding by consent 
under 28 U.S.C. §636(c), concluded that plaintiff had not adduced 
sufficient evidence to dispute the officers’ version of the arrest-- 
which, if accepted, entitles them to immunity if not to prevail 
outright. Hunter v. Bryant, 112 S. Ct. 534, 537 (1991); Mahoney 
v. Kesery, 976 F.2d 1054, 1057-59 (7th Cir. 1992). But the judge 
concluded that because the excessive force claim had to be tried, 
and because the plaintiff might come up with more evidence before 
trial, the false arrest claim also should be tried. 


This approach is mistaken. Summary judgement is not a dis- 
cretionary remedy. If the plaintiff lacks enough evidence, summary 


3a 


judgment must be granted. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 
U.S. 242, 249-51 (1986); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 
(1986). Immunity claims should be resolved as early in the case as 
possible-- and by the court rather than the jury. Elder v. Holloway, 
114 S. Ct. 1019, 1023 (1994): Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800. 
818 (1982); Elliott, 937 F.2d at 344-45. On the facts that this 
record presents, taken in the light most favorable to plaintiff, an 
arrest did not “violate clearly established . . .rights of which a 
reasonable person would have known”. Harlow, 457 U.S. at 818. 
The three appellants therefore are entitled to qualified immunity on 
the false arrest claim. 


The judgment on the false arrest claim is reversed, and the 
appeal is dismissed to the extent appellants seek review of the 
excessive force claim.

MEMORANDUM OPINION AND ORDER 


This matter is before the court on defendants’ motion for 
Summary judgment. For the reasons set forth below, the motion is 
denied. 


Background 


The five individual defendants in this action under 42 U.S.C. 
§1983 are John Heidelmeier (“Heidelmeier”), Tyson Johnson 
(“Johnson”), G. Esposito (“Esposito”), Robert Deevey (“Deevey”) 
and John Payne (“Payne”). All were police officers of the Village 
of Villa Park, Illinois (“Village”) at the time of the events giving 
rise to this dispute. Village is named as a defendant only in 
plaintiffs pendent claims under state law. Since the 
commencement of this lawsuit, foul counts of plaintiff's ten-count 
complaint have been dismissed, including a claim against Village 
under §1983. Defendants now seek judgment in their favor on the 


Sa 


remaining counts of the complaint. The claims in question are the 
alleged violations of Fourth Amendment rights in Count I, and state 
law claims of false arrest, false imprisonment, malicious 
prosecution, assault and battery. Since those claims are apparently 
subsumed into the claim of “willful and wanton misco duct” in 
Count V, that claim would not survive the dismissal of the other 
counts. 


Facts relevant to this motion are few. It is undisputed that on 
November 6, 1990, Heidelmeier responded to a report of an 
apparently intoxicated man in or near an intersection in Village. 
Upon his arrival, Heidelmeier found plaintiff hitting his head or 
forehead against the door of a building. Heidelmeier then 
approached plaintiff in an attempt to ascertain whether there was a 
problem. From this point, the parties’ versions of the facts 
diverge. 


Heidelmeier states that when he asked plaintiff what was 
happening, plaintiff placed his hands in his pockets and began to 
fidget with something that later turned out to be a pocketknife in a 
scabbard. When Heidelmeier attempted a pat-down search, plaintiff 
struck him in the arms with a clenched fist. At that point, 
Heidelmeier decided to place plaintiff under arrest and he forced 
plaintiff to the ground in order to handcuff him behind his back. 
Heidelmeier could not accomplish this task alone because of 
plaintiff's struggling. In the interim, the other individual 
defendants had arrived on the scene and they assisted Heidelmeier 
in handcuffing plaintiff and placing him in a squad car. Defendants 
state that due to plaintiff's extreme resistance to their attempts to 
place him under arrest, it took the efforts of all five officers to 
control him. 


Defendants contend that no one struck, beat or abused plaintiff 
while in the parking lot where he was arrested. Defendants 
Johnson, Payne and Deevey add that at that point their involvement 
in the arrest ended and that they saw no one abuse plaintiff at the 
police station either. PX D-F. Shortly after plaintiff arrived at the 
police station, paramedics were called. Plaintiff spent several days 
in the hospital for treatment of multiple trauma and several broken 
ribs. DX A at 76; PXF. 


6a 


Plaintiff contends that he was experiencing an insulin reaction 
at the time of his arrest. His physician has testified that as a 
consequence, plaintiff's recollection of relevant events would be 
very poor and he would probably have been greatly weakened. 
However, in a small percentage of cases, a person undergoing an 
insulin reaction may show greater than normal strength. According 
to his doctor, during an insulin reaction, plaintiff could be coherent 
for brief periods of time and he could engage in conversation and 
control motor functions. The motor deficit would resemble that 
caused by intoxication. DX B at 5-21. Consistent with that 
testimony, plaintiff disclaims recollection of specific actions taken 
by defendants. However, he claims that he can recall experiencing 
extreme pain at various points, as when his arm was placed in an 
armlock and he was forced to the ground. Plaintiff denies having 
resisted the officers’ efforts as they say he did, and he contends that 
the beating and verbal abuse continued after his arrival at Village’s 
police station. Plaintiff's expert witness would testify that 
defendants used excessive force in the arrest and otherwise 
responded inappropriately to the little risk that plaintiff posed. 


Plaintiff was charged with criminal offenses of disorderly 
conduct, battery and resisting arrest. At the time of his trial in the 
state court, plaintiff asserted the defense that he lacked the requisite 
mental state for the offenses charged. Plaintiff also testified that he 
had no recollection of what transpired from the time he first felt the 
insulin reaction until he noticed he was being dragged up the stairs 
of Village’s police station. In the interim, he only remembered 
feeling a lot of pain. DX A at 69-70, 78. However, plaintiff 
recalled seeing Heidelmeier and Esposito while being beaten at the 
police station. DX A at 73-74, 83. Having heard each side’s 
version of the events, and the medical testimony concerning the 
effects of an insulin reaction, the trial court found that plaintiff 
came into contact, struck, and resisted a peace officer. Although 
the judge found defendants’ testimony credible and felt that all 
other elements of the charges against plaintiff had been proven, he 
concluded that the State had not proven beyond a reasonable doubt 
that plaintiff acted knowingly. The basis of the finding as to mental 
state was the testimony concerning plaintiff's diabetes and insulin 
shock. DX B at 30-33.

Claims of Excessive Force, Assault and Battery 


The present motion for summary judgment asks the court to 
take into account plaintiff's inability to recall events that transpired 
prior to his arrival at the police station. Since plaintiff cannot 
recall specific details, only the individual defendants can provide 
eyewitness testimony concerning the circumstances of his arrest. 
All defendants having testified that no one beat or abused plaintiff, 
they ask the court to dismiss the claims of Johnson, Deevey and 
Payne. These three officers attest that they played no role in 
processing plaintiff after his arrest and plaintiff specifically recalls 
only Heidelmeier and Esposito. Plaintiff challenges the veracity of 
the three officers’ affidavit testimony and observes that even if an 
officer did not actually participate in a beating, a jury may consider 
an excessive force claim against him if the officer stood by and 
allowed others to beat the plaintiff. Byrd v. Brishke, 466 F.2d 6, 
11 (7th Cir. 1972); Wilson v. City of Chicago, 707 F. Supp. 379, 
385 (N.D. Ill. 1989). 


Regarding the claims against Heidelmeier and Esposito, 
defendants posit that the only competent testimony concerning the 
circumstances of the arrest is their own. Defendants argue that the 
court should not credit plaintiff's testimony, as his own doctor 
testified that he would not rely on anything plaintiff told him about 
the incident. Defendants further suggest that plaintiff should be 
estopped from taking a position in this litigation that is inconsistent 
with his defense to the criminal charges in the state court, but this 
court disagrees that such inconsistency exists. As already noted, 
plaintiff testified in the state court to feelings of great pain during 
the arrest and to some specific recollections of mistreatment at the 
police station. While present ability to recall in greater detail may 
diminish his credibility, there is no obvious contradiction of his 
former testimony such that his testimony cannot be considered here. 


Summary judgment shall be entered “if the pleadings, 
depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, 
together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine 
issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to 
a judgment as a matter of law.” Fed.R.Civ.P. 56(c). In examining 
the record, all reasonable inferences from it are drawn in the light 
most favorable to the nonmovant. Maxwell v. City of Indianapolis, 


8a 


998 F.2d 431, 433 (7th Cir. 1993). Plaintiff has the burden, 
however, of establishing a genuine issue for trial. 


Applying these principles here, defendants are denied summary 
judgment on the claims of excessive force, assault and battery. 
Plaintiff lacks eyewitness testimony supporting his theory of the 
events surrounding his arrest, but there is sufficient circumstantial 
evidence supporting his theory of the case. While the culpability 
of particular defendants may vary, on the present record, it cannot 


be determined whether the claims against them should be dismissed. 


Claims of Unlawful Arrest, False 
Imprisonment and Malicious Prosecution 


In moving for summary judgment on these claims, defendants 
rely on several propositions of law, all of which in themselves are 
correct. First, if defendants establish probable cause for plaintiff's 
arrest, his claims under §1983 of false arrest, false imprisonment 
and malicious prosecution necessarily fail. Biddle v. Martin, 992 


"F.2d 673, 678 (7th Cir. 1993); Mark v. Furay, 769 F.2d 1266, 


1268-1269 (7th Cir. 1985). The existence of probable cause is also 
a defense to state law claims of false arrest, false imprisonment and 
malicious prosecution. Myatt v. City of Chicago, 816 F. Supp. 
1259, 1267 (N.D. Ill. 1992) (citing Mosley v. La Mastus, 741 F. 
Supp. 724 (N.D. Ill. 1990)); Jones v. Village of Villa Park, 815 F. 
Supp. 249, 252-253 (N.D. Ill. 1993). Probable cause existed if at 
the moment of the arrest, the facts and circumstances within 
defendants’ knowledge were sufficient to warrant a prudent man in 
believing that a crime was being or had been committed. See 
Hunter v. Bryant, __ U.S. ___, 112 S.Ct. 534, 537 (1991). 


Besides challenging plaintiff's showing of probable cause, 
defendants raise qualified immunity as a defense. Qualified 
immunity shields law enforcement officers from damages if they 
“’reasonably but mistakenly conclude that probable cause is 
present.’” Jd. (quoting Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 641 
(1987)). If a case involves questions as to whether probable cause 
existed to support officers’ actions, the case should not be permitted 
to go to trial if there is amy reasonable basis to conclude that

probable cause existed. Biddle, 992 F.2d at 676.1 On the other 
hand, claims of qualified immunity are not susceptible to resolution 
on summary judgment if there is substantial Overlap between the 
issue of immunity and the principal issue on the merits. Maxwel/ 
v. City of Indianapolis, 998 F.2d 431, 435-436 (7th Cir. 1993). 
See also Meyer v. Robinson, 992 F.2d 734. 737 (7th Cir. 1993). 
Thus, in a false arrest case, an officer might be denied summary 
judgment on a qualified immunity defense if the evidence raises 
questions as to whether a reasonable police officer would have 
found probable cause for an arrest. See Maxwell, 998 F.2d at 436: 
Mahoney v. Kesery, 976 F.2d 1054, 1058 (7th Cir. 1992). This 
result may obtain even if all officers involved in the arrest 
subjectively believe the arrestee is committing or has committed a 
crime. dd. 


The establishment of probable cause for a disorderly conduct 
arrest requires only a reasonable belief that the arrestee’s conduct 
threatened a breach of the peace. See id. at 677-678. While 
defendants maintain that the test is easily met here, again that 
contention rests on their own testimony. Plaintiff has not been able 
to summon eyewitness testimony to the contrary, but he has a

weakened by the insulin reaction and unable to summon the level

detrimental to his claims of lack of cause to arrest than it is to his 
claims of excessive force, since the fact of his injuries supports an 
inference of excessive force used. As determined above. the 
excessive force claims must be tried. Since there is substantial
