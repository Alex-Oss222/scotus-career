SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Some loss of earning capacity is a prerequisite to any 
award of LHWCA compensation. Except in the situations 
in which the economic effects of injury are conclusively 
presumed,> proof that a work-related impairment has 
lessened the workers’ ability to earn is essential. Without 
that proof, no statutory disability exists. Without disabil- 
ity, no award of compensation can be entered. 


Despite the fact that the primary focus of the 
LHWCA is economic, the Ninth Circuit has ruled that 
compensation initially premised upon diminished earn- 
ing capacity must continue at the same rate even after the 


5 See 33 U.S.C. §§ 908(c)(1-20); Potomac Electric Power Com- 
pany v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 449 
U.S. 268 (1980). 


lost ability to earn is regained unless - and only unless - 
the worker’s physical condition improves. 


The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of Section 22 is not 
only at odds with that of the Fourth. It violates the basic 
rule that courts must give effect to the clear meaning of 
statutes as written. It also strays far from the guidance 
provided by the past opinions of this Court, the most 
recent decisions issued by the other Circuit Courts and - 
most significantly - the history and intent of the Act 
itself. 


Nothing in the history of the LHWCA or its many 
interpretations by this Court supports the view that Con- 
gress intended to distinguish between physical and eco- 
nomic conditions when it established the two 
prerequisites to award modification. Just as any mistake 
of fact may justify modification, so too does any change 
in the circumstances - the “conditions” - which together 
justified the original award suffice. As noted by Professor 
Larson, 


Although the beginning point and usually the 
main concern of an inquiry into change in con- 
dition for reopening purposes is claimant’s rela- 
tive physical condition, and although a number 
of states strictly confine change in condition to 
physical condition, [footnote omitted] it should 
not be forgotten that disability in the compensa- 
tion sense has an economic as well as a medical 
component; [footnote omitted] accordingly a 
change in claimant's ability to get or hold 
employment, or to maintain his earlier earning 
level, could logically be considered a ‘change in 
condition,’ even though claimant's physical con- 
dition may have remained unchanged. 


_ 


3 Larson, The Law of Workmen’s Compensation, § 81.31(e), 
p. 15-1030 (1993). 


The Ninth Circuit’s opinion identifies two bases for 
its conclusion that physical change is a prerequisite to 
modification. The first - the canon of statutory construc- 
tion that a lack of Congressional response to uniform 
court interpretations of statutory language implies 
approval - was misapplied. The decisions identified by 
the Ninth Circuit did not hold that physical change was a 
necessary prerequisite to adjustment of LHWCA awards.® 
This Court warned about the dangers of reliance upon 
dicta in Cohens v. Com. of Virginia, 19 U.S. 264, 369 (1821). 


It is a maxim not to be disregarded, that 
general expressions, in every opinion, are to be 
taken in connection with the case in which those 
expressions are used. If they go beyond the case, 
they may be respected, but ought not to control 
the judgment in a subsequent suit when the 
very point is presented for decision. The reason 
of this maxim is obvious. The question actually 
before the Court is investigated with care, and

sation Comm'n, 64 F.2d 84 (9th Cir. 1933), as requiring proof of

McCormick was both more narrow and, in this Petitioner’s judg- 
ment, unassailable. McCormick S.S. Co.’s request to enjoin mod- 
ification was granted because the worker had shown only a 
“decrease in wages earned” and failed to present any evidence 
of a “decrease in earning capacity.” 64 F.2d, at 86. In addition, 
the McCormick decision contains an express acknowledgment 
that adjustments to existing awards of permanent partial dis- 
ability may be properly ordered when changes in wage-earning 
capacity are proven. 


_ 


considered in its full extent. Other principles 
which may serve to illustrate it, are considered 
in their relation to the case decided, but their 
possible bearing on all other cases is seldom 
completely investigated. 


The Ninth Circuit ignored that warning in this case. 


The second underpinning of the Ninth Circuit’s nar- 
row interpretation of Section 22 must have been the unex- 
pressed belief that Congress intended Section 22’s scope 
to be narrow. The history of the LHWCA does not sup- 
port this view. 


Future changes to vocational circumstances are at 
least as likely as changes in physical condition. Either 
type of change can affect earning capacity and both may 
justify benefit adjustment. Absolutely nothing in the 
LHWCA’s lengthy history implies that Congress intended 
that injured workers who “recover” economically should 
maintain the benefits lost to those who recover physically. 
Nothing in the history suggests that only the medically 
stable should suffer when the combined effects of injuries 
and changes in economic realities dramatically decrease 
earning abilities. The Ninth Circuit’s opinion resulted 
from an over-focus on traditional rules of finality and res 
judicata. Those rules have been displaced by Section 22. If 
the prospect of an uncertain future troubles the parties, 
Congress has provided a single remedy: settlement. 


In both legal and general usage, the normal meaning 
of “conditions” is “circumstances,” precisely the term 
used by both House and Senate Committees in describing 
Section 22’s scope. The Ninth Circuit should have applied 


this natural reading of the statute and recognized that 


10 


any change to any of the circumstances upon which a 
determination of wage-earning capacity depends may 
justify modification. 


ARGUMENT 
THE STATUTORY SCHEME 


From its inception the purpose of the LHWCA has 
been to provide injured workers with compensation dur- 
ing periods of inability to earn earlier wages and has 
measured the amounts payable by the degree of economic 
harm sustained. The LHWCA’s approach to determining 
the amounts payable as compensation for work-related 
injury was reviewed at length by this Court in Potomac 
Electric Power Company, supra. 


The basic scheme is straightforward. Compensation 
is paid for “disability,” an economic concept. Except in 
the so-called “scheduled” cases for which Congress has 
conclusively determined the degree of economic harm 
caused by injury, compensation is not payable under the 
LHWCA unless the injury lessens the employee’s earning 
power. See 33 U.S.C. § 902(10); Liberty Mutual Insurance 
Co. v. Commercial Union Insurance Co., 978 F.2d 750 (1st 
Cir. 1992); McBride v. Eastman Kodak Co., 844 F.2d 797 
(D.C. Cir. 1988); Fleetwood, supra; and Stevens v. Director, 
Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 909 F.2d 1256 
(9th Cir. 1990), cert. denied sub nom., Lockheed Shipbuilding 
Co. v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensaton Programs, 
498 U.S. 1073 (1991). 


The amount of compensation payable is determined 
by the difference between the “weekly wage” calculated 


11 


in accordance with 33 U.S.C. Section 910 and the worker’s 
post-injury earning capacity. “Earning capacity” is mea- 
sured by actual post-injury earnings unless it is deter- 
mined that actual earnings do not fairly and reasonably 
reflect earning power. In such cases, the Act requires the 
factfinder to consider the degree of physical impairment, 
the worker’s customary employment, and “any other fac- 
tors or circumstances in the case which may affect [the 
worker’s] capacity to earn wages in his disabled condi- 
tion, including the effect of disability as it may naturally 
extend into the future” and fix a reasonable wage-earning 
capacity figure. See 33 U.S.C. § 908(h). 


Except in cases involving “scheduled” injuries, the 
mechanism for measuring the duration of entitlement is 
as flexible and case-specific as that for determining the 
amounts payable. Benefits for the type of permanent par- 
tial disability originally awarded Respondent Rambo are 
“payable during the continuance of such partial disabil- 
ity,” ie., for as long as the “incapacity because of injury 
to earn the wages which the employee was receiving at 
the time of injury in the same or any other employment” 
continues. 33 U.S.C. §§ 902(10) and 908(c)(21). 


The current version of the LHWCA contains only one 
mechanism - Section 22 - for adjusting or terminating 
benefits payable pursuant to an award. If “change in 
conditions” or “mistake in a determination of fact” is 
demonstrated and unless the parties agree to an adjusted 
payment, formal hearing conducted by an Administrative 
Law Judge in accordance with the requirements of the 
Administrative Procedures Act must occur and the 
Administrative Law Judge must reapply Section 8(h), 


reassess the worker’s permanent earning capacity, and if 


12 


that capacity to earn has changed, order an appropriate 
adjustment to the original award. 33 U.S.C. § 919(d). The 
scope of any appeal of the Administrative Law Judge’s 
decision to the Benefits Review Board or a Court of 
Appeals is limited to a review for errors of law and 
adherence to the substantial evidence standard. Port of 
Portland v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Pro- 
grams, 932 F.2d 836, 838 (9th Cir. 1991). 


Any attempt by an employer to unilaterally lessen or 
terminate payments due in accordance with an existing 
award is subject to enforcement proceedings and the 
probable assessment of substantial penalties. 33 U.S.C. 
§§ 914(f), 918, and 921(d); see also Tidelands Marine Service 
v. Patterson, 719 F.2d 126 (5th Cir. 1983); Providence Wash-

Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals 


The Seventh Circuit’s decisions are mixed and not 
particularly instructive. Eifler v. Office of Workers’ Compen- 
sation Programs, 926 F.2d 663 (7th Cir. 1991), presumes 
that physical change is required for modification. On the 
other hand, Pearce v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensa- 
tion Programs, 647 F.2d 716, at 727. n.16 (7th Cir. 1981), 
suggests that changes in interest rates may qualify as a 
“change in conditions.” Amax Coal Company v. Franklin, 
957 F.2d 355, at 358 (7th Cir. 1992), acknowledges that 
“the reopening provision is to be interpreted generously” 
but implies that the generosity was intended to extend 
only to the claimant. 


Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals 


Although far from necessary to their holdings, Ver-

District of Columbia Circuit 


The District of Columbia Circuit appears aligned 
with those approving Fleetwood’s analysis. In Washington 
Terminal Co. v. Hoage, 79 F.2d 158, at 160-61 (D.C. Cir.

1935), the Court concluded that modification was appro- 
priate despite no change in physical condition when a 
claimant started to use glasses after receiving an award 
for uncorrected vision loss. The decision in Chapman v. 
Griffith-Consumers Co., 107 F.2d 263, at 266 (D.C. Cir. 
1939), characterized the delayed appearance of persons 
entitled to death benefits as a “change in conditions” 
sufficient for modification of an order requiring payment 
of “in lieu of dependents” money to the Special Fund. 
Randall v. Comfort Control, Inc., 725 F.2d 791, at 794 (D.C.

Hoage, Chapman, and Randall are not mentioned when

CONCLUSION 


As used in Section 22, “conditions” is intended to 
encompass all of the circumstances determinative of earn- 
ing capacity. Neither plain usage nor the history and 
purposes of the LHWCA permit a construction which 
both strips the chosen term of its plural form and inserts 
a limiting proviso not selected by Congress. To paraphrase 
this Court’s concluding comment in Director, Office of 


35 


Workers’ Compensation Programs v. Rasmussen, 440 U.S. 29, 
47 (1979), when Congress has put down its pen, a court 
may neither rewrite Congress’s words nor insert addi- 
tional terms. 


The Administrative Law Judge determined that 
Respondent Rambo’s vocational skills and job oppor- 
tunities had changed following the date of the original 
award. He found that these changes had increased 
Respondent Rambo’s earning capacity to a degree that he 
no longer experienced a compensable disability and, as 
instructed by the statute, terminated benefits. All find- 
ings were supported by substantial evidence. The termi- 
nation of benefits based upon a “change in conditions” 
was in accordance with law. The Ninth Circuit’s opinion 
must be reversed.
