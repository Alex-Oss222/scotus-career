STATEMENT 


Petitioners are law enforcement officers who were sued 
under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for the allegedly excessive use of 
force. They sought summary judgment on qualified immunity 
grounds, asserting that their actions did not violate clearly 
established law because there was no evidence that their 
conduct was inconsistent with any legal standard. The district 
court denied the motion. The court of appeals then held that 
it lacked jurisdiction to entertain petitioners’ interlocutory 
appeal. This holding is logically insupportable, threatens to 
frustrate the purposes of the qualified immunity doctrine, and 
cannot be reconciled with the consistent course of this Court’s 
decisions applying the rule of finality. It should be set aside.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


1. The fundamental premise of the decision below — 
that immunity comes into play only when the defendant 
official maintains that the law under which he acted was 
unsettled — is manifestly wrong. Repeated decisions of this 


* The court did, however, reverse the district court’s denial of

before trial.” Pet. App. 2a. 


6 


Court make clear that the immunity doctrine protects all 
officials who did not violate clearly established law, a group 
that necessarily includes defendants who did not violate the 
law at all because they did not commit the acts alleged or 
because those acts were lawful. The Court thus held in 
Siegert v. Gilley, 500 U.S. 226 (1991), that a suit must be 
dismissed on immunity grounds when it fails to state a 
violation of law, and it made clear in Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 
U.S. 511 (1985), and Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635 
(1987), that immunity entitles the defendant to prevail when, 
after discovery, the plaintiff has failed to discover evidence 
that the defendant did what he was alleged in the complaint 
to have done. 


Those decisions follow necessarily from the purpose of 
the immunity doctrine, which is designed to protect public 
officials from harassing and vexatious litigation that might 
inhibit discretionary action, discourage able people from 
government service, and distract officials from their duties. 
These policies, which have led the Court to hold generally 
_that insubstantial suits against government officials should be 
terminated on immunity grounds at the earliest possible point, 
apply with full force to suits against officials who did nothing 
wrong. After all, a claim that has no legal or factual basis is 
at least as insubstantial (indeed, it seems considerably more 
so) than one in which the relevant law was unsettled. 


2. The conclusion that petitioners have asserted an 
immunity defense resolves the question whether they have a 
right to interlocutory appeal under Cohen v. Beneficial 
Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541 (1949). Under Cohen, 
appealability is determined for categories of claims. As a 
consequence, Mitchell’s holding that denials of qualified 
immunity are subject to interlocutory appeal is dispositive 
here. 


In any event, it is plain that the claim here satisfies the 
elements of the test for appealability stated in Cohen. 


0 DR te Roe hae 


7 


Mitchell makes clear that the district court’s order 
conclusively determines the disputed question and that the 
denial of immunity will be effectively unreviewable on appeal 
from the final judgment. And the interlocutory order here 
passes the final prong of the Cohen test, for the determination 
to be made on appeal is separable from the merits; the 
question whether there is a genuine issue of material fact (the 
issue for resolution on interlocutory appeal) surely is not 
identical to the question whether petitioners actually used 
excessive force against respondent (the issue for resolution at 
trial). This is particularly so because the Court tolerates a 
substantial overlap between the interlocutory appeal and the 
merits when, as in immunity cases, the right not to be tried 
is at stake. 


3. The Seventh Circuit’s rule relies on conceptual 
distinctions that are neither meaningful nor practically 
administrable. It is settled that an immunity claim must be 
resolved on the basis of what the defendant actually did, 
rather than what is alleged in the complaint; where the facts 
are disputed, on a summary judgment motion the court must 
determine (to the extent of deciding whether there are 
disputed issues of material fact) whose version of the facts to 
accept. Those facts then must be measured against the 
controlling law. 


This means that in a case where there is no genuine issue 
of disputed fact regarding the defendant’s account, the court 
will determine whether the conduct as described by the 
defendant violated clearly established law. There is no doubt 
that an appeal would lie if the legality of the defendant’s 
conduct had been unclear, because the question then would be 
(in the Seventh Circuit’s words) whether the defendant had 
“acted in the shadow of legal uncertainty.” That being so, it 
makes no sense at all to deny the defendant an appeal because 
(as here) his conduct was Clearly legal.

ARGUMENT 


The law enforcement petitioners in this case moved for 
summary judgment, asserting that they were entitled to 
qualified immunity because the plaintiff failed to establish that 
their actions had violated clearly established law — or, 
indeed, that their actions had violated any law at all. The 
decision below that the denial of such a motion by the district 
court may not be challenged in an interlocutory appeal is 
insupportable. The Seventh Circuit’s approach has been 
rejected by a substantial majority of the courts of appeals to 
address the issue,’ and for good reason: it frustrates the 
purposes of the qualified immunity doctrine by permitting the 
trial of insubstantial suits, departs from this Court’s 
precedents regarding interlocutory appeals, and rests on

A. A Defendant Who Denies Violating The Law At 
All May Assert Qualified Immunity 


1. The basic underpinning of the decision below was the 
court of appeals’ conclusion that this case does not involve 
immunity af all. In the Seventh Circuit’s view, immunity 
comes into play only when the official defendant maintains 
that the controlling law was unsettled; that court has opined 
that “interlocutory appeal to vindicate the [qualified 
immunity] right not to be tried is unavailable when there is no 
legal uncertainty.” Elliott, 937 F.2d at 341. It is this 
“protection from legal uncertainty,” the court has reasoned, 
that is “the genesis of doctrines of official immunity.” Pet. 
App. 2a. This approach, however, is premised on a 
fundamental misconception of the immunity doctrine. 
Immunity does not protect only defendants who acted in the 
shadow of unsettled law. To the contrary, it more broadly 
shields from suit all defendants whose “conduct d[id] not 
violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights” 
(Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982)) — a group 
that necessarily includes defendants who did not violate the 
law at all because they did not commit the acts alleged or 
because those acts were lawful. 


This understanding follows directly from the rationale for 
qualified immunity. As the Court explained in Harlow, the 
seminal decision in the area, 


it cannot be disputed seriously that claims [against public 
officials] frequently run against the innocent as well as 
the guilty — at a cost not only to the defendant officials, 
but to society as a whole. These social costs include the 
expenses of litigation, the diversion of official energy 
from pressing public issues, and the deterrence of able 
citizens from acceptance of public office. Finally, there 
is the danger that fear of being sued will “dampen the 


10 


ardor of all but the most resolute, or the most 
irresponsible [public officials], in the unflinching 
discharge of their duties.” 


457 U.S. at 814, quoting Gregoire v. Biddle, 177 F.2d 579, 
581 (2d Cir. 1949) (L. Hand. J.), cert. denied, 339 U.S. 949 
(1950). See Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc, 
114 S. Ct. 1992, 1997 (1994). 


The Court elaborated on this understanding in Mitchell v. 
Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985), explaining that “[t]he 
conception animating the qualified immunity doctrine as set 
forth in Harlow * * * is that ‘where an official’s duties 
legitimately require action in which clearly established rights 
are not implicated, the public interest may be better served by 
action taken “with independence and without fear of 
consequences.”’” Jd. at 525, quoting Harlow, 457 U.S. at 
819, in turn quoting Pierson v. Ray, 386 U.S. 547, 554 
(1967). The Court added that 


the “consequences” with which we were concerned in 
Harlow are not limited to liability for money damages; 
they also include “the general costs of subjecting officials 
to the risk of trial — distraction of officials from their 
governmental duties, inhibition of discretionary action, 
and deterrence of able people from public service.” 
* * * Indeed, Harlow emphasizes that even such pretrial 
matters as discovery are to be avoided if possible, as 
“[ijnquiries of this kind can be peculiarly disruptive of 
effective government.” 


Mitchell, 472 U.S. at 526, quoting Harlow, 457 U.S. at 816, 
817. 


These policies plainly apply with equal force to all 
unmeritorious suits against public officials, including those in 
which the defendant simply did not violate the law, either 
because the law was settled in his favor or because he did not 
engage in the illegal conduct alleged. After all, the fear that 


11 


an arrest or a search might lead even to a wholly baseless 
lawsuit (for example, a false allegation of brutality), followed 
by interminable and expensive discovery and then a trial, 
might well deter justified and desirable police action in 
particular cases; fear of such “harassing litigation will unduly 
inhibit officials in the discharge of their duties.” Anderson v. 
Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 638 (1987). In the absence of 
immunity, this concern inevitably would effect “close 
decisions” where “public officials should not always err on 
the side of caution” (Davis v. Scherer, 468 U.S. 183, 196 
(1984)), and at the margins would mean that “talented 
candidates” are “deterred by the threat of damage suits from 
entering public service.” Wyatt v. Cole, 112 S. Ct. 1827, 
1833 (1992). See Harlow, 457 U.S. at 819; Wood v. 
Strickland, 420 U.S. 308, 319 (1975); Scheuer v. Rhodes, 
416 U.S. 232, 240-241 (1974). Immunity therefore is 
necessary because “officials can act without fear of harassing 
litigation only if * * * unjustified lawsuits are quickly 
terminated.” Davis, 468 U.S. at 195. 


By the same token, “[o]Jne of the purposes of the Harlow 
qualified immunity standard is to protect public officials from 
the ‘broad-ranging discovery’ that can be ‘peculiarly 
disruptive of effective government.’” Anderson, 483 U.S. at 
646 n.6, quoting Harlow, 457 U.S. at 817. Again, this 
policy has nothing whatever to do with whether the official 
defendant acted under the shadow of legal uncertainty. 
Instead, it compels the conclusion, as Harlow itself put it in 
unambiguous terms, that all “‘insubstantial’ suits against high 
public officials should not be allowed to proceed to trial. 
* * * We reiterate this admonition. Insubstantial lawsuits 
undermine the effectiveness of government as contemplated 
by our constitutional structure.” 457 U.S. at 819-820 n.6. 
See id. at 816 (“insubstantial claims should not proceed to 
trial”). This concern with pretrial termination of 
“‘insubstantial claims’” was “the driving force behind 
Harlow’s substantial reformulation of qualified-immunity 


12 


principles.” Anderson, 483 U.S. at 640 n.2. And a claim 
that has no legal or factual basis at all is at least as 
insubstantial — indeed, it seems considerably more so — than 
one in which the relevant law was unsettled. 


It should be added that, while an immunity defense 
premised on the assertion that the plaintiff’s claim is legally 
or factually insupportable may well echo the defense on the 
merits, there is more than a semantic distinction between the 
two; they differ conceptually in a fundamental respect. A 
defense on the merits, of course, contests ultimate monetary 
liability. But qualified immunity “is an immunity from suit 
rather than a mere defense to liability; and like absolute 
immunity, it is effectively lost if a case is erroneously 
permitted to go to trial.” Mitchell, 472 U.S. at 526 
(emphasis in original). See Siegert v. Gilley, 500 U.S. 226, 
232 (1991). Practical consequences attend this conceptual 
distinction: defendants asserting immunity have rights to a 
quick termination of the litigation (including limits on 
discovery, see Anderson, 483 U.S. at 646-647 n.6, and an 
entitlement to interlocutory appeal) that are unavailable to 
litigants who defend only on the merits. 


2. That the qualified immunity doctrine protects 
officials who did not violate the law at all is dispositively 
established by Siegert v. Gilley, supra, where the plaintiff 
asserted that he was denied due proces. vhen the defendant 
defamed him after the termination of his public employment. 
The defendant contended both that his conduct was wholly 
lawful and that, if unlawful, it did not violate law that was 
clearly established at the time. See 500 U.S. at 229. This 
Court granted certiorari in the case “to clarify the analytical 
structure under which a claim of qualified immunity should 
be addressed.” Jd. at 231. 


In doing so, the Court explained that 


A necessary concomitant to the determination of whether 
a constitutional right asserted by the plaintiff is “clearly

violation of a constitutional right at all. Decision of this

weed out suits which fail the test without requiring a 
defendant who rightly claims qualified immunity to 
engage in expensive and time consuming preparation to 
defend the suit on its merits. One of the purposes of 
immunity, absolute or qualified, is to spare a defendant 
not only unwarranted liability, but unwarranted demands 
customarily imposed upon those defending a long drawn 
out lawsuit. 


500 U.S. at 232. And the Court concluded that the facts of

at 233. The Court accordingly ordered the suit dismissed. 
See id. at 234-235.

Mitchell and Anderson expressly indicate that immunity 
entitles the defendant to prevail when, after discovery, the

B. An Immunity Defense Of “I Didn’t Do It” May 
Be Raised On Interlocutory Appeal 


1. The determination that petitioners have asserted an 
immunity defense is dispositive of the question whether they 
have a right to interlocutory appeal under the collateral order 
doctrine of Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp. , 337 
U.S. 541 (1949). Mitchell, of course, held that the denial of 
a qualified immunity claim is subject to appeal under Cohen 
because “[t]he entitlement is an immunity from suit.” 472 
U.S. at 526 (emphasis in original). And the Cohen doctrine 
provides that “the issue of appealability under § 1291 is to be 
determined for the entire category to which a claim belongs.” 
Digital, 114 S. Ct. at 1996 (emphasis added). See Van 
Cauwenberghe v. Biard, 486 U.S. 517, 529 (1988) (“[iJn 


17 


fashioning a rule of appealability under § 1291, * * * we look 
to categories of cases”); Richardson-Merrell Inc. v. Koller, 
472 U.S. 424, 439 (1985); Carroll v. United States, 354 U.S. 
394, 405 (1957). This means that all claims in the 
“immunity category” may be raised on interlocutory appeal. 


Any departure from this principle in the circumstances of 
this case presumably would have to be grounded on the 
conclusion that the particular immunity claim here really is 
a “defense on the merits.” Elliott, 937 F.2d at 341. But the 
Court “has expressly rejected efforts to reduce the finality 
requirement of § 1291 to a case-by-case determination of 
whether a particular ruling should be subject to appeal.” 
Richardson-Merrell, 472 U.S. at 439. Thus, when the Court 
determines that a given category of claims (for example, 
those involving forum non conveniens) are not subject to 
interlocutory appeal because they typically involve a 
substantial overlap with the merits, it precludes appeal even 
of those forum non conveniens claims that would “not require 
significant inquiry into the facts and legal issues presented by 
the case.” Van Cauwenberghe, 486 U.S. at 529. By the 
same token, once the Court determines that a particular 
category of claims (for example, those involving double 
jeopardy) is subject to interlocutory appeal, it does not 
preclude even those appeals within that category of claims 
that do require some consideration of the merits.° That 
should be the end of the matter here; having determined that 
qualified immunity claims may be raised on appeal, the Court 
should not separate out such claims involving an “I didn’t do 
it” defense.

So, for example, if a plaintiff alleges that the defendant 
engaged in a particular act (say, that he violated the Fourth 
Amendment because he seized contraband that was not in 
plain view) and the defendant responds that he engaged in 
different conduct altogether (say, that he seized the 
contraband because it was in fact in plain view), the court 
will have to determine whether the plaintiff's version is 
supported by the record before determining whether the 
defendant’s conduct was inconsistent with clearly established 
law. If the plaintiff's evidence does not raise a genuine issue 
of material fact regarding the defendant’s story, the court will 
have to determine whether the conduct as described by the 
defendant violated clearly settled law. And there is no doubt 
that appeal would lie if the legality of the defendant’s conduct 
had been unclear (if, for example, the scope of the plain view 
doctrine had been unsettled), because the question then would 
be whether the defendant had “acted in the shadow of legal 
uncertainty.” Elliott, 937 F.2d at 342. 


In that light, it makes no sense at all to deny the 
defendant an appeal because it was clear that his conduct was 
legal. “To afford the * * * defendant[] immunity only if the 
law had been a little less favorable to [him] would seem 
ridiculous.” Unwin, 863 F.2d at 133 n.9. But that is the 
upshot of the Seventh Circuit’s approach: if the legality of

however, the court would have jurisdiction to entertain the 
appeal. This outcome may charitably be termed peculiar. 


The Seventh Circuit’s rule, moreover, will lead to bizarre 
results in a host of everyday situations. Imagine a case like 
Hunter, for example, in which the plaintiff asserts that the 
defendant law enforcement officers had no probable cause to 
arrest him. The defendants move for summary judgment, 
asserting that plaintiff was arrested because he was 
brandishing a gun; they assert that the arrest was lawful and 
that, if it was not, the law was unsettled on whether the 
plaintiff's action gave them probable cause to arrest. For his 
part, the plaintiff asserts that he had no gun at all. The 
district court denies the summary judgment motion. On 
interlocutory appeal, the appellate court finds that there is no 
evidence supporting (and therefore no genuine issue of 
material fact regarding) the plaintiff's assertion that he did not 
have a gun — and it further finds that clearly settled law at 
the time of the incident established (and established with 
crystal clarity) that police officers did have probable cause to 
make an arrest when the suspect was brandishing a gun. This 
determination establishes that there had been no legal 
uncertainty at the time of the arrest. And because the 
Seventh Circuit holds that jurisdiction exists only when the 
defendant acted in the shadow of legal uncertainty, its rule 
would seem to require that the appeal be dismissed. Having 
found that the defendant did nothing wrong, the court 
nevertheless would have to remand the case for trial. 


The preposterous case just hypothesized is essentially the

that there had been any uncertainty in the state of the law. If

In addition, the decision below does more than burden

practice, be extraordinarily difficult to apply. It often will be 
unclear whether the defendant genuinely “acted in the shadow 
of legal uncertainty” (Elliott, 937 F.2d at 342), and thus has

the defendant really is arguing (and is arguing legitimately)

In sum, the decision below will have perverse and 
destructive consequences. It will substantially reduce the 
efficacy of qualified immunity as a protection for diligent and 
conscientious public officials. And it will create enormous 
problems of judicial administration that greatly outweigh any 
benefit that would follow from a reduction in the number of 
interlocutory appeals. That decision should be reversed by 
this Court.
