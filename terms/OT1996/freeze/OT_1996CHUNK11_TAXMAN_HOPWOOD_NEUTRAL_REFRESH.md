## Current-law addition to the Taxman handoff

Hopwood was decided November 25, 1996, after the Taxman lower judgment but before this June 16 event. It is therefore earlier-effective current law. Its Part II affirmatively holds that a public university's interest in concrete educational benefits from a genuinely diverse student body can be compelling on a supported record. That proposition has six direct joins. Kennedy's agreement with vacating the categorical prohibition is not a join in the affirmative rule. The holding cannot be reduced to merely unsettled Bakke dicta or a blanket constitutional prohibition on every nonremedial educational use of race.

The same decision requires proof of the actual educational objective and narrow fit, preserves individual burdens, and rejects racial balance, viewpoint assumptions and a diversity label as sufficient justification. It invalidated the separate 1992 admissions system despite recognizing the possible interest. Its state strict-scrutiny rule does not replace the different federal Metro/Adarand rule or treat good educational intentions as permission for any racial classification.

## Relevance and limits in this vehicle

Taxman's fourth granted question expressly asks about the relationship between statutory employment rules and educational equal-protection principles. Hopwood therefore belongs in the legal comparison. The Board may invoke its recognition that a supported educational-diversity objective is not categorically illegitimate. Taxman may emphasize its insistence on real support, attention to burdens and the failure of the particular admissions means. Those are supported comparative arguments, not findings that faculty diversity, this department's circumstances or this layoff satisfy Hopwood's factual predicates.

Hopwood expressly leaves **race-conscious employment and admissions at other educational levels outside its decision**. It does not construe Title VII's voluntary-action boundary under Weber and Johnson, hold that diversity alone supplies a Title VII exception, or decide whether selecting an employee for discharge unnecessarily trammels that employee's protected interests. A constitutional interest available in a supported university-admissions case does not by itself create statutory permission to use a particular employment practice. Conversely, the unresolved statutory question may not be answered by treating Hopwood's affirmative constitutional holding as nonexistent. The existing neutral alternatives concerning statutory purpose, competing accounts of an equal tie, the concentrated loss of an existing tenured position, and the policy's limiting goal remain for analysis under the governing employment sources.

No constitutional claim was before the Taxman lower court, according to the validated packet. Mention of constitutional analogies in the granted questions supplies no new equal-protection cause or authority to replace the Title VII judgment with a constitutional one. Hopwood's remedial-duty discussion requires a supported traceable continuing wrong and fitted repair; it does not supply the nonremedial school board with an unasserted remedial imbalance, discrimination finding or Fordice predicate. Its Title VI intentional-treatment rule does not resolve Title VII's separate authorization question, and its admissions same-decision/remedy holdings do not automatically alter Taxman's established federal and NJLAD remedies or burden allocations.

The published Hopwood separate positions remain available at their recorded scopes in the linked public copy. Kennedy reserved the affirmative interest question; Scalia and Thomas rejected its nonremedial permission; the Chief Justice's additional operational and corrective formulations are separate grounds. None is an additional controlling rule or a precommitted Taxman vote. Published positions may inform ordinary later modeling only through their actual subject, ground and scope.

## Mechanically copied controlling text and express limits

The following passages are copied without substantive alteration from the bounded public-law reading copy. That copy identifies the controlling current Record; it is not new authority.

### I. The established unequal admissions process

**Holding and operative rule.** The State’s racial admissions classifications must satisfy strict scrutiny: the asserted objective must be compelling and the means narrowly fitted to it. The 1992 law-school process fails because it subjected applicants to unjustified, separate racial decision systems rather than genuinely competitive individual consideration; unequal opportunity to compete is an injury without proof that an applicant would have received a seat.

**Authority.** Part I of O’Connor’s opinion, joined by all nine Justices. The common holding concerns the actual separate competitive process. It neither declares each index differential independently unconstitutional nor adopts a general prohibition on every consideration of race.

**Controlling explanation.** City of Richmond v. J. A. Croson Co. requires a State to substantiate the justification and fit of its racial classifications; Wygant v. Jackson Board of Education requires attention to the evidence, alternatives and burdens on individuals. Bakke’s rejection of reserved seats and its recognition of competitive consideration distinguish a genuine comparison of applicants from separate racial allocation. Adarand’s equal-competition holding establishes the personal injury from unequal competition; its distinct federal merits rule does not govern Texas’s classification.

The 1992 process used different presumptive thresholds, separate discretionary committees and waiting lists, racial coding, and enrollment aspirations. There were individual exceptions to the score bands, and the aspirations were not an established fixed set-aside. Those qualifications do not answer the proved injury: applicants received individualized consideration within materially different racial decision systems, without a sufficient justification for separating their competitive treatment. Neither general educational benefits nor the State’s history establishes the necessary connection between that treatment and its asserted purpose. Correcting the appellate court’s categorical rules therefore does not reopen the established violation. The Court makes no finding that race fixed every final seat, that every feature independently failed, or that any of these four applicants certainly would have been admitted.

### II. A supported educational-diversity interest is constitutionally available

**Holding and operative rule.** A public university’s interest in concrete educational benefits from a genuinely diverse student body can be compelling on a properly supported record. The university must establish the educational objective and demonstrate that its actual racial classification is narrowly fitted to it; a diversity label, racial balance, or an assumption that racial identity determines viewpoint does not suffice.

**Authority.** Part II of O’Connor’s opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices adopt this affirmative proposition. Kennedy agrees with vacating the categorical appellate prohibition but does not join the affirmative interest holding. Scalia and Thomas oppose it. No Marks aggregation is used.

**Controlling explanation.** Bakke did not produce five votes for every part of Justice Powell’s educational-diversity reasoning. It did produce five express joins in Part V-C’s recognition that a properly designed program using competitive consideration of race and ethnic origin may serve a substantial state interest, and in rejection of an injunction against every use of race. The Court preserves that actual proposition and now adopts the more specific constitutional rule stated here; it does not attribute today’s holding to a nonexistent earlier majority.

A university may substantiate educational benefits from interaction among students with different experiences. It must show what the benefit is and why the means advance it, while accounting for the burden imposed on applicants. The argument that all racial consideration necessarily assigns a viewpoint to every member of a group does not defeat a supported, genuinely individual educational judgment. It does require rejecting racial proxies, numerical representation as an end, and assumed validity from academic preference alone. Strict scrutiny continues to govern the means. That distinction permits recognition of the interest while invalidating the actual separate process. The categorical prohibition also controls the admissions counterfactual used on remand, so correcting it has work to do in these live individual claims. No later Texas program is approved, and no university is ordered to use race.

### Limits and questions not reached

No separate intervention disposition or independent intervenor Title VI index claim is before the Court. The Court does not decide whether any particular holistic program with race as one factor is constitutional, prescribe a universal admissions model or numerical test, require race-conscious action, or adjudicate outreach, barrier removal, race-neutral contextual review or demographic data collection. It does not find a current Texas remnant, a particular applicant’s lawful admission outcome, the amount of actual loss, or a claim-specific immunity waiver. Federal classifications remain governed by their distinct existing authorities; admissions at other educational levels and race-conscious employment decisions are outside this decision.

## Law After Decision

Effective November 25, 1996, public-university educational diversity is a constitutionally available compelling objective when concretely established, with strict scrutiny of the actual means and individual burdens. This is an affirmative holding by six Justices, beyond Bakke’s previously joined competitive-consideration proposition. It validates no later program or racial balance requirement.

The State’s existing Fordice duty survives administrative division among its institutions. The categorical law-school-only cutoff is removed; traceability, continuing effects, educational justification, practicability and causal fit remain essential. The particular 1992 unequal process remains unlawful. No current remnant or justified preference is found.

These individual claims proceed under the school’s preponderance same-decision burden, with separate proof of actual loss, causes, defendants and immunity. Good faith does not erase purposeful racial treatment under the Title VI issue, and purposeful treatment does not itself establish punitive culpability. The full Smith alternatives remain in force. No broad future-program order issues. Federal Metro/Adarand law and unrelated admissions and employment settings remain unchanged. The separate opinions’ broader prohibitions, operational formulations and repair safeguards are noncontrolling except to the extent expressly adopted in the Court’s identified parts.
