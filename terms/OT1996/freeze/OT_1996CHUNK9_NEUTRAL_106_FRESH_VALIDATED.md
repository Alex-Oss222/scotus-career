## Granted scope and adjudicated posture

The question is whether equipment added by the initial user after the manufacturer's sale remains other property for maritime products-liability purposes when later transferred with the vessel to a subsequent user. The Ninth Circuit, 69 F.3d 1432, reversed the award for the skiff, net, fuel, spare parts and miscellaneous equipment present at resale, treating the second purchaser's integrated bargain as the product. The appeal was part of a mixed disposition; it did not overturn all liability and damages rulings.

Following a fourteen-day trial, the district court found defective hydraulic design and legal causation and imposed strict products liability on Martinac and Marco. The circuit affirmed those findings. Its footnote 8 expressly rejects treating occasional references to negligence as an independently supported negligence holding. The unsupported suggestion in the raw neutral that the fire was only allegedly defect-caused is replaced by this adjudicated posture. Subsequent modifications, overuse, poor maintenance and continued operation despite the seeping hose caused contributory fault; the award was reduced by two-thirds, and that allocation was affirmed. No superseding cause was found. The uncertainty about exactly how flooding occurred, and about removal of an inadequate original shield, did not undo the found causal connection. Those findings cannot be reopened merely because classification of lost equipment changes.

## Transactions, property, and remedies

Martinac constructed the vessel for Madruga in 1971; the completed vessel was launched in 1972. Martinac contracted with Marco to design the hydraulic system, installed it, and Marco inspected it. The captain took the vessel after sea trials to Panama for a seine and other fishing equipment. It was used for tuna fishing before resale in 1974 to the corporation later called Saratoga Fishing. At resale, five speedboats, a purse skiff, a seine and electronic equipment were aboard. The casualty occurred January 16–17, 1986.

The respondent describes Madruga as a sophisticated participant who selected specifications, supervised construction, and separately outfitted an incomplete fishing machine. That account supports the integrated-product argument; it does not erase the completed manufacturer-to-user delivery or intervening actual use. The respondent describes a six-month original merchantability/installation warranty, other warranties disclaimed, and a resale as-is/where-is with an operable-condition promise. These terms are not a finding of an express waiver of tort claims for third-party property. Its asserted lower resale price is an inference, not a finding.

The trial award of $452,326 was one third of the combined value of catch, net, skiff, fuel, equipment, crew losses, rescue costs and cash. It excluded the hull and replacement components installed by Saratoga. The circuit preserved catch recovery and rejected attempts to recover the hull from the hydraulic designer or recover ordinary replacement components. The present grant does not automatically revisit those distinct rulings. Spare inventory added by the user is not synonymous with an installed replacement of a manufacturer-supplied component. The remand must preserve that distinction rather than classify every item aboard solely by its date of acquisition.

The crew used the skiff to escape and were rescued; the respondent states it was scuttled afterward. Not every loss was physically consumed by the fire. The circuit upheld the currency finding and the decision to award prejudgment interest, requiring recalculation when the principal award changed. Classification here may require the corresponding damages and interest adjustment, but no new final amount or retrial of all elements is implied.

## Entering law and supported alternatives

East River, 476 U.S. 858, was read in its complete official text, including footnotes. It recognizes maritime products liability while denying negligence or strict-liability recovery for commercial product injury only to itself. At 867 it treats the integrated turbine supplied by the defendant as one product; a component cannot be atomized to turn ordinary product damage into other-property injury. At 871–875 it explains the contract/warranty allocation, applies the rule to charterers downstream of the manufacturer, and declines to free them from assumed repair and insurance responsibilities. It does not itself decide later user-added property at resale. Footnote 8 acknowledges possible express contractual allocation of tort risk in appropriate circumstances; no such unquoted term is invented here. The decision does not hold every conceivable economic tort unavailable.

The manufacturer-transaction path treats independently added user property as other property. The same physical injury that would support recovery while Madruga retained ownership does not become self-damage because title changes. Contract protection for the original product remains intact; ordinary additions are not atomized original components. The strongest contrary argument is that Saratoga bargained for an integrated fishing machine and assumed its commercial condition, much as the downstream charterers did in East River; foreseeable outfitting could be integral completion rather than other property.

The subsequent-purchaser path treats the vessel and installed package as one product at Saratoga's purchase. It emphasizes East River's downstream bargain and avoidance of tort recovery for the purchased asset's value. The strongest answer is that a resale warranty allocates duties between resale parties and does not necessarily extinguish the manufacturer's existing duty to protect external user property; the trial record shows initial user ownership and use before resale, rather than a distributor completing the manufacturer's initial product. Neither approach need create an exhaustive rule for every assembler, replacement part, or special risk-allocation contract.

Exxon, June 10, 1996, separates legal causation from comparative allocation and leaves fact review restrained. It supplies no basis to replace this case's affirmed nonsuperseding causal findings with new ones. Yamaha's territorial nonseafarer-death rule concerns a different field and does not alter the East River boundary. The actual May 27 delta was reviewed: Kinman, Ayeni, CompuServe, Vitronics and Hincapie concern distinct public-law questions and do not change this maritime rule. June 2 peers supply no antecedent law.
