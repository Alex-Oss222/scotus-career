## 126. United States v. Alaska, No. 84, Original

The United States filed the original action in 1979 concerning Beaufort Sea mineral leasing; Alaska counterclaimed to quiet title to coastal submerged lands within the Petroleum Reserve and Arctic Wildlife Range. The Master conducted hearings and briefing from 1980 through 1986. The parties identified fifteen issues. The matters presented by exceptions concern fringing-island baselines, Dinkum Sands, and retention of submerged lands within the two reservations.

### Fringing-island claim: competing historical materials

Alaska claims a general publicly announced federal practice, from the early twentieth century until 1971, treating waters behind near-fringing islands as inland when entrances do not exceed ten geographical miles. It does not claim historic title to Stefansson Sound through specific continuous federal assertions of sovereignty and foreign acquiescence. Its theory is that the general practice defined the coastline at statehood; it also asserts preclusion based on the Alabama/Mississippi boundary litigation. The Master catalogued diplomatic and domestic-boundary materials from 1903–1971 and found the asserted general practice intricate and insufficiently definite to require Alaska's proposed classification (Report 55). Alaska objects that he gave undue weight to minor variations and contradictions.

The competing materials include the United States' 1930 Hague codification proposals. The assimilation proposal would add small enclaves of high sea to territorial water where a line of no more than four nautical miles closed the enclave. Its separate straits proposal distinguished a strait connecting two high seas, whose entrances were less than six nautical miles, from a channel to an inland sea, to which ten-mile bay-closing principles would apply. These are the propositions in the proposed instruments; whether they establish or conflict with Alaska's asserted general practice is the disputed inference. Sources: 3 Acts of the Conference for the Codification of International Law, Territorial Waters 195–201 (1930); U.S. Exh. 85-223 (State Department Geographer Boggs's 1930 explanation). Alaska's submissions to the Master described assimilation as the official American position between 1930 and 1949 (Brief for Alaska on Island Fringes 54, 60–61), citing the federal 1949 California memorandum (Alaska Exh. 85-63) and the September 29, 1949 aide-mémoire to Norway (Alaska Exh. 85-82). Its exceptions characterize inconsistencies as insignificant.

Alaska also invokes the 1950 Chapman Line in Louisiana, enclosing Chandeleur and Breton Sounds and Caillou Bay; the November 13, 1951 Acting Secretary Webb letter to Attorney General McGrath in California litigation; and United States submissions in the 1958 Gulf States litigation treating specified island-screened waters as inland. The Master found no contemporaneous document explaining the Chapman Line solely as a simple ten-mile rule (Report 85–88). A 1950 State Department Geographer draft also considered limited foreign navigation and the islands' share of the territorial-sea arc (U.S. Exh. 85-400). The Webb letter describes each island's own territorial belt and repeats a distinction between high-sea and inland-sea straits (Alaska Exh. 85-94, pp. 3–4). The 1958 federal brief references are pp. 177, 254 and 261. These distinctions are evidence relevant to the claimed practice; no ultimate legal conclusion is supplied here.

The earlier Alabama/Mississippi materials include generalized bay and strait formulations and a 1961 Solicitor General letter to the Coast and Geodetic Survey proposing an island-entrance formulation (that case's Master's Report 40–52). Alaska's theory relies on that prior controversy as well as its independent documentary showing. Whether the earlier judgment necessarily decided a general rule, rather than the status of Mississippi Sound supported by specific assertions, is the presented preclusion issue. The 1971 federal baseline charts are the change Alaska identifies as contracting its statehood entitlement.

### Dinkum Sands and the Convention

Article 10(1), exact quoted definition copied from the source:

> a naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide.

Dinkum Sands is more than three miles from the nearest islands or mainland; its eligibility under Article 11's low-tide-elevation rule was not the presented basis for a separate three-mile grant. The parties agree that the high-tide datum is mean high water, averaged over approximately nineteen years. They contest how often a variable-height formation must be above that datum.

The Master construed Article 10 to require that an island generally, normally, or usually remain above mean high water. His findings describe a gravel-and-ice formation whose elevation changes seasonally; the federal expert attributed some slumping to melting internal ice. The Master found the formation frequently below mean high water. These are findings and an interpretive recommendation, not the Convention's own additional words.

The jointly commissioned monitoring project calculated local mean high water using a year's tidal readings with an error band for the ordinarily longer averaging period. An engineering firm surveyed the highest points in March, June, and August 1981. The Master found no surveyed elevation above mean high water; two readings within the error band might have measured gravel disturbed by earlier work. Alaska's 1982 measurements placed the feature above the datum in July and below it by September. Some 1983 observations were visual only; the Master considered that the feature might have stood inches above the datum in September 1983. He found it consistently below mean high water in 1981 and below it at the end of the open-water season in 1981 and 1982. Sources: Master's Report 255–283, 302–310; hearing testimony 7 Tr. 986–987, 8 Tr. 1060–1062 (July 23, 1984), as publicly recited.

Alaska relies on the 1949–1950 survey and ensuing charts, the 1971 baseline chart, the 1979 leasing map, and visual observations above water; the later chart and leasing map relied on the earlier survey rather than independent measurements. Alaska contests the Master's legal standard and factual conclusion and alternatively seeks island treatment during periods above the datum.

### National Petroleum Reserve

Executive Order 3797-A (1923), boundary wording copied from the source:

> coast line," measured along "the ocean side of the sandspits and islands forming the barrier reefs and extending across small lagoons from point to point, where such barrier reefs are not over three miles off shore.

This excerpt reproduces the source's intervening attribution; the quoted order terms are the coastline description and the ocean-side, small-lagoon and three-mile qualifications. The order's stated petroleum and naval purpose concerns petroleum seepages and favorable fields on the Arctic Coast. The Master included tidelands landward of barrier islands, small lagoons, river mouths, and bays within the boundary. Alaska disputes whether geographic enclosure and purpose sufficiently identify submerged lands and defeat the title otherwise passing at statehood, and disputes the Executive's authority under the Pickett Act. The Master recommended federal retention. Sources: Report 381, 433 and the 1923 order as publicly recited. The full first operative withdrawal sentence is not reproduced in these published excerpts; this handoff does not certify its complete wording. It should be obtained from Executive Order 3797-A itself if a distinction turns on language outside the quoted boundary and the reported purpose.

Section 11(b) of the Alaska Statehood Act, published excerpt copied exactly:

> power of exclusive legislation . . . as provided by [the Enclave Clause of the Constitution, Art. I, §8, cl. 17] . . . over such tracts or parcels of land as, immediately prior to the admission of said State, are owned by the United States and held for military . . . purposes, including naval petroleum reserve numbered 4 [the National Petroleum Reserve].

The Master's reading treats the reference to federal ownership as contemplating continued federal ownership; Alaska contends a reservation of legislative jurisdiction does not itself establish ownership of submerged lands. The independent Executive-authority objection and Congress's action in the Statehood Act are distinct issues.

### Arctic Wildlife Range application and timing

In November 1957 the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife applied to the Interior Secretary to withdraw approximately 8.9 million acres. Its stated purpose, copied exactly:

> to establish an Arctic Wildlife Range within all or such portion of the described lands as may be finally determined to be necessary for the preservation of the wildlife and wilderness resources of that region of northeastern Alaska.

The application remained pending when Congress passed the Statehood Act in July 1958 and when Alaska entered the Union in January 1959. Public Land Order 2214 formally reserved the Range on December 6, 1960, 25 Fed. Reg. 12598. The later 1980 enlargement is distinct from the original boundary and statehood inquiry.

The operative segregation rule, 43 C.F.R. §295.11(a) (Supp. 1958), copied from the published quotation:

> shall temporarily segregate such lands from settlement, location, sale, selection, entry, lease, and other forms of disposal under the public land laws, including the mining and the mineral leasing laws, to the extent that the withdrawal or reservation applied for, if effected, would prevent such forms of disposal.

Its additional qualification, copied from the separately reproduced regulatory text:

> [s]uch temporary segregation shall not affect the administrative jurisdiction over the segregated lands.

Section 6(e)'s wildlife transfer excludes the following lands (published excerpt):

> lands withdrawn or otherwise set apart as refuges or reservations for the protection of wildlife.

The federal contention is that the application plus regulation set the described lands apart as a refuge before statehood. The Master treated the application as segregation for a contemplated refuge but not yet segregation as an established refuge and recommended that the submerged lands passed to Alaska. The United States excepts to that recommendation. Alaska defends it and alternatively disputes sufficiently clear inclusion of submerged lands and intent to defeat state title.

The Master also resolved the geographic-boundary issue for the United States (Report 478–495): the original high-water boundary was changed to extreme low water; the application justification describes habitat for polar bears, Arctic foxes, seals and whales. He read the intended reservation as permanent and inclusive of submerged habitat (Report 495–499). Alaska did not separately except to that section of the Report but raises the inclusion and intent arguments in defense of the favorable recommendation on the application. These distinct procedural positions should be preserved.

## 130. Kansas v. Hendricks, Nos. 95-1649 and 95-9075

### Original commitment and statutory mechanisms

Kansas enacted the Sexually Violent Predator Act in 1994. Hendricks was nearing release after a sentence for 1984 indecent-liberties convictions. The State sought commitment before his scheduled release. A probable-cause hearing occurred August 19, 1994; Hendricks requested a jury determination of predator status and challenged the Act under due process, Double Jeopardy and the Ex Post Facto Clause. The Kansas Supreme Court's judgment rests on substantive due process; the latter two claims are preserved through the cross-petition. No constitutional classification follows merely from this chronology.

The original statute requires notice before anticipated release, a petition, probable cause and evaluation, and proof beyond reasonable doubt at the commitment trial. It places custody with Social and Rehabilitation Services for control, care and treatment until the person's mental abnormality or personality disorder has changed sufficiently that he is safe at large. Sections 59-29a06–07 provide counsel and an indigent person's professional examination, witness presentation and cross-examination, and documentary access. Section 59-29a09 requires constitutionally adequate care and treatment. The statutory existence of these requirements is distinct from whether a particular program administered them meaningfully.

Section 59-29a08 requires annual judicial review. Section 59-29a10 permits the Secretary to authorize a release petition when the person's condition changes; §59-29a11 also permits a petition without that permission. Do not infer that the statute lacks a release route from a criticism of actual operation, nor infer effective implementation merely from the statute's commands.

The published original-record recitations describe initial confinement in a segregated unit at a prison hospital under the social-services authority rather than ordinary corrections custody. Witnesses disputed the significance and treatment content of that arrangement. Terry Davis's testimony concerning physical setting and treatment appears at App. 52–54, 78–81. Original-record sources cited for the staffing, contract and program-start difficulties include App. 255, 367, 369. The Kansas Supreme Court, 259 Kan. 246, 258, 912 P.2d 129, 136 (1996), described treatment as not the primary objective and found significant treatment lacking. Those are the lower court's findings/characterizations, not statutory commands. Evidence of the available treatment for this kind of condition is distinct from evidence that Hendricks actually received it.

### Later evidence: keep its date and procedural status separate

A separate state habeas hearing occurred August 28, 1995, approximately a year after the initial commitment. The trial judge found, over conflicting testimony, that committed persons were receiving treatment (App. 453–454). Other testimony in that proceeding included program director Dr. Charles Befort's statements that meaningful treatment was not yet available to Hendricks and that staffing was inadequate (App. 391–394, 400, 420–421). His testimony must not be represented as part of the initial commitment trial.

The Kansas Supreme Court denied a motion to take judicial notice of that separate proceeding on March 1, 1996, No. 94-73039. That denial and the competing statements in the later hearing matter to whether those materials can properly establish the facts of the judgment under review. Do not resolve that record-admissibility issue merely by describing the later evidence.

At Supreme Court oral argument, Kansas's Attorney General asserted that persons in the program were by then receiving about 31.5 hours of treatment weekly (Tr. 14–16) and affirmed potential treatability (Tr. 12). These are later party representations, not initial-record findings or proof of the exact treatment Hendricks personally received at commitment. The temporal distinction bears on the presented as-applied question.
