## A. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Whether an employer may be held liable under Title VII for hostile-environment sexual harassment committed by a supervisor through misuse of delegated supervisory authority, notwithstanding the absence of actual or constructive notice to higher management.

2. Whether the principles of agency law governing an employer's responsibility for a supervisor's discriminatory misconduct differ from the negligence-based standard applicable to harassment by nonsupervisory employees.

3. Whether a supervisor's authority over hiring, firing, discipline, or working conditions is sufficient to establish employer responsibility, or whether additional evidence connecting that authority to the harassment is required.

## B. RECORD, POSTURE AND RELIEF

A cook alleged repeated sexual harassment by the administrator of an assisted-living facility. A jury awarded $25,000 on her TitleVII claim, but the district court entered renewed judgment as a matter of law for the employer for lack of notice. The administrator could hire and fire but reported to a corporate officer. The court also rejected constructive discharge. The Fourth Circuit affirmed. The selected questions concern employer attribution; they do not add the separately rejected discharge theory. Review of the Rule50 ruling requires evidence and permissible inferences favorable to the employee, without treating every allegation as a finding.

## C. ENTERING LAW APPLIED TO THE RECORD

Simulated Harris v. Forklift unanimously requires an objectively and subjectively abusive environment sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter conditions, without mandatory psychological injury. It expressly leaves Meritor's employer-responsibility issues unchanged. Thus proof of abusive conduct, even conduct satisfying Harris, does not itself resolve the corporate defendant's responsibility.

Pre-divergence Meritor directs attention to agency principles and rejects both automatic liability for every supervisor's misconduct and an assumption that lack of notice always defeats liability. TitleVII's reference to an employer's agent makes delegated authority relevant. Negligent failure to correct known coworker misconduct is a route distinct from attribution through misuse of delegated authority. Whether an agency relationship actually facilitated harassment requires more than a supervisor's title, but cannot be foreclosed solely because someone above the supervisor was uninformed. Later historical Faragher and Ellerth affirmative defenses do not govern. No simulated case establishes those later rules.

## D. PARTY POSITIONS, AVAILABLE DISPOSITIONS AND LIMITS

The employee relies on the administrator's workplace power; the employer argues that the personal misconduct was outside employment and higher management lacked notice. The reviewing court must distinguish a legally unavailable route from a route lacking sufficient proof. It may sustain judgment if the record cannot support attribution under the governing theory, or return the case for application of a corrected standard. Reinstating the verdict without examining instructions and preservation could bypass unresolved questions. Constructive discharge cannot be used as an unrequested alternative route to an award.

# 49. In re Rhone-Poulenc Rorer

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

**Merits docket status: SIM COURT GRANTED — TO BE HEARD ON THE MERITS.** Review is granted on the questions stated in this packet. This case is on the OT1996 merits docket under the established simulation calendar.

## A. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Whether a federal court may certify a nationwide class to adjudicate common issues of negligence in mass-tort litigation when the claims are governed by materially different state laws and individual factual circumstances.

2. Whether the predominance and superiority requirements of Rule 23(b)(3) permit certification when the proposed class trial presents substantial risks of inconsistent adjudications, unfair settlement pressure, and impairment of defendants' procedural rights.

3. Under what exceptional circumstances may a court of appeals use mandamus to review a class-certification order that is not immediately appealable under the ordinary final-judgment rule.

## B. ACTUAL RECORD, VEHICLE AND RELIEF

Blood-clotting-concentrate manufacturers sought Seventh Circuit mandamus ordering District Judge John Grady to withdraw a nationwide issue-class certification in Wadleigh v. Rhone-Poulenc Rorer, 157 F.R.D. 410. Plaintiffs were hemophiliacs allegedly infected with HIV through those products. Their principal negligence theories were failure to take earlier hepatitis-B precautions that would also have prevented HIV infection, and inadequate action after the HIV risk became known. Infection dates, products, knowledge, causation, state standards and defenses varied. Other theories existed but were not adjudicated in the mandamus opinion.

The district court declined certification of the entire liability-and-damages controversy. It used then-Rule 23(c)(4)(A) for common negligence questions, anticipating individual proceedings afterward. Plaintiffs expected a favorable common verdict to limit later litigation through issue preclusion. Uncertainty about final judgment prompted an appellate inquiry; the judge’s later letter stated that final judgment for the named plaintiffs was contemplated. The letter was obtained irregularly without a normal response opportunity, but the majority assumed eventual final review existed. An unentered special verdict was not an appealable judgment or a nationwide damages award.

The appellate record described approximately 300 suits involving 400 plaintiffs and twelve defense victories among thirteen completed trials. These figures do not establish that remaining claims lacked merit or that prior suits involved identical laws and proof. The majority estimated enormous exposure and possible bankruptcy while acknowledging assumptions in that arithmetic. It also identified state-law differences concerning foreseeability, professional versus ordinary-care standards and comparative fault. It feared that later juries resolving comparative negligence and proximate cause would reexamine the first jury’s negligence findings.

The Seventh Circuit granted mandamus and directed decertification; Judge Rovner dissented because final review remained available and anticipated settlement should not bypass it. Rehearing was denied April 27, 1995. Supreme Court review concerns the appellate mandamus judgment and the three granted questions. It is not an ordinary §1291 certification appeal. No §1292(b) certification and appellate permission are established. Rule 23(f)’s later discretionary appeal procedure is not operative in OT1996. The Court must consider the certification objections in light of the distinct extraordinary-writ threshold.

## C. GOVERNING SIMULATED LAW APPLIED

**Certification and substantive law.** Then-Rule 23(a) requires numerosity, commonality, typicality and adequacy. Rule 23(b)(3) requires predominance and superiority, considering individual control, existing litigation, concentration and management difficulties; notice and exclusion retain their roles. Then-Rule 23(c)(4)(A) authorizes appropriate issue treatment but supplies no permission to invent a national tort standard or disregard the certification conditions applicable to the proposed class. A common historical fact, such as a particular manufacturer’s knowledge on a date, differs from a negligence conclusion whose meaning changes with governing state law.

Erie and Klaxon require state substantive law and the governing choice-of-law inquiry, while §2072(b) forbids procedural modification of substantive rights. Simulated Gasperini applies a state recovery constraint through federal trial procedure and preserves federal judicial allocation. Its actual holding concerns excessiveness review; its stated distinction means that management convenience cannot average different states’ duties or import an entire state procedural system. A common instruction needs demonstrated legal equivalence, supported groupings or truly separable issues. Calling legal differences nuance does not establish their immateriality to these negligence theories.

Simulated O’Melveny & Myers rejects administrative uniformity as a sufficient reason for federal common law displacing state rules. Here litigation scale likewise supplies no federal negligence rule. Simulated Matsushita requires constitutionally adequate representation before absent members can be bound; notice and opt-out opportunities do not independently cure inadequacy. Its separate settlement-recognition holding does not certify this trial class or confer finality on a special verdict. Representation, notice, exclusion and lawful preclusion each require their actual predicates.

**Juries and issue separation.** Under the Seventh Amendment and pre-divergence Gasoline Products, separate trials require sufficiently distinct issues so that a later federal factfinder need not redetermine matters committed to the first jury. Rule 42(b) preserves the jury right. Comparative negligence can require assessing the defendant’s degree of fault, and state-law proximate causation can overlap the foreseeability and risk embedded in a negligence verdict. The actual proposed questions and instructions must show how that overlap is avoided. Calling one stage common and the next individual does not do so. Bifurcation is not categorically forbidden, and the Seventh Amendment does not itself impose a federal jury structure on independent state trials; the federal certification and asserted preclusive effect still require lawful boundaries.

Simulated Markman allocates tasks function by function and preserves genuinely factual work despite technical complexity. Its eight-Justice holding on embedded patent-construction disputes neither authorizes one tort jury to bind another on a different issue nor supplies a general class-trial rule. Gasperini permits bounded review of trial-court discretion, not a later tribunal’s first-instance revaluation of jury facts. Applied here, these holdings require precise allocation rather than a blanket approval or prohibition of issue classes.

**Mandamus.** The All Writs Act and pre-divergence Kerr, Allied Chemical and Mallard require a clear and indisputable entitlement, no adequate alternative remedy and circumstances warranting an extraordinary writ. Mere reversible error is insufficient. Coopers & Lybrand rejects ordinary immediate certification appeals despite their settlement effects. Simulated Digital Equipment preserves narrow collateral-order categories and rejects litigation burdens alone as jurisdiction; its separate adequate-final-review rationale has eight joins despite Stone’s nonjoin. Simulated Swint refuses to append an otherwise nonappealable matter merely because immediate review would save litigation. It expressly leaves mandamus distinct, subject to its own predicates.

Simulated In re Blodgett denies mandamus when focused relief from the court responsible for an operative abeyance order remains untried and has not been shown inadequate or futile. Its unanimous threshold proposition requires attention to actual ordinary alternatives; it does not impose a universal reconsideration prerequisite for every writ. Here the revisable certification plan and the expressly assumed eventual named-plaintiff judgment bear on adequacy. No omitted request by these manufacturers is inferred, and a demonstrated otherwise-unavoidable jury deprivation still requires its own analysis.

Beacon Theatres and Dairy Queen recognize mandamus’s traditional role in protecting an actual jury entitlement. Applying them requires identifying the threatened loss, not merely invoking the Amendment. The parties disagree whether this revisable plan already establishes an unavoidable violation, whether different issue definitions or instructions could cure the risk, and whether appeal after the named plaintiffs’ judgment is adequate. Settlement pressure supplies practical context but cannot alone make every substantial class certification an extraordinary case.

## D. COMPETING POSITIONS, REMEDIES AND LIMITS

Plaintiffs emphasize repeated manufacturer-conduct proof, litigation cost and express issue-class authority. Manufacturers emphasize different duties, individual defenses and the binding consequences of a common finding. Neither prior defense verdicts nor aggregate stakes establish the certification answer.

The Court may affirm a writ supported by an exceptional demonstrated defect, confine its rationale and scope to that defect, or reverse because adequate ordinary review and permissible correction remain. There is no existing common verdict or damages award to reinstate. Limitations, subsequent preclusion and binding absent persons require their own preserved issues and evidence.
