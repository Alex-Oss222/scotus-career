# Papai — respondent merits and petitioner reply source handoff

This source handoff reports party submissions and their cited record locations. It decides no disputed fact or legal issue and supplies no Justice position or preferred outcome. Source files numbered 08 and 09 in IA item micro_IA40385013_0713 are respectively the respondent merits brief and petitioner reply, regardless of the collection's generic labels. Both have been read in full. The petition, joint appendix and petitioner opening brief require the other assigned reader's coverage. Every factual claim below remains subject to the underlying record and any competing account.

## Assignments and actual work

Respondent pages 1–2 describes twelve earlier Harbor Tug engagements during 1989 as deckhand work and the March 13 painting assignment as a thirteenth engagement under the same union deckhand agreement and pay code 008. It cites Clinton testimony, trial volume 9, pages 28–29. Pages 8–16 argue that this was continuous qualified deckhand service rather than a changed essential assignment, and that each workday cannot be treated as a new occupation. The agreement provisions cited include maintenance and cure, helmsman/lookout/line-handling/painting qualifications and engine checks when underway. They describe contractual qualifications and potential work; they do not independently establish what Papai actually did on each earlier job. Respondent footnote 2, page 5, also asserts that all qualified deckhand work since January 1, 1989 was with this employer and calls the other-employer discussion unnecessary. Those assertions need to be checked against the deposition and dates.

Respondent says the tug was ready for operation (JA 56), that coworker Low was reassigned to seagoing work on another tug (JA 48–50), and that Papai was left painting alone from an allegedly defective ladder. It describes the Port Captain as in charge of operations rather than shoreside, citing trial volume 9, page 37. Reply footnote 36, page 20, disputes that characterization: the Port Captain worked in an office, was administratively above tug captains and rarely operated a tug, citing trial volume 7, pages 96–97 and volume 9, page 37. A supervisor's title alone cannot resolve Papai's actual duties or connection.

Reply pages 15–20 insist on the nature and duration of the actual assignment and exposure to sea duties rather than the worker's occupational title or possible contractual duties. They characterize the painting job as discrete and dockside. The reply accepts that a sufficiently substantial vessel connection could establish seaman status despite dispatch through a hiring hall; it does not treat hiring-hall employment as a categorical disability. It also distinguishes a one-day assignment involving entirely seagoing work, rather than claiming that every one-day assignment is too short. The respondent's same-employer work-history theory and broader common-hiring-hall fleet theory are distinct alternatives. Actual tasks on prior Harbor Tug jobs, their dates, vessel ownership or control, and the relationship of those jobs to the injury assignment remain the central record questions.

## District and administrative proceedings

Respondent pages 2–5 recount the January 1990 Jones Act, unseaworthiness and consortium suit, a May 29, 1990 adverse seaman-status ruling, amendment to assert vessel negligence under section 905(b), denial of interlocutory certification, and a renewed 1992 ruling following Wilander and Gizoni. It argues that the trial judge used a permanent-connection requirement. The final judgment and actual appellate disposition control the precise scope of surviving claims, including the vessel-negligence appeal; the status issue must not silently dispose of an independent preserved section 905(b) challenge.

Respondent characterizes the administrative hearing as necessary to determine the compensation lien before the 1992 negligence trial and says the employer argued there that Papai was a crew member, contrary to its position in court. It argues judicial estoppel, deficient adversarial litigation of status, and that the administrative judge relied on an older Benefits Review Board formulation despite Wilander. It says Papai's compensation attorney did not raise the inconsistency. These are submissions, not an adjudication of estoppel, issue identity or the adequacy of the administrative proceeding.

The reply's pages 10–11 and footnote 20 supply a competing chronology, visually checked against PDF page 10 because some OCR lines are missing. The employer says it filed form LS-206 on March 30, 1989 and paid compensation every two weeks through the June 2, 1992 administrative hearing, before and after the district status ruling. It says the payments originally addressed a left-knee injury; Papai filed an LS-203 on April 15, 1991 alleging an additional back injury; and the employer controverted that new injury on May 30, 1991. It identifies that dispute, rather than status, as the trigger for the hearing. The underlying administrative record, not either summary, must determine the actual sequence and issues litigated.

Respondent says the August 27, 1992 award was disclosed through a lien notice entered September 2, but preclusion was first urged on appeal in July 1993. It relies on the district court's JA 82 assurance that Papai could appeal after final judgment. Its text gives inconsistent December dates for final district proceedings and describes October 27 as the administrative appeal deadline. These are not adopted dates or legal deadlines: the actual judgment, award, notice and governing review statute must resolve them if material. The reply disputes respondent's account of when a formal compensation claim was first filed. Neither party's shorthand establishes waiver, acquiescence or litigation default.

## Separate legal theories concerning compensation

The employer's reply asserts two distinct grounds: section 905(a)'s exclusivity as an independent statutory barrier after a formal award, and ordinary administrative issue preclusion. It expressly disclaims reliance on an affirmative election-of-remedies defense. It distinguishes voluntary payments from an award after litigation and maintains that a claimant may pursue both routes until status is decided. Its statutory argument must not be collapsed into its separate preclusion theory.

Reply pages 1–9 acknowledge section 905(a)'s express exception when the employer fails to secure required compensation coverage and say no such claim is presented. They distinguish section 905(b)'s action against a negligent vessel, including an employer in its separate capacity as vessel owner, from employer liability under the Jones Act or unseaworthiness. They argue that the credit in section 903(e) prevents double recovery but does not itself erase exclusivity or issue preclusion, and emphasize the statutes' asymmetry. Respondent pages 16–22 argues that the statutory credit, dual-capacity vessel claim and the purposes of the compensation scheme undermine a categorical foreclosure. The availability of a vessel-negligence claim is not itself an answer to whether this worker may pursue seaman remedies.

Respondent pages 19–31 distinguishes the authorities relied on by the employer, argues that status was not adequately contested, invokes possible lack of standing to appeal a favorable award, and contends that an administrative judgment should not cut off review of a previously entered district ruling. It separately argues that applying a newly announced preclusion rule to the 1992 award would unfairly change the governing legal context. Its discussion of Sharp, Simms, Figueroa and the older Hagens ruling contains competing characterizations that require the actual opinions if adopted. The employer's reply disputes the legislative-intent argument, invokes Astoria's administrative-preclusion presumption, and argues that Papai had an incentive to establish eligibility for benefits. Neither a favorable award nor a theoretical appeal automatically proves an adequate opportunity to contest the status issue.

Reply pages 10–15 argues that a claimant can seek a stay, control the order of proceedings, withhold consent to a settlement order or withdraw a compensation claim before trial; it relies on the filing-extension provision in section 913(d). Those are proposed legal answers to respondent's practical-compulsion argument, not findings that Papai actually used or could successfully obtain each procedural option. The reply's section 22 discussion argues that the modification period had passed and that modification for changed conditions or factual mistake does not amount to reconsideration for legal error; it also contends that potential later modification does not destroy present finality. The scope and actual availability of modification must come from the governing law, not that party's compressed description.

Respondent seeks affirmance. The employer seeks reversal and foreclosure of further seaman remedies. The exact remedy on each appealed claim depends on the lower judgments and governing law. Quotations of post-1991 historical Supreme Court decisions in these filings are party authorities; the corresponding adjudicated law in this repository controls.
