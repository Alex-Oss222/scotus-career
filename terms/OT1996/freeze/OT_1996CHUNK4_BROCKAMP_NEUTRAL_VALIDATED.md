## Distinct records, thresholds, and remedy

McGill/Brockamp is not an adjudicated incapacity case. At age 93 McGill remitted $7,000 with a 1983-return extension application in April 1984 and never filed that return during life. He died in November 1988; Brockamp became administrator in January 1989. Her March 1991 refund request preceded the estate's March 27 return showing $427 tax liability. The district court granted the Government summary judgment. The Ninth Circuit reversed and remanded because the dementia evidence raised a triable incompetence issue. A statement that the entire $7,000 was tax not owed would erase the reported $427; a statement that incompetence was already proved would erase the actual posture.

McGill's separate contention that the remittance was a deposit rather than tax payment remains unresolved by the appellate court. The district court rejected it, relying on the extension application and lack of evidence of an intended deposit; the appellate majority did not reach it after allowing tolling. The United States acknowledges this preserved alternative in its merits brief. A ruling against tolling must specify whether the appellate court may still consider that alternative. This packet neither finds a deposit nor silently extinguishes the issue.

Scott made estimated payments totaling $30,096 for 1984. His late return, found filed November 29, 1989, showed no liability. After a bench trial the district court found that alcoholism rendered him mentally incompetent and unable to file from January 1985 through early November 1989. It considered his business dealings, others' management of his affairs and his incarceration, and found no unfair government prejudice. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, reviewing the incapacity finding for clear error. The refund judgment included statutory interest from the November 1989 filing, not the earlier payment date. That trial finding must not be redescribed as merely an allegation; its legal adequacy matters only if tolling is available. The granted government question does not independently reopen the taxpayer's rejected demand for earlier interest.

Section 7422(a)'s duly filed administrative claim requirement precedes a refund suit. Section 6511(a)'s filing period and §6511(b)'s limits on allowance and amount do distinct work. The alternative return-based filing period runs from when the return **was filed**, not categorically from its due date. Even if a late return supplies an otherwise timely return-based claim route, ordinary lookback still reaches only specified earlier payments. Each taxpayer's relevant payments fall outside the ordinary three-year-plus-applicable-filing-extension and two-year windows on the source record. That does not by itself decide whether an equitable extension applies to either component.

## Statutory snapshot

The exact contemporaneous text of §6511(a), (b)(1), (b)(2)(A)–(C), and §7422(a) is mechanically incorporated from the full-source receipt below. In ordinary operation, §6511(a) uses the later of three years from return filing and two years from payment, or two years from payment if no return was filed; the stamp-tax clause separately uses three years from payment. Section (b)(1) prohibits untimely allowance absent a claim filed in the prescribed period. Under (b)(2)(A), a claim within the three-year filing route ordinarily reaches tax paid within three years plus any extension for filing the return, immediately before the claim; its stamp-tax proviso uses three years. Under (B), the alternative lookback is two years before the claim. Under (C), an allowance without a filed claim is limited as though the claim were filed on the allowance date.

The scheme also contains express specialized provisions: §6511(c) concerns a timely agreement extending the assessment period and its related refund consequences; §6511(d) supplies specified special claim/amount periods, including bad debts/worthless securities and carrybacks; §§1311–1314 permit correction of enumerated inconsistent tax treatments on their own conditions. The source establishes no qualifying agreement, special-period item or mitigation circumstance for either taxpayer. Their existence and specificity are interpretive evidence, not alternative relief already available on these facts. The model must not announce a new operative rule for an unused exception from shorthand descriptions; any exception actually made dispositive requires its complete date-effective text and conditions. ## Entering law and strongest competing paths

Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89 (December 3, 1990), establishes a rebuttable presumption of equitable tolling against the United States analogous to private litigation after waiver; Congress may provide otherwise. It allows relief sparingly and does not authorize a more favorable rule against the Government than against private defendants. Its examples include a timely but defective judicial filing and adversary misconduct; ordinary attorney neglect did not suffice. Those examples were not a holding resolving every incapacity claim. O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy joined the Court's entire opinion. Stevens expressly agreed both that equitable tolling was available and that the asserted neglect did not justify it; his dissent concerned who must receive the triggering notice. Souter did not participate. The White/Marshall concurrence rejecting tolling was not the Court's rule and must not be attributed to Scalia.

Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (March 20, 1990), held that equitable recoupment could not create independent refund jurisdiction after the required administrative claim period had elapsed. Kennedy wrote the majority joined by, among others, O'Connor and Scalia. Its integrated treatment of §§1346(a)(1), 7422(a) and 6511, rejection of a discovery-based payment date, and attention to express mitigation remain relevant. Its particular ground was recoupment without an independently timely proceeding, not a holding expressly adjudicating disability tolling under the later Irwin presumption. Stevens's dissent, joined by Brennan and Marshall, emphasized the Government's inconsistent taxation of one transaction and the taxpayer's practical lack of a forum; it is neither a general right to disregard every tax deadline nor controlling law.

Canonical Lundy requires separate attention to claim route and amount lookback, but decided a Tax Court deficiency-notice hypothetical, not disability tolling of these actual administrative claims. Williams recognizes the express refund waiver for its timely lien payer while retaining applicable administrative/timing conditions. Henderson requires attention to the function and text of a particular deadline and distinguishes procedure from consent; it supplies no general equitable extension of tax periods. O'Gilvie's current-term refund-recovery clock turns on receipt of the refund payment, and expressly creates no tolling rule. The exact rules, effective dates, coalitions and limits appear in the copied annex.

One supported path assumes Irwin's presumption applies and holds it rebutted here: repeated claim/allowance restrictions, independently expressed amount windows, and tailored exceptions can establish a deliberately closed tax-refund mechanism. The Government emphasizes administrability and stale incapacity inquiries and urges that even tolling the filing period leaves the separate lookback unsatisfied. The strongest answer is that Irwin adopted a general presumption despite waiver language, specialized exceptions do not necessarily exclude ordinary equity, and inability to act differs from excusable neglect or Dalm's chosen sequential litigation. An actual justification must show how this statute defeats the presumption, not simply invoke sovereign immunity as an automatic answer.

A competing supported path applies Irwin and finds the presumption unrebutted for legally sufficient incapacity. The taxpayers rely on refund actions' historic private-collector analogues, the lack of an express general disability prohibition, and a coordinated reading in which tolling the incorporated period affects both filing and lookback. Their proposal to toll the Government's assessment period by the same amount is an offered judicial safeguard, not enacted text. The strongest objection is that a court cannot preserve a filing remedy by rewriting an independent ceiling on recoverable payments, and that an equitable enlargement plus a newly extended assessment period may alter rather than interpret the statutory scheme. Any adoption must specify the legal standard, its application to Scott's findings, and what remains to be proved in Brockamp; sympathy or lack of prejudice alone does not supply the rule.

A narrower route may decide only the independent amount limit, leaving whether a filing period could be tolled unresolved; conversely, a decision against all nonstatutory mental-incapacity tolling must explain its reach to both components rather than treating them as identical. The private-analogue objection can be decided or assumed without announcing a universal rule for all claims against the Government. No path authorizes new findings about either taxpayer, a generalized equitable refund outside the statute, or a categorical classification of every tax deadline.

If tolling is unavailable, Scott's refund judgment cannot stand on its tolling ground; Brockamp's further proceedings must preserve any properly remaining deposit issue. If tolling is available, Scott's adjudicated incapacity and Brockamp's unresolved factual issue require different treatment. A legal ruling for the taxpayers does not automatically establish the entire $7,000 as an overpayment or eliminate other preserved statutory questions.

## Standards and Tests selection: Notice-date selection of the Tax Court refund lookback

### Notice-date selection of the Tax Court refund lookback

**Governing question:** The payment window under 26 U.S.C. §6512(b)(3)(B) for an ordinary refund claim.

**Current rule:** The hypothetical claim filed on the deficiency-notice date both selects and measures the incorporated §6511(b)(2) lookback. Ordinarily, §6511(a) permits a claim within three years after a return or two years after payment, whichever ends later; without a return, two years after payment applies. A claim within the return-based three-year period reaches payments within three years plus an applicable filing extension; otherwise the payment lookback is two years. The notice-date hypothetical supplies a claim, not a return: a later-filed return cannot retrospectively select the three-year window or bring earlier payments inside it.

## Standards and Tests selection: Service timing in Suits in Admiralty Act actions

### Service timing in Suits in Admiralty Act actions

**Governing question:** Whether §742 supplies a separate jurisdictional or procedural deadline alongside Rule 4.

**Current rule:** Section 742's forthwith-service timetable is a procedural direction implementing existing consent, not an independent jurisdictional condition. In a timely commenced domestic SAA action, Congress's later enactment of former Rule 4(j) displaces the inconsistent shorter timetable; the applicable Rule 4 timing scheme and authorized enlargement govern without enlarging substantive consent or removing designated-recipient requirements. Former Rule 4(j) required dismissal without prejudice after notice when service exceeded 120 days without good cause, except foreign service under former subdivision (i). Amended Rule 4(m) requires, after notice, dismissal without prejudice or an order for service within a specified time; good cause requires extension, while enlargement without good cause is permitted but not compelled. Foreign service under (f) and (j)(1) is excepted; a limitations bar may support discretion but does not mandate relief.

**Authority by component:**

- Procedural classification, later-enactment displacement and distinct Rule schemes: Henderson — Holdings I–III, May 20, 1996; Scalia for Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer and himself (seven).

**Present operation effective:** Henderson, May 20, 1996.

**Effective transition:** Amended Rule 4 took effect December 1, 1993 for new cases and, insofar as just and practicable, proceedings then pending. It does not automatically relabel completed 1993 service acts or validate a particular enlargement.

**Exceptions and limits:** The two-year commencement limit and substantive waiver remain. This is no general rule displacing every service statute and no independently sufficient Court-promulgation ground under §2072; the operative conflict is with Congress's direct enactment.

**Holdings navigation:** Henderson v. United States, 517 U.S. 654.

#### Henderson v. United States, 517 U.S. 654

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-232.
**Decided:** May 20, 1996.

##### The SAA forthwith-service timetable is procedural, not a jurisdictional consent condition

**Holding:** Section 742's direction to serve the filed complaint forthwith regulates procedure in an already authorized Suits in Admiralty Act action; it does not independently condition consent to suit or remove subject-matter jurisdiction. Congress can make procedure a jurisdictional consent condition, but this timetable's placement, function and surrounding provisions establish that it did not do so here.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; the same seven Justices adopt all three Henderson holdings. O'Connor and Thomas dissent.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 743's private-party practice direction supports classification without erasing its other prescriptions. Section 745's two-year commencement limit, substantive consent limits and designated service recipients remain. Neither proximity to a waiver nor procedural subject matter alone determines jurisdictional effect.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Nordic Village retains clear consent to the actual monetary remedy and distinguishes consent from general jurisdiction; it does not classify this service timetable. McNeil's prefiling administrative-exhaustion sequence and Keene's filing-time bar are distinguished from this postfiling event. Carlisle supplies the procedural-authority/jurisdiction distinction without categorically classifying all procedural commands.

##### Congress's later Rule 4 enactment displaces the incompatible service timetable

**Holding:** In a timely commenced domestic SAA action, the applicable Rule 4 timing scheme, including authorized enlargement, governs service; §742 cannot independently require dismissal for failure to serve sooner. Congress's direct enactment of former Rule 4(j) displaces the incompatible procedural timetable without enlarging substantive consent or dispensing with the designated-recipient requirements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Part II, the same seven Justices.

**Material application:** Former Rule 4(j), directly enacted in Pub. L. 97-462, required dismissal without prejudice for failure to serve within 120 days without good cause, on motion or the court's initiative after notice, except foreign service under former subdivision (i). A separately fatal shorter timetable would deny that scheme's service allowance and authorized enlargement; the possibility of prompt compliance with both texts does not eliminate the conflict.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 2072 supplies a procedural/substantive framework, not an independent direct-nullification ground through a rule Congress itself enacted. Rule 82's jurisdictional limit is preserved. No deliberate disregard of service responsibilities is licensed; no universal repeal of special service statutes, government/private procedural differences or registered-versus-certified-mail requirement is decided.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Hanna and Mississippi Publishing Corp. v. Murphree support service's procedural role without replacing the actual-conflict inquiry. Walker and Ragan retain a state-created right's distinct state-law commencement condition; unlike Rule 3 there, the rival provisions here address the same service event.

##### Removing the statutory dismissal does not validate an extension or compel a new service hearing

**Holding:** The statutory dismissal must be reversed as to both designated recipients, with the governing Federal Rules applied to genuinely available, properly presented service questions in further proceedings. Any necessary use of amended Rule 4 must honor its express application to proceedings in cases pending on December 1, 1993 only insofar as just and practicable; this judgment neither validates the existing extension nor orders a new service hearing.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Part III, the same seven Justices.

**Material application:** The action was timely commenced. Attorney General receipt on day 47 and United States Attorney service on day 148 cannot sustain the displaced §742 dismissal. The latter service was outside the original 120 days but within the district court's fifteen-day direction; absence of express good-cause findings neither invalidates that order by itself nor requires renewed factfinding. No distinct Rule 4 ground supported the judgment reviewed.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Former Rule 4(j) governed the April–September 1993 acts. Amended Rule 4(m), if applicable under the transition, requires dismissal without prejudice or a direction to serve within a specified time after notice when service exceeds 120 days; good cause requires an appropriate extension, while enlargement without good cause is permitted, not compelled. Foreign service under subdivisions (f) and (j)(1) is excepted. A limitations bar may inform discretion but does not require enlargement. No Clarification Act/Public Vessels Act classification is decided. No Rule 12 waiver ground is adopted because the transfer motion's legal effect and appellate preservation are unestablished. Section 745's narrowly dated 1950 savings provision creates no general new refiling opportunity.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand, 7–2, for ordinary further proceedings. No new service objection, mandatory good-cause hearing, immediate trial, injury finding, maintenance or cure award, damages judgment or separate due-process ruling follows.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Pioneer preserves flexibility authorized by the particular rule, not general SAA tolling or a substitute good-cause test. Landgraf requires attention to express transition and does not automatically reopen completed acts merely because a case is pending or a provision procedural.

#### United States v. Williams

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-395.
**Decided:** April 25, 1995.

##### Express refund consent includes this lien-backed collection claim

**Holding:** Section 1346(a)(1) permits a refund suit by a person who, although not personally liable for the assessment against another, paid under the coercive force of a federal tax lien to free property claimed as her own and alleges that the Government wrongfully collected that payment. Section 7422(a)'s duly filed administrative claim and §6511's applicable timing requirements remain mandatory; §6511's taxpayer terminology does not implicitly exclude this timely collection claim from the express waiver.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's express-waiver ground, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Breyer: seven Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., February 25, 1992: applies its demand for unequivocal monetary consent and distinguishes its text permitting nonmonetary readings; the present refund waiver expressly authorizes monetary recovery.
- United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (1990): applies its connected claim-and-timing requirements, while distinguishing its untimely action from this timely nonassessed claimant.
- United States v. California, April 26, 1993: preserves its refusal to create a common-law refund remedy; this action rests on the express congressional remedy it left open.
- Stahmann v. Vidal, 305 U.S. 61 (1938): applies its distinction between collection from one's property and a volunteered payment, while retaining its different statutory incidence and collector-action limits.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 6511(a) allows, for a tax requiring a return, a claim within three years of filing the return or two years of payment, whichever expires later; if no return was filed, the period is two years from payment. Its separate stamp-tax clause allows three years from payment. No lateness is alleged here. Section 7422(b) permits suit whether or not payment was under protest or duress. The coercive lien and property connection identify the facts and limited plaintiff category decided; they do not create a universal protest or duress prerequisite or decide every other claimant's eligibility.

**Independent alternative holding:** Independently, a person whose claimed property and own payment are subjected to the lien-backed tax collection presented here is a person subject to an internal-revenue tax within §7701(a)(14) for the connected refund provisions, even though she is not personally liable for the assessment. This construction allows her timely administrative refund claim; it neither makes every payer a taxpayer for all Code purposes nor adjudicates the debt or lien's validity.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Ginsburg's independent taxpayer-definition ground, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer: five Justices; Stone-Zsela and Scalia do not join.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Dalm: applies the integrated treatment of waiver, claim, and timing provisions without converting its late-claim holding into a decision about this plaintiff category.
- Nordic Village: applies clear-consent analysis to the actual refund text and its connected definition; no general jurisdictional or equitable label supplies consent.

##### Distinct third-party mechanisms do not foreclose this refund action

**Holding:** The cited quiet-title, wrongful-levy, and substitute-fund provisions do not make those mechanisms exclusive of this §1346(a)(1) collection claim or require their exhaustion before her duly claimed refund suit. Their own triggers and restrictions remain in force: a lien is not a levy, discharge into a substitute fund is permissive and requires an agreement, and the fund remedies presuppose the corresponding fund.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's third-party-remedies ground, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Breyer: seven Justices.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Dalm: preserves the statutory refund-claim requirement without adding an exhaustion duty for quiet-title, levy, or substitute-fund proceedings.
- United States v. California: preserves the distinction between an express remedy and one newly created by a court; the practical limitations of other remedies corroborate statutory construction but do not create consent.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding is jurisdictional. Lien validity and priority, purchaser status and consideration, the effect and scope of any binding Government concession, wrongfulness of collection, payment components, and recoverable amount remain for further proceedings as necessary. No interest, fees, damages, or unrestricted challenge to Rabin's underlying assessment is awarded or authorized by this decision.

Section 2410 permits quiet-title proceedings against a claimed federal lien, without automatically creating a refund action after payment. Section 7426(a)(1) requires a levy; none occurred here. Section 6325(b)(3) permits discharge of sold property upon an agreement holding the proceeds in a substitute fund with the same lien priority; it neither requires an agreement nor establishes that Williams obtained one. Sections 7426(a)(3) and (b)(4) presuppose such a fund. Section 7426(c)'s conclusive presumption that the assessment is valid remains confined to proceedings under that section and is not silently transferred to §1346.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Refund jurisdiction is affirmed, 7–2, leaving the Ninth Circuit's further-proceedings remand in force. Lien validity and priority, purchaser status, any binding concession, wrongfulness, payment components and amount remain as necessary. No refund, interest, fees, damages or unrestricted challenge to Rabin's assessment is awarded.

#### Holdings

#### I. The Government's appeal in the father's action was timely

**Holding and operative rule:** The December 7, 1993 judgment, which finally resolved the punitive-damages claim after withdrawal of the August judgment and an intervening interest-only ruling, started the Government's sixty-day civil appeal period. Its February 1, 1994 notice was timely; identifying that actual final judgment does not establish that every amended or clerically corrected judgment restarts an appeal period.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appeal period runs from the judgment that actually disposed of the claim. The August 26, 1992 judgment was withdrawn. The November 30, 1993 order addressed interest rather than finally disposing of the punitive-damages claim. December 7 supplied that final disposition, and February 1 fell within Rule 4(a)(1)'s sixty days when the United States is a party. The objection that a later amendment cannot revive an expired appeal is sound in its proper setting, but its premise is absent here. Jose directs attention to the conclusive effect of the actual judgment. Ibarra concerns timely substantive reconsideration in criminal proceedings; it creates no civil extension here. Smith v. Barry permits timely functional notice but does not excuse lateness. No tolling or substitute-notice ruling is necessary.

**Precedent treatment:** Jose's ordinary-finality approach is applied; Ibarra's criminal-reconsideration rule and Smith's timely-notice requirement remain distinct and unchanged.

#### II. These noncompensatory punitive receipts are taxable

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the applicable pre-1989 §104(a)(2), the taxpayer must satisfy both the tort-or-tort-type-right requirement and the independent requirement that the particular damages be received on account of personal injuries or sickness; amounts attributable to §213 deductions allowed in a prior year remain excepted. The Kansas punitive component serves punishment and deterrence rather than compensation for injury, fails the independent causal requirement, and is included in gross income under §61.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the function of this separate payment matters independently of the character of the lawsuit. Schleier establishes the two requirements and refuses to treat tort-type status as sufficient by itself. Its treatment of willfulness-based liquidated damages supplies the causal reasoning applied here, although it reserved a general punitive-damages question. The wrongful-death injury is real and necessary to the Kansas action. But this distinct award responds to the wrongdoer's culpability and the purposes of punishment and deterrence; it does not restore the taxpayers' injury loss. Injury's role as an indispensable antecedent cannot alone establish the required connection for every resulting receipt. The statutory phrase is broader than an express compensation formula, as the taxpayers emphasize, but reading every injury-dependent recovery as excluded would erase Schleier's independent inquiry. Burke supplies no controlling remedial-menu or health-only test, and none is added. The decision concerns the actual noncompensatory award; compensatory payments, mixed allocations and awards serving a materially different function remain open. Later punitive-damages and physical-injury amendments do not govern these receipts.

**Precedent treatment:** Schleier's receipt-specific causal rule is extended to this wholly punitive Kansas award, without pretending its reserved question was already decided. Burke's fractured judgment remains without a general controlling statutory rationale.

#### III. Receipt of the refund checks starts the children's recovery period

**Holding and operative rule:** For these refund checks, a refund is made for §§6532(b) and 7405(b) when the taxpayers receive payment, rather than when the Government merely writes or mails the checks. Receipt on July 9, 1990 makes the recovery actions filed July 9, 1992 timely under the two-year period.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six direct joins. O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas do not reach the children's limitation defense.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that delivery of the mistaken payment connects the refund to the Government's enforceable recovery claim. Wurts distinguishes payment from administrative allowance and explains why an allowance does not itself begin the period for recovering money. A written or mailed check has not necessarily delivered the benefit to its recipient. The traditional mistaken-payment action likewise rests on receipt of that benefit. A postmark might offer convenient evidence, but convenience does not replace the statutory connection between making and recovering a refund. The children conceded receipt on July 9; the suits were brought exactly two years later. July 6 is the check-writing date, not an established mailing date. The Court adopts the receipt rule for these checks without announcing equitable tolling, a new deadline exception or a rule for every other payment mechanism.

**Precedent treatment:** Wurts's payment-and-recovery reasoning is applied to the receipt question it did not itself decide; administrative allowance remains distinct from payment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Commissioner v. Schleier, June 14, 1995:** Extends its independent receipt-specific causation rule to the noncompensatory punitive award; its two statutory conditions and prior-§213 exception survive.
- **United States v. Burke, May 26, 1992:** Preserves the fractured judgment; no remedial-menu, health-only or general punitive-damages rationale is promoted to controlling law.
- **United States v. Wurts, 303 U.S. 414:** Applies its distinction between administrative allowance and actual payment to determine when these recovery claims begin.
- **United States v. Jose, December 2, 1996:** Applies ordinary finality by examining what the order actually completed; its distinct IRS-condition merits reservation is unaffected.
- **United States v. Ibarra, October 15, 1991:** Distinguishes timely criminal reconsideration from this civil final-judgment sequence.
- **Smith v. Barry, January 14, 1992:** Preserves the requirement of timely notice; no functional-notice exception to lateness is created.

#### Law After Decision

Effective December 10, 1996, the former exclusion does not cover punitive receipts having the noncompensatory character established here. The receipt-specific rule develops Schleier without adopting Burke's noncontrolling rationales. For the checks at issue, receipt starts the two-year erroneous-refund recovery period. Actual civil finality governs the father's appeal without any general restart rule. Compensatory damages, mixed allocations, different payment mechanisms and later statutory texts retain their separate questions.

### 1996-12-10 — O'Gilvie v. United States; Nos. 95-966 and 95-977

O'Connor, joined by Scalia and Thomas, would exclude the punitive receipts. The physical injury is a legal cause of the damages, even though the award's purpose is punishment. The former text says on account of personal injury, while neighboring provisions use compensation language; the dissent rejects adding a compensation-only restriction. It distinguishes Schleier's employment backpay and injury-independent willfulness payment from a traditional tort award legally dependent on the physical injury. The dissent applies both statutory conditions and does not claim every tort-type receipt is excluded. Because the children prevail on that merits ground, the dissent does not decide their limitation defense; it also regards a choice between receipt and later clearance as unnecessary once mailing is rejected. All three agree that the father's appeal is timely.

Scalia separately retains the construction expressed in his Burke concurrence: the exclusion concerns physical or mental health injuries. This wrongful-death recovery satisfies that premise, unlike the employment entitlement in Burke. He joins the shared textual and causal dissent without converting his health-injury construction into the view of O'Connor or Thomas. Both dissents are noncontrolling.
