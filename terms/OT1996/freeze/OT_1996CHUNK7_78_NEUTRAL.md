## Undisputed record and statute at issue

In April 1994 the New Party, a legitimate Minnesota minor party, selected Andy Dawkins, the incumbent Democratic-Farmer-Labor representative for House District 65A. Dawkins was unopposed for that party's primary nomination. He accepted the New Party nomination and signed its candidacy affidavit; the DFL did not object. The New Party obtained the required petition signatures. Officials rejected the filing because Dawkins already had a DFL candidacy affidavit on file.

Minn. Stat. §204B.06 subd. 1(b) (1994) required the affidavit to state there was no other affidavit on file for any office at the same primary or next general election. Section 204B.04 subd. 2 prohibited a primary candidate for partisan or nonpartisan office from nomination by petition for that same office, subject to the partisan exception in §204D.10 subd. 2 and nonpartisan vacancy exception in §204B.13 subd. 4. Under the partisan exception, if any primary candidate of a major party received ten percent of the average vote cast for that party's state officers at the last state general election within the relevant district, its highest-vote candidates became nominees; if none met that threshold, none were nominated and all its candidates could seek nomination by petition under §§204B.07–.09. State officers for this calculation are governor, lieutenant governor, secretary of state, state auditor, state treasurer and attorney general. The nonpartisan exception allows a nominating petition within one week of the vacancy, no later than four calendar days before election, and permits an eligible voter's signature despite primary voting or signing another petition. Neither exception was applicable to Dawkins below. The distinct subd. 1 prohibition on nomination by more than one **major** party was not involved or reached and is outside this as-applied challenge. Full official 1994 §§204B.04, .06, .13 and 204D.10 are copied into the reading slice.

Fusion means multiple-party nomination of one candidate for the same office in the same general election. In the practice described below, a voter chooses one party line; the candidate's votes from the lines are aggregated. The claim is not a request that one voter cast several votes. The statute prevented this party label and nomination, not all speech supporting Dawkins or all New Party candidates. The State's position that endorsement, campaigning and alternative nominations remain available is pertinent to burden; it is not a conclusion that those alternatives are constitutionally equivalent to the desired nomination.

The appellate court described absent evidence of voter confusion and mechanisms already limiting ballot crowding, including required support signatures. That description establishes what the reviewed opinion reports; it does not establish that confusion could never arise, that every proposed design is administratively feasible, or that anticipatory regulation always requires evidence of past fraud. There is no claim that Dawkins objected or lacked the Party's support.

## Reasoning below and strongest competing arguments

The unanimous appellate panel regarded selection of the party's standard bearer and consensual alliances as core association, found the burden severe and required a narrowly tailored compelling justification. It rejected the suggestion that choosing someone else eliminated the injury. As to stability, internal party conflict and splintering, it thought candidate and major-party consent a narrower response. It considered fusion informative, thought instructions could address ballot confusion, and relied on existing signature rules against overcrowding. It held §§204B.06 subd.1(b) and 204B.04 subd.2 unconstitutional in this application. Its historical statements about the political effect and origins of fusion bans rested on scholarship; they are context, not independently verified constitutional facts or judicially established motive of these current officials.

The appellate opinion acknowledged Swamp v. Kennedy, 950 F.2d 383 (7th Cir. 1991), which sustained Wisconsin's anti-fusion regime on a lesser-burden approach, with a concurrence focusing on party identity. That judgment supplies a contemporaneous competing lower approach, not Supreme Court authority. Its different statutory setting and consent analysis must remain distinct.

The State's strongest supported path treats the rule as a reasonable, nondiscriminatory restriction on official ballot form and the candidate-selection system. The Party retains endorsement, speech and a route to nominate another candidate; a candidate has no unconditional entitlement to every party label. The State invokes understandable ballots, stable party identities, prevention of manipulation and splintering, and orderly elections. Under calibrated review, reasonable preventive judgments may carry weight without proof that the particular attempted nomination already caused confusion. Strongest objection: this is an actual consensual candidate nomination meeting support requirements, not an arbitrary protest message, and the ban prevents a significant associational choice even though no additional person is added to the race.

The Party's strongest supported path treats the restriction as severe, or materially substantial even if short of total exclusion. Choosing an actual standard bearer and communicating a consensual alliance under the Party's name are central functions. The candidate and other party agreed; signature thresholds limit frivolous listings; ordinary instructions and consent may address specific concerns. Strongest objection: the ballot's primary function is selecting officials, the election system must be considered as a whole, and a party's preferred means of improving influence is not itself a constitutional entitlement. The existence of a conceivable less restrictive method does not automatically invalidate a reasonable restriction under the governing standard.

These analytical paths do not attribute unproduced arguments to a brief. Neither the proposition that every interest in party stability is illegitimate nor the proposition that every anti-fusion restriction is valid is supplied by entering law. Distinguish permissible stability and administration from suppressing competition merely to protect incumbents. The constitutional connection, burden and fit require decision; they may not be assumed by calling fusion either coalition speech or confusion.

## Official 1996 statutory development and threshold paths

The official 1996 code includes Laws 1996 chapter 419 amendments. Section 204B.04 subd. 2a allows simultaneous major-party and one-or-more minor-party nominations for the same partisan office with the written consent of the state chairs of the parties concerned; petition nomination also requires the candidate's written consent. Failure to obtain the major-party primary nomination forfeits the simultaneous minor-party nominations. A candidate may not seek party nomination and file as an independent for the same election. The amended subd. 2 retains the nonpartisan vacancy exception and bars a defeated major-party primary candidate from the general ballot except to fill a §204B.13 vacancy. Section 9 repeals the prior §204D.10 subd. 2 primary-turnout provision.

Amended §204B.06 subd. 1 changes the general duplicate-affidavit restriction to any **other** office, requires acceptance of a petition nomination and inclusion of the simultaneous-nomination consent forms. It also contains a separate narrow dual-office exception for a soil-and-water district supervisor in a district outside Anoka, Hennepin, Ramsey and Washington counties and a mayor/council office in a city, or town-supervisor office in a town, with population no more than 2,500 and wholly or partly within the conservation district. That exception concerns different offices, not Dawkins's multiple nominations for one office. The supplied 1994 consent and lack of objection do not prove compliance with every later written-chair-consent requirement.

The Act also requires the candidate's name to appear only once, with the nominating party names beside it; a major/minor nominee uses the major-party ballot position, and a multiple-minor nominee uses the position of the first filing party. The Act's stated design does not give the voter a separate party vote. Its party-status amendments exclude votes for multiply nominated candidates from the specified qualification/retention counts. These are enacted features of the conditional scheme, not evidence that it was applied to Dawkins, and their independent constitutionality is not granted here.

Chapter 419 was signed April 2, 1996. Section 10 makes it effective for the 1996 state primary and thereafter, but suspends its amendments while the McKenna judgment is stayed or its mandate recalled; if McKenna is reversed, the amendments expire and prior law revives.
