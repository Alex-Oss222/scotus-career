## Record facts, testimony and lower-court grounds

1. The officers held a warrant to search Room 138 for controlled-substance evidence. The requested no-knock authorization was refused; Pharo expressly confirms that refusal at JA 42. The warrant's search authority and the manner of executing it are distinct. The affidavit described informant information, motel practices, packaging material and an earlier arrest with cocaine packets. General statements about drug traffickers carrying firearms are not particular information that Richards had a weapon. See JA 2–10.

2. Pharo testified that Richards opened the door about an inch and a half, looked over Pharo's right shoulder in the direction of a uniformed police officer three or four feet away, appeared surprised, and slammed the door as Pharo began trying to enter. Pharo had represented himself as a maintenance man and wore a Ramada Inn shirt. One of at least three officers to his right was in uniform; the others to his right and two to his left were in plain clothes. JA 38–44. The transcript calls the attire a police officer's full military uniform. It supplies both that wording and the witness's identification of its wearer as a police officer.

3. Richards testified he saw only Pharo, saw no uniformed officer, and shut the door when Pharo tried to force it. He denied hearing police identification before he exited the window and an officer outside told him to stop. JA 45–48. Petitioner merits p.6 and reply pp.17–18 say his field of vision *permitted* him to see the maintenance-shirted person and uniformed officer; that is counsel's description of possible visibility, not an admission that Richards actually saw or recognized police. The reply disputes whether the record unequivocally establishes a recognizable police officer within his line of sight.

4. The trial court discounted Richards's testimony as self-serving and lacking some credibility and inferred awareness of the uniformed officer. It considered the surprise expression, closing and locking the door, unsuccessful ruse, announcement before crossing the threshold, disposability of the sought drugs, and Richards's flight. JA 54–56. Report recognition as the trial court's inference over conflicting testimony, not an uncontested admission. The trial court's later knowledge of flight is not information automatically available to the officers before the first attempted forced opening.

5. Identity and purpose announcements have separate timing. Pharo testified identity was announced two or three seconds after the door shut. His account describes turning/pushing the knob, then kicking, and finally another officer shouldering the door open. On cross-examination he said he was probably trying to open the door while announcing identity; he said kicking followed the identity shout. JA 39–45. Thus a statement that identity preceded successful entry does not establish identity preceded every application of force.

6. On the reopened hearing Pharo said other officers shouted that they had a search warrant. Asked when, relative to the forced opening, he answered that he recalled the bang of the door opening and agreed the search-warrant shout was simultaneous. JA 64–68. The court allowed supplementation over Richards's finality/waiver objection and stated the added testimony did not change its decision. JA 60–69. Do not describe the purpose announcement as an undisputed completed announcement before force or entry.

7. The evidence places breaking glass/window flight as successful entry occurred. Richards's account says the other occupant remained inside; do not convert counsel's shorthand to a finding that both occupants escaped. Drugs and money were discovered above the bathroom ceiling; later discovery is not evidence of noises of destruction before entry. See JA 18–32, 47 and warrant return JA 10–12.

8. Richards entered no-contest pleas after denial of suppression, and the state courts affirmed. The state Supreme Court majority's blanket exception covered warrants for evidence of felonious drug delivery or felonious possession with intent to deliver, with the cited state statutory scope; it was not an exception for every drug offense. Its majority did not decide the level of justification required outside its categorical premise. Abrahamson concurred in the judgment based on the trial facts and useless-gesture rationale while rejecting the categorical rule. Petition pp.2–4 and appended Wisconsin opinions; Wisconsin Supreme Court, 201 Wis. 2d 839, 549 N.W.2d 218 (1996).

## Preserved submissions and requested remedies

The following are attributed party positions, not adopted law or this reader's conclusion about appellate preservation.

- The petition challenges dispensing with a particularized reasonableness inquiry solely because a warrant seeks drug evidence. It accepts the Wisconsin majority's succinct factual recital *for purposes of the petition* and expressly says that if the blanket rule is invalid, further factual analysis on remand would determine whether specific exigent circumstances existed. Petition pp.2–4. That limited acceptance does not concede every disputed recognition or announcement detail.
- Respondent merits pp.38–40 argues that closing and locking the door after looking toward the uniformed officer made further announcement a useless gesture. It invokes virtual certainty that the occupant knew the police purpose and argues the visible uniform supplied identity. Respondent's footnote says this issue was briefed in the Wisconsin Supreme Court and in opposition to the petition and asks this Court or the state court on remand to reach it if necessary.
- Reply pp.17–18 disputes sufficient record support for recognition and knowledge of purpose, invokes Miller's virtually-certain standard, disputes whether Richards heard the identity shout and requests remand. The petitioner's assertion that findings were obviated by the state Supreme Court's approach must be read alongside the actual trial findings at JA 54–56; it does not erase them.
- Respondent merits pp.41–46 separately argues against suppression even if entry was unconstitutional: a valid warrant authorized seizure; the information supporting it preceded the entry; independent-source principles and avoiding a worse position for police preclude exclusion. It relies on Harris, Crews, Segura, Murray and Nix. It says the exclusion issue was briefed below and in opposition, and requests its consideration here or on remand. This is separate from whether the entry was reasonable.
- Reply pp.14–17 objects to reaching that remedy issue, asserting no cross-petition and citing the prior reservation in Wilson. It relies on Miller and Sabbath suppression, deterrence, the protected interests of innocent occupants and guests, and the contention that bypassing announcement to preserve drug evidence exploits the challenged conduct. It opposes treating the warrant itself as automatic inevitable discovery and concludes with a request for reversal and remand. No preservation ruling follows merely from either side's assertion.
- On the general rule, the reply argues for particularized evaluation and distinguishes limited roadside detention from forcible entry into a home; it also opposes a presumptive no-knock rule rebutted only by police knowledge that no exigency exists. It asks whose knowledge counts, what duty exists to gather/share facts, what evidentiary standard and burden apply, and how such a rule permits meaningful review. Its empirical and public-safety examples remain advocacy and contextual claims, not findings about this entry.
