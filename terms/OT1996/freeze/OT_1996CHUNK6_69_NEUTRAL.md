## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Chandler v. Miller
- **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 305 (1997); No. 96-126
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued merits case on review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit.
- **Simulated Event Date:** April 15, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether Georgia may require every candidate for designated state office to pass a suspicionless urinalysis drug test as a condition of ballot qualification.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the candidate-testing requirement is a reasonable special-needs search under the Fourth Amendment.
- **Legal Field:** Fourth Amendment, suspicionless testing, and election qualifications.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Eleventh Circuit affirmed final judgment for Georgia, relying on special-needs drug-testing precedents and the State's asserted interest in drug-free officeholders.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** The candidate plaintiffs ask the Court to reverse and enjoin enforcement. Georgia seeks affirmance.
- **Material Facts and Record:** Georgia required candidates for numerous legislative, executive, and judicial offices to submit a state-approved certificate showing a negative test for specified drugs within thirty days before qualifying. The specimen could be provided through a personal physician and results were initially disclosed to the candidate. The State showed no concrete drug problem among officeholders, no safety-sensitive duties common to all covered offices, and no operational program beyond the symbolic qualification.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The completed 1994 election does not moot the case because Chandler represented an intention to run again and the State did not contest it. The Court reaches only the Fourth Amendment search and does not decide separate First Amendment or state-law qualifications claims.

### Issue 1: Suspicionless drug testing of candidates

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton (1995) unanimously holds that urine collection and analysis are searches and that voluntary athletic participation is not a waiver. Its five-Justice merits holding sustains the actual school-athletic program through its protective safety need, athletic privacy expectations, limited collection and information, confidentiality, and restricted consequences. It requires neither a confirmed drug catastrophe nor quantified efficacy or universal exhaustion of less intrusive means; Stone’s stronger necessity/proportionality proposal is not Court law.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Suspicionless drug testing of public-school athletes is limited to its non-law-enforcement setting and does not create a general waiver. More generally, special-needs departures from individualized suspicion require a substantial need connected to the program's actual operation.
- **Other Governing Law:** The Fourth Amendment; Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association; Treasury Employees v. Von Raab; Georgia candidacy law.

## F. VERIFIED SIMULATED-LAW APPLICATION

### Application outside the school-athletic setting

Political candidacy does not carry the school’s custodial responsibility or athletes’ contextual privacy expectations. Georgia’s limited disclosure and collection method reduce intrusion but cannot by themselves supply a qualifying program need. Conversely, absence of a documented drug crisis is relevant, not a universal dispositive element under Vernonia. The State’s claimed needs must be evaluated for the covered offices and this test’s practical operation, including whether advance scheduling actually advances the asserted purpose.
