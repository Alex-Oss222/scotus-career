# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Boggs v. Boggs
* **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 833 (1997); No. 96-79
* **October Term:** 1996
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued ERISA-preemption merits case reviewing whether state community-property law may give a nonparticipant spouse a testamentary interest in undistributed pension benefits contrary to the surviving-spouse and anti-alienation provisions of ERISA.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 2, 1997 (historical)
* **Question Presented:** Does ERISA preempt application of Louisiana community-property law that permits a deceased nonparticipant spouse to transfer by testamentary instrument an interest in the participant’s undistributed pension benefits to her sons, thereby competing with the surviving spouse’s ERISA-protected rights?
* **Question Granted:** Whether the sons’ state-law claims to the undistributed pension benefits and related plan distributions are preempted by ERISA.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fifth Circuit held that ERISA did not preempt the sons’ claims based on their mother’s community-property interest and affirmed relief against the participant and surviving spouse. Boggs v. Boggs, 82 F.3d 90 (5th Cir. 1996).
* **Relief Requested:** The participant’s surviving spouse seeks reversal and rejection of the testamentary community-property claim. The sons seek affirmance of their inherited interest and associated state-law remedies.
* **Material Record:** Isaac Boggs participated in ERISA pension plans during his marriage to Dorothy. Dorothy died and attempted to transfer her asserted community-property share to their sons. Isaac later retired, received benefits and rolled some distributions into an individual retirement account, and married Sandra. After Isaac’s death, the sons sued Sandra for a share traceable to Dorothy’s asserted interest. ERISA’s survivor-annuity, anti-alienation, beneficiary, and qualified-domestic-relations-order provisions regulate interests in plan benefits.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The Court must identify the property and claim at issue, distinguish undistributed plan benefits from assets already distributed and transformed, and determine whether the state rule conflicts with ERISA’s enacted allocation. Preemption does not itself decide every postdistribution state-law claim.
* **Relevant Dates:** The Fifth Circuit judgment issued in 1996; argument occurred January 15, 1997; the Supreme Court event date is June 2, 1997.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None identified as a separate matter requiring adjudication.

### Issue 1: ERISA preemption of testamentary community-property claims

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated Travelers requires attention to ERISA's statutory objects and operation rather than an unbounded 'relate to' formula; general applicability alone does not defeat preemption. ## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Conflict between the testamentary interest and ERISA’s allocation

* **Reasoning Below:** The Fifth Circuit treated Dorothy’s community-property interest as arising before ERISA distribution and surviving through testamentary transfer.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** The asserted transfer would divide and alienate pension benefits outside ERISA’s exclusive domestic-relations mechanism and defeat the surviving spouse’s statutory protection.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** ERISA protects plan administration but does not extinguish ownership rights created by state marital-property law, especially once assets have been distributed.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may hold the undistributed-benefit theory preempted while reserving genuinely independent claims to nonplan assets after distribution.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** ERISA preemption should not be described as a universal federal law of marital property.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The conflict-preemption path examines whether enforcing the testament would recognize a beneficiary or alienation route that ERISA excludes. If so, the judgment must be reversed as to claims dependent on ownership of undistributed plan benefits. The Court should identify but not unnecessarily decide the outer boundary for postdistribution assets and independent obligations. Affirmance would require state law to operate alongside the survivor and anti-alienation provisions without frustrating them.

## Stipulated facts and separate claims

The joint stipulation fixes Dorothy's death on August 14, 1979; Isaac's marriage to Sandra on April 5, 1980; retirement on September 1, 1985; and death on February 16, 1989. Sandra therefore married Isaac before retirement. Dorothy left him one-third of her estate and a lifetime usufruct over the sons' two-thirds. The 1980 succession judgment and Louisiana ownership characterization are distinct from the federal question whether the asserted interest survives ERISA. The respondents' legal claim of ownership vested at acquisition must be tested, not turned into a stipulated federal entitlement.

Sandra filed the federal declaratory action; two sons filed the state accounting action. The four stipulated claims are: an accounting for the asserted Dorothy-derived portion of pension payments Isaac received during life; a percentage of Sandra's survivor annuity; a percentage of the IRA funded by Isaac's lump-sum plan distribution; and a portion of the distributed ESOP shares. The stipulation describes $151,628.94 rolled into the IRA, no withdrawals before Isaac's death, and a $180,778.05 death-date value. Sandra is the named IRA beneficiary and receives the survivor annuity. The stipulation also mentions life-insurance policies, but does not list a separate claim to their proceeds; do not invent one. Its claim paragraph contains the internally inconsistent date September 1, 1995, followed by Isaac's 1989 death; preserve the source error while using the independently stipulated 1985 retirement chronology.

The federal judgment rejects Sandra's preemption position. It is not a quantified accounting award or a final division of each asset. The lower court's postdistribution rationale and respondents' arguments that relief runs only against recipients or the succession are available for assessment; naming a recipient instead of the plan does not itself settle the federal conflict.

## Both legal paths, with statutory limits

One path treats the claim as recognition or transfer at Dorothy's death of an interest in then-undistributed ERISA benefits and asks whether the survivor protections, anti-alienation command, beneficiary definitions and QDRO exceptions foreclose that transfer. The opposing path treats Louisiana law as recognizing preexisting community ownership and imposing an independent accounting after lawful plan distribution without changing benefits or administrative obligations. Neither path is assumed to win. Analyze each of the four claims separately, including the survivor annuity's specific statutory protection and whether a later-distributed asset remains claimed solely through the earlier disputed interest. A limitation on an antecedent transfer does not decide every independent postdistribution obligation, and a general postdistribution characterization does not answer the survivor-annuity question.

The 1994 official Code text supplies complete sections 1055, 1056 and 1144, plus the participant/beneficiary definitions and the relevant REA transition provision. Section 1055 requires the applicable joint-and-survivor or preretirement annuity, subject to its actual plan categories and exceptions. Its consent and waiver conditions are not replaced by mere testamentary intent. The joint-and-survivor definition protects a lifetime survivor amount of at least 50 percent and no more than 100 percent of the joint-life annuity, with the stated actuarial equivalence. Section 1056(d)(3) permits only a qualifying domestic relations order and preserves the specified alternate payees, required identifying/payment information, prohibition on new benefit forms or increased benefits, and protection of prior alternate-payee orders. Section 1144(b)(7) is the linked preemption exception. Do not describe every domestic decree or probate order as automatically qualifying.

Respondents invoke REA section 303(d)'s treatment of pre-January 1, 1985 domestic relations orders: mandatory treatment if the administrator was already paying pursuant to that order on that date, and permissive treatment of other earlier orders even if the amendments' requirements are unmet. The saved text preserves may versus shall. The 1980 succession judgment is an identified source of the argument; the record does not stipulate administrator recognition or payment pursuant to it on January 1, 1985. Whether the asserted order comes within this transition rule and what consequence follows are legal issues, not facts supplied by the word probate. No missing exhibit creates a litigant default.

The IRA is separately identified in the stipulation. The saved 26 U.S.C. 408(g) text governs application of that tax section without regard to community-property laws; subsection (d)(6) separately addresses tax treatment of divorce transfers. Neither sentence should be expanded into a general federal ownership rule without construction. The 1994 text and its amendment/effective-date notes are reading sources for the relevant accrued rights and 1985/1989 transactions, not a claim that every subsection remained unchanged after 1994. No later statutory amendment is applied backward merely because the decision occurs in 1997.

Travelers and Dillingham require examination of an actual plan command, legal benchmark, administration or remedial conflict. They neither create a domestic-relations exemption nor settle this transfer.
