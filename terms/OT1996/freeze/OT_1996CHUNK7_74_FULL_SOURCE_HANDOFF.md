# Brown — primary-record factual and procedural handoff

## Source keys and status

**Matter:** Board of County Commissioners of Bryan County v. Brown, No. 95-1100. The dispute concerns municipal liability under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for the selection of reserve deputy Stacy Burns and the relationship between that selection and Jill Brown's constitutional injuries. The County also contests the effect of Oklahoma personnel standards. The record contains a distinct municipal training verdict and separate state-law findings.

Source keys refer to the saved filings in `sources/chunk7-filings/`: **P02**, petition for certiorari and its appendices; **JA04**, Joint Appendix; **P05**, petitioner merits brief; **R06**, respondent merits brief; **P07**, reply. All share the prefix `micro_IA40385013_0686`. References to a printed page identify that filing's own pagination; references to PDF pages identify physical source pages. The reproduced district orders, judgment, verdict, stipulation and transcript excerpts retain their different evidentiary status. A brief's account of testimony is counsel's representation unless the cited excerpt itself appears in JA04. JA04 is an excerpted appendix, not the whole trial transcript or the complete collection of exhibits.

The verbatim material passages are in [Brown primary extracts](../sources/chunk7-neutral-a/Brown_primary_extracts.md), divided into source sections cited below. Petitioner merits and reply passages are in [Brown petitioner extracts](../sources/chunk7-neutral-a/Brown_petitioner_extracts.md). Spelling and occasional errors in those quotations are source OCR; the prose below distinguishes documentary language, witness testimony, party arguments and lower-court determinations.

## Incident, claims and the judgment actually entered

On May 12, 1991, Jill Brown rode as a passenger while her husband Todd drove from Texas toward their Oklahoma home. Todd turned away from a checkpoint near Cartwright and returned across the Denison Dam into Texas. Deputy Robert Morrison and reserve deputy Burns followed; the vehicles stopped in Grayson County, Texas. The parties sharply disputed the speed and circumstances of the pursuit, whether the Browns initially perceived pursuit, their compliance with commands, and Jill's movements before force was used. The Browns described ordinary driving followed by stopping at the first safe opportunity once pursuit was perceived. The officers described a high-speed pursuit, delayed compliance and a movement that Burns feared was reaching for a gun. The later discovery of firearms in the truck did not establish that Burns had seen them before using force. (P02 appendix 5a–12a; JA04 21a–25a; R06 4–6 and notes 6–9; P05 5–8.)

Burns removed Jill from the truck with an arm-bar maneuver, took her to the pavement and handcuffed her. Jill testified that she was complying, made no threatening reach, could not break her fall because Burns held one arm and her other hand was raised, and landed on her knees. She described being pulled up later by the chain connecting the cuffs. The officers' account and the justification and execution of the maneuver were contested. The plaintiff's expert recognized the arm bar as the lowest level of physical force for extracting and subduing an arrestee, but disputed the need for that force here; R06 also distinguishes proper execution from the described throw. This concession is not a finding that any force was necessary. Jill underwent four knee operations and the medical evidence anticipated further surgery including replacement of both knees. She was detained in cuffs for approximately thirty minutes to over an hour in the various accounts, received no ticket, and was never charged. (P02 appendix 5a–12a and notes 5, 7, 10; R06 5–6 and notes 7–9.)

The district court's December 9, 1992 summary-judgment order dismissed claims against Moore and Morrison and the Eighth Amendment claim against Burns; it retained the relevant Fourth Amendment, County hiring/training and state-law claims. The docket separately records dismissal of intentional infliction of emotional distress on December 11, 1992. The summary-judgment order's recital of a January 4, 1990 assault incident is not an independent trial finding about the date of the campus encounter; Burns later testified that the encounter preceded the January dispositions. (JA04 13a, 19a–29a, 89a–90a.)

The April 19, 1993 jury answered that Burns arrested Jill without probable cause, used excessive force, and was liable for false imprisonment; it rejected qualified immunity for the false-arrest and excessive-force claims. It separately found deliberately indifferent County hiring (interrogatory 6), deliberately indifferent County training (7), negligent County hiring (8), and negligent County training (9). These are distinct findings, not a single general hiring verdict. The interrogatories on County hiring and training did not themselves identify particular underlying offenses, specific screening information, or the jury's precise theory of causation. The instructions supplied the constitutional causation requirement, and interrogatory 10 asked for damages proximately caused by Burns or the County through Moore. (P02 appendix 38a–44a; JA04 122a–124a, 134a–136a; extracts P02-VERDICT and JA-CHARGE.)

The damages actually awarded in the verdict and judgment are as follows. The verdict's fifteen actual-damage items total **$927,302**: past/future physical pain $5,000/$10,000; past/future mental anguish $1,000/$1,000; past/future physical impairment $75,000/$360,000; past/future disfigurement $1,000/$2,000; past earnings $36,000; future earning capacity $180,000; deprivation of the right against excessive force $50,000; deprivation of liberty $50,000; reputation $500; past medical expenses $65,802; future medical expenses $90,000. The district court removed the $36,000 and $180,000 earnings items, leaving **$711,302**. It entered that actual-damage award and **$65,000 trial attorney fees jointly and severally against the County and Burns**, and **$20,000 punitive damages against Burns alone**. The judgment provides interest at 3.43 percent annually from judgment, costs, and conditional joint-and-several attorney fees of $12,500 for the Fifth Circuit appeal, $5,000 for making or responding to a certiorari application, and $5,000 if that application is granted. The last three amounts total $22,500; combined with trial fees they account for $87,500. P02's narrative total differs from the actual verdict line items; the reproduced signed judgment and verdict are the sources for these amounts and allocation. (P02 appendix 34a–44a; JA04 135a–136a; extracts P02-ORDER and P02-VERDICT.)

The Fifth Circuit's operative substituted opinion, 67 F.3d 1174, dated October 23, 1995, replaced the earlier opinion reported at 53 F.3d 1410 in full. It affirmed the district judgment. It sustained the jury's officer-liability determinations, rejection of qualified immunity, punitive award against Burns, and municipal liability on inadequate hiring. It did not analyze and reject the distinct training verdict. Its opening statement limited discussion to points meriting review; its municipal discussion and final explanation rest on hiring. Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied November 29, 1995. (P02 appendix 1a–29a; P05 8–10, 24–26; R06 7–8 note 10.)

The County asks that the withdrawn appellate training analysis be followed and argues that deletion of that analysis did not create a second basis for affirmance. Brown responds that the separate training verdict remained undisturbed and that the County's petition did not challenge it. These are competing submissions about review and relief. Neither the withdrawn opinion nor a party's account is an operative appellate disposition rejecting training. Rejection of the hiring ground alone therefore does not, by itself, establish that all County liability disappears. The independently submitted training theory and state-law findings must be accounted for in any description of the scope of relief; no result on either follows simply from the hiring question. (P05 24–26; R06 7–8 note 10; actual verdict and judgment.)

## What the criminal-history evidence does and does not establish

**Documented conviction and Burns's account.** The plaintiff's expert, Otto Schweizer, enumerated **one assault-and-battery conviction**, nine moving-traffic convictions, false identification, public drunkenness, expired tags, driving under suspension, and an alcohol-related driving conviction. Burns distinguished the latter from DUI: he said he pleaded guilty to actual physical control of a motor vehicle while intoxicated, after pulling over and falling asleep; on cross-examination he acknowledged having driven about half a mile while intoxicated before pulling over. These labels should not be collapsed into a proved DUI conviction contrary to the testimony. (JA04 40a–43a, 88a, 98a; extracts JA-EVIDENCE and JA-BURNS.)

Burns described a **September 1989 campus encounter** in Norman: a member of a fraternity pledge group hit his car; he stopped, confronted the group, was pushed, and pushed back. He acknowledged an assault-and-battery charge and guilty plea, with disposition in January 1990. He said his lifetime fights numbered two, identifying the other as a freshman-year high-school fight. His description of who started the campus altercation is his testimony, not a special jury finding of justification. The January date on multiple record entries should not be converted into another proved assault episode. (JA04 89a–91a, 104a–105a.)

Burns connected other charges to the same arrest episode, acknowledged public drunkenness and possession of false identification, and said the assault was the principal charge; when pressed about resisting arrest he did not recall that charge but acknowledged other charges accompanied the assault arrest. The record thus distinguishes a resisting-arrest charge from a demonstrated resisting-arrest conviction. P05 3–4 and 21–22 argues that the assault, public-drunkenness and related charges concerned the campus encounter. It expressly leaves uncertain whether the traffic violations occurred then or earlier. Calclazier, Burns's grandfather, testified that he asked counsel to consolidate matters for one hearing so the family would not repeatedly travel to court; that all charges were disposed of at one hearing does not prove all charged conduct occurred at one time. The trial court restricted the grandfather's account of counsel's statements to explaining his actions, not their truth. (JA04 97a–100a, 119a–121a.)

**What the printout displayed.** In testimony reading plaintiff's exhibit 29, the OSBI printout, defense expert Ken Barnes identified a February 25, 1989 alcohol/APC entry; September 7, 1989 assault, resisting-officer and public-drunkenness entries; and multiple January 4, 1990 entries including traffic, two further assault references, public drunkenness, tax/revenue and false identification. Together the questioning identified four assault references on the printed record. Barnes thought repeated assault entries could be duplications; counsel suggested separate complainants, and Barnes conceded possibility while explaining that multiple counts ordinarily would be separately designated. He then read a January 4 guilty disposition for assault and battery, as well as dispositions for false identification, public drunkenness and several driving offenses. R06 1–2 describes assault as two counts; that is counsel's characterization, not a warrant to turn all four printed references into four proved assaults. The testimony and the one enumerated conviction must be retained with the printout ambiguity. (JA04 80a–83a; extracts JA-BARNES.)

The distinction matters in both directions: a reasonable review of a confusing printout may call for inquiry even if several entries ultimately describe one incident; duplicated record references cannot establish repeated actual violent acts without supporting evidence. The filed record does not provide a complete narrative of the campus episode beyond the testimony and counsel's presentations. The jury's general verdict does not itself specify which interpretation of the printout it accepted.

**Probation and warrant.** Schweizer testified that probation on the alcohol offense was subject to conditions, that revocation materials identified nonpayment and unperformed community service, and that further offenses occurred during probation. Burns read conditions from the misdemeanor judgment requiring no legal violations, no association with persons with criminal records, no intoxicants, a fine and three days' community service. He admitted that an arrest warrant was outstanding on May 12, 1991 and that he later resolved the matter, but claimed he had not understood himself to be on probation. His explanation about undercover drug work as a substitute for punishment was struck, and the jury was expressly told to disregard it; it is not usable proof of an alternative arrangement. Barnes agreed that unresolved disposition, probation and an outstanding warrant warranted inquiry, and said that, honestly, he was unsure but hiring would be doubtful on the stated facts. His answer was qualified rather than an absolute declaration that he would never hire Burns. (JA04 41a–43a, 83a–84a, 100a–104a.)

**Evidentiary limits.** The County objected to arrests without convictions, their prejudicial effect, the expert's imposition of standards beyond Oklahoma law, lack of causation and use of one incident to establish custom. It obtained a running objection to arrest testimony. The judge admitted the evidence to consider the propriety of allowing Burns to serve, not to prove his character or conduct during the encounter with Jill; the explicit jury instruction prohibited using it to determine whether Burns did a particular act on May 12. That limitation was repeated for probation evidence. Brown's brief later invokes Burns's record in discussing credibility, but a merits analysis must retain the actual evidentiary limit and distinguish that rhetoric from permissible proof. (JA04 33a–43a; R06 4–6 and notes 5, 8.)

## Moore's knowledge, selection, restrictions and training

Burns was the son of Moore's nephew; Calclazier was Burns's grandfather and had worked with the department for about sixteen years. Moore said he had not been around Burns much while Burns grew up, knew that he liked fast driving and had several tickets, had heard of the APC incident, did not know of the campus fight, and did not know of an aggressive reputation. He said a simple assault did not necessarily preclude becoming a good officer and referred to the possibility that a young person could develop into a good officer. These are Moore's stated knowledge and judgments. The family connection does not itself establish knowledge of every offense or constitutional culpability. (JA04 86a, 110a–111a, 114a.)

Moore obtained a driving record and an NCIC check. The OSBI record reflected a May 8, 1991 police-applicant transaction. Schweizer regarded that transaction as a request for criminal history; Barnes said it might reflect material sent in rather than a request for the printout. The lower court and the parties describe Moore as having obtained a criminal-history printout. Moore acknowledged that he noticed or probably noticed suspended-license entries, but said he did not notice assault, false identification or resisting arrest, and responded that Burns had a long record when asked about public drunkenness. He acknowledged no inquiry into dispositions, uncertainty concerning the alcohol charge's disposition, no knowledge of probation, and no further effort to determine it. He testified that the NCIC check **showed no warrant**. The outstanding county warrant and this asserted negative NCIC result must both remain visible; the record does not establish that the NCIC output itself disclosed the warrant. (JA04 45a–46a, 82a–84a, 114a–116a; P02 appendix 18a–23a.)

The Fifth Circuit regarded the evidence as permitting an inference either that Moore closed his eyes to the history or that he knew and disregarded it for family reasons. It found adequate evidence of failure to investigate in good faith, deliberate indifference and direct causation. It stated that the history and assigned law-enforcement duties made further investigation obviously necessary; its footnote 20 allowed the jury to conclude that violent propensity and disregard of law precluded Burns's employment despite no felony record. Those are the **lower court's conclusions about permissible jury inferences**, not separate special jury answers and not a factual finding that Moore actually read every entry. (P02 appendix 21a–24a.)

Schweizer's opinion tied the combined recent record to disregard of law, risk of abusing the public or excessive force, and the need to investigate. He treated the overall pattern, concentrated in the preceding two and a half years, as significant rather than treating one teenage traffic violation or intoxication incident as automatically disqualifying. His cross-examination conceded compliance with Oklahoma hiring qualifications while arguing that reasonable screening and departmental training required more; he also identified an asserted duty to arrest someone with an outstanding warrant. This was expert testimony and legal argument, not an independent adjudication that hiring Burns violated Oklahoma certification law. (JA04 40a–57a; R06 2–3 citing trial 316.)

The County presented Moore's detailed history of other hires: experienced and certified deputies, prior work with officers, references obtained for Morrison, and employees sent for certification before patrol duty. Moore denied previous complaints of police misconduct or abusive patrol stops during his four-year tenure. The operative lower opinion acknowledged adequate screening and certification of the other deputies but held the departure for Burns could still support liability. R06 cites the Browns' prior complaints about delays and harassment at these checkpoints; those complaints are not evidence of prior incidents identical to the excessive force at issue. The two descriptions of complaints must not be converted into an unqualified finding that no one ever complained about any departmental activity. (JA04 106a–112a; P02 appendix 24a note 22; R06 5 note 6.)

The CLEET records custodian's deposition, used to cross-examine Barnes, gave **May 6, 1991** as both application and start of training. Barnes withdrew his earlier six-or-seven-week estimate in light of it. R06 argues that, because sessions occurred three days weekly, Burns could have attended at most three sessions before May 12 and that credibility evidence permitted an inference of none. The exact attendance inference is the respondent's argument; the appendix does not establish the number of classes actually attended. Burns could not recall dates at trial. (JA04 74a–75a, 92a–94a; R06 3–4 and note 5.)

Burns admitted no prior patrol experience and no formal local training. He had said in deposition that he received no County training; at trial he distinguished formal training from watching LETN police-training broadcasts. P05 cites trial 579–580 for ride-along instructions from Howell and Calclazier about drunk driving, stops, custody, radio codes and law-enforcement techniques. R06 notes earlier rides with his grandfather in civilian clothes and challenges whether that supplied meaningful instruction. Burns expressly admitted no County instruction on roadblocks, interstate pursuit, or authority to arrest in Texas. (JA04 93a–95a; P05 4–5; R06 3–4.)

Both Burns and Moore identified restrictions against carrying a firearm and driving a police car. Moore testified that Burns could not make an arrest unless a deputy was present and told him to arrest or assist; he said he had told Burns that the officer he rode with was in charge and Burns was there to observe and learn. Burns said the officer he rode with was more or less in charge, but acknowledged no one directed him to enter the patrol car, participate in the pursuit, approach Jill, remove her, take her down or handcuff her. He believed Moore authorized those acts. The authorization and effective supervision must therefore be described with these qualifications, not as either an undisputed prohibition of all forcible activity or an undisputed grant of unrestricted independent arrest authority. (JA04 95a–96a, 116a–118a.)

R06 4 cites trial 628 and 394 for five takedowns among Burns's first thirteen arrests and an expert's characterization of that ratio as excessive. P07 invokes prior arm-bar uses without complaints in support of the County. The JA04 excerpt at 44a–45a stops a different deposition-based question after a sustained hearsay objection; it does not erase the later trial testimony cited in the briefs. The later trial pages are represented through the briefs here, not supplied as a complete direct transcript segment. Neither the arrest count nor absence of complaints alone establishes the constitutionality of those other encounters, and neither becomes evidence of the applicant's preselection record without a chronology supporting that use.

## Submitted law, preserved objections and appellate limits

The operative municipal charge required inadequacy, deliberate indifference and direct causation. Its actual wording follows, including the distinct formulations for screening and training:

**Verbatim municipal constitutional instructions (JA04 122a?123a; PDF page 64, both sides):**

In order to prove that a county hiring or training policy violated the Plaintiff's rights under Section 1983, the Plaintiff must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that: One, the training or hiring policies of the county's policymaker, B.J. 123a Two, the County policymaker, B.J. Moore, was deliberately indifferent in adopting the hiring or training policy. And three, the inadequate hiring or training policy directly caused the Plaintiff's injury. Sheriff B.J. Moore would have acted with deliberate indifference in adopting an otherwise constitutional training policy if in light of the duties assigned to Deputy Sheriff Stacy Burns the need for more or different training was so obvious and the inadequacy so likely to result in violations of constitutional rights, that the Sheriff B.J. Moore can be reasonable said to have been deliberately indifferent to the constitutional needs of the Plaintiff.

The stipulated fact was that Moore was **the policymaker for Bryan County regarding the Sheriff's Department**. The pretrial contested issues included the Commissioners' asserted absence of participation in policy decisions. The charge identified Moore's acts as final official policy. The Fifth Circuit held that the County stipulated to final policymaking authority and, additionally, waived objection to the charge's identification of Moore by failing to object to that point. P05 and P07 contest that interpretation and point to earlier argument and charge objections; R06 insists that those did not preserve a challenge to **who** held final authority. A challenge to the identity or state-law scope of final authority is distinct from the preserved claim that one decision by that official is not enough to establish the necessary policy, fault or causation. (JA04 30a–32a, 122a, 124a–132a; P02 appendix 16a–18a and note 17; P05 18–20; P07 2–3; R06 1 note 1, 23–24.)

At the close of Brown's case, the County sought judgment as a matter of law for insufficient evidence on § 1983 hiring, training and supervision. It argued absence of a pattern of similar misconduct or widespread incompetence, compliance with state certification requirements, failure to demonstrate policy and a moving causal force, and conclusory expert evidence. It also raised the discretionary-function and recognized-standards exceptions under the Oklahoma Tort Claims Act; Burns separately challenged the force evidence and immunity. The judge denied judgment on the § 1983 claims and took the state exceptions under advisement. P05 reports renewal at the close of all evidence. The reproduced September Rule 50 order states the four postverdict grounds: insufficient evidence for municipal interrogatories; one arguably negligent hire and incident versus required persistent practice; the two state exceptions; and Burns's qualified immunity. It found enough evidence to submit both hiring and training. (JA04 58a–71a; P02 appendix 30a–35a.)

The actual charge-objection transcript is extensive. Among other matters, the County objected to the failure to define custom; treatment of Burns's particular hire as policy; failure to identify a specific hiring/training deficiency and the requisite causal connection; omission of a general policy/pattern prerequisite; denial of municipal good-faith protection based on state compliance; interrogatories 6 and 7; use of constitutional **needs** rather than **rights**; and treating one person's treatment as a policy. It sought a formulation referring to a deficiency in training police officers generally rather than Burns individually, and it challenged pursuit/arrest instructions and aspects of Burns's immunity. The judge overruled the objections. These materials foreclose a blanket statement that no municipal instruction or causation objection was made. They do not automatically resolve whether a particular later proposed formulation of applicant-specific constitutional risk was distinctly requested or preserved: that is a question to evaluate from the actual objections, not an absence to infer from silence in a summary. (JA04 124a–132a; extract JA-CHARGE.)

Separate preservation rules govern the **earnings cross-appeal**. The Fifth Circuit said defendants had not challenged those earnings items in their directed-verdict or postverdict motion; the district court therefore erred in removing them on that basis. Brown, however, failed to object in the district court. The court reviewed only for plain error and found no manifest miscarriage of justice because the proffer consisted of Brown's testimony about prospective commission income of roughly $1,500–$1,800 monthly. It left the removal in place. This waiver is Brown's earnings issue, not waiver of every municipal sufficiency challenge. (P02 appendix 14a–16a.)

On **state-law liability**, the district court applied Oklahoma law to Moore's Oklahoma hiring and training acts under comity principles, finding the Tort Claims Act not contrary to Texas public policy. It applied the planning/operational distinction to § 155(5), regarding formulation of general hiring and training policy as discretionary but selection and deployment of Burns as operational; it rejected § 155(28) because the evidence did not support conformance with then-recognized standards. Those are district-court determinations, not a finding that statutory certification requirements were themselves violated. The Fifth Circuit said it need not address the state-law issues once constitutional liability was sustained. The state-law verdicts and district rulings cannot simply be omitted when identifying what remains if a federal theory fails. (P02 appendix 12a note 13, 33a–34a; JA04 26a–28a; actual verdict.)

## Parties' strongest legal grounds and express limits

**County.** Monell excludes liability merely because Burns was employed by the County. Tuttle demands proof of the County's own policy, fault and a causal connection beyond one employee's tort. The County distinguishes an order to perform an unconstitutional act, as in Pembaur, from a facially lawful selection. It argues that where state law leaves discretion to hire and no pattern puts the County on notice, one imperfect background review and an eventual constitutional injury cannot establish the required policy or causation. Its narrower argument is that these particular misdemeanors, including the campus fight, driving and alcohol history, do not establish a sufficiently certain connection to excessive force despite training and supervision; but-for employment and ordinary foreseeability are insufficient. It questions reliance on an expert's opinion of possible physical aggressiveness when physical action can be lawful in police work. (P02 7–18; P05 15–26; P07 4–11.)

The County also invokes federalism and state personnel choices, contending that the lower approach would impose federal disqualifications beyond lawful state qualifications and invite routine second-guessing. The full cited Oklahoma certification text bars conviction of a felony **or a crime involving moral turpitude**, requires the prescribed psychological evaluation and suitability certification, applies that provision to reserve officers, and includes educational requirements. Trial testimony also describes age, fingerprint/background checks, certification within a year and additional annual instruction. Counsel's shorthand references only to felonies or no misdemeanor bar do not exhaust the statute. Nor does the record justify a blanket statement of compliance with **every** administrative requirement: Barnes conceded probable lateness in the employment-status notice, the judge questioned documents filed after the incident, and counsel disputed their causal relevance. (P02 2–3, 16–17; P05 2–3, 20 note 7; JA04 47a–50a, 62a–63a, 72a–79a.)

The County advances broad single-hire rhetoric but also a conditional alternative: P07 8–10 recognizes that, if the law permits such a claim, knowledge of a serious psychiatric condition manifested by grave violent attacks certain to recur could establish an adequate connection. That is a hypothetical, not a fact about Burns and not a concession that this record meets the standard. The requested municipal good-faith defense is an argument, not an established immunity.

**Brown.** Attribution, culpability and causation must be separately proved, but a single official decision by the final policymaker within his actual authority can be the County's own action under Monell and Pembaur. Brown disclaims reliance on employment alone. She relies on combined proof of Moore's decision, the preemployment record and ready avenues of investigation, the absence of meaningful preparation for foreseeable coercive duties, and the link between the very risk ignored and the injury suffered. She argues that refusing to count preemployment notice while demanding a prior unconstitutional act after hiring would create an arbitrary first-victim bar. She reads Canton to allow obvious-need liability without a prior pattern in appropriate circumstances and rejects both a universal two-hire requirement and a universal two-constitutional-injury requirement. (R06 8–12, 17–35, 41–47.)

Brown does not assert that every decision of a policymaker binds the County, that negligence is enough, or that any bad hire establishes all elements. She excludes decisions outside the official's sphere of final authority and personal acts unconnected with official municipal power; an accidental or merely negligent lapse in generally adequate hiring/training does not suffice. She recognizes that patterns may be necessary to prove custom or notice in cases where fault cannot otherwise be established. She describes causation as proximate cause, requiring the particular constitutional injury threatened by the identified deficiency rather than any injury by the employee: her examples expressly distinguish violent propensity from unrelated racial discrimination, sexual misconduct or retaliatory firing. (R06 28–35, 37–38, 47–48.)

Brown further argues that Canton's reference to moral certainty concerns the recurring need for officers to make force judgments, not certainty that a particular constitutional deprivation will occur; requiring intended or inevitable injury would raise fault beyond deliberate indifference. She stresses the instructions' actual deliberate-indifference and direct-causation requirements and the jury's resolution of contested evidence. Footnote 17 at R06 29–30 floats an alternative argument that a hiring claim's difficult causation showing might obviate a separate deliberate-indifference standard; the body of the brief nevertheless accepts and argues that the jury met that standard. These are distinct arguments, not a holding of law. (R06 29–34.)

On federalism, Brown argues that § 1983 protects federal rights even against conduct allowed by state law; Oklahoma's minimum eligibility rules authorize but do not require hiring every qualifying person. State compliance may be evidence against deliberate indifference, and the County was allowed to present it, but it is not conclusive federal immunity. She distinguishes damages for a particular constitutional wrong from an injunction supervising an entire department and distinguishes Congress's Fourteenth Amendment enforcement power from Commerce Clause limits. On Tuttle she argues that proof of the injury alone could not establish every municipal element, but that her case contains independent proof of decision, indifference and causation. (R06 12–17, 44–45.)

Brown seeks preservation of her judgment, including reliance on the independent training finding. The County seeks relief from municipal liability and resists treating training as an independent surviving basis. Their requests do not choose the permissible disposition, enlarge the reviewed questions, or settle the independent state-law and training consequences.

## Remaining source qualifications material to use of this record

The criminal-history printout is discussed and read aloud in JA04 but its complete original image is not itself reproduced there. Its four assault references, the one expressly enumerated conviction, the witness's duplication explanation, counsel's contrary suggestion and the campus-episode testimony are all preserved above; none can be replaced with an invented precise count of separate assaults. A different conclusion about the source document's formatting would require exhibit 29 itself.

Likewise, the precise contents of some trial pages cited by the parties but not reproduced in JA04 remain attributable brief recitations: examples include LETN/ride-along instruction at trial 579–580 and later takedown testimony at trial 628. Missing full transcripts are not proof that these pages lack the asserted testimony, and a sustained objection in a different excerpt cannot establish exclusion of all later evidence on the subject.

State-law statutory commands, expert views on compliance, the judge's questioning of the timing of documents and the district court's recognized-standards ruling are separate propositions. No broader statutory violation, automatic federal immunity, adverse inference, litigant default or appellate remedy follows merely from the incompleteness of a filed excerpt.
