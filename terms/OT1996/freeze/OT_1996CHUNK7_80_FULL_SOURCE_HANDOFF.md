## Record and actual work, with contrary accounts preserved

John Papai injured his left knee on March 13, 1989 while painting the housing of Harbor Tug's Pt. Barrow at its Alameda home dock. Papai testified that he believed the portable ladder moved as he descended, then qualified that he did not initially remember precisely what happened, and later said he remembered movement where the ladder's wood met the metal housing. He did not notice the boat rock or roll. These are his accounts, not an established finding of vessel negligence. JA 49–50. The eventual nonjury trial made a contrary finding, addressed below.

The job was expected to last one day. Papai knew before arriving that he would do maintenance, chipping and painting, rather than the moving-vessel work he called deckhand work. He expected to go home and possibly receive another standby call, or return to the hiring hall. There was no commitment that he would return to Pt. Barrow the following day, and he would not sail when the tug left. Its engines remained idle, it stayed docked, and no permanent officers or other permanent crew were aboard. Papai received hourly straight-time pay. JA 35, 44–51. Those physical circumstances do not establish that the vessel was out of navigation or that Papai could not be a replacement crew member; those classifications were disputed.

Papai and Edwin Low began painting together under Don Dawson's directions; Dawson later reassigned Low to another tug, Sea King. Papai testified that he asked whether he would work alone and should continue on the ladder, and Dawson told him to finish. Papai thereafter chose the order of painting while alone. JA 45–48. The opening brief calls Dawson a shoreside port captain, consistent with Papai's description of an office at the dock (JA 37). Respondent argues that he directed operations, citing trial volume 9, page 37. Reply footnote 36 disputes any suggestion that he was Pt. Barrow's operating captain, citing trial volume 7, pages 96–97 and volume 9, page 37. Those trial pages are cited in the briefs but are not reproduced in the recovered JA. The title alone does not settle the employment relationship.

Marina Secchitano, the union regional director, supplied contrary classification evidence. Her declaration says Papai was dispatched as a deckhand replacing one more regularly assigned to Pt. Barrow, that Papai and Low served in place of the tug's full two-deckhand complement, and that he was dispatched into the operational crew rather than a separate maintenance-and-repair gang. Painting the housing was ordinary deckhand work. She said the dispatch did not permit Harbor Tug to assign him shoreside, and shoreside workers were differently classified, paid, and credited for seniority. She also said idle engines did not mean a tug was dead or withdrawn from navigation, she had no reason to think the machinery inoperable, and such tugs did not have ships' articles. These are declaration statements, including her express limits of knowledge, not findings that the tug sailed or that Papai performed sea duties that day. JA 56–58, visually checked against PDF pages 31–32.

There is a direct dispatch-timing conflict. Papai recalled Harbor Tug calling his home from a standby list on Sunday evening and his arrival at approximately 8 a.m. Monday. JA 41–44. Secchitano's deposition describes a March 13 telephone dispatch at 8:45 a.m. to report as soon as possible. JA 69. Respondent's statement says Harbor Tug requested two maintenance deckhands at 8:05 a.m. March 13. JA 80. The handwritten slip at JA 59 visibly lists Papai, Harbor Tug, March 13, deckhand maintenance, and an 8:45 dispatch; it also lists Low. The other reproduced slips/log cover March 13–14 and other workers. They are not a ledger of Papai's twelve earlier assignments. Neither competing timing account should be silently converted into the single established sequence.

### Earlier assignments: what the record establishes and does not establish

* The lower majority and respondent's JA 80 statement identify twelve previous Harbor Tug deckhand occasions during 1989. Petitioner's submissions describe thirteen days between January 1 and March 13, including the injury period; its earlier JA 55 statement ambiguously says thirteen days "before" the accident. Respondent describes the injury job as the thirteenth. The record supports repeated work, but the distinct formulations should not be turned into a newly calculated day count or a list of twelve sea voyages.
* Papai testified that he had worked aboard Pt. Barrow three or four times before the accident, always doing maintenance while it was tied to the dock, specifically chipping rust and painting; he had not otherwise been aboard it. JA 34–35, 38. This is specific actual-duty evidence for those Pt. Barrow jobs, not proof that all earlier Harbor Tug jobs were the same.
* The immediately preceding job was maintenance aboard Sea Fox. Papai first estimated March 4, approximately nine days earlier, but counsel consulted the record and corrected the date to March 3, which Papai accepted. JA 44–45. This particular maintenance job can be identified; it cannot supply all remaining assignments' duties.
* In his general account of work obtained through the hiring hall since January 1987, Papai estimated 70 percent deckhand work, most while boats were moving. His examples were docking boats, throwing lines, going from boat to dock to secure a line. He distinguished maintenance while boats were docked from deckhand work while moving. JA 30, 34. This percentage concerns the general period and the question asked, not a proved percentage of the twelve Harbor Tug engagements or of the injury assignment.
* He also did longshoring, including lashing, fish meal, and steel unloading, some aboard ship and some ashore; shore maintenance for Golden Gate; and some shoreside bartending in 1987. Most hiring-hall jobs lasted one day, some two or three days. A forty-day Golden Gate job involved chipping and painting the ferry-terminal dock. JA 29–34. The ALJ's later description that no jobs lasted more than two or three days is therefore its characterization of its hearing record, not a reason to erase the documented forty-day dock job.
* He worked for Red and White, Golden Gate, Harbor Tug, and longshoring employers. JA 38. Respondent footnote 2, page 5, asserts that all qualified-deckhand work from January 1, 1989 was for Harbor Tug and regards the broader multi-employer discussion as unnecessary. Respondent also cites trial volume 9, pages 28–29 for the same contract and pay code 008 on successive engagements. These are filed assertions with their cited locations; the complete payroll and trial pages are not in the recovered JA. The general 1987–1989 account neither proves nor disproves that narrower 1989 assertion by itself.

The Deckhands Agreement prescribes qualified-deckhand knowledge of lines, towing winches, splicing, helmsmanship/lookout, chipping, painting, tools, and safe work; it includes maintenance-and-cure terms, casual-worker relief guarantees, deckhand maintenance duties, and checks of the engine room while underway. JA 71–79. These provisions establish contractual classifications and potential duties. They do not establish that Papai actually steered, kept lookout, checked an engine room underway, or sailed during each earlier Harbor Tug job. No ownership or operational control common to every vessel using the hiring hall is demonstrated merely by the common labor source. The same-employer prior-engagement theory and the common-hiring-hall theory remain separate alternatives.

## Civil proceedings, preservation, and the unaddressed vessel-negligence appeal

The January 12, 1990 complaint alleged Jones Act negligence, unseaworthiness, maintenance and cure, and Joanna Papai's loss of consortium, with a jury demand. It alleged unsafe equipment, insufficient help, unsafe working arrangements, deficient instructions and other breaches; the allegations are not findings. JA 14–18. On May 29, 1990 the District Court dismissed the Jones Act and general maritime-law causes, including maintenance and cure, with prejudice for lack of seaman status, while expressly leaving possible LHWCA or common-law negligence claims unaffected and permitting amendment. Pet. App. 22a–23a.

The First Amended Complaint, filed May 24, 1990 according to the docket, alleged vessel negligence under section 905(b), general maritime principles, and consortium; it retained a jury demand. JA 1, 11, 63–66. The fact that the later trial was nonjury does not itself establish how or why a jury right was relinquished. Reconsideration was denied by the order signed August 17 and filed August 21, 1990. That order certified a controlling question under section 1292(b), but the Ninth Circuit denied interlocutory review October 30, 1990. Pet. App. 4a, 24a–25a. Thus the correct description is denial of permission for interlocutory appeal, not denial of district-court certification.

Following Wilander and Gizoni, the District Court rebriefed status and on April 6, 1992 reaffirmed summary adjudication because Papai lacked a more-or-less permanent vessel connection and had not performed substantial work on the vessel sufficient for seaman status. Pet. App. 26a–27a. The negligence trial ran August 31–September 17, 1992. The court delivered reasons December 16; the judgment was signed December 28 and entered December 29. Pet. App. 28a–29a; JA 81–86. These documents resolve inconsistent dates in party narrative, without adopting respondent's conflicting December dates.

The trial judge applied Scindia to vessel-operator duties, distinguished employer conduct from vessel conduct, treated OSHA standards as relevant but not controlling or establishing a violation by themselves, and placed the preponderance burden on Papai. He found that the ladder did not slip and that Papai instead slipped, missed a rung, or stepped down improperly. He rejected corroboration from Low as unreliable, compared the contemporaneous accounts, and found that Papai also had choices about equipment and its use. He found no proven negligence under either section 905(b) or general negligence principles and did not reach medical or economic damages. These are the trial findings and grounds, not neutral concessions and not necessarily facts properly resolved against Papai on the earlier status summary judgment. JA 82–85.

Papai's January 20, 1993 appeal challenged both partial summary judgment and the post-trial negligence judgment. The Ninth Circuit majority expressly did **not** reach the challenge to the trial findings because it reversed the status summary judgment. Pet. App. 2a, 4a–5a, 12a. Its footnote 4 expected Jones Act negligence under a more favorable standard and jury consideration; that expectation does not adjudicate the independent section 905(b) challenge. A disposition defeating seaman remedies must identify the proper treatment of the unresolved vessel-negligence appeal rather than silently treating it as adjudicated on appeal. The sources do not authorize deciding the ultimate consortium recovery or damages independently of the actual surviving claims.

## Compensation proceedings and formal award

Voluntary compensation preceded the formal award. The ALJ records a stipulation that Papai received weekly disability benefits of $238.46 from injury to the June 2, 1992 hearing. Pet. App. 34a. Reply pages 10–11/footnote 20 give more detailed claimed filing dates: employer LS-206 March 30, 1989; Papai LS-203 April 15, 1991 alleging an additional back injury; and employer LS-207 May 30, 1991 controverting the back claim. The reply says this injury dispute, not status, caused the hearing. Respondent describes the hearing as necessary to establish the compensation lien before the civil trial. The LS forms themselves are not reproduced in the recovered originals; these competing accounts remain attributed. The ALJ identifies five actual disputed issues: jurisdiction, average weekly wage, total-disability period, permanent partial disability, and section 8(f) relief. Pet. App. 34a.

The formal June 2, 1992 hearing included appearances by claimant and employer/carrier counsel, oral evidence, exhibits, later admission of Dr. Dye's deposition, and post-hearing briefs. The Director submitted a prehearing statement but did not appear. Pet. App. 32a. The ALJ's order directly addressed crew status. Harbor Tug conceded in substance the navigable-water and employment nexus but argued that Papai might be a Jones Act seaman and thus excluded by section 902(3)(G). The ALJ noted that each party's compensation position conflicted with its civil position. He refused to treat the District Court's status orders as preclusive because the still-pending section 905(b) claim left those orders interlocutory. Pet. App. 34a–35a and footnote 2.

The ALJ applied Wilander's land/sea distinction and vessel-connection focus, referred to Robison's permanent assignment or substantial work on a vessel/fleet plus contribution test, and recognized that Wilander displaced Bullis's aid-in-navigation prong. He described some duties as those crew members might perform, but found random, sporadic and transitory assignments, no permanent vessel/fleet connection, and noncrew status. He cited Griffin, 25 BRBS 196 (1991), as support. Pet. App. 35a–37a. That is an express status adjudication, not merely a benefits check or compromise; its preclusive scope and correctness under relevant law remain legal questions.

The order denied an increase in average weekly wage based on projected union seniority as speculative; found total disability until suitable work became available November 12, 1990; awarded permanent partial benefits for the left knee and right foot; denied additional monetary compensation for hip/back symptoms on the reasons given; and denied section 8(f) relief because the compensable disabilities were scheduled injuries. The ordered rate was $238.46, with 106.56 weeks of scheduled partial benefits starting November 12, 1990, credit for prior payments, interest on past-due compensation, calculation by the District Director, and a subsequent fee petition. Pet. App. 38a–55a. This was not an award granting every claim Papai advanced.

There is a small internal date inconsistency: the ALJ's analysis repeatedly uses February 5, 1990 as the point the knee became permanent/stationary, but ordered paragraphs 1–2 use February 2. Both are in the actual source, not just OCR. No outcome here depends on choosing one without correction. The ALJ signed the decision August 27, 1992; the certificate records filing and certified-mail service August 31. Neither side appealed; the lower majority and both merits sides treat the award as final. Pet. App. 4a, 54a–57a.

Section 921(a) makes an order effective on filing and final at expiration of the thirtieth day thereafter unless the specified review proceedings are instituted. Section 921(b)(3) permits Board appeals by a party in interest raising a substantial question of law or fact and makes adequately supported fact findings conclusive on Board review. Section 921(c)'s sixty-day period concerns review of a **final Board order** by an adversely affected or aggrieved person in the court of appeals. It is not a sixty-day first appeal from this ALJ award. Respondent's asserted October 27 date must not be adopted as the ALJ appeal deadline. No computation of an exact last day or ruling on appellate standing is needed merely to state the documented no-appeal/finality posture.

Respondent says the award was disclosed through a lien filing entered September 2, 1992 and preclusion was first urged on appeal July 1993. The district docket shows a lien notice dated August 27 entered September 2 and an amended lien request December 9. JA 6, 9. The Ninth Circuit judicially noticed the award: Papai challenged the propriety of notice but not the order's existence or copy's authenticity; the court deemed his reply and oral argument a sufficient opportunity to address notice. Pet. App. 9a–10a, footnote 5. A filed award and a judicial-notice ruling do not themselves decide waiver, equitable estoppel, adequacy of litigation, or every predicate of issue preclusion.

## Lower appellate grounds and each side's actual legal alternatives

Takasugi, joined by Reinhardt, read the total-circumstances inquiry to include daily assignments through a common labor pool: a worker ordinarily doing seaman work should not lose status solely because employers used daily hiring. Separately, twelve prior Harbor Tug engagements might themselves supply sufficient connection. It held a jury question remained. Pet. App. 5a–9a. Poole disagreed: an identifiable fleet required common ownership/control, not any vessels a worker happened to board; he read the cited changed-assignment discussion as changing the relevant period within an employment rather than eliminating the employer/fleet boundary. He regarded Papai's own vessel/fleet connection as legally insufficient. Pet. App. 13a–19a. Those are competing lower-court grounds, not new Supreme Court conclusions.

The majority also **decided and rejected** the formal-award preclusion argument. It distinguished Gizoni's unlitigated voluntary-payment facts but extended its credit and anti-forced-election reasoning, expressed concern about rewarding an employer that forced compensation litigation, and noted the opposed status positions in the two proceedings. Pet. App. 9a–12a. Poole's dissent resolved status and did not independently decide a separate preclusion rationale. The lower opinion therefore cannot be summarized as merely reserving preclusion.

Harbor Tug's opening brief advances two independent compensation grounds. First, it reads section 905(a) to bar further seaman remedies after a formal award, including an approved section 8(i) settlement, **without** requiring ordinary issue-preclusion elements. Second, it says this particular litigated order satisfies administrative collateral estoppel because status was presented, actually litigated, necessary, and explicitly found; it invokes Astoria and Utah Construction absent contrary statutory intent. Petitioner merits 19–26, 30–31. It distinguishes voluntary payments and allows initial pursuit of alternative remedies. Reply expressly disclaims an affirmative election-of-remedies defense. A statutory bar, issue preclusion, judicial estoppel, and compelled initial election are different theories.

Harbor Tug answers the statutory-credit argument by noting that section 903(e) expressly credits Jones Act payments against compensation, not the reverse; it argues that a Jones Act settlement without a status finding can give that provision work while preserving preclusion. It cites Massey and Bundens to argue credits and attorney fees are not necessarily symmetrical. It accepts reciprocal consequences when a prior tribunal decides the claimant is a seaman or not a seaman, as described in opening brief footnote 23. It distinguishes Bassett's direct review standard from collateral attack and says no unsupported-administrative-finding exception would help on these facts. Those are submitted alternatives and qualifications, not adopted rules.

Respondent challenges categorical statutory foreclosure and issue preclusion separately: statutory credit and vessel negligence show some overlap; status was not adequately adversarially litigated; the parties took inconsistent positions; the ALJ used an inadequate connection standard; a claimant obtaining an award might lack a proper route or incentive to appeal; and later administrative proceedings should not cut off review of the earlier judicial status ruling. He also argues unfair retroactive use of a newly announced preclusion rule and distinguishes Sharp, Fontenot, Figueroa, Hagens, and Simms. The cited decisions must be examined directly if any such distinction becomes dispositive. Respondent seeks affirmance; employer seeks reversal and foreclosure of further seaman remedies.

Reply argues that the claimant could seek a stay, withdraw the compensation claim before trial, control sequencing, or withhold consent to settlement; it invokes section 913(d). The statutory tolling text applies when a damages recovery is denied because the claimant is a covered employee, the defendant a covered employer, and required compensation was secured; it is not an unconditional guarantee that every denied tort claim leaves compensation timely. None of these general arguments establishes that Papai actually obtained, requested, or was assured a stay or withdrawal. Reply also invokes expiry of section 922 modification, while respondent disputes finality consequences. Section 922 allows specified modification for changed conditions or factual mistake within its periods, but excludes settlement modification; the last-payment date and actual availability of a particular modification remedy cannot be invented from the date the award was signed.

On status, Harbor Tug argues for the actual injury assignment or, in limited fleet circumstances, vessels of the same employer; it characterizes each engagement as self-contained and the injury work as dockside maintenance. Respondent argues that normal deckhand maintenance was part of continuing qualified-deckhand service and did not change his essential assignment; the repeated Harbor Tug jobs supply a narrower path independent of the common-hall theory. Reply accepts that hiring-hall dispatch is not categorically disqualifying and distinguishes an all-seagoing one-day assignment. Thus neither a categorical bar for every short job nor a finding that all prior jobs were sea voyages accurately describes the contested record.

## Statutory limits that abbreviated brief quotations do not erase

The official 1994 text supplies the following material qualifications, copied in full in the statutory extracts:

The source edition is not itself the effective date. The six sections' amendment and effective-date notes show their last listed changes in 1984; the relevant 1984 injury-based amendments apply after September 28, 1984, and the pertinent review/modification amendments were effective that date. Thus the provisions quoted here predate the 1989 injury and 1992 award. No materially intervening enactment is identified in these sources; the 1994 edition alone does not independently certify the absence of any later 1995–1997 change. These texts are verification of existing statutes, not a new legislative event in this matter.

* Section 902(3) excludes crew members under (G). Its state-compensation condition attaches to exclusions (A)–(F), not the separate crew exclusion or small-vessel-master hiring exclusion in (H). Do not transplant one exclusion's qualification into another.
* Section 903(e) credits any amounts paid under another workers' compensation law or the Jones Act for the same injury, disability, or death against LHWCA liability. The parties dispute its consequences for sequential suits and preclusion; the text itself does not decide those consequences.
* Section 905(a)'s exclusivity includes claims of the worker, spouse and other enumerated derivative claimants, but expressly permits an election if the employer fails to secure required compensation; in that damages action fellow-servant negligence, assumption of risk and contributory negligence are unavailable defenses. Its contractor/subcontractor sentence has its own failure-to-secure condition. Reply acknowledges the exception and says no such failure is claimed here.
* Section 905(b) permits a negligence action against a vessel, bars employer indemnity to the vessel, excludes seaworthiness liability, and makes its remedy exclusive against the vessel except LHWCA remedies. It also has distinct restrictions for stevedoring negligence and for shipbuilding/repair/breaking workers whose employer is the vessel owner or other listed operator: the latter bars suit against that employer in any capacity and against its employees. These restrictions cannot be omitted from an unconditional general statement of dual-capacity liability. The recovered lower disposition addressed this Papai claim on negligence; it did not newly decide that every painting task necessarily falls within the statutory repairing-services restriction. Section 905(c)'s separate Outer Continental Shelf reciprocal-indemnity qualification is reproduced, but no such factual setting is presented here.
* Section 921 distinguishes filing-based effectiveness, thirty-day finality, Board review, sixty-day review of a final Board order in the court of appeals, and enforcement. A legal right to review does not itself prove each preclusion predicate. Section 922's changed-condition/factual-mistake modification has its own one-year periods and payment-adjustment limits; no settlement modification is authorized by that section.
