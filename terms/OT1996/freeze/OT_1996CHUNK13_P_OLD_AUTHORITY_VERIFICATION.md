## Verified sources and confined propositions

- **[In re Terry, 128 U.S. 289 (1888)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/128/289), opinion paragraphs 14–27.** The Court rejected an original habeas attack on a summary commitment for an observed violent disruption and resistance to the marshal. Within that contemporary collateral-review jurisdiction, it accepted the commitment's facts and distinguished jurisdictional defects from errors. The offender's voluntary movement to a nearby room did not defeat jurisdiction already attached; judgment was entered during the same continuous occasion. The Court reserved materially later punishment at another day or term. Its contemporary habeas review limitation is not a replacement for former §2254(d). The observed conduct and immediate institutional need matter to the exception; this is not a holding that every in-court act is summarily punishable.

- **[Cooke v. United States, 267 U.S. 517 (1925)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/267/517), opinion paragraphs 70–87.** A privately delivered letter was distinct from contempt in open court. Personal knowledge of authorship did not eliminate notice, counsel on request, opportunity for defense, witnesses or mitigation. The Court contrasted direct court-disrupting conduct whose immediate suppression protects public judicial authority. It reversed unfair procedure and directed that another judge hear the matter because the record showed marked personal involvement. Neither a universal substitute-judge rule nor a finding that the judge in Pounders was similarly embroiled follows.

- **[In re Oliver, 333 U.S. 257 (1948)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/333/257), opinion paragraphs 18–25.** The state court's secret adjudication lacked basic notice and an opportunity to defend. Its narrow summary exception requires misconduct openly before the judge, disturbance of the court's business, essential elements actually observed, and the institutional need identified in Cooke. A finding relying partly on others' testimony outside the accused's presence did not satisfy that exception. The Court reserved whether false/evasive testimony could ever be detected solely through observation. The exception's personal-observation and necessity predicates are distinct; neither a short sentence nor contempt's label supplies them.

- **[Sacher v. United States, 343 U.S. 1 (1952)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/343/1), Court opinion paragraphs 5–16 and footnotes 11–15.** The accepted question concerned procedure, with the underlying contempt facts not reopened. Rule 42's summary character concerned dispensing with ordinary trial form, not mandatory instantaneous judgment. A judge could defer qualifying punishment to protect the ongoing trial and defendants' representation without losing the power. Repeated warnings and sustained misconduct described that record; the opinion did not prescribe a minimum number of violations for every case. The surviving specifications were independently sufficient after the alleged-conspiracy specification fell. The dissents advocated materially different hearing, disqualification or jury protections; those positions are not the holding. Rule 42 remains a federal procedural source, not the California statute.

- **[In re McConnell, 370 U.S. 230 (1962)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/370/230), Court opinion paragraphs 6–18.** Applying §401's limited federal contempt power, the Court found no actual obstruction from counsel's announced intention to ask forbidden questions when he asked none after the recess. Persistent good-faith argument differs from an obstacle to judicial performance. The Court reserved whether counsel's underlying understanding of Rule 43(c) was correct. This does not establish that actual disobedient questioning is identical to an unfulfilled threat, or that only physical disruption can obstruct proceedings. The dissent favored affirmance and ordinary appellate discretion; it supplies no controlling rule.

- **[Harris v. United States, 382 U.S. 162 (1965)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/382/162), Court opinion paragraphs 4–10 and footnotes.** The Court required Rule 42(b) process for the grand-jury refusal repeated before a judge to place it within the judge's view. The delay needed for a hearing would not imperil those proceedings, and no exceptional direct threat required immediate action. A hearing could disclose defense or mitigation; the Court did not find that any hypothesized extenuation actually existed. Harris expressly overruled Brown's contrary treatment. It concerns the federal rules and must be read with Wilson's later retained but distinguished treatment.

- **[United States v. Wilson, 421 U.S. 309 (1975)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/421/309), Court opinion paragraphs 11–17 and footnotes 5–10.** Respectful refusal to testify after immunity and a direct order could obstruct an ongoing trial as seriously as disorder. The majority required neither insulting language nor physical violence, distinguished Harris's interruptible grand-jury setting, and retained least-adequate-power and abuse-of-discretion constraints. Its record involved actual thwarting of trial proof, clear orders, warning and an opportunity to speak. The majority rejected an unsupported hypothesized psychiatric defense; that does not erase a different supported notice/intent dispute. Blackmun's narrower reading of Harris was additional concurrence reasoning; Brennan's dissent urged the contrary process requirement. Neither becomes the Court's rule.
