## 48. Andrade v. Mayfair Management, Inc.

**Channel, reach and disposition:** Review under the stipulated §1254 merits grant of 88 F.3d 258. Vacate the employer-attribution judgment and remand for application of agency principles, preserving genuinely unresolved Rule 50, instructions and preservation questions. Do not reinstate the $25,000 verdict directly. Do not reopen constructive discharge. All eight Associates support this disposition and the common ground below.

**Common propositions:** Title VII employer attribution is not confined to negligence in learning of and correcting harassment. An employer may be responsible where delegated supervisory authority actually aided the supervisor's creation or maintenance of the unlawful environment, although higher management lacked actual or constructive notice. A supervisor's title or mere opportunity to encounter an employee is insufficient by itself; the inquiry concerns the authority actually delegated and its connection to the misconduct. Making or threatening employment decisions is a strong route, but an express threat, completed discharge or measurable economic loss is not an indispensable condition of every agency-supported claim. The employer's notice and response remain relevant to a distinct negligence route. Harris and DeAngelis determine whether the environment crosses the statutory threshold; neither resolves attribution.

The administrator's hiring/firing authority and actual supervision require examination with verdict-favorable permissible inferences. Reporting to a corporate officer does not itself negate agency. This is a correction of the no-notice barrier, not a finding that this record necessarily establishes the relevant connection or that every allegation was proved. The mandate leaves available ordinary review of the verdict under the correct legal standard, including properly preserved alternative grounds and any instruction problem. It orders no new trial automatically.

**Adversarial paths:** The employer's strongest lawful route is affirmance because misconduct for personal ends may fall outside ordinary employment scope and because Meritor rejects status-based automatic liability. That objection warrants a genuine agency connection, not a universal notice defense: misuse of delegated power can explain how an otherwise unauthorized wrong became possible. The competing employee route would impute every supervisor's harassment or restore the verdict outright. It exceeds the established connection and unresolved trial-predicate record. The corrected-standard remand answers both objections without introducing later affirmative defenses.

**Operative construction:** The later-sentencing federal court may not direct a §924(c) term to run concurrently with any preexisting state term of imprisonment. The unqualified words covering any other term include state as well as federal terms. The nearby restriction to a predicate crime prosecutable in federal court defines the federal firearm offense; it is not repeated in the separate concurrency command. ### 1. Community conditional liberty

When the State actually releases a prisoner to live at his own residence, work in the community, and conduct ordinary community life subject to parole-like supervision and conditions, termination of that conditional liberty requires the protections of Morrissey v. Brewer, 408 U.S. 471, 480–490 (1972). The State cannot avoid that protection merely by calling the release preparole, retaining legal custody, identifying overcrowding as a program purpose, or reserving a separate decision about formal parole.

Morrissey protects the existing conditional freedom to live outside prison. It already accounts for the original conviction, continuing sentence, restrictions, supervision, and the State's need to return a person to custody on legally sufficient grounds. Harper's residence, work, and reporting arrangement present that liberty, rather than a routine transfer between prison settings. Five months of release reinforce the concrete nature of the deprivation but are not a new minimum duration for constitutional protection.

Greenholtz v. Inmates, 442 U.S. 1, 7–12 (1979), distinguishes a request for release not yet obtained from the loss of conditional freedom already possessed. The Governor's refusal of formal parole is not automatically a constitutionally adequate revocation proceeding for existing preparole liberty. ### 3. Process and its operative qualifications

Morrissey's ordinary protections govern termination of this liberty. They protect reliable determination of the legally asserted ground and the decision whether revocation is warranted, while preserving the State's authority to use informal administrative proceedings. The case does not add a criminal-trial model, a new evidentiary burden, a fixed numerical deadline, or an absolute requirement that every final revocation hearing precede initial arrest or detention.

**Preliminary stage.** When the participant is arrested and detained pending revocation, a reasonably prompt inquiry at or reasonably near the place of the alleged violation or arrest determines whether probable cause or reasonable ground supports detention for a parole-condition violation. A person not directly involved in the case makes that determination; the officer need not be a judge and may be a different parole officer. The participant receives notice of the inquiry, its purpose, and the alleged violations; may appear, speak, and provide relevant documents or witnesses; and, on request, may question an adverse informant in his presence. If the hearing officer determines that disclosure of the informant's identity would expose the informant to a risk of harm, confrontation and cross-examination of that informant may be withheld. The officer summarizes the proceedings and gives the reasons and evidence supporting the probable-cause determination. Formal judicial findings are not required. A supported probable-cause determination permits continued detention and return to the institution pending the final decision; no such determination is manufactured here.

**Final stage.** The State must afford an opportunity for a final revocation hearing, if desired, within a reasonable time after custody and before the effective final revocation decision. The inquiry resolves contested relevant facts and whether the established circumstances warrant revocation; the participant may deny a violation or present mitigation explaining why a violation should not result in revocation. Its minimum protections are:

1. Written notice of the claimed violations or asserted revocation grounds.
2. Disclosure of the evidence against the participant.
3. An opportunity to be heard in person and to present witnesses and documentary evidence.
4. Confrontation and cross-examination of adverse witnesses, unless the hearing officer specifically finds good cause for withholding confrontation.
5. A neutral and detached decisionmaker, such as a parole board whose members need not be judges or lawyers.
6. A written statement of the evidence relied on and reasons for revocation.

These proceedings may consider letters, affidavits, and other material that a criminal trial would exclude. Morrissey's reference to two months as not apparently unreasonable on the circumstances it discussed is not a universal deadline or safe harbor. A parolee cannot relitigate issues already determined against him in another forum, including a criminal conviction that supplies a revocation ground. No such prior determination, valid waiver, admission disposing of an actual violation, risk-of-harm finding, or good-cause finding is supplied for Harper, and none is presumed. The preliminary informant-risk qualification and final-stage specific-good-cause qualification remain distinct. Morrissey itself reserved the counsel question; this case announces no separate counsel holding or claim that all later date-eligible counsel doctrine is unresolved.

**Citizen-suit channel and statutory zone:** Section 1540(g)'s authorization to any person, read with its particular enforcement channels and broad remedial setting, removes an additional species-preservation zone restriction for a claim that subsection actually authorizes. Section (g)(1)(C) permits an action alleging failure of the Secretary to perform a nondiscretionary act or duty under §1533; petitioners' allegation of a de facto critical-habitat designation without §1533(b)(2)'s required consideration of economic effects is within that asserted duty channel at this threshold stage. The characterization and ultimate merits remain disputed: no actual designation, triggered duty or omitted legally required consideration is found.

Section (g)(1)(A), addressing a person alleged to violate the Act or regulations, is not a general authorization to challenge every alleged maladministration by the Secretary of §1536. Read in context with the distinct Secretary-duty provision, it does not replace ordinary APA review of these consultation claims. Section (g)(1)(B)'s emergency enforcement mechanism is different and is not invoked. Section 1533(b)(2)'s mandatory consideration of economic and other relevant impacts when designating or revising critical habitat remains distinct from discretionary exclusion when exclusion benefits outweigh inclusion benefits, subject to the prohibition on exclusion where the best scientific and commercial data show failure to designate will result in extinction. Reviewability does not command a particular exclusion or import that economic-balancing rule wholesale into §1536 consultation.

**APA route and provision-specific zone:** Sections 702 and 704 supply review of these §1536 consultation claims because the ESA establishes no scheme-specific preclusion of that route and the citizen-suit channel supplies no other adequate judicial remedy for these particular administrative claims. Section 1540(g)(5) preserves other available enforcement/remedial rights; it does not itself establish every APA prerequisite. Dalton's unanimous preclusion holding rested on the indivisible base-closure package, deadlines and confined review scheme, which this ESA challenge does not present. Its four-Justice recommendation-finality theory is not law. Section 1536(a)(2)'s best-scientific-and-commercial-data command serves sound decisionmaking and protects affected interests against restrictions unsupported by the required data; the irrigators' alleged injury from such restrictions is at least arguably within that provision's zone. The inquiry concerns the provision sued upon, not an assumption that every ESA provision protects only species or that every economically affected person may enforce every conservation duty. Agency scientific judgment and the merits of the data claim remain for lawful review, not de novo judicial biology.

**Final agency action:** The Service's Biological Opinion completes its consultation determination and has legal consequences through the incidental-take statement: compliance with its conditions changes the taking liability regime under §1536(o)(2). It is not merely tentative advice. The Bureau retains its own obligations and operational choices, but that does not erase the completed Service action or the conditions attached to the protection from prohibited-taking liability. Franklin's controlling five-Justice finality holding requires examination of completed subordinate action despite a later governmental step; its presidential-status reservation remains irrelevant here. This reasoning satisfies both completion and consequence requirements without holding that every advisory report or prediction is final. The Bureau's stated compliance separately reinforces the pleaded causal account; practical influence alone is not silently made a substitute for finality's legal consequences.

**Preserved statutory qualifications:** Under §1536(a)(2), agencies must avoid likely jeopardy and destruction or adverse modification of designated critical habitat unless a Committee exemption under (h) has been granted, using the best scientific and commercial data available. No exemption is found here. Under (b)(4), an incidental-take statement requires the Secretary's specified no-violation/alternative and incidental-take conclusions, and, for a marine mammal, the separate statutory taking authorization; it specifies impact, reasonable/prudent minimization measures, applicable marine-mammal measures and implementing terms including reporting. Under (o)(2), taking complying with those terms is not prohibited taking; this is not an unconditional immunity from every ESA obligation. The distinct exemption-based protection in (o)(1) is not this statement's source. No marine-mammal finding is needed or made for these fish.

The citizen-suit notice limits also remain intact: (g)(1)(A) generally requires 60 days' written notice to the Secretary and alleged violator and is barred by a commenced civil penalty action or a commenced diligently prosecuted United States criminal action addressing the violation. The (B) channel separately requires 60 days' notice explaining the claimed emergency and is barred by the Secretary's commenced diligent emergency-determination action. The (C) channel requires 60 days' notice to the Secretary, except suit respecting an emergency posing significant risk to the well-being of a fish, wildlife or plant species may follow notification immediately. No unproved compliance, applicable emergency exception or enforcement bar is adjudicated. Section 702's nonmoney-relief waiver preserves other legal/equitable limits and statutory prohibitions; §704 retains its no-other-adequate-remedy and finality rules, including its specified treatment of reconsideration and administrative appeals. This reviewability disposition neither invents a notice defect nor silently waives a preserved one.

**Adversarial paths:** Respondents' strongest Article III path identifies the Bureau's independent choice and uncertain ultimate water outcome; it does not defeat sufficiently pleaded predictable compliance and the possibility of concrete relief from the challenged constraints. Their statutory path correctly rejects using (g)(1)(A) to supervise every Secretary act, but it does not establish preclusion of a distinct APA action. Their finality path emphasizes that a biological opinion does not itself dictate every Bureau operation; it fails to account for the completed consultation and conditional taking protection. Petitioners' broadest path would treat “any person” as enough for all claims and relief; it fails because Article III, each citizen-suit channel, provision-specific APA interests and finality remain independent requirements. The shared reverse-and-remand path gives each limitation its work and preserves all merits disputes.
