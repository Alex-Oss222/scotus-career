## Material record, with evidentiary status preserved

**Warrant and advance knowledge.** The officers had a warrant for Room 138 at the Ramada Inn in Madison, Wisconsin, covering cocaine and related evidence. The issuing judge refused requested no-knock authorization; Pharo confirms the refusal at JA 42, and the request and warrant language are struck in the warrant materials, JA 2–10. The search's authorization is distinct from its manner of execution. The affidavit describes informant information, cash motel rentals, calls, refusal of maid service, packaging material in a vacated room, and an earlier arrest with 63 cocaine packets. These supported the warrant application. Its general description of drug traffickers possessing firearms supplies no particular finding that Richards or his room contained a weapon.

**Ruse, visibility, and recognition.** At about 3:40 a.m. on December 31, 1991, Pharo wore a Ramada Inn shirt, knocked, and identified himself as a maintenance man. His testimony describes the door opening about an inch and a half, Richards looking over his right shoulder toward a police officer in full uniform three or four feet away, a surprised expression, and the door being slammed as Pharo started to enter. One of at least three officers to his right was in uniform; others there and two to his left were in plain clothes. JA 38–44. Richards testified that he saw only Pharo, saw no uniformed officer, and closed the door when Pharo tried to force it. JA 45–48. The merits brief and reply describe a field of vision that permitted observation of the uniformed officer; that is counsel's statement of possible visibility, not an admission of actual recognition. The reply disputes whether a recognizable police officer was unequivocally within view.

**Identity, purpose, force, and entry are different events.** Pharo said the identity shout followed closure by two or three seconds. He describes trying the knob and using arm force before or while identity was announced, kicking after the shout, and another officer finally shouldering the door open. JA 39–45. Thus identity before crossing the threshold is not identity before every application of force. In supplemental testimony, he recalled other officers shouting that they had a search warrant when he heard the bang of the door opening, and agreed this was simultaneous. JA 64–68. Do not report purpose as an undisputed completed announcement before force or successful entry. Richards denied hearing police identification until the officer outside the window stopped him.

**Trial findings and later events.** The trial court discounted Richards's testimony as self-serving and lacking some credibility and inferred his awareness of the uniformed officer. It considered the surprised expression, closing and locking, the failed ruse, identification before threshold crossing, disposability of drugs, and flight. JA 54–56. This is a credibility determination and inference on conflicting testimony, not an uncontested admission, a finding of actual evidence destruction, or proof of every officer's pre-force knowledge. Breaking glass and window flight occurred as the door opened. The later flight may bear on the factfinder's inference about earlier awareness, but cannot itself be treated as information the officers already possessed when first using force. Richards's testimony says the other occupant remained inside; do not report that both escaped. Drugs and money were later found above bathroom ceiling tiles, not in plain view, and their discovery does not establish pre-entry sounds of destruction. JA 10–12, 18–32, 47.

**Supplemental hearing.** Wisconsin moved to supplement after the first hearing; Richards objected on finality and waiver grounds. The trial court allowed supplementation and said the added testimony did not change its ruling. JA 60–69. This record neither erases that objection nor establishes a new appellate ruling on it. The lawful scope of review and the significance of the existing findings must be assessed before adding findings or ordering a hearing.

**Judgment below.** The Wisconsin Supreme Court majority reaffirmed its State v. Stevens exception for warrants seeking evidence of felonious delivery or felonious possession with intent to deliver under the identified state provisions. It was not a rule for every drug offense. It assumed danger and potential destruction were always present within that category and did not decide the quantum of individualized justification outside it. Justice Abrahamson concurred in the judgment on the trial circumstances and useless-gesture rationale while rejecting the categorical rule. The existence of a narrower concurrence does not turn its grounds into the majority's holding.

## Law entering the case

The [entering-law slice](../entering-law/OT_1996CHUNK7_76_ENTERING_LAW.md) is a copied reading aid; canonical Holdings and effective public Court actions remain authority.

1. **Wilson v. Arkansas, May 22, 1995:** The unanimous, Thomas-authored rule treats common-law announcement of identity and authority as part of Fourth Amendment reasonableness. Supported countervailing circumstances—danger, futility, escape, or threatened evidence destruction—can justify unannounced entry. Actual circumstances matter; the rule is not inflexible. Exact suspicion thresholds, constitutional incorporation of every federal statutory detail, and automatic exclusion remain open. It remanded actual justification and remedy rather than finding a violation or ordering suppression. The present categorical question is to be decided from that rule, not described as though a later Richards holding already exists.
2. **Ornelas, May 28, 1996:** O'Connor's controlling rationale distinguishes historical facts, credibility, and factual subsidiary inferences from ultimate reasonable suspicion/probable cause. The former receive clear-error protection and grounded contextual inferences real weight; ultimate constitutional sufficiency in its decided stop/warrantless-search setting is independently reviewed. It neither manufactures facts nor announces a universal rule for all mixed questions. Any use here must explain the analogy rather than treat Ornelas as an automatic direct holding on knock-and-announce appellate review. Scalia's recorded disagreement with that review allocation must not be erased.
3. **Maryland v. Buie, 494 U.S. 325 (1990):** The pre-divergence rule permits a limited protective sweep beyond immediately adjoining attack spaces on specific, articulable facts supporting reasonable belief that a dangerous person is present; it rejects an automatic violent-offense premise for that broader sweep. Its categorical adjoining-space permission has a distinct immediate-attack justification. The rule offers an analogy for a practicable threshold and for differentiating intrusions; it does not itself decide destruction-of-evidence announcement exceptions. Stevens and Kennedy both joined the rule and wrote separately about its application and remand, with materially different assessments of the record.
4. **Robinette, November 18, 1996:** O'Connor's controlling public opinion rejects an indispensable release script, preserves objective detention and total-circumstances consent analysis, and keeps voluntariness separate from attenuation. No automatic suppression or admission follows simply from correction of a legal rule. Its traffic setting does not establish that announcement before forcible home entry is dispensable.
5. **Maryland v. Wilson, February 19, 1997:** Thomas's six-Justice rationale permits the limited passenger-exit direction during an independently lawful traffic stop under its safety/incremental-intrusion comparison. It reserves distinct continued restraint, frisk, search, and duration questions. Kennedy's dissent, joined by Stevens, requires a circumstance tied to the actual added passenger restraint; preserved grounds matter. Neither writing decides this home-entry issue.
6. **Chandler, April 15, 1997:** All nine Justices require a supported governmental justification for the actual candidate drug-testing program and reject its stated general interests as sufficient. Its special-needs context is different; it creates no universal anti-categorical rule for warrants. The April 21 public entries, Blessing and Joseph A., concern provision-specific section 1983 enforcement and decree termination; no change to this Fourth Amendment law is supplied by them.

Miller, Sabbath, Ker, Harris, Crews, Segura, Murray, and Nix appear in the authenticated submissions and lower analysis. Their party-described application to this entry is argument, not an already adopted disposition. Miller's discussed futility premise concerns an occupant's knowledge of authority and purpose; identity and purpose are not interchangeable. Federal statutory entry decisions cannot be treated as having already settled every constitutional remedy question expressly reserved in Wilson. If independent modeling would resolve the case on a new exclusion rationale, it must verify the precise controlling pre-divergence sources and joins for that ground rather than rely solely on the briefs' accounts.
