## Washington v. Glucksberg

**Historical event and topology:** No. 96-110, argued January 8, 1997, decided June 26, 1997; certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. All nine supported reversal and remand. Rehnquist wrote the Court opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. O'Connor concurred, with Ginsburg and Breyer joining in part. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer each concurred in the judgment. The unanimous judgment is not a unanimous endorsement of the majority's test or all its grounds.

**Court opinion:** The claimed right to suicide with another person's assistance is not a fundamental liberty protected by substantive due process. The opinion requires a careful description of the claimed interest and asks whether it is objectively deeply rooted in national history and tradition and implicit in ordered liberty. It distinguishes Cruzan's assumed right to refuse unwanted treatment, grounded in battery and informed-consent traditions, from an affirmative entitlement to assistance in suicide. Historical Casey's protection of particular intimate choices does not establish constitutional protection for every important personal choice. Its formulation and use of historical Casey are comparator material only; simulated Casey cannot be replaced with historical Casey.

The prohibition rationally serves preserving life, preventing suicide, protecting medical integrity, protecting vulnerable people from coercion, error, prejudice and indifference, and preventing expansion into voluntary or involuntary euthanasia. Washington may protect all lives without rating their quality. The Court does not precisely balance the relative strength of each interest or decide whether regulation would be preferable policy; rational relationship is sufficient. Its slippery-slope analysis combines practical containment concerns with skepticism about a principled limiting line (n. 23); Souter's distinct approach is narrower on that point.

**Scope and qualifications:** The Court rejects facial invalidity and the Ninth Circuit's class-wide holding for competent, terminally ill adults seeking prescribed medication to hasten death. Footnotes 6 and 24 identify that class-wide as-applied ruling as the judgment under review. Footnote 24 expressly declines absolutely to foreclose a more particularized claim, but requires one quite different from respondents' because the asserted assisted-suicide interest receives no heightened protection. Footnote 7 expresses no view on the Oregon Lee litigation and directs the historical equal-protection issue to same-day Vacco. Cruzan is described as assuming, rather than finally holding without qualification, the constitutional right to refuse lifesaving hydration and nutrition. Remedy: reverse the en banc Ninth Circuit and remand for proceedings consistent with the opinion.

**Souter's separate ground:** Souter accepts the undisputed summary-judgment premises of competent, terminally ill patients voluntarily seeking a prescription, and provisionally accepts the doctors' asserted professional justification. His claim is narrower than a general entitlement to suicide assistance: responsible patients near death requesting medical counsel and help. Using Harlan's Poe dissent, he asks whether the restriction is arbitrary or purposeless in light of the relative strength and fit of the competing interests. History includes traditions retained and traditions abandoned; neither historical prohibition alone nor an abstract autonomy premise resolves the case. Bodily integrity, medical relationships, treatment refusal, and the abortion cases make the individual interest sufficiently important for careful scrutiny, but he does not decide that it ultimately constitutes an enforceable fundamental right.

His decisive state interest is protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and voluntary or involuntary euthanasia. Mere possibility of family pressure would not alone settle the matter, because physician screening is part of the proposed safeguard. The stronger concern is difficulty in assessing a knowing, responsible decision, combined with uncertainty that physicians would consistently preserve the boundaries. He finds a genuine, substantial empirical dispute over the Dutch experience and the effectiveness of safeguards. At this time courts cannot resolve it with sufficient assurance, while legislatures can investigate and experiment; he therefore rejects this challenge without deciding for all time against recognition. He expressly leaves legislative foot-dragging for another case. His concern is practical inability to contain the claimed right, not inevitable logical expansion of every right into another.

Souter's footnotes preserve material limits: Compassion in Dying is not a party in the Supreme Court (n. 1); he understands the claim as narrowly as-applied (n. 2); incidental difficulty exercising a liberty does not automatically demand compelling justification (n. 8); sufficiently important interests trigger more than rational-basis review, so not every claim requires complex balancing (n. 9); strength and tailoring depend on the individual interest and burden (n. 12). His distinctions concerning palliation and treatment withdrawal rest on their purpose, bodily intrusion, and likelihood of reflecting a responsible patient's choice, rather than denying their sometimes life-shortening consequences (nn. 16–17). These remain his reasons, not five-Justice holdings.

## Foreman v. Dallas County

**Historical event and topology:** Nos. 96-987 and 96-1389, June 27, 1997, direct appeals from a three-judge Northern District of Texas court; unsigned per curiam, with no separate writing or numerical tally reported. Do not manufacture a disclosed individual lineup from silence.

**Rules and limits:** Section 5 reaches informal administrative changes as well as formal enactments. The County's discretion under state law and its use of partisan affiliation do not themselves exempt changed appointment practices. Hampton County supplies that principle. The relevant question is whether the County administered a voting practice different from the one in force November 1, 1972. The 1985 submission disclosed a change in appointment start date and duration, but neither the recodification nor its explanation disclosed specific partisan selection formulas; Young, Lopez, and Clark require sufficient notice of the actual change sought to be precleared. Therefore that submission did not establish preclearance of those methods.

The parties agreed the record lacked the November 1, 1972 appointment procedure. The Court consequently did **not** finally decide whether preclearance was required. It vacated the final dismissal in No. 96-1389, dismissed the interlocutory appeal in No. 96-987 under Shaffer, and remanded both cases. Do not convert the missing baseline into a substantive finding, default, or final coverage judgment.

## Pounders v. Watson

**Historical event and topology:** No. 96-1383, June 27, 1997; certiorari summarily granted and Ninth Circuit judgment reversed. Per curiam, 7–2: Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg supported the judgment; Stevens dissented, joined by Breyer. No express remand appears in the disposition.

**Court opinion:** The only issue before the Court was the Ninth Circuit's conclusion that Watson's conduct lacked the disruption necessary for summary contempt. The Ninth Circuit did not decide whether she willfully disobeyed. Nothing in the cited cases requires a pattern of repeated violations pervading the courtroom or a finding that misconduct would recur without immediate summary punishment. Wilson demonstrates that a single refusal may disrupt and frustrate an ongoing proceeding. Here the trial judge's findings of willful defiance and permanent jury prejudice, together, placed the conduct within the permissible range.

The opinion retains Oliver's restriction to misconduct in open court, observed by the judge with the essential elements under the court's eye, disturbing the court's business and requiring immediate vindication of authority. It acknowledges due-process limits and the least-power principle, but declines to comprehensively define those limits because it regards this conduct as within them. It does not announce that every violation of an evidentiary order automatically warrants summary contempt. Jury prejudice and awareness are findings by the trial judge relied upon by the Court, not the content of the contempt statute itself.

**Stevens/Breyer dissent:** Stevens assumes Watson defied an order and committed contempt. He disputes whether the propriety of summary adjudication by the same judge is so clear that reversal without full briefing and argument is appropriate. Her two questions occurred during a three-and-a-half-month trial, which continued without incident for two weeks; summary punishment was being used for completed conduct rather than preventing further obstruction. He invokes Frankfurter's Sacher dissent and McConnell to emphasize necessity and procedural safeguards, including the substantial question whether a disinterested judge should hear the charge. The final footnote recounts McConnell's more disruptive conduct, its cessation after a recess, and the later sanction. The dissent requests plenary consideration; it does not conclusively adjudicate Watson's entitlement to habeas relief on the merits.

Primary sources: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1478.ZS.html), [Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1478.ZO.html), [O'Connor](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1478.ZC.html), [Thomas](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1478.ZC1.html), [Stevens](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1478.ZD.html), [Souter](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1478.ZD1.html), [Breyer](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1478.ZD2.html), and [official Volume 521](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/521bv.pdf).

**Historical event and exact topology:** Nos. 95-1478 and 95-1503; argued December 3, 1996; decided June 27, 1997; certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. Judgment reversed, 5–4. Scalia wrote the Court opinion, joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas. O'Connor and Thomas each wrote a concurrence, with no other joins. Stevens dissented, joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Souter separately dissented alone, expressly qualifying his join to Stevens. Breyer separately dissented, joined by Stevens. The dissenting Justices' disagreement with reversal does not erase Souter's specific proposed remand.

**Controlling rule and reasons:** Congress may not compel States or their executive officers, including political-subdivision officers, to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program. Directing officials in their official capacities does not evade New York's prohibition on directing the State. The Court rejects an exception for limited, temporary, ministerial or nonpolicymaking assistance, and declines to balance this direct command's burden against the federal program's benefits. Constitutional structure and New York are decisive; early practice tends to support the rule but is expressly not conclusive by itself.

The majority regards forced use of state officers as inconsistent with dual sovereignty, political accountability, and the Constitution's allocation of federal execution to the President. A means that violates that structural protection is not proper under the Necessary and Proper Clause; the Supremacy Clause makes supreme only constitutionally authorized federal laws. Testa concerns state adjudication; FERC also involved adjudicative duties and conditions on continued regulation of an otherwise preemptible field. Footnote 14 confirms that Congress may require officers conducting adjudications traditionally performed by judges to take account of federal law; the rule does not depend exclusively on the officer's formal title. Footnote 15 rejects importing municipal exclusion from Eleventh Amendment immunity into this federalism protection.

**Exact disposition of distinct provisions:** The required reasonable-effort background check in §922(s)(2) is unconstitutional, and the implicit CLEO duty to accept the dealer's Brady notice/form falls with it. The Court does not hold the destruction requirement, §922(s)(6)(B)(i), or requested written-reasons requirement, §922(s)(6)(C), unconstitutional. They are inoperative for these nonparticipating petitioners, who need not accept forms, investigate, or make ineligibility determinations. These related duties could apply only if a CLEO voluntarily participated; their validity in that setting is not adjudicated here. Footnote 18 finds Montana's state-law prohibition clear, treats the Arizona prohibition as arguable, and leaves its resolution unnecessary because neither State requires participation and both petitioners decline it.

**Material comparator correction:** The Court expressly declines to decide whether dealers must continue forwarding Brady materials and waiting five business days after the CLEO requirements fall. No dealer or purchaser is a plaintiff, so it will not adjudicate their rights and duties. The scoped comparator's statement that the Court treated the invalid duties as severable from the remaining federal scheme is therefore too broad as a statement of this Court's holding. The district courts had reached severability; the Supreme Court expressly reserves that question as framed by the parties. Its express mandate is reversal, without an express remand clause.

The Court also leaves other currently operative funding-linked or information-only requirements for appropriate later challenges. It distinguishes direct commands to administer a program from incidental application of a generally applicable federal law. Footnote 17 explains that the challenged duty demands investigation using State-owned information available to officers only in their official capacities; simply recasting it as a private-data reporting duty would not describe this law. The Act permits, but does not require, notification to a dealer of an unlawful proposed sale; a purchaser's request triggers the separate written-reasons duty if the CLEO has made an ineligibility determination.

**O'Connor:** She accepts the Court's holding on compelled checks and receipt of forms and emphasizes that States and CLEOs may voluntarily participate, and Congress may arrange contractual cooperation supported by federal funds. She expressly reserves purely ministerial reporting obligations, giving the missing-children reporting law as an example. Her concurrence does not reserve or withhold her join to any portion of the Court opinion.

**Thomas:** He expressly joins the Court opinion in full. Separately, he questions Commerce Clause authority over wholly intrastate point-of-sale transfers and urges his Lopez understanding of enumerated powers. He also identifies a possible personal-right Second Amendment objection even under broader Commerce Clause doctrine, but states that the parties did not raise it and the Court need not consider it. Footnote 1 discusses Miller's treatment of the sawed-off shotgun without claiming that Miller fully defined the substantive right. His Commerce Clause and Second Amendment discussion supplies no additional controlling holding.

**Stevens, joined by Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, subject to Souter's qualifications:** The Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses authorize this temporary use of local executive officers; the Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated powers rather than independently disabling an exercise of delegated power. The oath and Supremacy provisions, Federalist passages, early use of state officials, and Testa/FERC/Branstad support required executive cooperation. He distinguishes compelling state legislation and substantial legislative-scale policymaking from these modest interim duties, and does not decide whether permanent executive assignments would be valid. Garcia's political safeguards, the unfunded-mandates legislation, and Congress's accountability support legislative latitude. He considers the majority's executive-power concern inconsistent with accepted cooperative arrangements and doubts that greater federal bureaucracy better protects States or liberty. His dissent does not purport to approve every imaginable federal demand on state institutions.

**Souter:** The early statutes and emergency examples alone would not persuade him. The straightforward reading of Federalist No. 27, supported by No. 44 and consistent with Nos. 36 and 45, finally determines his position. He retains New York's rule against compulsory state legislation, while rejecting its broader administrative dicta: legislation embodies discretion not subject to command, whereas an executive may be directed to execute law. He also reserves demands inconsistent with an official's proper branch function. Crucially, he does not understand these sources to authorize required assistance without fair compensation. If his view prevailed, he would remand to develop the petitioners' budget and liability-for-unauthorized-expenditure objections. Do not render his view as an unconditional affirmance of every aspect of the Act.

**Breyer, joined by Stevens:** Foreign federal systems offer empirical evidence that decentralized execution can protect local autonomy and liberty better than a larger central bureaucracy. He acknowledges relevant differences and does not treat foreign constitutions as governing American law. The Act's reasonable-effort language can account for cost and time and avoid overwhelming a state civil service. Text, precedent, and the prevalence of alternative cooperative mechanisms do not justify the majority's absolute rule. His separate writing supplements his join to Stevens, and no other Justice joins Breyer's writing.
