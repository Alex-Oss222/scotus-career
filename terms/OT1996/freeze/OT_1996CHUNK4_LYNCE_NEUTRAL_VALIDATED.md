### Issue 1: Retroactive cancellation of release credits

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** California Department of Corrections v. Morales (1995) invalidated the actual three-year withdrawal of offense-date mandatory annual parole consideration during a period of legal eligibility where no ordinary substitute for meaningful intervening review was established. Its five-Justice rule requires the prisoner to show the prior entitlement, retrospective withdrawal, and concrete connection to possible earlier release; the State must substantiate any substitute it invokes, while the prisoner retains the ultimate punishment-increase burden. It did not adopt the historical speculative-risk disposition.
- **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Retrospective withdrawal of mandatory parole reconsideration: Morales requires a demonstrated meaningful loss connected to legally possible earlier release and substantiation of any invoked substitute, without proof that the lost hearing certainly would have yielded release. Direct cancellation of already effective release credit presents an even more immediate claimed increase in confinement.
- **Other Governing Law:** Article I, § 10; surviving Weaver v. Graham and Collins v. Youngblood; Florida credit statutes.
- **Open Point:** The exact amount of credit protected depends on which statutory awards were materially continuous with law applicable to Lynce's offense.

## Neutral legal forks and source-supported positions

**Punishment effect and purpose.** Petitioner argues that formally awarded credits actually shortened custody, and their retrospective offense-based withdrawal produced rearrest; their overcrowding purpose does not negate their penal operation. Florida argues that these credits were contingent population-management devices distinct from basic and incentive gain-time, and that the later exclusion did not enlarge the punishment attached to the original offense. Weaver and Collins v. Youngblood remain predivergence sources. Simulated Cavanaugh and Morales, copied in the entering-law slice, reject an automatic 'procedure' label and require attention to actual meaningful punishment effects. That law does not itself establish that every post-offense benefit is an offense-date entitlement.

**Offense-date rule and later enactments.** The parties identify section 944.598 emergency gain-time, enacted in 1983 and available at the offense date, as distinct from section 944.276 administrative gain-time adopted in 1987 and section 944.277 provisional credits adopted in 1988. The State emphasizes different triggering capacities, exclusions, administration and available awards, and says the emergency mechanism was never implemented. Petitioner argues the later mechanisms continued the same offense-date overcrowding-release opportunity. This is a material legal and remedial fork, not a finding that the statutes were identical. The reported statutory descriptions identify the earlier emergency trigger as 98 percent; the later provisional threshold is described as 97.5 percent in petitioner's brief and the statutory appendix. Do not use the State brief's compressed historical table to erase provision/year differences or presume all 1,860 days necessarily would have accrued under the offense-date law.

**Mandatory consequence versus discretionary eligibility.** Distinguish eligibility to receive an overcrowding award, executive determination of a statutory trigger, an actually made award, and the release consequence of credits already applied. Petitioner's JA objections distinguish the separate control-release system in Hock because that system expressly disclaims an inmate right to control release, while he invokes section 944.277(5)'s release-on-provisional-date requirement. Florida contests legal equivalence and characterizes the October 1992 release as mistaken under the July 1992 amendment. Characterizing the release as an error presupposes the constitutional applicability of the disputed cancellation and therefore cannot itself resolve the Ex Post Facto question.

**Remedy and reach.** A legal ruling on the retrospective cancellation may leave the lower court to determine the protected portion of credits and correct current custody calculation. Recognition of a violation would not establish a new offense-date statute, protect every later expansion, undo unrelated discipline, reverse the separate 335-day cancellation, or decide an unpresented due-process theory. Conversely, the mere existence of a remaining calculation does not erase the live claim challenging reincarceration. The record supports alternatives ranging from rejection of the claim on a supported offense-date distinction, to correction of the lower courts' categorical administrative-purpose rule with a bounded remand, to relief on protected credits if the necessary statutory continuity and resulting custody are established. No disposition is selected here.
