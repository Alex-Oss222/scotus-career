### C1 and C2: constitutional grounds independent of Title VII

Smith preserves genuinely neutral rules concerning work and resources. Lukumi's operative targeting and tailoring rules require a religion-specific burden to satisfy the constitutional justification its discrimination demands. The orders here reached religious expression as such and religious possession having no established operational consequence. Preventing official worship, supervisory pressure, favoritism and diversion of County resources supplies legitimate and potentially compelling concerns in a proper case. Those concerns do not justify removing a personal desk Bible or the directive's undifferentiated prohibition of conduct characterized as witnessing, proselytizing or counseling. Targeting that broad class is not narrowly fitted to the supported resource problems.

The Court can resolve the challenged breadth without holding that a supervisor enjoys a general constitutional right to organize prayers during department meetings. The record describes those prayers as voluntary, spontaneous and irregular; it contains no finding of compelled participation. Supervisory power and the workplace setting nevertheless remain relevant to attribution and pressure. Voluntariness and infrequency do not make every official religious exercise constitutionally permissible. The present constitutional disposition preserves lawful controls on the two resource activities and on genuinely governmental worship; it invalidates the broader commands at their demonstrated scope.

Pickering, Connick and Givhan preserve the actual employment-speech inquiry. NTEU supports examination of the reach of a broad prospective restriction on protected citizen expression, but does not transform supervisory instructions into citizen speech. Waters concerns factual mistake and actual reliance on a disciplinable account; it is not a general approval of the religious orders. Pinette has no comprehensive majority establishment test. Chabad and Good News distinguish actual private access and sponsorship from governmental adoption; they do not establish a forum in this office. The constitutional judgment rests on the supported religion-specific overreach, with those limits, rather than declaring all workplace religion constitutionally protected. The equal-protection theory is not used to announce an additional scrutiny rule or to revive the statutory race claim; the free-exercise ground suffices to sustain this constitutional disposition.

**Constitutional two-path comparison.** The County's strongest lawful path is a tailored restriction on the supervisor's use of institutional authority: department meetings, paid staff and employer premises can create pressure and government attribution without an express threat. Its strongest objection to affirmance is that an instruction written in religious terms may describe the very conduct the employer must prevent. Brown's lawful path is that these particular orders extend well beyond that objective into religious possession and personal expression. The latter path controls C1/C2 because the scope mismatch is established without presuming a forum or finding all actual conduct private. A narrower operational rule remains available; the present blanket command is not saved by the fact that a narrower one could have been lawful. The lower court's automatic Title VII-to-First-Amendment syllogism is expressly rejected.

### S1: accommodation and the four activities

Under §2000e(j), Hardison, Ansonia and the controlling **Beadle** opinion, the employee must establish the sincere religious practice, the employment conflict, employer knowledge and the adverse treatment implicated by his claim. The employer must then demonstrate reasonable effective accommodation or inability to accommodate without more than a de minimis hardship under the law governing this conduct. Concrete noncash burdens and supported professional predictions count; realized injury, an experiment and an actual complaint are not indispensable. An effective accommodation need not be the preferred one, and there is no independent duty to negotiate or try every possible alternative after effective accommodation or adequate hardship has been shown. The County knew of the practices and attempted no accommodation, but that fact alone is not the liability rule.

1. Directing the secretary to type Bible-study notes diverted paid labor. That burden supports the lower court's refusal to protect the activity; the County may discipline that resource use.
2. The prework prayers in this office do not establish an entitlement to personal access. The reported County rule treats arrival as the start of work and prohibits personal use of its resources; neither an actual public/limited forum nor comparable permitted personal use was shown. The disposition sustains this record-specific rejection of protection, without a categorical rule against all off-clock prayer.
3. The department-meeting prayers were described by all testimony as voluntary, spontaneous and irregular. The context matters, but supervisor status alone does not establish coercion or an operational burden. The indirect reports, absence of direct complaints or observed discomfort, denials of favoritism, and testimony locating disputes in departmental organization support the rejection of the asserted polarization/favoritism account on this record.
4. The affirmation of Christianity and Bible references about sloth and work ethics occurred at one meeting. The same need for a supported hardship account applies. Infrequency alone supplies no legal immunity; the County failed to connect these particular acts to a sufficient burden through the evidence and reasonable inferences available at trial.

**Accommodation two-path comparison.** A Beadle-consistent County path would identify a concrete supervisory, efficiency, pressure or attribution burden, including a sound professional prediction, and explain why the relevant accommodation cannot avoid it. Brown's path separates the activities instead of exporting the proved cost of secretarial work to every religious act. The second prevails for the latter two practices because the reported trial proof does not supply the missing operational connection. This does not adopt the lower court's suggestion that spontaneity makes conduct legally inconsequential or that only realized hardship counts. The actual proof fails even under the proper predictive standard. Nothing establishes a neutral prohibition of all comparable spontaneous meeting comments whose application must be sustained here.

### S2: the pre-1991 liability burden and completed trial

Price Waterhouse supplies the original mixed-motive framework. For this disposition, Brown must first prove by a preponderance that protected religion was a substantial factor actually relied on in the discharge decision, with direct decisional evidence sufficient to satisfy O'Connor's narrower concurrence as well as the plurality. The decisionmaker's express acknowledgment of the religious reprimand's role, considered with the connected testimony and the protected activities that the reprimand encompassed, supplies that showing. This is a conclusion drawn from the authenticated trial account, not an invented quotation identifying the exact weight of each activity. Mere knowledge that Brown was Christian, a stray remark or proof of unprotected resource use alone would not suffice. The holding does not settle every disagreement between the Price Waterhouse writings about other evidence.

Once that predicate is met, the County must prove **by a preponderance**, not clear and convincing evidence, that it would have made the same discharge decision without the protected religious consideration. In this original regime, success defeats liability; it does not merely reduce relief. The employer need not prove a sole legitimate cause, supply a special category of objective evidence or obtain a decisionmaker's formulaic hypothetical answer. Testimony, contemporaneous evaluations and reasonable factual inferences remain available. Conversely, reasons sufficient to justify a discharge do not alone establish that this employer would have discharged Brown on those reasons alone. The 1991 Act's later liability/remedy structure is not applied to this preenactment conduct.

The district court's conclusion that inadequate performance rather than religious animus caused termination cannot end the inquiry by treating legitimate and illegitimate motivations as mutually exclusive. The administrator admitted a role for the reprimand. The lower majority then considered the whole completed record and rejected the same-decision defense. The performance evaluations, delays, outside evaluation/restructuring, budget reprimand and computer investigation are genuine contrary evidence, not erased from the account. They support the existence of legitimate concerns. Taken with the administrator's inability to say whether discharge would have occurred without the religious reprimand and the absence of adequate contrary proof found on the appellate record review, they do not establish the required counterfactual by a preponderance. The selected disposition sustains that conclusion. It does not treat the administrator's uncertainty as a conclusive admission under a universal rule.

**Liability/remand two-path comparison.** The strongest alternative is a bounded remand for the trial judge to make the same-decision finding under the correct allocation, based on the existing record. Rule 52 requires respect for permissible factual choices; appellate disagreement alone does not authorize replacement factfinding. The lower dissent's broader reading of the performance record gives that alternative genuine force. Here the selected path accepts the majority's determination that the record permits no finding carrying the employer's burden after the direct mixed-motive predicate is recognized. The district's no-religious-cause premise does not itself supply the missing same-decision finding. The appellate conclusion is being sustained on its legal and evidentiary support, not declared unreviewable.

A further opportunity to add same-decision proof is a still different remedy. The August 21 rehearing order rejects the County's claim that it was denied that opportunity: the five-day trial allowed the pertinent evidence. No authenticated procedural ruling shows exclusion of the evidence or a newly imposed burden after trial. Price Waterhouse preceded the conduct and litigation. Unlike Warner-Jenkinson's newly express explanatory burden for older prosecution files, this is not a newly announced burden requiring contextual consideration of a previously unnecessary explanation. The prior provisional reference to unresolved causal defenses is therefore withdrawn. The statutory remand is **for appropriate relief only**; it does not reopen the failed same-decision defense.

### R1: distinct remedies and time of law

The model neither invents an express waiver nor adjudicates that unargued theory. Mt. Healthy and Waters do not become a constitutional termination holding in this case. The statutory religious-discharge claims sought backpay and reinstatement under Title VII and the identified Iowa provision. The lower court treated the state requirements as equivalent; preserving that coordinated judgment does not create an independent Supreme Court construction of Iowa law. Backpay, its lawful amount and mitigation, and whether reinstatement is the appropriate equitable relief require the ordinary remedial determination. They do not invite relitigation of the closed federal same-decision liability issue. The statutory race judgment remains outside the religious grant.

Landgraf/Rivers bar retrospective use of §102's new Title VII compensatory/punitive damages and its dependent jury election. No conclusion about a particular defendant's immunity is supplied without that preserved question and its conduct-time authority; no new immunity defense is affirmatively authorized. A remedy-stage reservation is not a fresh opportunity to undo adjudicated constitutional invalidity or statutory discharge liability.
