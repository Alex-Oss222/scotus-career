It then concluded that *Tully* and *Morgan Guaranty* demonstrate an adequate route for this Fund and would dismiss the prospective federal claims. The original District Court opinion, appendix 19a, already identifies declaratory judgment as the Fund's affirmative New York route, expressly cites *Morgan Guaranty* as evidence that New York courts regularly adjudicate these matters, and then explains that the competing federal exclusivity authorities leave plainness uncertain. The historical record thus already contained the material state-adjudication evidence offered as a changed premise. Its recovery and fuller reading are valuable source verification, but do not constitute a new filing, new state remedy or changed law.

*Tully* establishes New York's declaratory route for constitutional objections to sales taxation despite tax-review exclusivity language. It preserves ordinary preliminary relief in a proper case and distinguishes its holding from Article 78 review. Its special ninety-day procedure and the Attorney General's tolling representation do not govern this HFA action. Most importantly, *Tully* does not decide whether ERISA's federal exclusive-jurisdiction provision makes a state fiduciary/preemption remedy sufficiently plain. *Morgan Guaranty* adjudicates ERISA preemption in an administrative real-estate-tax refund proceeding, not this affirmative HFA fiduciary injunction action. Its existence strongly challenges an absolute claim that state courts can never decide ERISA, but does not itself decide the disputed route here.

## Common threshold propositions and differing reach

All eight recognize that the HFA is a state tax for this inquiry: it raises general public revenue through gross receipts rather than imposing a case-specific regulatory sanction. No holding that every revenue collection device is necessarily a tax is required. Section 1341 limits federal injunction and declaration where a plain, speedy and efficient state remedy may be had. A private ERISA fund is not thereby the United States or its regulatory agency. Neither an automatic instrumentality exception nor State silence establishes jurisdiction. Section 1144(d) preserves other federal law; §1132's federal cause of action and forum provisions must be assessed together with §1341, not used as a conclusory repeal formula.

**Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reach the ERISA merits.** At the present record and procedural scope they accept that the identified affirmative New York remedy is not sufficiently plain to trigger §1341's bar. The uncertainty concerns the interaction of an exclusively federal fiduciary action with an affirmative state tax declaration, as the original District Court and lower *Travelers* jurisdiction discussion show. They do not adopt a permanent ERISA exemption from the Tax Injunction Act, hold that state courts cannot decide preemption in any proceeding, or treat continued litigation as a waiver of statutory jurisdiction. *Tully* and *Morgan* establish important neighboring routes but do not require a finding of sufficient plainness for this action. Their resolution permits the present federal prospective merits adjudication without declaring every New York tax remedy inadequate.

**Scalia and Thomas do not reach the merits yet.** They would set the jurisdictional question for supplemental briefing and argument and resolve it first. They preserve the model's distinction between the federal cause of action and state adjudication of a federal issue as a substantial candidate answer, but not an established answer warranting dismissal. The lower circuit's treatment of §1132(e)(1) is a federal question; local-law familiarity and the State's participation do not resolve it. *Tully* does not decide that statutory interaction, and *Morgan* shows a different procedural route. These two neither find jurisdiction nor require dismissal now. Their procedural position is an actual judicial request for adversarial consideration of a jurisdictional question, not a research-production order or a finding that any party defaulted.

This threshold disagreement is not a vote on whether the tax is preempted. Nor is a court's determination that the statutory plain-remedy condition is unsatisfied the same as assuming jurisdiction in order to decide merits. The six make the former bounded determination; the two insist that the present adversarial treatment is insufficient to resolve it. Assembly must preserve that difference.

## Operative merits commitment of the six; conditional commitment of the two

Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reject the ERISA preemption challenge. Scalia and Thomas preserve the same substantive view only conditionally, if jurisdiction is lawfully established; their present procedural position supplies no join in an operative merits holding.

Section 514(a) does not preempt the reviewed HFA application merely because a self-insured ERISA fund owns and operates the taxed diagnostic and treatment centers and therefore bears the provider charge directly. Licensed provider activity and assessable receipts trigger the tax independently of ERISA status. The measure does not use a covered plan's benefit content as its mandatory legal benchmark, prescribe benefits or eligibility, dictate fiduciary standards, create a competing benefit-enforcement route, or require a particular system for adjudicating benefits. Ordinary costs of providing medical services do not become regulation of a plan as a plan solely because the provider is plan-owned.

*Travelers* supplies the distinction between ordinary economic influence and direct or effective plan commands. Its reserved direct-self-funded application is addressed here rather than misdescribed as already decided. *Dillingham* establishes that encountering an independent state criterion does not make ERISA status the rule's legal reference. *Board of Trade* preserves preemption when covered-plan benefits supply a mandatory legal measure, which the provider receipts tax lacks. *Inter-Modal* preserves the independent force of an enacted ERISA protection; it supplies no general exemption from provider taxation. *Mackey* and *Fort Halifax* preserve the distinction between ordinary external obligations and interference with the protected plan operation, at their own factual and statutory scopes.

The Fund's strongest argument is that the assessment directly depletes assets used for delivering promised care and prompts different routing and accounting in New York and New Jersey. Those are real economic effects. But purchasing services from a separately operated provider would also expose the Fund to ordinary provider costs reflected in prices; direct ownership does not itself establish a different protected plan command. The accepted grant exclusion and the Fund's restructuring are actual applications and choices, not proof that every receipt can be recharacterized or that New York orders a change in benefit administration. Reporting provider receipts is not itself regulation of who receives plan benefits or how those benefits are determined.

General applicability and the 0.6 percent rate are not independent safe harbors. A specific plan reference, mandatory covered-plan benchmark, competing enforcement mechanism or economic effect sufficiently acute to dictate substantive coverage or effectively restrict choices could alter the inquiry. No such operation is established here. The Court need not require insolvency, fix a numerical threshold, or deny every effect on the Fund to conclude that the present effect is outside §514(a)'s prohibition. Section 1144(b)(5)(B)(i)'s tax language preserves ordinary preemption analysis notwithstanding the Hawaii exception; it does not impose automatic preemption on all taxes affecting plans. Insurance saving and deeming remain separate and supply no general self-insured exemption from this provider tax.

The reviewed rate is 0.6 percent of assessable diagnostic-center receipts, not a tax on every dollar. The exact public-health provisions and administrative materials retain their separate status. Section 2801's hospital definition excludes the specified principally mental-disability facilities, subject to the separate hospital-part exception, and preserves the described prayer-based religious-institution exclusion. Section 2807-d(1)(a) retains personal-needs and refund deductions and its paragraph-(b) exclusion; the supplied appendix does not reproduce that paragraph or every intervening subdivision. No complete present-liability formula is adjudicated.

The administrative instructions separately exclude the identified restricted-investment income; specified charity/bad-debt distributions and county public-health grants; grants, contributions, donations, bequests and government deficit financing; sales and excise taxes; identified HMO subscriber receipts; and the specified distinct-rate Medicaid family-planning receipts. Their qualifications are not converted into new enacted exceptions. Fund-to-center transfers excluded without dispute here remain outside the tax application decided; miscellaneous receipts the Fund did not challenge and materially different future applications remain outside the holding.

The six would reverse the Second Circuit's ERISA-preemption judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with nonpreemption at this scope. They do not simply reinstate every aspect of the District Court judgment, decide a current tax balance, award or deny a fixed refund, create new penalties, order collection, or impose a reporting or state-filing deadline. The separate Eleventh Amendment defense and any other properly remaining threshold issue concerning repayment were not adjudicated below and remain distinct. Federal jurisdiction over prospective relief does not itself authorize recovery of paid taxes from the State. The adopted nonpreemption rule removes the asserted ERISA ground for such relief; any remaining repayment disposition must respect its own threshold and remedial requirements before an affirmative federal order. No statutory-immunity waiver is inferred from State participation.
