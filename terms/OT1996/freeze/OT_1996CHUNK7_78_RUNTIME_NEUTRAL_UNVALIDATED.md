* **Question Presented:** Whether Minnesota’s prohibition on a candidate appearing on the general-election ballot as the nominee of more than one political party unconstitutionally burdens the associational rights of a recognized minor party and its members.
* **Question Granted:** Whether Minnesota’s anti-fusion statutes violate the First and Fourteenth Amendments when they prevent a recognized political party from listing a consenting candidate whom another party has also nominated.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Eighth Circuit reversed summary judgment for Minnesota election officials and held the anti-fusion prohibition unconstitutional as applied to the Twin Cities Area New Party.

* **Material Record:** The New Party was a recognized minor party entitled to nominate candidates. In April 1994 its members selected state representative Andy Dawkins, who was also pursuing and later held the Democratic-Farmer-Labor nomination. Dawkins consented and signed the New Party affidavit. Minnesota officials refused the nominating petition because state law barred an individual from appearing as the candidate of more than one party. The Party remained free to endorse Dawkins, advocate for him, or nominate another candidate, but the law prevented it from communicating its chosen nomination through the ballot and prevented voters from registering support under the New Party label. ## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Magnitude of burden

* **Reasoning Below:** The Eighth Circuit treated the law as a severe burden on the Party’s choice of nominee and associational message and applied strict scrutiny.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Minnesota argues that the burden is modest because the Party may endorse, support, and vote for Dawkins, may nominate another candidate, and is not excluded from the ballot as an organization.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** The Party argues that choosing and identifying a nominee is a central associational act, that the ballot is the legally designated place where nominations are communicated, and that forcing a choice between its candidate and its identity materially disables coalition-building.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may find a substantial burden and require correspondingly weighty fit without declaring the restriction equivalent to total ballot exclusion.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** States may require candidate consent, prevent misleading labels, count a ballot only once, and demand a meaningful showing of party support.

### Issue 2: Justification

* **Reasoning Below:** The Eighth Circuit found Minnesota’s interests insufficiently supported and the absolute ban not narrowly tailored.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Minnesota invokes ballot integrity, avoidance of voter confusion, party stability, prevention of splintering, and preservation of a stable political system.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** The Party argues that fusion forms coalitions rather than multiplying candidates, the candidate consented, recognized-party status already supplied a support threshold, and political stability cannot mean protecting major parties from competition.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** A State need not adopt fusion and may regulate its administration if a materially supported burden and fit are shown; disagreement with fusion’s policy merits does not decide the constitutional question.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

A lawful path treats the ban as a substantial burden on a recognized party’s selection and ballot identification of a consenting nominee. It then asks whether Minnesota’s precise interests justify an absolute ban rather than less restrictive ballot-management rules. A broader categorical holding for or against fusion is unnecessary.
