#### West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy, 512 U.S. 186

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-141.

**Decided:** 1994-06-17.

##### The assessment and dedicated local distribution must be examined together

**Holding:** A State discriminates against interstate commerce when it assesses sales in a commodity market without regard to origin but dedicates the proceeds to competing in-state producers so that the linked arrangement offsets the local side's burden while leaving out-of-state production exposed. Courts must examine the supported competitive connection between assessment and distribution; different legal payors and recipients do not prevent discrimination, but merely sharing a general treasury does not establish it.

**Proposition-level authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg: six Justices adopt this rule and its application. Justices Scalia and Thomas concur in the judgment on the narrower dedicated-industry-tax ground.

**Material application:** The assessment covers Class I milk sold to Massachusetts retailers regardless of origin, at one-third of the difference between $15 per hundredweight and the federal blend price, multiplied by covered sales. Dedicated payments go only to Massachusetts producers in proportion to the preceding month's production. Credited production is capped at 200,000 pounds per producer per month; the federal minimum plus fund payment cannot exceed $15 per hundredweight, and surplus returns to dealers. These limits do not remove the exclusive competitive benefit. The State neither buys nor sells milk.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Baldwin v. G. A. F. Seelig, Inc., 294 U.S. 511 (1935): applies its prohibition on protecting local milk production from interstate competition to the operation of the linked fund.
- Bacchus Imports, Ltd. v. Dias, 468 U.S. 263 (1984): applies its rule against local-product tax preferences despite the State's ordinary taxing authority.
- New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach, 486 U.S. 269 (1988): applies its discriminatory-tax rule while preserving the distinction for ordinary direct assistance; no general subsidy immunity is adopted.
- Associated Industries of Missouri v. Lohman, May 23, 1994: follows its requirement to examine the relevant competitive comparison rather than treating a broader fiscal description as a cure.

##### The local preference lacks a sufficient justification

**Holding:** Once the linked assessment and distribution discriminate, the State must establish a legitimate local purpose that adequate reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot serve; protecting local producers against interstate competition is not itself a sufficient purpose. Massachusetts has not made that showing for this order, so the judgment sustaining it is reversed and the case remanded for relief consistent with its invalidity, without deciding refunds, severability, or other retrospective monetary consequences.

**Proposition-level authority:** Justice Stevens's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg: six Justices adopt the justification analysis and its application. Eight Justices support reversal and remand; Justice Blackmun dissents.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- New Energy Co. of Indiana v. Limbach, 486 U.S. 269 (1988): applies the demanding justification inquiry and its distinction between compulsory taxation and proprietary participation.
- Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality, April 4, 1994: follows origin-discrimination scrutiny; no new compensatory-tax entitlement is created.
- Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc., 397 U.S. 137 (1970): distinguishes its incidental-burden balancing because this arrangement discriminates.
- Milk Control Board v. Eisenberg Farm Products, 306 U.S. 346 (1939): distinguishes the local minimum-price arrangement; that decision does not sustain this dedicated assessment-and-distribution fund.
- C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown, May 16, 1994: follows the distinction between compulsory commercial regulation and state participation in a market; the dissenting municipal-service characterization there does not describe this fund.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The judgment sustaining the amended order is reversed and the case remanded for relief consistent with its invalidity. Refunds, severability, retrospective monetary consequences and licensing consequences remain for their own governing law and record. No farmer repayment, dollar award or reconstructed pricing program is directed.

#### Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality and Columbia Resource Co. v. Environmental Quality Commission, 511 U.S. 93

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 93-70 and 93-108.

**Decided:** 1994-04-04.

##### An express waste-origin surcharge triggers the discrimination rule

**Holding:** A State discriminates against interstate commerce when it imposes a higher governmental disposal charge on otherwise comparable waste because it was generated outside the State; a small amount or environmental objective does not remove that express geographic preference. Such a differential can survive only if the State establishes a legitimate local purpose that reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot adequately serve; ordinary Pike balancing of evenhanded incidental burdens does not govern this classification.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; eight Justices directly adopt this proposition and its application.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Philadelphia v. New Jersey, 437 U.S. 617 (1978): applied to geographic discrimination in waste commerce.
- Chemical Waste Management, Inc. v. Hunt, 504 U.S. 334 (1992): applied to an origin-based disposal surcharge and its purpose-and-alternatives burden; its unpressed compensation theory remains distinct.
- Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill, Inc. v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources, 504 U.S. 353 (1992): applied to regulation of private landfills.
- Wyoming v. Oklahoma, 502 U.S. 437 (1992): its express-geographic-discrimination rule is preserved and applied.
- Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc., 397 U.S. 137 (1970): distinguished as governing evenhanded regulation with incidental burdens.

##### General income taxes do not establish a compensatory disposal tax

**Holding:** A compensatory-tax defense requires an identified intrastate burden, an interstate charge that roughly approximates and does not exceed that burden, and substantially equivalent taxable events capable of operating as mutually exclusive proxies. Oregon's reliance on residents' general income-tax contributions fails because income generation and disposal of waste are not substantially equivalent events; even a quantified revenue comparison would not cure that independently sufficient defect, and the statutory cost command is not proof of a matching burden.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; eight Justices directly adopt this proposition and its application.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Maryland v. Louisiana, 451 U.S. 725 (1981): applied to require identification of the actual domestic burden being compensated.
- Henneford v. Silas Mason Co., 300 U.S. 577 (1937): preserved for genuinely complementary taxes on substantially equivalent events; general taxation is not such a proxy.
- Armco Inc. v. Hardesty, 467 U.S. 638 (1984): applied to the independently fatal absence of substantially equivalent taxable events.

##### Conservation and cost-recovery labels do not justify this private-facility differential

**Holding:** Oregon's conservation purpose, comprehensive regulatory program and statutory direction to recover otherwise-unpaid costs do not establish that this origin differential satisfies the discrimination rule: the record supplies no demonstrated origin-specific added cost that defeats reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. Because the facilities are private, regulation does not make this a proprietary charge by a government owner, and a user-fee analogy cannot eliminate nondiscrimination as a separate requirement; the Court leaves government-owned landfill charges and materially different proven cost justifications undecided.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; eight Justices directly adopt this proposition and its application.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Chemical Waste Management v. Hunt: applied to preserve legitimate environmental aims while requiring an adequate nondiscriminatory-alternatives showing.
- Sporhase v. Nebraska ex rel. Douglas, 458 U.S. 941 (1982): distinguished; conservation concerns do not validate this origin preference at private landfills.
- Evansville-Vanderburgh Airport Authority District v. Delta Airlines, Inc., 405 U.S. 707 (1972): reasonable approximation does not displace its independent nondiscrimination condition.
- Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. County of Kent, January 24, 1994: its distinct nondiscrimination requirement is applied; unlike that record, this fee expressly classifies by interstate origin.
- Fort Gratiot Sanitary Landfill v. Michigan Department of Natural Resources: applied to distinguish private-facility regulation from state market participation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The statutory direction to recover costs is not a lower-court finding of numerical equivalence. The Court supplies no contrary numerical finding, no universal bar to cost recovery and no rule for government-owned landfills. Materially different proven origin-specific costs remain subject to the governing justification requirement.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Both matters are reversed and remanded for relief from the origin differential through the existing state review proceeding. No replacement rate, refund entitlement or amount, alteration of the separately unchallenged charge, required waste shipment or reinstatement of severed state-law provisions is ordered.

#### Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner, 516 U.S. 325

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1239.

**Decided:** February 21, 1996.

##### The issuer-based deduction discriminates against interstate investment

**Holding:** A State discriminates against interstate commerce when the tax on a resident's otherwise comparable corporate shares increases as the issuer's local income-tax exposure decreases. Neither the shareholder's local residence nor drafting the geographic advantage as a deduction removes that discrimination, and proof that a particular issuer already lost an investment is unnecessary.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Material application:** The tax is twenty-five cents per $100 of share value, reduced according to the issuer's North Carolina-apportioned income. For corporate shareholders the linked dividend-deduction proportion applies without the ordinary $15,000 dividend-deduction cap.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Oregon Waste's express-geographic-discrimination rule and West Lynn's examination of linked operation apply; a deduction label or shareholder residence does not remove discrimination.

##### Corporate income and share ownership are not shown to be equivalent taxable events

**Holding:** A compensatory-tax defense requires the State to identify the actual intrastate burden being offset, show a roughly equivalent and nonexcessive interstate charge, and establish substantially equivalent taxable events reasonably treated as mutually exclusive proxies. North Carolina's corporate-income and shareholder-property taxes do not satisfy those requirements merely because corporate profits may influence share value; the failure to establish equivalent events independently defeats the defense.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Different nominal taxpayers do not invariably prevent compensation, and income and property taxes may coexist. This State failed to establish equivalent events.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Oregon Waste supplies the cumulative compensatory conditions, Maryland v. Louisiana the actual-local-burden requirement and Armco the independently fatal equivalent-events defect. Henneford preserves genuinely complementary taxation.

**Independent alternative holding:** A State does not establish the compensatory defense's actual-burden and approximate-equivalence requirements merely by invoking typical price/earnings ratios or aggregate corporate tax contributions. North Carolina's comparison fails to demonstrate the required matching burden on this record, an additional ground for rejecting its defense.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Opinion of the Court (Thomas), unanimous.

**Limits, remedy, transition, or precedent treatment:** The 1990 claim is not governed by a new universal calculation using the later 7.75% rate; the state discussion also identifies the earlier 7% rate. Associated Industries requires the relevant actual matching burden, rather than aggregate or other-transaction offsets.

### Discriminatory routing, charges and linked subsidies in interstate commerce

**Governing question:** When a state or local economic measure must satisfy the discrimination rule rather than ordinary incidental-burden balancing.

**Current rule:** A measure discriminates when it favors local economic interests by burdening interstate competitors, including a waste charge expressly keyed to out-of-state origin, compulsory routing that reserves a processing service to a designated local private operator, or an integrated assessment-and-distribution scheme that collects from the whole market but returns the proceeds only to local producers. The entire linked operation matters; formally equal collection does not cleanse its discriminatory distribution. The government must show a legitimate local purpose that cannot adequately be served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. Ordinary incidental-burden balancing does not validate discrimination. Conservation, sanitation, cost recovery and financing a favored private facility do not themselves meet that burden; contracting with a private operator does not make compulsory market regulation governmental market participation. A municipality that actually purchases collection and disposal may specify the destination as part of that purchased performance, including through a paid hauler and privately owned facility. Separate suppliers or prices do not themselves turn purchased disposal into unrelated downstream business. This bounded purchasing authority does not reach independent transactions merely because government has a contract or financial stake.

### Compensatory taxes and the actual equivalent local burden

**Governing question:** When an otherwise discriminatory tax can compensate for a tax already borne by the corresponding intrastate activity.

**Current rule:** The State must identify the actual intrastate burden for which it compensates, show that the interstate charge roughly approximates and does not exceed that burden, and establish that the taxed events are substantially equivalent, reasonably treated as mutually exclusive proxies for one another. General income taxation and waste disposal are not such equivalent events. For a use tax said to equalize local sales taxes, the comparison must use the sales tax actually imposed in the affected locality. Statewide averages and burdens elsewhere cannot cure an excess there. Equal or lower use-tax burdens in other localities are not invalidated merely because an excess exists elsewhere; relief addresses the established inequality through lawful remedial procedures. A resident shareholder’s location and asserted substantial equality with a separate corporate-level tax supply no exception: an issuer-based tax preference favoring local corporate activity must satisfy the same actual-burden and equivalent-event requirements. Typical price/earnings ratios or aggregate corporate contributions do not themselves establish the matching burden.

**Authority by component:**

- Three compensatory-tax predicates: Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality — General income taxes do not establish a compensatory disposal tax, 1994-04-04. Thomas's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; eight Justices directly adopt this proposition and its application.
- Local comparison, bounded invalidity and relief: Associated Industries of Missouri v. Lohman — The compensating burden must exist in the affected locality; Relief must address the established inequality through lawful state procedures, 1994-05-23. Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; eight direct joins. Blackmun agrees with the judgment without joining this full framework.
- Fulton — holdings 1–4, investment discrimination and rejection of Darnell’s shortcut, 1996-02-21; Thomas for all nine.

# General Motors v. Tracy: authorized current-term neutral reading copy

Source: workspace/neutral-projection.md; public law and published positions only.


### 1997-02-18 - General Motors Corp. v. Tracy; No. 95-1232.

Authority: [Record](../records/General_Motors_v_Tracy_merits_1997-02-18.md).

#### Holdings

##### I. The assessed purchaser may bring this challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** A purchaser directly liable for a tax has standing to challenge an exemption that allegedly burdens its interstate purchases. The adverse assessment supplies the purchaser's own economic injury, caused by the challenged tax rule and redressable through lawful relief from that assessment, whether or not payment has been established.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** General Motors contests a charge imposed on its own purchases. Its injury does not become another seller's claim merely because the tax also affects competing suppliers. Bacchus recognizes a taxed purchaser's ability to challenge a discriminatory exemption; Fulton likewise examines the taxpayer's own liability although the distinction concerns another market participant. Relief from an unlawful assessment would remove the asserted burden. Ohio's seller-only objection therefore fails. Standing establishes access to review, not the truth of the claimed comparison between the sellers. The Court does not find an assessment amount or prior payment and orders no refund on the strength of standing alone.

**Precedent treatment:** Bacchus Imports, Ltd. v. Dias supplies purchaser standing; Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner confirms that the claimant may contest its own tax burden without being the disfavored seller. Their merits and remedial boundaries remain.

##### II. This record does not establish the asserted discrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio's exemption for sales by regulated local distribution companies does not violate the Commerce Clause on this record, because their compulsory rate, reliability and service obligations materially distinguish their regulated supply business from independent marketers' commodity sales. Commodity overlap alone does not establish equivalence of those offerings where the record does not establish a distinct industrial-sales category separated from the obligations relied upon; the holding confers no general immunity on utilities or all of their competitive transactions.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Stone-Zsela and Stevens do not join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court must examine the practical competitive relationship, rather than accept a utility label or the fact that local marketers also pay the tax. West Lynn requires examination of an integrated fiscal preference; Carbone requires attention to the actual service market. Here the favored sellers must maintain regulated supply and meet reliability and human-needs obligations rather than select only profitable customers. Those duties materially affect their offering. General Motors bought virtually all its gas from interstate marketers and often obtained local delivery, but the record gives little detail about the industrial market and does not establish the asserted unregulated negotiated LDC sales during the assessment period. The common gas commodity does not by itself remove the established service differences.

The objection that duties in a captive market cannot justify preference in a separate competitive market would require considering such a supported separation. This record does not establish it. The Court neither finds that every industrial sale funds a particular public duty nor presumes that any alternative would endanger service. The 1996 enactment cannot prove the character of 1986–1990 purchases. Oregon Waste and Fulton continue to require demanding justification and actual tax equivalence once discrimination is established; no compensatory defense is adopted here. A materially different proved sales category remains for its own case. No independent incidental-burden balancing claim or congressional-authorization theory is decided.

**Precedent treatment:** West Lynn's integrated-operation rule and Carbone's practical service-market inquiry apply without categorical utility immunity. Oregon Waste's discrimination-and-justification rule and Fulton's cumulative compensatory conditions remain unchanged; the Court does not reach justification after rejecting the asserted comparison.

##### III. The economic classification has a rational basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio may distinguish regulated local distribution companies from independent natural-gas marketers under ordinary economic equal-protection review because the companies' actual rate and service duties rationally relate to the tax classification. Competitive overlap does not eliminate that legitimate distinction, and this rational-basis holding does not reduce the independent Commerce Clause requirements.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Stone-Zsela and Stevens reserve this question on their proposed Commerce Clause dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** Beach permits an ordinary economic classification when a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis supports it, without requiring perfect fit or formal legislative findings. Ohio's rate, reliability and service obligations supply such a basis here. An industrial buyer's opportunity to purchase from either class does not make it irrational to distinguish the regulated service business from independent marketers. The Court does not equate rationality with proof that utility taxes exactly offset the challenged charge. Nor does the Court attribute this analysis to the Ohio court, which treated equal protection as submerged in its Commerce Clause discussion. The ordinary economic challenge fails on this separate ground; independent constitutional prohibitions retain their force.

**Precedent treatment:** FCC v. Beach Communications supplies the ordinary economic-classification rule and its limits; it does not displace Commerce Clause scrutiny.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Bacchus Imports, Ltd. v. Dias: applied to the assessed purchaser's own standing; local-product tax preferences remain subject to its substantive rule.
- West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy: its examination of integrated fiscal operation remains controlling; the present record differs in the established obligations of the compared service businesses.
- C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown: its practical service-market comparison is applied; neither a nominal utility classification nor equal taxation of local outsiders is sufficient by itself.
- Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality: the discrimination trigger and demanding justification survive unchanged; no new public-service exception is created.
- Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner: preserves all three cumulative compensatory-tax requirements and lawful fact-dependent relief; no exact compensation or payment is found here.
- FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.: supplies ordinary economic rational-basis review, separately from the Commerce Clause.

#### Law After Decision

The regulated service differences defeat the particular Commerce Clause comparison established here; no universal utility exemption or new evidentiary presumption follows. The economic classification independently survives equal protection. A taxpayer may challenge its own assessment even when the alleged competitive discrimination affects suppliers. The existing discrimination, compensation and conditional tax-remedy rules remain in force, and a materially different proved industrial-sales category is unresolved.

### 1997-02-18 - General Motors Corp. v. Tracy; No. 95-1232.

Authority: [Record](../records/General_Motors_v_Tracy_merits_1997-02-18.md).

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part; no other Justice joins.** He joins Part I on General Motors's own assessment injury. He would reverse the judgment insofar as it sustains the tax on the competitive commodity-gas purchases and remand for nondiscriminatory treatment. His comparison focuses on the transaction bought by a noncaptive industrial customer: LDCs and interstate marketers can compete to supply that commodity even though LDCs also serve captive customers. West Lynn's examination of the actual fiscal preference and Oregon Waste's rule against unjustified discrimination require a connection between the favored transaction and the service burden said to justify it. In his view, a blanket seller exemption lacks that connection on this record. This is a disagreement over the legal significance of the established competitive commodity purchase, not a finding that particular unregulated special contracts existed. He would preserve local-distribution regulation and fitted support for documented backup, low-income, credit or distribution obligations through nondiscriminatory means, including a neutral subsidy, cost-based credit or exemption confined to regulated bundled service. State courts would select lawful consequent tax relief, including a refund only where actual payment and governing remedial law support it, or a lawful neutral adjustment. He would not decide equal protection.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part; no other Justice joins.** He joins Part I but would vacate and remand the Commerce Clause merits for an actual industrial-market comparison. West Lynn requires examination of how the linked fiscal scheme operates; Carbone prevents equal treatment of local and interstate outsiders from curing a preference for a favored local service. Regulated obligations are material, but in his view do not justify closing the inquiry into sales competing for noncaptive buyers. He would not find unregulated contracts, calculate cross-subsidies or conclusively invalidate the exemption on this sparse record. If the comparison establishes discrimination, Ohio must satisfy the existing justification rule; any compensatory defense must meet Fulton's actual-burden, approximate-equivalence and equivalent-events requirements. He would reserve equal protection and order neither a particular subsidy nor a predetermined refund.
