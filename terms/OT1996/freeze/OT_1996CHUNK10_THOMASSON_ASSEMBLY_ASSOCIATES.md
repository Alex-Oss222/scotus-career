### Entering law, components and disposition paths

Walmer's January 13, 1997 Part I controls the ordinary rational-basis standard in the conduct-based preliminary setting it decided. It permits conceivable legitimate grounds, reasonable military predictions and imperfect categories; it requires a relation to the actual disadvantage and excludes bare harm or irrational prejudice. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer joined that part. Walmer expressly reserves the statutory statements rule and an orientation-only inference. Its preliminary disposition neither decides this final discharge nor creates an individualized-impairment requirement.

Romer's ordinary-review holding and the separate proof-burden and family-participation holdings in Heller remain operative. Neither Romer nor Virginia adopts heightened orientation scrutiny. Dawson and Mitchell permit speech to establish a constitutionally relevant fact; they do not transform an ordinary identity statement into an admission of a particular act. Weiss preserves substantial respect for military judgments with independent constitutional review. Waters supplies no automatic actual-disruption requirement, no mandatory investigation checklist and no least-severe-discipline rule.

Section 654(b)(2) is the route used here. It requires separation on the approved statement finding unless the further finding establishes that the member is not a person who engages in, attempts to engage in, has a propensity to engage in, or intends to engage in homosexual acts. The directive's reproduced definition makes propensity a likelihood of acts, beyond abstract preference or desire. The (b)(1) five-part actual-act retention exception is a different rule; (b)(3) marriage is not this finding. Section 654(e)'s exception to mandatory processing requires both a service-avoidance purpose and a determination that separation would not serve the military's best interest; neither premise is established here. Congress's subsection (a) findings are legislative judgments, not findings of Thomasson's conduct or impaired unit performance.

The two principal lawful paths are:

* **T-A — Affirm at the conduct-related, rebuttable-inference scope.** A voluntary statement of orientation may rationally indicate likely sexual conduct even though it proves no completed prohibited act. Congress may use that inference for preventive military personnel decisions, subject to genuine rebuttal. This Board received the submitted evidence; favorable general service evidence does not necessarily rebut the distinct conduct/propensity predicate, and the member declined further specific rebuttal. The First Amendment does not immunize a relevant statement from that evidentiary use. The strongest objection is that the policy permits private orientation but imposes a burden to disprove future noncompliance upon its disclosure, while an exemplary service history supports obedience. This path answers that likelihood need not be certainty, predictive personnel rules need not await misconduct, and the offered rebuttal remains materially different from evidence negating the predicate. It does not rest on military immunity or a universal civilian-employment rule.
* **T-R — Reverse this application under ordinary equal protection.** When the statement discloses identity and opposition to the policy without admitting an act or intent to disobey, the Government cannot sustain this discharge by treating that disclosure alone as an unrebutted likelihood of prohibited conduct. The service history supplies context, but the legal defect is the relation of this statement-based burden and discharge to the claimed conduct predicate, not a right earned by good performance. The strongest objection is that ordinary rationality tolerates classwide predictions and places the burden on the challenger, especially under Walmer. This path accepts those principles but finds the inference insufficient at its actual scope: a general attraction does not establish likely noncompliance merely because it is honestly disclosed. A statement admitting acts, plans, intent or otherwise contextually showing a likelihood of prohibited conduct is different. This application ruling neither invalidates all conduct rules nor creates heightened scrutiny.

An additional **T-S** path treats the same as-applied defect as an independent First Amendment violation: the Government may use relevant statements as evidence, but a protected disclosure cannot itself supply the missing disqualifying fact through an impermissible inference. Its strongest objection is the ordinary evidentiary relevance of orientation and the military's preventive role. A Justice selecting T-S must reject that connection here, rather than say that statements can never be evidence.

A purported construction-only remand is not selected. The reproduced directive already distinguishes likelihood from abstract preference, and the source does not show that the Board excluded evidence, demanded a denial of identity or applied an unreported different instruction. It would be improper to manufacture that legal-error premise to avoid choosing between T-A and T-R. Nor does this handoff adopt Luttig's separate invalidation of the directive: the scope and preservation needed for that additional disposition are not established by the grant.

## 112. Thomasson v. Perry

**Event and research cutoff:** June 9, 1997 merits decision; argument February 19, 1997. ### Historical comparison and governing scope

T1 confirms a nine-judge affirmance: Wilkinson, Russell, Widener, Murnaghan, Wilkins, Niemeyer, Hamilton, Luttig and Williams. Murnaghan wrote separately for himself. Luttig's separate opinion was joined by Russell, Widener, Wilkins, Hamilton and Williams. Hall dissented with Ervin, Michael and Motz. This completes the abbreviated sanitized comparator's description; it changes no neutral fact or modeled Justice commitment.

Wilkinson sustained the statement inference under ordinary rationality and substantial military deference, treated the statement as evidence rather than a speech offense, and rejected the due-process and APA objections discussed below. Murnaghan found the unrebutted rational presumption sufficient without the majority's extended deference account; his proposed nonmilitary breadth is not adopted by the reconciled affirmers. Luttig would invalidate the directive's narrowing definition and uphold a broader statutory exclusion based on known homosexuality. His preservation discussion, including note 14, identifies competing party accounts. It supplies neither an uncontested grant nor an entered invalidation. No reconciled Justice selects that separate route.

Hall contests the disclosure-to-misconduct inference, gives independent constitutional review decisive weight, and would reverse. His opinion supplies a substantial contrary argument but is not an undisputed finding that the offered evidence disproved every relevant propensity. His broader reliance on absence of empirical or individualized harm is not imported into the reconciled minority's rule. The controlling simulated Walmer rule expressly permits reasonable predictions without either showing.

All eight Associates apply ordinary rationality. Walmer permits conceivable legitimate grounds, reasonable military predictions and imperfect classifications, while requiring a connection to the actual disadvantage and excluding bare harm or irrational prejudice. It did not resolve §654(b)(2), an orientation-only inference or this final discharge. Romer's broad civil disability and Virginia's sex-classification burden therefore provide neither an automatic result nor a new orientation tier. Bowers does not independently settle this equality question.

The legal route is §654(b)(2): a specified statement requires separation under approved procedures unless the further finding establishes that the member is not a person who engages in, attempts to engage in, has a propensity to engage in, or intends to engage in homosexual acts. The reproduced directive defines propensity as a likelihood of acts beyond abstract preference or desire. Its listed rebuttal considerations and the actual two-day Board proceeding matter. Thomasson's service record, supporting accounts and expert evidence were received; the Board found the presumption unrebutted and he declined further specific rebuttal. There is no established exclusion of his evidence, forced recantation, denied hearing or new Board instruction.

The statute's other terms retain their separate operation. Section 654(b)(1)'s actual-act exception requires all five additional findings: departure from usual behavior, unlikely recurrence, no force/coercion/intimidation, consistency of continued service with discipline/good order/morale, and no propensity or intent. They are not (b)(2)'s rebuttal requirements. Section 654(b)(3) addresses same-sex marriage or attempted marriage; it is not this discharge ground. Section 654(e) removes a processing obligation only when both service-avoidance purpose and the military's contrary best interest are determined; it permits omission of processing rather than commanding omission. Neither predicate is established here. Congress's §654(a) findings remain legislative judgments rather than individualized findings about Thomasson.

### Alternatives tested

**T-A — Affirm the conduct-related, genuinely rebuttable inference and its evidentiary use.** The voluntary declaration rationally bears on likely conduct Congress may address preventively. Ordinary rationality does not require proof of a completed act or impaired unit. This Board received the offered rebuttal, and general excellence does not necessarily negate the separate likelihood predicate. Dawson and Mitchell permit relevant expression to prove a lawful predicate; policy criticism itself remains protected. The strongest counterargument is that the policy allows orientation while burdening only its disclosure and that exemplary obedience undermines the asserted likelihood. T-A accepts imperfect prediction, distinguishes private attraction from a conclusive status disqualification, and relies on the actual opportunity for rebuttal.

**T-R — Reverse this application on ordinary equal-protection grounds.** This statement discloses identity and policy disagreement without admitting conduct or intent to violate a conduct rule. In its actual context, those words do not supply the relationship needed to put the burden of disproving likely prohibited conduct on this officer and sustain his discharge. Service evidence is relevant context rather than a universal performance defense. The strongest counterargument is Walmer's permission for classwide predictions and the challenger's ordinary burden. T-R accepts those rules but finds this particular identity-to-noncompliance inference insufficient; it does not require a completed act in every preventive personnel decision. A contextual admission of intended acts or other evidence of likely conduct would present a different case.

**T-S — Independent as-applied speech ground.** Stevens additionally concludes that using the protected disclosure to supply the missing disqualifying fact penalizes expression without the constitutionally relevant connection Dawson and Mitchell require. The strongest objection is that voluntary words can furnish evidence and that the military need not wait for harm. His answer concerns the inadequacy of the particular inference, not a categorical evidentiary privilege. Souter and Ginsburg do not join this independent constitutional ground once equality supplies complete relief.

No Justice selects a facial invalidation of every §654 route, a newly transferred Virginia tier, an actual-purpose proof burden, a universal individualized-impairment condition, military immunity from review or an independent directive-invalidity judgment.
