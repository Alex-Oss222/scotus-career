## Supported record and lower proceedings

Atchison transferred cargo-handling work from its wholly owned subsidiary Santa Fe Terminal Services to independent In-Terminal Services. Association members were former Terminal Services employees who lost their jobs because of the transfer. The individual plaintiffs alleged that defendants conspired to transfer the work for the express purpose of avoiding Railroad Retirement Fund contributions and minimizing Teamster benefit payments. They had retirement rights under the Railroad Retirement Act and pension, health and welfare benefits under Teamster collective agreements. The discharge caused loss of the former and substantial reduction in the latter, according to the reported allegations. These are allegations accepted at Rule 12(b)(6), not findings of specific intent, sham restructuring or actual entitlement.

The supplied packet's more specific characterization of bidding, yard identity and which employees continued with the successor is unnecessary to the granted issue and is not established by the full lower opinion read here. It is omitted rather than certified as recovered fact. A benefits-reducing business change is not itself a proved purpose to interfere with protected attainment.

The district court dismissed the ERISA and FELA claims. The Ninth Circuit found no association standing to recover individualized damages because individual members' participation was necessary; it preserved the request that named individuals act as class representatives if proper. It reinstated the Teamster pension-interference claim: prohibited motivation alleged in the complaint could not be resolved against plaintiffs at pleading. It affirmed welfare-claim dismissal under circuit cases distinguishing interference with use of supplied benefits from alteration of an unvested welfare package. DeVoll and West treated the absence of a present right to anticipated welfare benefits as fatal; the court acknowledged contrary Seventh and Eleventh Circuit authority emphasizing prohibited purpose rather than vesting.

The Ninth Circuit separately affirmed exclusion of Railroad Retirement Act plans from ERISA; reversed the categorical dismissal of FELA class claims for proper Rule 23 consideration; and vacated attorneys' fees imposed for a premature appeal because required recklessness or bad-faith findings were missing. Its footnote 5 rejected the contention that §510 necessarily excludes a nonemployer who conspires with an employer, relying on the statute's reference to any person and circuit law. Its footnote 4 declined a limitations defense first raised at appellate oral argument. Those matters are source-supported lower rulings, not additional granted Supreme Court questions or issues to adjudicate silently.

## Operative text and entering law

Section 510, 29 U.S.C. §1140, prohibits specified action—discharge, fine, suspension, expulsion, discipline or discrimination—against a participant or beneficiary for exercising a right under an employee benefit plan or the identified statutes, **or for the purpose of interfering with attainment of any right to which the participant may become entitled under the plan or identified statutes**. Its final sentence makes §502, 29 U.S.C. §1132, applicable to enforcement. The separate witness/information protection is not presented. The statutory phrase at issue is not limited in terms to pensions or vested rights. ERISA's definitions include welfare as well as pension plans; §1051(1) exempts welfare plans from the statutory participation and vesting part. Whether that exemption also limits §510's prospective protection is the question, not an already answered inference.

## Supported alternative paths and remedial constraints

1. Read prospective attainment of any plan right to include welfare benefits while the plan exists on its lawful terms. The vesting exemption permits lawful amendment or termination; it need not permit an otherwise covered discharge undertaken for the prohibited purpose. On that view the categorical welfare exclusion fails, but remand still requires the statutory intent, participant status, actual plan right, action and causal predicates and an authorized remedy. Strongest counterargument: an employer may change the welfare package itself, and §510 must not silently convert anticipated benefits or ordinary business restructuring into a vested promise.
2. Read the existing-right and vesting structure as excluding this asserted future welfare expectation, or distinguish a lawful alteration of the package from interference with an existing protected entitlement. Strongest counterargument: the enacted prospective words may become entitled do work beyond already vested benefits; a categorical welfare exclusion could erase the distinction between changing a plan and targeting participants through discharge.
3. A narrower remand can reject vesting as an automatic exclusion yet leave whether these allegations involve a qualifying right or action for ordinary adjudication. Conversely, affirmance on an independently supported narrower ground would need an actual record and preservation basis; this packet supplies no concession of lawful purpose, no finding that the transfer was a plan amendment, and no admitted failure of another §510 element.

The opinion should keep the exercise-of-right clause and attainment clause distinct. A rule on statutory coverage does not prove intent, require lifetime benefits, prescribe a business structure, decide all nonemployer liability, reinstate every dismissed count or award damages. Remand for the welfare claim may preserve the unaffected lower dispositions. A procedural disposition must identify a supported scope or vehicle problem rather than manufacture one.
