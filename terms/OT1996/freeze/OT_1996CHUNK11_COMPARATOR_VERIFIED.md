## 123. Klehr v. A. O. Smith Corp., No. 96-663 — June 19, 1997

**Judgment and writings:** Affirmed, 9–0. Breyer wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout, and for Scalia and Thomas in Part III. Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurred in part and in the judgment. No dissent or other writing.

**Court:** A later predicate act does not restart limitations for every earlier injury in the pattern. Separate accrual requires an additional injury caused by the later act; it cannot revive earlier losses. The opinion preserves the distinction between speculative damages that cannot yet be proved and damages for an already actionable injury. It rejects the Third Circuit's last-predicate-act approach without selecting among the other generally proposed accrual rules. It accepts the lower court's conclusion that the claims fail even under the favorable injury-and-pattern-discovery approach used there, declining fact-intensive review outside the legal questions accepted. Reasonable diligence is necessary to claim fraudulent concealment; concealment alone is insufficient. That Part III has all nine joins.

**Scalia/Thomas:** They would decide the unresolved accrual question and borrow the Clayton Act injury-accrual rule with its associated rules, rather than borrow only its four-year period and construct a different combination. They agree the claim fails under that rule and join Part III's diligence requirement. Their opinion is not a join in the Court's reservation of the general accrual question.

**Record caution:** The Supreme Court opinion describes an extensive record and the lower court's discovery findings; it does not establish that every asserted later loss is a new injury, or that the lower court's single-injury classification was factually inevitable. Any comparison involving that classification should use the lower opinion and merits submissions, distinguish allegations from findings, and preserve the granted-question boundary.

## 124. Metropolitan Stevedore Co. v. Rambo, No. 96-272 — June 19, 1997

**Judgment and writings:** Vacated and remanded, 6–3. Souter for Rehnquist, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. O'Connor dissented with Scalia and Thomas. No other writing.

**Court:** A worker without present lost earning capacity may obtain a nominal award if the work injury has a significant potential to reduce capacity below preinjury earnings under future conditions. The standard is not presumed from physical impairment alone. An employee must prove the significant potential by a preponderance; that burden does not mean proving a greater-than-fifty-percent chance that the actual future loss will occur. The employer seeking modification first proves a capacity change; after proof of higher capacity reflected in market wages, the claimant bears the nominal-award predicate. Statutory future-effects language and the one-year reopening limit support this nominal category. The ALJ must determine it; the Ninth Circuit could not direct a nominal award from its own appraisal. Actual benefits were terminable on substantial evidence, but the nominal-award question required findings.

**O'Connor/Scalia/Thomas:** Section 8(h) requires a present monetary assessment of earning capacity that includes predictable future loss. Nominal awards intended merely to keep a case open defeat Congress's one-year finality choice. They would require future economic harm established by the APA standard and regard the ALJ as already having addressed and rejected it on substantial evidence. They would reverse with judgment for the employer, not remand for the new nominal inquiry. Both sides accept economic rather than merely physical disability and the employer's initial modification burden.

**Footnote fidelity:** Court note 9 separates the substantive probability predicate from confidence in proof and rejects deference to the Director's APA interpretation; note 10 identifies the parties' actual disagreement about what the ALJ found. A generic statement that the dissent ignores future capacity would be incorrect.

## 125. O'Dell v. Netherland, No. 96-6867 — June 19, 1997

**Judgment and writings:** Affirmed, 5–4. Thomas for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. Stevens dissented with Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. No other writing.

**Court:** Simmons supplied a new rule relative to October 3, 1988 finality. Gardner and Skipper did not compel it when read alongside Ramos and Caldwell and their treatment of state choices concerning information about the postsentencing legal regime. The opinion distinguishes defendant-specific historical facts from information about that regime. The narrow rebuttal rule did not have the watershed character required by Teague's procedural exception; the limited substantive exception is also described, not enlarged. The judgment therefore does not adjudicate the complete underlying Simmons claim or order resentencing.

**Stevens/Souter/Ginsburg/Breyer:** Two sufficient objections: Simmons applied the already established opportunity-to-answer protection endorsed unanimously in Skipper; and, even if new, the protection is a fundamental accuracy-and-fairness requirement within the watershed exception. Ramos and Caldwell demand accurate information, not permission to mislead a jury about the actual alternative to death. The limited number of affected States does not make a core rebuttal right less important.

**Qualification:** Court note 1 says only future dangerousness is before the Court notwithstanding the jury's additional aggravator language. The district court's resentencing relief was contingent on establishing actual parole ineligibility. Note 4 observes that O'Dell's prior prison murder could weaken the factual force of a parole-ineligibility answer; it is not a finding that legal parole eligibility or harmlessness has been conclusively resolved. The actual-innocence claim was outside the grant.

## 126. United States v. Alaska, No. 84, Original — June 19, 1997

**Component topology:** O'Connor's Parts I–III command all nine. Parts IV–V command six: O'Connor, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Thomas, joined by Rehnquist and Scalia, concurs in I–III and dissents from IV–V. The single disposition must not obscure the unanimous baseline/Dinkum components and 6–3 reservation components. Cornell files the mixed writing as `ZX`, not `ZD`.

**Baseline and Dinkum:** The claimed simple ten-mile fringing-island rule was not a firm continuing federal policy governing the Arctic waters. Ordinary Convention rules therefore control; the Alabama/Mississippi historical-waters example depends on its particular historical claims and does not establish the proposed estoppel. The Court does not adopt a new general nonmutual-estoppel rule. Dinkum's normal seasonal slumping below mean high water differs from abnormal temporary inundation. The nineteen-year tidal datum and the joint 1981 study, with later measurements, matter; occasional visibility and derivative charts do not settle island status. The twelve-mile international territorial sea does not enlarge the statutory three-mile state grant.

**Petroleum Reserve:** Congress may reserve submerged lands for an appropriate public purpose, not only convey them away. The strict inquiry requires clear inclusion and intent to defeat future state title, for these coastal lands as well as inland beds. Boundary wording embracing tidelands, the petroleum purpose, and Statehood Act §11(b)'s recognition of the Reserve support retention. The Court assumes Alaska's narrower reading of the Pickett Act for argument and rests on congressional ratification; it does not decide independent executive authority to defeat title.

**Wildlife Range:** The 1957 application, regulatory segregation and §6(e)'s lands set apart as refuges provision operate together despite formal establishment after statehood. The application and supporting habitat/boundary materials identify submerged lands and the intended retention. The relevant §6(e) exclusion is distinct from §6(m)'s application of the Submerged Lands Act.

**Thomas/Rehnquist/Scalia:** The statutory retention requirement demands greater express specificity for the submerged lands themselves; boundary inclusion and purpose do not suffice. They also distinguish temporarily preserving land for a possible refuge from setting it apart as a refuge already established. The regulation preserves administrative jurisdiction and the application allows a later determination of the actual acreage. Thomas note 4 declines Question 10's geographic inclusion issue because Alaska filed no exception to that portion; it must not be converted into an affirmative contrary boundary determination. Notes 1–2 preserve distinctions about pre-1953 States and the statutory versus equal-footing inquiries.

**Disposition:** Alaska's exceptions overruled, the United States' exception sustained, and recommendations adopted to the stated extent; proposed decree to be submitted, jurisdiction retained, each party bearing its own costs. The opinion is not itself the final detailed boundary decree.

## 127. Agostini v. Felton, Nos. 96-552 and 96-553 — June 23, 1997

**Judgment and writings:** Reversed and remanded, 5–4. O'Connor for Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Souter dissented with Stevens and Ginsburg throughout and Breyer in Part II only. Ginsburg separately dissented with Stevens, Souter and Breyer. Breyer's partial join must not be expanded into Souter Part I.

**Court:** Anticipated compliance costs do not establish the asserted factual change; views expressed in separate judicial writings are not themselves changed law. The historical intervening Establishment Clause decisions, especially Witters and Zobrest, undermine the presumptions sustaining Aguilar and the Shared Time portion of Ball. The Court rejects presumptions of indoctrination solely from public staff entering sectarian premises and automatic invalidity of direct aid, treats entanglement within effects, and applies the indoctrination, religious-recipient-definition and excessive-entanglement criteria to the safeguarded Title I program. It overrules Aguilar and Ball's Shared Time holding, not Ball's Community Education holding, and directs relief from the September 26, 1985 injunction under Rule 60(b)(5).

**Souter:** Part I defends the distinction between incidental/private-choice aid and direct substantial public provision of a religious school's educational work; absence of proved indoctrination does not remove structural subsidy and monitoring concerns. Part II argues the cited intervening decisions do not unsettle the governing rules and that stare decisis and the unchanged facts bar the reversal. Breyer joins only Part II.

**Ginsburg:** Rule 60(b)(5) does not supply a perpetual rehearing mechanism for challenging a precedent where the lower court correctly followed the law binding it. A proper new vehicle should present reconsideration. All four dissenters join this procedural ground.

**Limits:** Lower courts must continue to follow directly controlling Supreme Court precedent until the Supreme Court overrules it. The Court's own reconsideration of prospective relief is not authority for lower courts to anticipate overruling or for reopening all final judgments. Reconciliation must separately test whether the historical intervening-law premises exist in the entering law; the historical characterization of another decision is not self-validating.

## 129. Idaho v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe, No. 94-1474 — June 23, 1997

**Judgment and writings:** Reversed in part and remanded, 5–4. Kennedy's Parts I, II-A and III are an opinion of the Court joined by Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas. Parts II-B, II-C and II-D have Kennedy and Rehnquist only. O'Connor concurred in part and in the judgment with Scalia and Thomas. Souter dissented with Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Necessary correction to a generalized comparator description:** The narrow Part III rationale has five direct joins. It does not require a Marks synthesis combining the Kennedy plurality with O'Connor's concurrence. The broad state-interest/forum-availability analysis has two votes; seven Justices reject making that approach a general Young test, although the three concurring Justices and four dissenters differ on the case's result.

**Court's narrow ground:** This requested relief is functionally equivalent to quiet title in submerged sovereign lands and would eliminate Idaho's regulatory control of the disputed area. It is not merely an ordinary prospective federal-law suit in which title happens to arise. The importance of sovereign submerged-land ownership distinguishes the private-owner possession examples. The judgment decides immunity to this officer action, not ultimate ownership.

**Kennedy/Rehnquist only:** Young must be assessed in light of sovereign interests, available state forums and the nature of the right, with a suggested special-factors comparison. Those general propositions are not the Court's five-Justice rationale.

**O'Connor/Scalia/Thomas:** Reject the general balancing departure while joining the exceptional functional quiet-title rationale. Ordinary Young doctrine remains available without proving absence of a state forum; the degree of intrusion in this particular sovereign-land case supplies the limit.

**Souter/Stevens/Ginsburg/Breyer:** An ongoing federal violation and prospective official relief satisfy Young; necessary resolution of title does not itself turn the action into a forbidden suit against the State. The relief sought does not include retrospective damages or an accounting. Young itself implicated strong sovereign interests, and Lee/Tindal undermine the proposed title bar.

**Scope:** The dismissed direct claims against the State and agencies were not the certiorari issue. Separate federal title litigation is not resolved. No legal conclusion about the Tribe's actual ownership follows from this immunity ruling.

## 130. Kansas v. Hendricks, Nos. 95-1649 and 95-9075 — June 23, 1997

**Judgment and writings:** Reversed, 5–4. Thomas for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. Kennedy concurs while joining the Court in full. Breyer dissents with Stevens and Souter throughout and Ginsburg in Parts II–III only. Ginsburg does not join Part I's due-process discussion.

**Court:** The danger-plus-mental-abnormality/volitional-impairment criteria sufficiently narrow the class; a particular psychiatric label is not constitutionally controlling. The civil legislative intent is not overcome by the clearest proof of punitive purpose or effects. Past crimes supply evidence, not a new retributive sentence; scienter and deterrence do not operate as in criminal punishment, and procedural protections and present-condition release remain important. Nonpunitive classification defeats both Double Jeopardy and Ex Post Facto objections. Treatment need not be curative or the overriding purpose; statutory treatment duties and the treatment information discussed in the opinion support at least an ancillary purpose.

**Kennedy:** Full agreement on this record, coupled with express concern that sham treatment masking retribution or added incapacitation, or an excessively imprecise class, could alter the constitutional analysis in a later case. His concurrence is not a withheld join or a finding that those conditions presently exist.

**Breyer:** Part I accepts the substantive distinction based on recognized disorder, inability to control dangerous conduct and dangerousness; Stevens and Souter join, Ginsburg does not. Parts II–III conclude that this application imposes retrospective punishment based on the combined statutory operation, delayed or absent meaningful treatment despite asserted treatability, prisonlike confinement and absence of consideration of less restrictive alternatives. No single feature invariably makes civil commitment punitive. The dissent would affirm on the as-applied Ex Post Facto ground, does not invalidate all prospective applications, and does not separately resolve Double Jeopardy.

**Crucial original/later-record distinction:** Court note 5 cites an August 28, 1995 state-habeas finding at App. 453–454 that treatment was being provided over conflicting testimony. The opinion also cites the Attorney General's later oral representation of roughly 31.5 weekly treatment hours. Breyer states that judicial notice of the separate habeas proceeding was denied March 1, 1996 and contests using later representations to establish the original commitment's facts. He further cites Dr. Befort's later testimony at App. 393, 400 and 420–421 concerning inadequate or unavailable meaningful treatment for Hendricks. The original-record citations for implementation include App. 255, 367 and 369. A comparison must distinguish the original application, later developments, actual receipt of treatment and potential treatability; statutory review/release provisions cannot be described as nonexistent merely because their practical operation is questioned.
