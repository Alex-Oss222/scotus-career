Dillingham's Sonoma County detention-facility contract was a public work. Sound Systems Media performed electrical work. After IBEW withdrew representation, Sound Systems joined NCESCA, which agreed with NESTU; its associated new training program lacked state approval when used. Later approval was not retroactive. The District Court found the program covered by ERISA and related to the state scheme, but held the Fitzgerald Act saved the application. The Ninth Circuit rejected that saving theory and reversed. It refused a state estoppel argument because it had not been raised in the District Court. It did not reach the separate NLRA preemption issue or whether an NLRA-based deprivation supported §1983 damages, and rejected §1988 fees on the ERISA theory before it. ## 33. General Motors — fresh neutral validation

Sources: [Ohio Supreme Court, 73 Ohio St.3d 29, 652 N.E.2d 188, August 9, 1995](https://static.case.law/ohio-st-3d/73/cases/0029-01.json), complete primary body at `../sources/CHUNK3_GM_TRACY_LOWER_BODY.txt`; sanitized published factual recitations in `OT_1996CHUNK3_TRACY_CONTROL_FACTS.md`; metadata extraction above. No same-case Supreme Court outcome or reasoning was received.

**Replace the payment/refund premise.** The supported proceeding reviews the Tax Commissioner's use-tax assessment, affirmed by the Board of Tax Appeals and Ohio Supreme Court. The purchases were October 1, 1986 through June 30, 1990. Liability and an adverse assessment establish a concrete economic dispute; neither the lower body nor the sanitized recitation proves prior payment, an assessment amount or an initiating refund application. Describe relief as reversal of the adverse assessment and lawful consequent tax relief. A refund, if relevant, depends on actual payment and governing remedial facts. The lack of recovered payment evidence proves neither nonpayment nor forfeiture.

**Tax classification.** The state-law exemption concerned sales by statutory natural gas companies. The Ohio court applied its same-day Chrysler construction excluding independent marketers lacking distribution assets. Its opinion reports testimony that qualifying sellers owned or operated transportation/distribution equipment and delivered gas to Ohio consumers. Both in-state and out-of-state nonqualifying marketers were taxed. Thus do not call statutory wording an express residence classification. The competing argument is that the legally defined favored class and the measure's practical operation favor local utility interests in a market also served by interstate sellers.

**Regulatory and record limits.** GM bought virtually all plant gas from out-of-state marketers and used local delivery for much of it. LDCs had service, rate, reliability and human-needs duties and could not select only profitable customers. The record has very little detail about the competitive large-user market. Assertions that particular exempt LDC sales during this period were unregulated negotiated transactions have no identified record support. The 1996 deregulation enactment postdates the purchases; it cannot retroactively establish their character. System obligations are not transaction-specific cost findings. The separate utility property, assessment and gross-receipts taxes versus marketer property/franchise taxes are not proved substantially equivalent burdens matching this tax.

**Lower grounds and scope.** The Ohio court first found in-state and out-of-state nonqualifying sellers treated alike, then denied GM standing to assert sellers' injury. It treated equal protection as submerged in that Commerce Clause argument; it did not conduct the full utility-obligation rational-basis analysis attributed to it in the original packet. It also sustained partial statutory penalty remission. The supplied Supreme Court questions concern standing, Commerce and Equal Protection; the independent state-law classification and penalty-remission challenge are not newly granted questions. Do not convert the penalty discussion into a separate federal claim.

**Entering law.** Apply the exact standards copied in the slice: “Discriminatory routing, charges and linked subsidies in interstate commerce” (present operation SSC, March 11, 1996; discrimination rule otherwise unchanged); “Compensatory taxes and the actual equivalent local burden” (present operation Fulton, February 21, 1996); and “Rational-basis review of ordinary economic classifications” (Beach, June 1, 1993). West Lynn requires examining an integrated fiscal operation, Oregon Waste requires a real comparison and demanding justification after discrimination is found, and Fulton preserves the three cumulative compensation predicates. Those holdings do not already decide whether these regulated services and independent commodity sales are materially comparable. Beach governs a separately reached ordinary economic-classification claim and does not erase independent Commerce Clause constraints. No earlier OT1996 holding in the authorized projection changes these rules.

**Supported alternatives for independent modeling.** A customer can challenge its own assessment and resulting economic burden without prevailing on comparability. On the merits, identify the challenged classification and actual competitive relationship before selecting scrutiny. One path treats the regulated bundled service and associated obligations as material differences and rejects the alleged discrimination; another treats the industrial commodity transactions as materially competing and tests the integrated preference and any justification under the discrimination rule. Each must confront the thin transaction-specific record. The narrow industrial-sale path is an analytical possibility, not a finding that these were unregulated sales or that neutral support measures would fully cover universal-service costs. If a material factual premise is needed to select that path, consider lawful remand rather than finding it from silence. No independently preserved Pike claim or congressional authorization theory is established by the supplied grant and sources; do not silently add either. Equal protection may be separately decided under its own rule or left unnecessary after sufficient Commerce Clause relief.

## 35. Robinson — fresh neutral validation

Sources: [Fourth Circuit en banc, 70 F.3d 325, No. 93-1562, November 29, 1995](https://static.case.law/f3d/70/cases/0325-01.json), primary opinion and both dissents at `../sources/CHUNK3_ROBINSON_LOWER_BODY.txt`; [OpenJurist primary text](https://openjurist.org/70/f3d/325/robinson-v-shell-oil-company); source quotes of Title VII §§701(f), 704(a), 703(a), 706(g); completed Walters in the authorized neutral projection. An attempted direct 1994 govinfo §704 retrieval returned a page-not-found document and was removed; no official-text verification is falsely claimed for it.

**Posture and facts.** Shell discharged Robinson in 1991. He filed an EEOC racial-discharge charge; during its pendency, a prospective employer sought a reference. His complaint alleged false information and negative references in retaliation. The quoted complaint and court recitation establish allegations, not findings of falsity, motive, causation, injury or an unsuccessful job application. Shell obtained Rule12(b)(6) dismissal on former-employee coverage. The January 1995 panel disposition was vacated upon rehearing; the en banc judgment is the judgment under review. The supplied question does not reopen the separate discriminatory-discharge merits.

**Full lower grounds.** The majority treated the statutory definition and current-employment reading as unambiguous, regarded the express applicant category and omission of former employees as significant, and argued that an adverse employment action must concern current employment. Hall's dissent, joined by Ervin and Michael, gave both contextual ambiguity and statutory-enforcement arguments, emphasizing retaliation connected to prior employment; Murnaghan separately disputed the asserted temporal limitation, with Ervin joining. EEOC participated as amicus supporting coverage. Those are supported positions, not invented briefs. Other circuits' statutes and formulations retain their own scope.

**Entering-law correction.** Walters was decided January14, 1997, not merely prepared. Its unanimously controlling numerosity holding counts genuine employment relationships on each relevant working day under §701(b), ordinarily evidenced by payroll; already separated workers do not count on later days. Its question and statutory time units differ from §704(a)'s protection against retaliation for protected activity. It also leaves retaliation liability, causation and remedy unresolved. Darden's standard, “ERISA employee status under general common-law agency” (March24, 1992), distinguishes worker status from benefit consequences; Town & Country's unanimous November28, 1995 construction preserves each statute's actual exclusions and compatible service. Neither is a universal rule that a formerly employed person remains employed for all purposes or that protection ends whenever payroll counting ends. No current-wording change to these propositions is inferred.

**Supported legal paths.** The current-only path must defend its temporal reading from the enacted definition, applicants clause and surrounding uses, and answer how a former worker may pursue discharge enforcement without protection from reference retaliation. The coverage path must show that statutory context supports former employees rather than treating desirable policy as permission to replace an unambiguous limit; it must explain the §701(b) distinction and avoid unlimited coverage for strangers. A narrow conclusion could concern alleged retaliation by a former employer tied to that employment and protected Title VII activity. That is an available construction, not a finding of retaliation or an adopted extra statutory element. If coverage is sustained, remand addresses unresolved elements; if excluded, the statutory dismissal may stand. Neither path justifies new factfinding at this Court.

Do not import a later general “materially adverse” test: the original packet's phrase identifies an unresolved harms question, not existing adopted doctrine. No universal postemployment tort action, automatic damages, reinstatement to Shell, new trial or fixed filing schedule follows from coverage. The statutory quoted text and reported pleading posture adequately support modeling without possession of an original complaint or employment-reference document. A new claim about their contents would require further source support.
