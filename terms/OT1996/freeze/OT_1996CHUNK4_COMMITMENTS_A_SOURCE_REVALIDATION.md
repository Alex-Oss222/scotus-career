## Lynce: revised operative commitment

All eight Associates now support **vacating the reviewed certificate-denial disposition and remanding through the Court of Appeals for resolution of the actual exhaustion predicate and claim scope before a favorable merits adjudication**. This is a threshold-only return. It neither grants the writ nor orders release, restored credits, a merits finding of an Ex Post Facto violation, or an obligatory rejection of the claim. It does not establish a mixed petition or a state procedural default. The proper lower-court review and certificate procedures remain applicable; no appellate merits explanation is attributed to the earlier one-line order.

**Exact basis:** The newly established submissions leave no adequately determined statutory exhaustion predicate on this record. This is **not** a holding that every claim-specific reading of the State's qualified concession is legally impossible, that the State expressly waived exhaustion, or that no exceptional circumstances can ever excuse presentation. The lower courts must determine the concession's actual scope and legal effect against the unavailable-or-ineffective-process standard and the remaining state-law questions. They must not infer waiver from silence, treat hypothetical additional claims as asserted, or equate adverse precedent alone with a necessarily ineffective process.

### Supported scope and competing readings

The actual district answer admits Lynce did not exhaust, lists administrative and judicial procedures, and expressly refuses waiver. It says it would agree that exhaustion was futile if the facts and issues concerned only the retroactive credit cancellation affecting prisoners still in custody; it then distinguishes Lynce's release as potentially giving rise to additional reincarceration theories. Its transition to the merits nevertheless already calls the Ex Post Facto theory the sole claim. The later respondent merits footnote confirms an election to proceed only on that claim. No additional due-process or state-law count may be invented.

The claim-specific reading favoring immediate consideration is substantial: Florida identified possible additional claims as its concern, agreed that the common cancellation question was futile, and later confirmed that only that common claim remains. A court could determine that its qualified concession governs the actually pursued issue. The obstacle is that the same answer explicitly maintained nonexhaustion despite identifying the sole federal theory, confined its favorable premise to prisoners still in custody, and identified available procedures. The later election passage does not expressly abandon that objection or explain why the state process cannot protect the precise claim. The State's legal characterization is relevant but cannot itself amend §2254.

The original merits remand would also leave a material state-law comparison: whether the later credit mechanisms continued the offense-date framework and which portion was protected. That issue makes it particularly inappropriate to assume that nothing meaningful remains for state corrective proceedings. The new threshold disposition does not prejudge the answer to that comparison or hold that each accounting detail must always be separately exhausted.

### Governing rule and limits

Former §2254(b)–(c) requires exhaustion, an absence of available corrective process, or circumstances making that process ineffective before granting habeas relief. Duckworth rejects merely clear constitutional error or judicial economy as a substitute. Engle's predicted-futility reasoning concerns procedural-default cause, not an adjudicated default here; it cautions against assuming that a state court can never reconsider an adverse rule. Schall's cited passage reports a lower court's futility conclusion; it is not a general Supreme Court rule excusing every first presentation after adverse precedent.

Granberry establishes that exhaustion is not an appellate jurisdictional bar. Its discretionary holding, however, concerns a State that failed to raise the defense in district court. This State expressly raised it. Its omission-based rationale cannot establish a deemed waiver here. The present Court has power to vacate and remand for a proper threshold determination without issuing the earlier proposed favorable constitutional ruling. Calling the matter a remand would not justify silently evading a statutory prerequisite; this addendum deliberately removes the merits rule from the operative commitment.

Rose concerns claims actually in the petition. Excluding possible additional reincarceration theories avoids a manufactured mixed-petition problem, but does not itself exhaust the sole remaining theory. Neither this Court nor the lower court should hold that all future omitted claims are foreclosed merely because respondent argued so.

The 1996 amendment does not resolve this disposition through a new implied-waiver theory. Its permission to deny an unexhausted claim on the merits is not permission to grant relief; its express-waiver provision cannot be satisfied by an invented affirmative abandonment. The threshold-only return does not decide the amendment's general application to this pending proceeding because it relies on no relevant difference between versions. Any later proposed disposition requiring such a difference must address temporal applicability with proper date-eligible support.
