# 153. Harrison v. Eddy Potash, Inc.

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Whether an employer may be held liable under Title VII when a supervisor uses authority over an employee's work assignments and working conditions to facilitate sexually harassing conduct.

2. Whether an employee must prove that the offending supervisor possessed substantial managerial authority, including authority to hire or fire, before employer liability may be imposed under agency-law principles.

3. Whether a federal court commits reversible error by instructing a jury that supervisory harassment can create employer liability only if the supervisor possessed actual or apparent authority to commit the harassment, rather than authority over the employee that aided its commission.

4. Whether an employee covered by a collective bargaining agreement must exhaust contractual grievance or arbitration procedures before pursuing an independent Title VII discrimination action in federal court.

## B. RECORD, POSTURE AND RELIEF

Harrison, an underground miner, alleged that shift foreman Brown used work orders to take her into isolated mine areas and subjected her to repeated unwanted sexual conduct. Brown controlled assignments and participated in discipline. The employer later investigated, credited her unwillingness but Brown's account of perceived consent, and prescribed leave, treatment, reassignment and restrictions. Its harassment policy had not been included in her new-hire materials. A jury awarded $102,500 compensatory and $40,000 punitive damages against Brown on state torts, but found for Eddy Potash on Title VII.

Harrison challenged employer-attribution instructions. Potash cross-appealed on contractual nonexhaustion. The Tenth Circuit ordered further proceedings on Title VII and rejected the cross-appeal. Potash omitted the CBA and the order addressing exhaustion from the appellate record; no identified clause committed statutory claims to arbitration. ## G. FRESH VALIDATION: INSTRUCTIONS, ARBITRATION AND REMEDY

**Source and cutoff.** The complete May 8, 1997 Tenth Circuit opinion, including both cross-appeal footnotes and the complete reproduced instructions, was read in `../sources/chunk13_c/harrison_ca10_complete.txt`. **Record detail and status.** The court recites Harrison’s account that Brown repeatedly used work assignments to take her from her buddy or usual station to isolated mine locations during May and June 1993. Her account includes repeated refusals, physical sexual misconduct, and after one refusal an order to work through lunch. Those recitations are not new Supreme Court findings. Potash’s investigator regarded her participation as unwilling while crediting Brown’s account of perceived consent; that employer determination is not a court finding that the conduct was welcome or legally blameless. Meritor distinguishes unwelcome conduct from apparent voluntary participation. Potash promised lost-time compensation, treatment and a different crew, reprimanded Brown, imposed permanent probation for recurrence and restricted contact to necessary work. Harrison remained on payroll approximately a year without returning, and was terminated in a June 17, 1994 reduction in force. This review does not newly adjudicate that termination. Harrison discovered a policy posted in another mine area only after several incidents; it was absent from her new-hire materials. Neither universal ignorance throughout the period nor a clearly effective reporting route is inferred.

**The instructions actually at issue.** The charge required Harrison to prove Brown was the employer’s agent, acted under apparent authority in the acts, and was reasonably relied upon. It defined agent as exercising significant control over hiring, firing or conditions equivalent to managerial control, making Brown the employer’s alter ego. The apparent-authority instruction said the employer could not be liable unless Brown had authority, apparent or otherwise, to commit the acts, and focused on Harrison’s knowledge; it also allowed consideration of a policy and measures making clear that harassment was forbidden and could be reported without adverse consequences. The [exact instruction attachment](../entering-law/OT_1996CHUNK13_HARRISON_INSTRUCTIONS.md) mechanically preserves the reproduced instruction passages; no wording is invented.

**Ruling below and its limits.** The Tenth Circuit rejected the alter-ego prerequisite, explaining that Sauers’s discussion concerned whether a suit naming a supervisor constituted a timely suit against the employer. It found the charge omitted a supported actual-authority-aid route and that the demanding managerial prerequisite was prejudicial where Brown controlled daily work but was not top management. It separately found apparent authority incorrectly defined: the relevant reasonable belief must derive from the principal’s words or conduct, not merely from the employee’s knowledge of the agent’s claimed power. It did not condemn consideration of an effectively implemented policy within apparent-authority analysis. It found prejudice on the charge as a whole and required further proceedings, without imposing employer liability.

The court also rejected Harrison’s proposed rule that significant supervisory status alone automatically establishes employer liability. It approved neither every word of her requested instructions nor strict liability for every supervisory act; it required an appropriate instruction on aid supplied by delegated authority. Its adopted aid rule excluded mere workplace proximity. In light of Andrade the same distinct questions remain: the controlling rule, what charge was given, whether evidence supports that route, preservation and prejudice, and the lawful scope of further proceedings.

**Contractual exhaustion.** Potash characterized its defense as jurisdictional, but a contractual arbitration assertion does not itself remove federal subject-matter jurisdiction. Footnote 1 establishes that the CBA and the district court’s exhaustion order were missing from the actual appellate record; this is a reported litigation-record limitation, not an inference from the operator’s research files. Footnote 2 states that the court did not know the clause’s scope and that Potash’s appellate brief did not indicate an agreement to arbitrate statutory claims. The defense must be addressed on those actual limits, not by inventing either an absent statutory clause or a never-existing contract.

Gardner-Denver preserves independent Title VII rights despite arbitration of contractual claims. Gilmer enforces an actual agreement to arbitrate the statutory ADEA claim in a securities-registration contract and distinguishes the earlier labor cases by statutory agreement, union/individual conflicts and FAA setting. Its footnote 2 expressly reserves the general scope of the FAA employment-contract exclusion. Thus the Tenth Circuit’s separate broad FAA/CBA rationale is a lower-court premise to evaluate, not Supreme Court law. First Options’s assent/delegation discipline prevents an asserted agreement from supplying its own proof; it does not itself decide collective statutory arbitration. Livadas and Hawaiian Airlines concern distinct statutes and preemption, not a new immunity from all contractual arbitration. No later historical rule resolves this question.
