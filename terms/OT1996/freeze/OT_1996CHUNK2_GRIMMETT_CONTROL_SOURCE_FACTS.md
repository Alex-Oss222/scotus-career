## Record and posture facts

The Ninth Circuit reviewed limitations dismissal as summary judgment because materials outside the pleadings were considered. It applied injury discovery but also said a complete RICO claim, including a pattern, must exist before time runs. It separately rejected (i) claimed later independent injury; (ii) fraudulent concealment for insufficient pleading and, alternatively, insufficient supported active concealment and diligence; (iii) automatic tolling during the bankruptcy proceeding because the nondebtor lawyer could be sued separately; and (iv) deferral of accrual merely because later bankruptcy recovery might mitigate a known injury. These distinct grounds must remain separate.

The petition contains two questions: discovery of injury, causation and pattern; and whether accrual precedes clear damages when an ongoing bankruptcy or other proceeding may make the claimant whole. Petitioners' merits brief recasts and reverses their order, emphasizing that the alleged fraud had not yet caused a concrete debt loss before final discharge. The lower opinion's footnote 4 states that plaintiffs did not ask the Ninth Circuit to adopt the last-predicate-act rule. Do not present that rule as an independently preserved request without further source support.

**Filing-date discrepancy requiring correction or exact source confirmation:** The reported Ninth Circuit text repeatedly says November 1994. ## Settlement/mootness issue and its documented answer

Beckley argues the case became moot because the 1994 settlement with Vincent included revocation of the bankruptcy discharge; its claim depends on reading petitioners as having abandoned lien impairment and making final discharge necessary to all RICO injury. It also argues prior settlements/receipts compensated the debt. These are **respondent arguments**, not adjudicated findings or a stipulated mootness result.

Petitioners' reply pp. 4–7 disputes both premises. It identifies $160,000 toward alimony arrearages and $40,000 to the estate; it says the release of Vincent preserved claims against the estate and the remaining lawyer defendants. It disputes full compensation because estate expenses, other creditors, allocation across causes of action and trebling could affect recovery. It alternatively argues that the 1994 personal release might first crystallize concrete loss if defendants' conduct caused the inability to pay more. Those are **petitioners' arguments**, not findings of accrual, damages, or causation.

The attached agreement provides direct documentary support for limits that must not be omitted:

- Paragraph 3 disclaims admissions of wrongdoing, lack of merit, or adequacy of consideration to fully compensate plaintiffs, and reserves claims against nonparties.
- Paragraph 4A expressly excludes Patricia L. Brown, her firm, Beckley and their identified related persons from the releases.
- Paragraph 4B preserves Joanne's claims against the bankruptcy estate, including her proof of claim.
- The subsequent stipulation (appendix pp. 39–41) calls for discharge revocation and dismissal with prejudice only of the other claims against the debtor defendants; it repeats the nonadmission provisions. Whether and when each resulting order was entered must be checked before making the corresponding procedural fact decisive.

Thus, existence of settlement papers does not itself establish full satisfaction, release of these respondents, or mootness. No adjudicative conclusion on that question is supplied here.
