I. PETITIONERS’ RICO ACTION DID NOT ACCRUE 
IN 1989, NOR IS IT MOOT 


Both Respondents present several arguments which 
merely serve to re-emphasize one of Petitioners’ principal 
points: until Joanne Siragusa is compelled to accept less 
than 100% of her pre-existing debt she has not suffered 
concrete RICO damage. In attempting to evade the logic 
of Bankers Trust Co. v. Rhoades, 859 F.2d 1096 (2d Cir. 
1988), cert. denied, 490 U.S. 1017 (1989), Respondents can- 
not agree amongst themselves as to the time of Joanne’s 
RICO injury. Respondent Brown argues that Joanne knew 
of her potential injury in 1989 so the limitations period 
has already run. Respondent Beckley, Singleton argues 
that Joanne may have been injured as a result of her 1994 
settlement with her husband but inexplicably concludes 
that the RICO case is moot. Neither position has merit. 


1. As detailed in Petitioners’ initial brief, the var- 
ious divorce proceedings between Joanne and Vincent 
Siragusa, summarized by the Supreme Court of Nevada 
in Siragusa v. Siragusa, 843 P.2d 807 (Nev. 1992), resulted 
in Vincent's substantial indebtedness to Joanne in terms 
of both alimony and property settlement obligations. At 
the time Vincent filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy protec- 
tion, on November 10, 1987, these obligations included 
both arrearages and future payments. Id. at 809. 


Respondent Brown urges that Joanne’s RICO injury 
occurred when she filed her Adversary Complaint in 
Bankruptcy Court in 1989, yet she tacitly admits that 
Joanne, at best, had suffered only “potential damage.” 
Respondent Brown Answering Brief at 23 (emphasis 


added). As Petitioner explained in her initial Brief, “an 


attempt, even a successful attempt, to subvert an under- 
lying security interest . . . is not itself a RICO injury 
unless and until the debt that is being secured is ‘lost.’ ” 
Petitioners’ Brief at 20. 


Respondent urges that Joanne was aware of her 
potential injury when she learned that her payments from 
Vincent were in jeopardy. Specifically, Respondent Brown 
suggests that Joanne became “aware of the extent of 
potential damage by May of 1989.” Respondent Brown 
Answering Brief at 23. However, being aware of the 
potential damage is not sufficient to meet the injury 
requirement mandated by this Court in Sedima S.P.R.L. v. 
Imrex Co., 473 U.S. 479 (1985), a requirement charac- 
terized by the Ninth Circuit as an injury that amounts to 
a “concrete financial loss.” Imagineering, Inc. v. Kiewit Pacific 
Co., 976 F.2d 1303, 1310 (9th Cir. 1992) (emphasis added), 
cert. denied, 507 U.S. 1004 (1993). 


Respondent Brown also points to Vincent's prelimi- 
nary discharge on May 4, 1988, as another point when 
Joanne would have known the extent of her potential 
damage. Respondent Brown's Brief at 24. Yet again this 
event did not cause Joanne a RICO injury because she 
was not at that point compelled to accept less than 100% 
of her pre-existing debt and because the determination of 
Vincent's legal obligation was not final. The preliminary 
discharge was granted on May 4, 1988, as an automatic 
statutory requirement because none of the creditors 
objected to Vincent's filing within the time allotted. See 
Siragusa v. Siragusa, 843 P.2d at 809; see also 11 U.S.C. 
§ 727(a); Fed. R. Bankr. P. 4004. 


Such a discharge, however, remains subject to revoca- 
tion for one year by a creditor or Trustee upon proof that 
the discharge was obtained by fraud and the moving 
party did not know of the fraud at the time of discharge. 
11 U.S.C. § 727(e). Less than a year later, on May 2, 1989, 
Joanne and the Trustee filed their adversary complaint in 
Bankruptcy Court seeking revocation of Vincent's dis- 
charge for fraud. (J.A. 186). From that point on, Joanne 
and the Trustee sought to untangle the defendants’ var- 
ious fraudulent transfers and to effect the return of the 
property to the Bankruptcy Estate. As the Second Circuit 
recognized in Bankers Trust, a RICO cause of action can- 
not be said to have accrued in such a situation. 


Notably, Respondent Brown's Answering Brief is 
silent with respect to the Second Circuit's decision in 
Bankers Trust, 859 F.2d 1096. The defrauded creditor in 
Bankers Trust faced nearly the exact same circumstances 
as Joanne has faced in the instant case. Moreover, the 
creditor in Bankers Trust already had accepted a Chapter 
11 Reorganization Plan, agreed to receive only 17.5% of 
its debt, and agreed to relieve the debtor of its remaining 
debt obligation. Id. at 1098. When it learned of evidence 
of fraudulent transfers, the creditor moved in Bankruptcy 
Court to revoke the reorganization plan on the ground 
that it had been obtained by fraud. Id. at 1099. 


The reopened Bankruptcy Proceeding meant that the 
creditor's debt had not been finally “lost,” and the ques- 
tion of a RICO injury was, therefore, found to be specula- 
tive. Id. at 1106. In light of these facts, the court held as 
follows: 


[Ajfter the claims . . . are dealt with by the 
bankruptcy court — through recovery of all or 
part of [the fraudulently transferred corporate] 
assets, an order of abandonment, or some other 
final disposition of the lost-debt claims - [plain- 
tiff’s] injury will be ascertainable, an action as to 
the injury will accrue, and [plaintiff] will then 
have four years to bring a civil RICO action to 
recover the damages it has suffered in this area, 
subject of course to the normal rules and pro- 
cedures governing such claims. 


Bankers Trust, 859 F.2d at 1106. In short, the Second Cir- 
cuit concluded that the creditor had not yet suffered a 
RICO injury since the Bankruptcy Proceeding was still 
open and the Trustee was attempting to recover fraudu- 
lently transferred assets. 


2. Respondent Beckley, Singleton argues that two 
settlements within the Bankruptcy Proceeding have ren- 
dered the RICO case moot or have fully compensated 
Joanne for her RICO injury. Neither assertion is correct. 


Respondent asserts that a settlement between Joanne, 
the Trustee, and Vincent, renders moot Petitioner's RICO 
case. Respondent Beckley, Singleton Brief at 2-3. This 
argument, however, ignores the clear terms of the Settle- 
ment Agreement — a Settlement Agreement that Respon- 
dents Beckley, Singleton, Delanoy, Jemison & List 
themselves attached to their brief as an appendix. 


In the summer of 1994, Joanne and the Trustee 
entered into a comprehensive settlement agreement with 
Vincent that, subsequently, was approved by the Bank- 
ruptcy Court. Respondent Beckley, Singleton Brief, 
Appendix pp. 1-53. Vincent agreed to pay Joanne 


$160,000 toward his alimony arrearage obligations and 
agreed to pay to the Bankruptcy Estate $40,000 toward its 
claims in the Adversary Proceeding. In return, Joanne 
released any personal claims against Vincent (other than 
future alimony claims), and the Bankruptcy Estate 
released its claims against Vincent. As part of the agree- 
ment, Vincent agreed to have his preliminary discharge 
revoked. App. at 20, 21. 


The Settlement Agreement, however, explicitly pre- 
served Joanne’s right to receive payment against the 
Bankruptcy Estate for her pre-existing debts, as follows: 


B. The Releases by Joanne shall not extend or 
operate with respect to any claims by Joanne 
against the Bankruptcy Estate, including with- 
out limitation Joanne’s Proof of Claim, all of 
which such claims by Joanne shall survive 
against the Bankruptcy Estate. 


App. at 17. Moreover, the settlement explicitly preserved 
both Joanne’s and the Estate’s rights to proceed against 
the Respondents, by providing that the releases “shall not 
extend to or operate in favor of [the Respondents].” App. 
at 16. 


In short, the settlement left Joanne in much the same 
position she had been in since Vincent filed his Chapter 7 
Bankruptcy petition in 1987. Vincent's personal obliga- 
tion to Joanne may have been extinguished, but Joanne’s 
right to collect from Vincent's Bankruptcy Estate on the 
pre-existing debt remained. At the moment of Vincent's 
filing, a Bankruptcy Estate was created comprised of all 
of Vincent's property, “wherever located and by whom- 
ever held... .” 11 U.S.C. § 541. A Trustee was then 
appointed to “collect and reduce to money the property 
of the estate.” 11 U.S.C. § 704. This money ultimately 


would be distributed to those creditors who had filed 
timely proofs of claim. 11 U.S.C. §§ 501, 726. Joanne’s 
settlement with Vincent left her awaiting payment of her 
pre-existing debt from the funds collected by the Trustee 
belonging to the Bankruptcy Estate.'

requests for relief, including one for punitive damages 
and one for costs. (JA 210, 216). The settlement with other

Il. THE “INJURY AND PATTERN DISCOVERY” 
ACCRUAL RULE IS REQUIRED BY THE CIVIL 
RICO STATUTE AND BY GENERAL PRINCIPLES 
OF ACCRUAL 


Respondents and their amici cite numerous judicial 
opinions discussing the purposes served by statutes of 
limitations. Yet there is no dispute that RICO has a statute 
of limitations and that the limitations period is four 


years. See Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff & Associ- 
ates, Inc., 483 U.S. 143 (1987). Rather, the issue before the 
Court here is when does the statute of limitations period 
begin to run. As to this accrual question, the Respon- 
dents’ discussion of statutes of limitations adds nothing. 


In their briefs, the Respondents completely ignore 
the civil RICO statute itself because the language and 
structure of the statute plainly argue for an accrual rule 
where the limitations period does not begin to run until a 
plaintiff knows or should have known both her injury and 
that her injury was the result of a pattern of racketeering. 
The Respondents also fail to discuss the accrual rules 
adopted by this Court and the lower courts in federal 
fraud and continuing violations cases, even though civil 
RICO actions closely resemble these types of cases. 
Respondents, instead, argue that this Court should be 
guided by the antitrust rule for accrual. The accrual rule 
developed under the Clayton Act is an anomaly unique to 
antitrust law and does not even contain the “discovery” 
requirement applied in all other federal statutes. Because 
having the limitations period run upon discovery of 
injury and a pattern of racketeering is the only rule 
consistent with the RICO statute and the circumstances 
faced by civil RICO plaintiffs, the decision of the court of 
appeals should be reversed. 


1. It is striking that Respondents and their amici do 
not even attempt to address the language and structure of 
RICO. The reason for this omission is clear: the language 
and structure of the statute compel the “injury and pat- 
tern discovery” rule. The crux of a civil RICO cause of 
action is that a plaintiff must demonstrate a series of 


predicate acts constituting a pattern of racketeering activ- 
ity. “[T]he heart of any RICO complaint is the allegation 
of a pattern of racketeering.” Agency Holding Corp., 483 
U.S. at 154 (emphasis in original). The “pattern of rack- 
eteering” concept was the new approach to fighting orga- 


nized criminal activity which lies at the core of the RICO 
statute. 


Contrary to the concern about “stale claims” 
expressed by Respondents, the RICO statute also pro- 
vides that courts accept evidence relating to racketeering 
activity that is up to ten years in the past. 18 U.S.C. 
§ 1961(5) of the RICO statute defines a “pattern of rack- 
eteering activity” as at least two predicate acts of rack- 
eteering activity, “the last of which occurred within ten 
years .. . after the commission of a prior act of racketeer- 
ing.” Congress plainly believed that attacking criminal 
activity took precedence over a defendant’s concern 
about stale evidence. The restrictive “injury discovery” 
accrual rule conflicts with Congress’ clear intent to allow 
plaintiffs to introduce evidence relating to older remote 
acts. “It would be inconsistent with this breadth of defini- 
tion for us to state that we will look to the past ten years 
to see if a civil RICO claim exists but if we find ten years 
of racketeering activity, all related and all perpetrated by 
the same defendants, we will provide a remedy only to 
those who were victimized within the past four years.” 


Keystone Ins. Co. v. Houghton, 863 F.2d 1125, 1132-33 (3d 
Cir. 1988). 


In enacting RICO, Congress also understood that the 
criminal activity that the statute was attacking would be 
difficult to root out. The statute, through its “enterprise” 


10 


element and other provisions, was aimed at the infiltra- 
tion of legitimate businesses for criminal ends. See United 
States v. Turkette, 452 U.S. 576, 591 (1981). For this reason, 
the statute listed numerous fraud-related crimes as the 
predicate acts constituting a pattern of racketeering? In 
the typical RICO case, a plaintiff must uncover not one, 
but at least two, underlying frauds which are part of a 
pattern and which are being perpetrated by a seemingly 
legitimate business or union. Given the difficulty inherent 
in developing such a civil RICO claim, the “injury and 
pattern discovery” rule is superior in that it provides a 
less restrictive standard for victims of RICO schemes and 
links the accrual of the action to discovery of the RICO 
pattern. 


2. Respondents and their amici also fail to explain 
why the accrual rule for federal fraud cases is not the 
most appropriate rule here. As noted above, most RICO 
actions are based on fraud as the predicate acts. In addi- 
tion, the prototypical RICO scheme is in essence a com- 
plex fraudulent scheme whereby a legitimate business or 
union enterprise is infiltrated and operated for illicit 
ends. The federal fraud accrual rule therefore is highly 
relevant to the civil RICO context. The federal fraud 
accrual rule is not “injury discovery.” Rather, in federal 
fraud cases, the limitations period commences only after 
the plaintiff discovered or should have discovered the 
fraud itself. See Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 397

5. Finally, Respondents and their amici maintain 
that the “injury and pattern discovery” rule should be 
rejected because it would allow a plaintiff “to slumber on 
his rights without investigating whether the defendant 
has committed a pattern of racketeering activity.” 
Respondent Beckley, Singleton Brief at 13. This argument 
misconstrues the rule advocated by Petitioners. Under 
the “injury and pattern discovery” rule, the action begins 
to accrue “as soon as the plaintiff discovers, or reasonably 
should have discovered, both the existence and source of his 
injury and that the injury is part of a pattern.” Association 
of Commonwealth Claimants v. Moylan, 71 F.3d 1398, 1402 
(8th Cir. 1995) (emphasis added). Therefore, if a civil 
RICO plaintiff sits on her rights or is otherwise less than 
diligent in investigating and bringing her action, the

pursued Vincent Siragusa for the monies rightfully due
