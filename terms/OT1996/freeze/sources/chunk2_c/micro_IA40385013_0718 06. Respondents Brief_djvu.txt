SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 
The first question presented is: 


Whether a civil RICO action accrues prior to the 
conclusion of the bankruptcy proceedings where the 
defendants’ unlawful scheme is based on fraudulently 
obtaining a discharge of a pre-existing debt and where 
plaintiff may suffer no injury until the final discharge in 
bankruptcy. 


This question is moot and, indeed, the entire case is 
moot because Joanne and the trustee have settled with 
Vincent, and one of the terms of settlement is the revoca- 
tion of the discharge in bankruptcy. 


The second question presented is: 


Whether a civil RICO action accrues when the plain- 
tiff knows or should know that she has been injured, even 
though she does not know that her injury was part of a 
pattern of racketeering activity or that defendants caused 
her injury. 

The injury discovery rule affords four years for the 
injured person to discover any pattern that may exist and 
the identity of the defendants. This rule encourages and 
rewards diligence and prevents injustice which can result 
from stale claims, faded memories and lost evidence. 


The pattern discovery rule could result in a 14-year 
statute of limitations. Rodriguez v. Banco Central, 917 F.2d 
664, 667 (ist Cir. 1990) (if the last predicate act of the 
pattern is committed ten years from the date of the first 
predicate act and the four-year statute of limitations 
starts to run at that time). This cannot be reconciled with 
this Court's determination that there was a need for a 


uniform statute of limitations lest the memory of wit- 
nesses fade or evidence be lost. Agency Holding Corp. v. 
Malley-Duff & Associates, Inc., 483 U.S. 143, 156 (1987).

ARGUMENT 
I. 
THE CASE IS MOOT 


It is the duty of all counsel to call to the Court's 
attention facts which might conceivably indicate that the 
case has been rendered moot. This action is necessary so 
that the Court may not unknowingly exercise its author- 
ity in cases in which it no longer has jurisdiction. Board of 
License Commissioners of Tiverton v. Pastore, 469 U.S. 238 
(1985).! 


Respondent Beckley, Singleton, DeLanoy, Jemison & 
List, Chtd. (“Beckley”) suggests that in June 1994 Tom 
Grimmett, as Trustee for the Bankruptcy Estate of Vincent 
Siragusa, and Joanne Siragusa (“Joanne”) settled with 
Vincent Siragusa (“Vincent” or “Dr. Siragusa”). One of 
the terms of this settlement was the revocation of the 
discharge in bankruptcy of Vincent. See, section 7(E)(2) of 
the settlement documents attached to the affidavit of 
George Foley, Jr. App. 21. 


! Similarly, where there is no actual case or controversy, the 
parties may not have sufficient interest in the dispute to raise 
such arguments as would assist the court in resolving the issue 


supposedly presented. 


That this fact renders the action moot is seen from 
two concessions made in Petitioners’ brief. There, Joanne 
first abandons her principal claim for damages, i.e., for 
interference with her lien rights. She now claims only her 
“marital payments.” She then concedes that she has not 
been injured and will not be injured unless Vincent is 
granted a final discharge in bankruptcy. Brief for Peti- 
tioners at 10. 


As the bankruptcy court has revoked Vincent's dis- 
charge, she cannot have been injured. Under Joanne’s 
own analysis, this case is moot. 


A. JOANNE HAS ABANDONED THE CLAIM 
FOR DAMAGES BASED UPON INTERFER- 
ENCE WITH HER LIEN RIGHTS 


Joanne brought a RICO claim for $6 million in current 
damages for impairment of her lien rights. (Joanne’s com- 
plaint, paragraph 101, part B; Joint Appendix at 70.) Liens 
constitute property. See, Matagorda County v. Russell Law, 
19 F.3d 215 (Sth Cir. 1994). Accordingly, she brought a 
RICO claim as a “person injured in his business or prop- 


erty....” 18 U.S.C. § 1964(c). 


Joanne has now abandoned this claim, however. Her 
present claim is only for the marital payments. Joanne 
apparently now recognizes that her claim of $6 million in 
damages to her lien claims was untenable. 


Indeed, basing a RICO claim upon the dispute 
involving this divorcing couple was untenable, as the 
facts reveal. Vincent was a partner in three entities. Heart 
Institute Property (“HIP”) was a partnership which 


owned the medical building in which Vincent and others 
practiced. Vincent had an interest of 27.55%. See, Joint 
Appendix at 27. Heart Institute Cath Lab (“HICL”) was a 
partnership of Drs. Siragusa, Bowers and Heeren in 
which each owned one-third. This partnership owned the 
personal property utilized in the practice of medicine. 
See, Joint Appendix at 26. Heart Institute of Nevada 
(“HIN”) was the vehicle used for the actual practice of 
medicine. See, Joint Appendix at 25. The assets of this 
partnership were the accounts receivable and personal 
good will of each doctor consisting of their individual 
patient following. Each doctor had a one-third interest in 
this partnership. 


Vincent transferred his interest in HIP and HICL to 
Drs. Bowers and Heeren. Joint Appendix at 26, 27. Joanne 
was compensated for her liens by the payment of $1.4 
million by Dr. Bowers. This case focused on HIN. 


Vincent's interest in HIN had two very different 
values. For purposes of divorce in calculating alimony 
and community property values, a doctor’s personal 
good will has a value. Ford v. Ford, 105 Nev. 672, 679, 782 
P.2d 1304, 1309 (Nev. 1989). Vincent’s good will *ould 
have great value because of his great earning capacity. 
See, Joint Appendix at 30. 


However, a doctor’s personal good will is not an 
asset in bankruptcy. The doctor retains his personal good 
will in bankruptcy free and clear of the claims of a 
creditor. In re Cooley, 87 B.R. 432 (Bkrtcy. S.D. Tex. 1988); 
In re Molina Y Vedia, 150 B.R. 393 (S.D. Tex. 1992). For this 


reason the bankruptcy estate was smaller than the com- 


munity property estate. Joanne’s lien on HIN would only 
attach to the accounts receivable. 


Vincent then entered into an employment contract 
with Cardiology Associates of Nevada (“CAN”), a new 
corporation formed by Drs. Bowers and Heeren. 


Joanne, however, was dismayed to see that Vincent 
was earning as much money as an employee of C AN as 
he had earned as a partner in HIN. See, Joint Appendix at 
203. There is no reason why Vincent should not earn the 
same amount. He brought to CAN the same good will, 
services and patient following that he had brought to the 
HIN partnership. These assets were free and clear of the 
claims of the trustee in bankruptcy. Joanne asserted the 
novel theory that, if she had been allowed to foreclose 
against HIN, she could have operated the medical part- 
nership and generated $6 million in profits. Joanne’s com- 
plaint, paragraph 101, part B; Joint Appendix at 70. 


Joanne has now abandoned any such claims.

Because of the settlement agreement, this cannot occur.

the sum of $1,250,000, to be paid over 15 years in increas- 
ing monthly installments. 


By mid-1987, however, Vincent had fallen into 
default on both the alimony and property settlement 
obligations. On November 5, 1987, the Nevada state court

BRIEF ON THE MERITS 


A. THE ACTION ACCRUED MORE THAN FOUR 
YEARS PRIOR TO THE FILING OF THE COM- 
PLAINT 


Analyzing Joanne’s complaint is an arduous task. 
Joanne utilized a pleading style which has been con- 
demned by two circuits. In Hartz v. Friedman, 919 F.2d 
469, 471(7th Cir. 1990), the court indicated that it might 
well have dismissed the plaintiff’s complaint on the 
ground that it was an egregious violation of Rule 8(a) of 
the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure in that the complaint 
contained 125 pages, including a mass of detail which 
might have been relevant at an appropriate trial but 
which was clearly surplusage in stating a claim. The court 
noted that the volume and form of the pleading made it 
difficult to sort out the necessary elements of a RICO 
claim. 


A similar pleading was condemned by the court in 
Pelletier v. Zwiefel, 921 F.2d 1465, 1517-18 (11th Cir. 1991). 
In that case the court concluded that the manner in which 
plaintiff pleaded his case buttressed the court’s conclu- 
sion that the suit was brought purely to harass the defen- 
dants. The court noted that the pleading style required 
the court to sift through the facts presented and decide 
for themselves which were material to the particular 
cause of action, a difficult and laborious task. On page 
1522 the court concluded that the plaintiff had utilized 


the complaint to “shake down his opponent” with the 
expenses of defending a RICO charge to extort a settle- 
ment. 


After reviewing Joanne’s complaint, the court made 
two conclusions: 


First, Joanne knew of her injury in May 1989 when 


she filed her adversary complaint in the bankruptcy 


court. 


Second, four years before the filing of the present 
action in November 1993, the pattern existed although 
Joanne did not know of the existence of the pattern until 
December 1990. 


The pattern existed more than four years prior to the 
filing of the complaint in November 1993. This fact is 
shown by Joanne’s complaint at 180-81 in the Joint 
Appendix. There, Joanne alleges that Pat Brown utilized 
telephone and mail service to accomplish the fraud 
involved in the Heeren stock sale over the period May 29, 
1987 to October 27, 1989. 


In the Joint Appendix at 180 Joanne asserts that Pat 
Brown utilized the mail services as described in Exhibit 2 
which asserts that mail fraud was utilized from June 29, 
1987 to March 3, 1988. Joint Appendix at 159. Also, on 
page 180 of the Joint Appendix, Joanne relies upon inter- 
state telephone calls described in Exhibit 3. These calls 
were described in Joint Appendix at 160 and occurred at 
various times in 1987 and 1988. 


10 


The latest time referred to in the complaint regarding 
the Heeren stock sale fraud was October 27, 1989. Joint 
Appendix at 182. Thus, it appears that the requisite pat- 
tern existed no later than October 27, 1987 and that more 
than four years elapsed before the filing of the complaint 
in November 1993. 


Petitioners and amici assert that the Ninth Circuit 
rule is illogical because the statute of limitations can run 
before the cause of action accrues. This is not the Ninth 
Circuit rule. The court made this clear in Grimmett v. 
Brown, 75 F.3d 506, 512 (9th Cir. 1996). There the court 
stated that a RICO cause of action cannot accrue until all 
the elements exist and the statute of limitations cannot 
begin to tick until a pattern exists. The pattern consisted 
of many predicate acts involving the claimed bankruptcy 
fraud and the claimed Heeren stock sale, which existed 
over the period of 1987 through 1989. 


B. THE “INJURY DISCOVERY” RULE BETTER 
SERVES THE INTERESTS BEHIND BOTH 
THE RICO ACT AND STATUTES OF LIMITA- 
TIONS 


The injury discovery rule is consistent with the pur- 
pose behind statutes of limitation. Statutes of limitations 
promote justice by prompting litigants to be diligent. As 
claims grow old, evidence is lost, witnesses disappear, 
and recollections fade. Order of Railroad Telegraphers v. 
Railway Express Agency, Inc., 321 U.S. 342, 348-49 (1944). 
The court’s own fact-finding process is hobbled. Defen- 
dants face revived claims from which exculpatory 


’ 


11 


evidence is more difficult to accumulate. Thus, a statute 
of limitations promotes a common moral sense that valid 
claims should be brought quickly. Establishing a time bar 
for stale claims reduces the risk of false claims, which 
increase in number as the facts at issue become more 
remote. Riddlesbarger v. Hartford Ins. Co., 74 U.S. 386, 390 
(1886). Outdated cases also place a heavy burden on the 
court system. Burnett v. New York Cent. R.R., 380 U.S. 424, 
428 (1965). Simply put, a statute of limitations limits the 
time period in which a plaintiff may bring his cause of 
action. Thus, statutes of limitation prompt plaintiffs to be 
diligent in bringing their claims. 


Requiring plaintiffs to be diligent in discovering the 
pattern will also serve the purpose of RICO. The 
“declared purpose” of Congress in enacting the RICO 
statute was “to seek the eradication of organized crime in 
the United States.” United States v. Turkette, 452 U.S. 576, 
589 (1981).4

growing problems caused by organized crime, Congress 
enacted the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations 
Act (the “Act”). See 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961-1968 (1994). 


The civil RICO section of the Act had three main purposes: 
(1) to afford victims of organized crime some opportunity at 
compensation for their injuries (2) to deter future organized 


12 


The crime organizations always have pattern and 
continuity. They are engaged in the business of crime. 
This “comes almost by definition.” Hindes v. Castle, 937 
F.2d 868, 874 (3d Cir. 1991). The pattern is there, the 
plaintiff must find the proof. Congress has commissioned 
the injured plaintiff as a private attorney general or, more 
accurately, as a bounty hunter to bring civil actions to 
eradicate organized crime. The bounty offered is not a 
gift. It must be earned. 


Those injured by organized crime are encouraged to 
investigate and discover RICO violations. An injured 
plaintiff who is diligent will file a common law tort action 
to vindicate his injury. 


If the injury was committed by an organized crime 
family and the plaintiff fails to discover the pattern 
within the four years allowed, the plaintiff has simply 
failed to earn the bounty. If the injury was committed by 
an ordinary criminal who had not yet attained the status 
of a statutory racketeer under RICO, then the statute of 
limitations may bar a RICO action. However, no violence 
is done to the Congressional plan. The ordinary criminal 
was not the target of the Act and is only an incidental 
player. See, the legislative history reported by Justice 


crimes and (3) to undermine the social and economic power of 
organized crime. See Organized Crime Control: Hearing before 
Subcomm. No. 5 of the Comm. on the Judiciary, House of 
Representatives, 91st Cong., 2nd Sess. 520 (1970). To accomplish 
these objectives, Congress equipped the Act with a treble 
damages remedy, forfeiture of assets for criminal violations of 
RICO provisions, nationwide service of process, expedition of 
actions, and a civil investigative demand. See 18 U.S.C. §§ 1963, 
1964(c), 1965(b)-(d), 1966, 1968 (1994).

Powell in his dissent in Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 
U.S. 479, 525 (1985). The ordinary criminal will still be 
liable for the common law tort for which punitive dam- 
ages may be assessed. When the ordinary criminal com- 
mits enough predicate acts to become a statutory 
racketeer, the person then injured by his action may rely 
upon the past predicate acts to obtain a RICO recovery. 


The adoption of the injury and pattern discovery 
rule, in contrast, would allow a plaintiff, even though he 
is aware of the injury and the predicate act which caused 
it, to slumber on his rights without investigating whether 
the defendant has committed a pattern of racketeering 
activity. Such an interpretation does not serve the pur- 
poses of the RICO Act, and it makes for an inappropriate 
statute of limitations.® In Rodriguez v. Banco Central, 917 
F.2d 664, 667 (Ist Cir. 1990), the court noted that the 
pattern discovery rule could result in a 14-year statute of 
limitations and observed that such a rule would be diffi- 
cult to reconcile with this Court’s determination that 
there was a need for a uniform statute of limitations for 
civil RICO lest the memories of witnesses fade or evi- 
dence be lost. Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff Associ- 
ates, Inc., 483 U.S. at 156, quoting Wilson v. Garcia, 471 
U.S. 261, 271 (1985). 


* This analysis demonstrates petitioners’ misplaced 
reliance on United States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111 (1979). There, 
the Court held that a Federal Tort Claim Act cause of action 
accrues “when the plaintiff knows both the existence and the 
cause of his injury.” That is the situation here. By May 1989, 
Siragusa knew of both her injury and the predicate act which 
caused the injury.

Adoption of the injury discovery rule will also assist 
in preventing the civil RICO action from being used 
outside its intended purposes. It is an unfortunate reality 
that organized crime accounts for only 9% of RICO cases. 
See Sedima, supra, 473 U.’». at 506. The Act’s incentives - 
treble damages, attorney's fees, nationwide service of 
process, expedition of actions and a civil investigative 
demand® - make it alluring to any plaintiff. RICO charges 
are also often brought to harass defendants and extort a 
settlement from them. Pelletier v. Zwiefel, supra, 921 F.2d at 
1517. This Court also has noted the potential for extor- 
tion. Sedima, supra, at 473 U.S. 506.

SUPPORTING POINTS AND AUTHORITIES 


The trustee and Joanne M. Siragusa have filed the 
above captioned adversary proceedings herein against 
the Debtor Defendants and various others (the “remain- 
ing Defendants”) based on various claims for relief as set 
forth more fully in the pleadings filed therein. Joanne M. 
Siragusa also has various other marital claims against the 


App. 7 


debtor which are the subject of proceedings in various 
other courts. 


After first giving due consideration to the respective 
positions of the parties, the uncertainties and expenses of 
the Adversary Proceedings, the benefits of this Agree- 
ment to the parties, and, in the case of the trustee and 
Joanne M. Siragusa, the potential ability to obtain the rest 
of the damages to which the trustee and Joanne Siragusa 
believe they are properly entitled for the actions which 
are the subject of the Adversary Proceedings from the 
Remaining Defendants and others, the trustee and Joanne 
M. Siragusa have agreed to compromise and settle their 
respective claims with the Debtor Defendants only on the 
terms set forth in the Settlement Agreement and Mutual 
Release attached as Exhibit “A”, subject to the approval 
and determinations by this court set forth in Section 8 
thereof. The basic monetary terms of this settlement and 
compromise are that the Debtor Defendants will (i) pay 
the trustee and Joanne M. Siragusa $200,000 in cash, 
though the full Agreement should be consulted for the 
precise and complete terms. 


The trustee and Joanne M. Siragusa believe that this 
compromise and settlement are in the best interests of 
this estate and that this court should grant: 


1. The requested approval pursuant to Bankruptcy 
court rule 9019 based upon the overall facts and circum- 
stances of this case with which this court is familiar. 


2. The requested “good faith” determination that: 


A. The Releases do not discharge or in any 
manner impair the Plaintiffs’ claims for relief in the 


App. 8 


Adversary Proceedings, or any other claims, against the 
Remaining Defendants. 


B. The Releases discharge the Settling Defen- 
dants from contribution to any of the Remaining Defen- 
dants, and the Dismissed Defendants, based upon the 
following authorities: 


C. NRS 17.245 and Velsicol Chemical Corp., v. 
Davidson, 811 P2d 561 (Nev, 1991) broadly construing the 
trial court’s discretion to make a good faith determination 
under that statute. 


D. Avery v. US, 829 F2d 817 (9th Cir, 1987) 
which holds that release of one joint tortfeasor does not 
release the others absent an agreement to that effect. 


E. First Fed S&L v. Oppenheim, et al, 631 F Supp 
1029 (SDNY, 1986) whose rationale would make Nevada 
the proper state contribution law to apply. 


F. Wieboldt Stores, Inc. v. Schottenstein, 111 BR 
162 (ND IIl, 1990) which holds that there are no express 
or implied rights to contribution in bankruptcy fraudu- 
lent conveyance actions.
