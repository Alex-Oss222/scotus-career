SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Two polar accrual methods have been set before this 
Court in the context of a civil RICO action. The majority 
rule, espoused by the Ninth Circuit, most clearly accords 
with fundamental principles of fairness and equity - 
while ensuring the remedial purposes of RICO are met. It 
requires a plaintiff to timely prosecute for damages based 
on discovery of injury without requiring concurent 
knowledge of a pattern. In this case, that accrual point 
occurred, at the latest, over four years before the instant 
complaint was filed. 


Even under the minority rule, Petitioners knew, or 
should have known, of the existence of a pattern of 
alleged racketeering activity designed to injure them by 
May of 1989 — over four years before this complaint was 
filed. Thus, under either approach, the claims are time- 
barred and were properly dismissed. 


Petitioners not only had knowledge of the proscribed 
alleged behavior and the associated pattern, they also 
knew of the injury by May of 1989. The property settle- 
ment was discharged the year before due to behavior the 
May 1989 adversary proceeding monickered as fraudu- 
lent. It was a sum certain. Petitioner sought compensa- 
tory and punitive damages in that 1989 proceeding. 
Therefore, any damages were not speculative in nature so 
as to warrant any delay in the accrual of the RICO claim. 


With definite damages by May of 1989, under either 
accrual approach, the matter was time-barred when this 
complaint was filed over four years after the May 1989 
adversary proceeding was filed. Accordingly, the Ninth 
Circuit’s decision must be upheld. 


ARGUMENT 


I. PETITIONERS’ 1994 LITIGATION WAS PROPERLY 
BARRED BY THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS 


It is not disputed this Honorable Court, in Agency 
Holding Corp. v. Maley-Duff & Assoc., Inc., 483 U.S. 143 
(1987), held a four-year statute of limitations applied to 
litigation promised upon the Racketeering and Corrupt 
Influences Organization Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961-68. The 
only question before this Court - in the present context - 
is whether knowledge of a pattern is required before the 
cause of action can properly accrue. It is respectfully 
submitted, in this case, Petitioners’ cause of action would 
have been barred under the utilization of either method 
of calculating when a RICO violation accrues. 


A. GENERAL STANDARDS REGARDING STAT- 
UTES OF LIMITATIONS 


Statutes of limitations “have long been respected as 
fundamental to a well-ordered judicial system.” Board of 
Regents v. Tomanio, 446 U.S. 478 (1980). 


Statutes of limitations, which are “found and 
approved in all systems of enlightened jurispru- 
dence,” [] represent a pervasive legislative 
judgment that it is unjust to fail to put the 
adversary on notice to defend within a specified 


period of time and that “the right to be free of 
stale claims in time comes to prevail over the 
right to prosecute them.” 


United States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111, 117 (1979) (citations 
omitted). Indeed, statutes of limitations “have been part 
of the law of every civilized nation from time imme- 
morial.” Hargraves v. Brackett Stripping Machine Co., 317 F. 
Supp. 676, 682 (E.D. Tenn. 1970). 


[NJo class of laws is more universally sanc- 
tioned by the practice of nations, and the con- 
sent of mankind. . . . Such laws have frequently 
passed in review before this court; and occa- 
sions have occurred in which they have been 
particularly noticed as laws not to be impeached 
on the grounds of violating private right. What 
right has anyone to complain, when a reason- 
able time has been given him, if he has not been 
vigilant in asserting his rights? All the reason- 
able purposes of justice are subserved, if the 
courts of the state have been left open to the 
prosecution of suits for such a time as may 
reasonably raise a presumption in the occupyer 
[sic] of the soil that the fruits of his labor are 
effectually secured beyond the chance of litigation. 


It is not to be questioned, that laws limiting the 
time of bringing suit, constitute a part of the lex 
fori of every country: they are laws for adminis- 
tering justice; one of the most sacred and impor- 
tant of sovereign rights and duties: and the 
restriction which must materially affect both 
legislative and judicial independence. 


Hawkins v. Joshua Barney's Lessee, 5 Pet. (30 U.S.) 457, 466 
(1831). Therefore, if an injured party fails to assert a claim 


10 


in a timely manner, the cause of action, irrespective of 
merit, will be forfeited forever. Pangrazzi v. United States, 
511 F. Supp. 648, 649 (E.D. Pa. 1981). The question of 
whether a statute of limitations applies is a matter of law 
and may be decided pursuant to a Fed. R. Civ. Pro. 
12(b)(6) motion. Jablon v. Dean Witter & Co., 614 F.2d 677, 
682 (9th Cir. 1980). 


The proper focus is on the time of the discriminatory 
or injurious acts, not the time when the consequence of the 
acts becomes most painful. Delaware State College v. Ricks, 
449 U.S. 250, 258 (1980). Each injurious act begins the 
running of the statute of limitations at the time of its 
occurrence, as opposed to when the plaintiff becomes 
aware of her or his legal rights regarding the same. 
Kubrick, 444 U.S. at 122. It is respectfully submitted the 
policy followed by the majority of federal circuits, includ- 
ing the Ninth Circuit, of holding a cause of action for 
RICO accrues at the time the injured party knows of or 
reasonably should have discovered the injury most 
closely comports with these concerns. Since Petitioners 
knew of the “injury” by May of 1989, at the latest, this 
action was time-barred when filed in November of 1993. 


B. A CIVIL RICO ACTION ACCRUES WHEN A 
PARTY KNOWS OR SHOULD HAVE KNOWN 
OF THE INJURY 


The elements of a civil RICO claim are simple 
enough. They require an individual be injured through 
the conduct of an enterprise by a pattern of racketeering 
activity (known as “predicate acts”) causing injury to 
business or property. Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 U.S. 
479, 496 (1985); 18 U.S.C. §§ 1962(c) & 1964(c). In analyzing 


11 


the RICO statute of limitations question, this Honorable 
Court specifically referenced anti-trust law and the Clay- 
ton Act to guide its decision-making process. Agency 
Holding, 483 U.S. at 151; see also 113 Cong. Rec. 18,007 
(1967) (introducing the forerunner to RICO as an addition 
to the Sherman Act instead of an independent act). This 
Court also noted RICO bears a similarity to 42 U.S.C. 
§ 1983 — the civil rights statute. 483 U.S. at 149. It follows 
reason, therefore, to apply an accrual method similar to 
that utilized under these Acts in determining when a 
Civil RICO cause of action accrues. 


Utilizing these guidelines, the accrual method 
employed by the Ninth Circuit most clearly conforms 
with the accrual methods utilized by these other acts. In a 
pre-Agency Holding case, the Ninth Circuit Court of 
Appeals first addressed the issue of when, under RICO, a 
cause of action accrued. In Compton v. Ide, 732 F.2d 1429 
(9th Cir. 1984), the Circuit held the mere existence of a 
conspiracy did not generally postpone accrual of causes 
of action arising from the conspirator’s separate wrongs; 
hence, it was “the wrongful act, not the conspiracy, which 
[was] actionable in a civil case.” 732 F.2d at 1433. Noting 
RICO did not provide a civil statute of limitations, the 
Court adopted the general federal rule that a statute of 
limitations begins to run when the plaintiff knew or had 
reason to know of the injury which was the basis for the 
action. Id. 


We see no reason here to depart from the gen- 
eral rule. Although RICO does not specifically 
outlaw conspiracies to violate the substantive 
provisions of the statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1962(d), 
RICO’s civil remedy section permits suit by per- 
sons injured in their business or property, 18 


12 


U.S.C. § 1964(c). The civil remedy provision’s 
focus upon injury as opposed to existence of a 
conspiracy suggested the normal federal rule on 
accrual should apply to civil RICO actions alleg- 
ing conspiracy. 


Id. Accordingly, it held the RICO claim was barred since 
it was based on the same facts as a § 1983 conspiracy 
claim which was also time-barred. 


Since Agency Holding, the Ninth Circuit has continued 
to maintain that position on accrual. In State Farm Mutual 
Automobile Ins. Co. v. Ammann, 828 F.2d 4 (9th Cir. 1987), 
the Circuit re-affirmed its position regarding when a 
cause of action under RICO accrued. Then-Circuit Judge 
Kennedy concurred in the decision — noting the accrual 
rule utilized was the same found in § 1983 and anti-trust 
actions. 828 F.2d at 5. He further stated that position 
allowed a new cause of action to be brought when a 
separate and distinct injury was incurred as the result of 
additional acts specifically proscribed in the RICO provi- 
sions. Id.% 


Other Circuits have allied themselves with the posi- 
tion correctly articulated by the Ninth Circuit. Each has 
predicated its analysis on § 1983 or anti-trust law. For 
example, in Rodriguez v. Banco Central, 917 F.2d 664 (lst 
Cir. 1990), then-Chief Judge Breyer offered an opinion 
setting forth the position of the First Circuit on when a 
cause of action under RICO accrued. Justice Breyer 
claimed to start “[t}he statute running when a plaintiff
