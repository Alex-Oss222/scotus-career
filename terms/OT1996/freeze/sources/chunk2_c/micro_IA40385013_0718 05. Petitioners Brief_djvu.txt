QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether a civil RICO action accrues prior to the 
conclusion of the bankruptcy proceedings where the 
defendants’ unlawful scheme is based on fraudulently 
obtaining a discharge of a pre-existing debt and where 
plaintiff may suffer no injury until the final discharge in 
bankruptcy. 


2. Whether a civil RICO action accrues when the 
plaintiff knows or should know that she has been injured, 
even though she does not know that her injury was part 
of a pattern of racketeering activity or that defendants 
caused her injury.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case involves an unlawful scheme orchestrated 


by attorney Patricia Brown and doctors Vincent Siragusa 


and John Bowers to defraud plaintiff Joanne Siragusa of 
the multi-million dollar divorce settlement debt which 
was owed her by Dr. Vincent Siragusa and_ partially 


2 


secured by a lien on his medical practice. Defendants also 
engaged in a scheme to defraud Dr. Paul Heeren of his 
interest in the medical practice, which was the largest 
cardiology medical practice in the State of Nevada. As a 
consequence of defendants’ unlawful and fraudulent 
activities, Dr. Bowers became the sole owner of the medi- 
cal practice and, with the active participation of Brown, 
engaged in further fraudulent transactions relating to the 
sale of a minority interest in the practice to a number of 
other physicians. In this RICO action, Joanne Siragusa 
seeks to recover for injuries that she has suffered as a 
result of Patricia Brown’s wrongful conduct.

Brown and her law firm (“Beckley”) moved to dis- 
miss the RICO suit as barred by the four-year statute of 
limitations. The defendants claimed that Joanne and 
Grimmett knew of their injury in May 1989 when they 
filed the adversary complaint in bankruptcy court. Brown 
and Beckley argued that the district court should invoke 
an “injury discovery” rule, under which a civil RICO 
cause of action accrues once the plaintiff reasonably dis- 
covers her injury. Defendants asserted that, because the 
civil RICO action was filed more than four years after 
Joanne and Grimmett knew of the injury, it was barred 
under RICO’s four-year statute of limitations period. 


In response, Joanne and Grimmett argued that 
although they were aware of Joanne’s injury by May 
1989, they were not aware that there were other victims 
of the scheme and that their injury was part of a pattern 
of racketeering activity. They also were unaware of Patri-

this wrongdoing when they filed their adversary pro- 
ceeding in bankruptcy. Petitioners argued that a civil 
RICO action does not accrue until the plaintiff discovers 
that her injury was part of a pattern of racketeering 
activity and defendants’ involvement in the RICO 
scheme. Under the “injury and pattern discovery” accrual 
rule followed by several circuits, petitioners’ RICO claims 
did not accrue until December 1990, when Dr. Heeren 
revealed the scope of the fraudulent scheme and the fact 
that he too was victimized by it. Until that time peti- 
tioners had no basis for alleging a pattern of racketeering 
activity or for charging Brown and her law firm with any 
wrongdoing. 

Joanne and Grimmett also argued that the accrual of 
the statute of limitations period should not run during 
the course of the bankruptcy proceedings. Petitioners 
relied upon a line of cases beginning with Bankers Trust 
Co. v. Rhoades, 859 F.2d 1096 (2d. Cir. 1988), cert. denied, 
490 U.S. 1007 (1989), to support their argument. In closely 
comp arable circumstances involving bankruptcy proceed- 
ings, the Bankers Trust court held that a RICO action does 
not accrue before the existence and amount of any injury 
becomes clear. Because petitioners might suffer no injury 
depending on the outcome of the bankruptcy proceed- 
ings, Bankers Trust and subsequent cases held that the 
time for bringing a civil RICO action should not begin to 
run until after the bankruptcy. In addition, because 
Brown actively concealed her wrongdoing from peti- 
tioners, the doctrine of fraudulent concealment should 
also toll the running of the limitations period.

1. A RICO cause of action cannot exist without an 
“injury to business or property.” Sedima, S.P.L.R. v. Imrex 
Co., 473 U.S. 479, 496 (1985). Here, it cannot be deter- 
mined whether Joanne Siragusa will suffer such an injury 
until the conclusion of the bankruptcy proceedings. Vin- 
cent’s property settlement debt to Joanne predated the 
RICO activity of the defendants. The RICO scheme, 
including the fraudulent reorganization of the medical 
practice and false bankruptcy filings, was aimed at

achieving a final discharge of Vincent's debt in the bank- 
ruptcy proceedings. The bankruptcy proceedings, how- 
ever, have not yet concluded, as the bankruptcy trustee is 
still continuing to pursue assets of the estate. Only if 
Joanne is ultimately compelled to accept less than 100% 
of her pre-existing debts at the conclusion of the bank-

property. 

Although defendants have engaged in a pattern of

or until a discharge is granted and Joanne receives less

cause of action has not yet accrued. Zenith Radio Corp. v. 
Hazeltine Research, Inc., 401 U.S. 321 (1971). The Second 
Circuit has applied this principle to a defendant's attempt 
to obtain a fraudulent discharge of a debt in bankruptcy, 
holding that the RICO injury is incurred only when the 
trustee is unsuccessful in recovering the assets necessary 
to pay off the pre-existing debt. Bankers Trust Co. v. 
Rhoades, 859 F.2d 1096 (2d Cir. 1988), cert. denied, 490 U.S. 
1007 (1989). The statutory provision governing bank- 
ruptcy fraud also provides that the statute of limitations 
does not begin to run until final discharge of the debtor. 
18 U.S.C. § 3284. 


Vincent's pre-existing debt to Joanne is unaffected by 
the attempted fraudulent transfers and false bankruptcy 
filings. At most, these events reveal an attempt by the 
defendants to injure Joanne’s security interest that pro- 
tected the pre-existing property settlement debt. Until the 
debt is “lost” and the security interest is called upon and 
found insufficient, Joanne has suffered no RICO injury.

At the time that Joanne filed her adversary complaint

2. Although RICO does not contain a specific provi- 
sion directly addressing accrual, RICO’s language, struc- 
ture and policies demonstrate that the statute of 
limitations should not begin to run until the plaintiff 
reasonably knows both her injury and that her injury was 
the result of a pattern of racketeering. “[T]he heart of any 
RICO complaint is the allegation of a pattern of racketeer- 
ing.” Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff & Associates, 
Inc., 483 U.S. 143, 154 (1987) (emphasis in original). In 
defining a “pattern of racketeering activity” as at least 
two predicate acts “the last of which occurred within ten 
years .. . after the commission of a prior act,” 18 U.S.C. 
§ 1961(5), the RICO statute plainly anticipated that proof 
of the RICO scheme would involve an examination of 


12 


defendant's activities up to ten years in the past. In 
addition, the predicate acts identified in RICO include 
numerous fraud-related violations. 18 U.S.C. § 1961(1). 
As a result, the potential plaintiff will face significant 
obstacles in discovering a RICO cause of action because 
the RICO scheme will likely involve uncovering multiple 
frauds operating through legitimate businesses and 
unions. The “injury and pattern discovery” rule is supe- 
rior in that it recognizes the inherent problems that con- 
front victims of fraud schemes in developing a RICO 
claim and ties the accrual of the civil RICO action to 
discovery of the RICO pattern. Furthermore, the “injury 
and pattern discovery” rule is consistent with the stat- 
ute’s mandate that RICO’s provisions “be liberally con- 
strued to effectuate its remedial purpose.” RICO, ch. 96, 
§ 904(a), 84 Stat. 947 (1970). 


The “injury and pattern discovery” rule also comports 
with general federal law principles of accrual. In United 
States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111 (1979), the Court held that the 
limitations period would begin to run only after the plaintiff 
reasonably discovered the “factual predicate” underlying a 
claim, including the “critical facts” of the injury and who 
“inflicted the injury.” Here, Joanne Siragusa did not know 
the “factual predicate” of her claim or that Patricia Brown 
had knowingly participated in these wrongful acts until Dr. 
Heeren came forward in December 1990 to describe the 
pattern of racketeering conduct. In federal fraud cases, courts 
also have required that plaintiff discover the fraud, and not 
just the injury, before the limitations period will begin to run. 
In addition, the Court's treatment of “continuing violations” 
postpones the commencement of the limitations period for 
older unlawful acts where some of the improper conduct is 
within the requisite limitations period. See Havens Realty 
Corp. v. Coleman, 455 U.S. 363 (1982) (“a ‘continuing violation’ 
of the Fair Housing Act should be treated differently from 


13 


one discrete act of discrimination,” rejecting defendant's 
“wooden application” of the statute of limitations). Because 
RICO is plainly a continuing violation statute, see H.J. Inc. v. 
Northwestern Bell Telephone Co., 492 U.S 229 (1989), the 
broader “injury and pattern discovery” accrual rule is appro- 
priate. 

The “injury and pattern discovery” accrual also is neces- 
sary to avoid anomalous and indefensible results. The most 
astounding outcome under the “injury discovery” accrual 
rule adopted by the court of appeals is that, where the two 
predicate acts are more than four years apart, a plaintiff's 
RICO action can be barred by the statute of limitations before 
her RICO cause of action ever existed. In fact, the “injury 
discovery” rule would enforce a de facto shortening of the 
statute of limitations in virtually every case, since the poten- 
tial plaintiff would “lose” the time until the second predicate 
act takes place. In addition, the victim of a “closed-ended 
scheme” would suffer a constricted limitations period since 
courts have generally required at least one year of racketeer- 
ing activity in such circumstances. See Religious Technology 
Center v. Wollersheim, 971 F.2d 364, 366-67 (9th Cir. 1992). The 
RICO plaintiff has a difficult burden, often having to dis- 
cover a cause of action that does not exist until years after 
the plaintiff's injury and having to discover wrongs perpe- 
trated against other victims. The “injury and pattern discov- 
ery” accrual rule takes this into account and allows the 
injured party sufficient time to investigate, discover, and 
prepare her RICO claim.

A. Plaintiff Must Suffer Concrete Injury To Business 
Or Property To Bring A Civil RICO Action 


A RICO cause of action cannot exist without an injury to 
the plaintiff. This Court has made clear that, for purposes of 
civil RICO, a plaintiff only has standing to bring a claim once 
she “has been injured in [her] business or property by the 
conduct constituting the violation.” Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex 
Co., 473 U.S. 479, 496 (1985). This “injury to business or 
property” requirement has been characterized by the courts 
as a need to show a “concrete financial loss.” See, ¢.g., 
Imagineering, Inc. v. Kiewit Pacific Co., 976 F.2d 1303, 1310 (9th 
Cir. 1992) (citing Fleischhauer v. Feltner, 879 F.2d 1290, 
1299-1301 (6th Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 493 U.S. 1074 (1990)), 
cert. denied, 507 U.S. 1004 (1993). In the absence of such a loss, 
a RICO claim is not ripe for adjudication. 

The concrete RICO injury has not yet occurred in the 
instant case. As a result of her divorce proceedings, 
Joanne gave up her community property interest in Vincent's 
medical practice in return for regular property settlement 
payments. In addition, she was to receive alimony payments 
from Vincent. These debts predated the RICO activity of the 
defendants, who embarked on an illegal plan to defeat Vin- 
cent’s legal debts to Joanne without losing control of the 
valuable assets owned by Vincent. The fraudulent reorgani- 
zation of the medical practice and the fraudulent bankruptcy 
filings were the predicate acts designed to achieve the ulti- 
mate financial goal: the legal discharge of Vincent's debts 
without a diminution or loss of his substantial financial 
assets, i.e., the medical practice. The scheme also aimed to

by fraudulently defeating the ownership rights of Dr. 
Heeren. 

Only if Joanne is compelled to accept less than 100% of

suffered an “injury to business or property.” Yet, since the 
bankruptcy proceedings are not finally concludsi, and since 
the bankruptcy trustee is still pursuing assets of the estate, it 
is as yet uncertain whether Joanne will be forced to accept 
less than 100% of her pre-existing property settlement debt. 
There still may be substantial recovery in bankruptcy of the 
fraudulently transferred assets which will then be used to 
repay creditors, including Joanne.

The court of appeals identified Joanne’s RICO injury

interest.” (Pet. App. 24a) The appeals court apparently 
refers to that aspect of the bankruptcy fraud by which the 
defendants attempted to hide Dr. Siragusa’s assets by 
fraudulently transferring them to third parties. This char- 
acterization of Joanne’s injury is inapt, however, because 
Joanne’s underlying debt, based on the property settle- 
ment with Vincent and subsequent state court judgment, 
is unaffected by the attempted fraudulent transfers. 


Vincent’s fraudulent bankruptcy petition, purpor- 
tedly indicating that ownership of his medical practice 
was transferred to others, does not in and of itself consti- 
tute an injury to Joanne. At most, these events reveal an 
attempt by the defendants to injure Joanne’s security

interest that protected the pre-existing property settle- 
ment debt. Joanne released her one-half community prop- 
erty interest in Vincent’s medical practice in return for 
periodic payments that were themselves protected by a 
security interest in Vincent’s medical practice. But an 
attempt, even a successful attempt, to subvert an under- 
lying security interest or insurance policy is not itself a 
RICO injury unless and until the debt that is being 
secured is “lost.” 


The First Circuit twice has looked at this situation 
and, each time, declined to find a RICO injury, citing 
Bankers Trust with approval in each case. In Lincoln House, 
Inc. v. Dupre, 903 F.2d 845 (1st Cir. 1990), the plaintiff had 
sued the defendant in state court for breach of contract 
damages. The plaintiff subsequently filed a civil RICO 
case alleging that the defendant was fraudulently trans- 
ferring his property in an effort to become judgment- 
proof should there be damages in the state court proceed- 
ing. The court of appeals found that the plaintiff’s RICO 
claim was not ripe for adjudication because the plaintiff's 
alleged RICO injury “is clearly contingent on events that 
may not occur as anticipated or may not occur at all.” Id. 
at 847. Since the state court action had not been decided, 
and since no potential debt had been “lost” as yet, the 
“hypothetical inability to recover” was not the type o/ 
injury actionable under RICO. 


The same result ended the plaintiff's attempt to pur- 
sue a civil RICO claim in Bennett v. Centerpoint Bank, No. 
91-1603, 1991 U.S. App. LEXIS 32856 (1st Cir. Dec. 31, 
1991). The plaintiff was the chairman of the board of a 
failed banking venture, and the defendants were, among 
others, his former board members. The plaintiff and the 
defendants had given personal guarantees for a corporate 
loan used to get the banking venture underway. The 


21 


plaintiff alleged, in essence, that the defendants fraudu- 
lently used corporate assets to enrich themselves person- 
ally and to obtain releases from their personal guarantees 
on the corporate loan. Id. at *7. His alleged RICO injury 
was exposure to suit for the banking venture’s unpaid 
debt without the financial support of his co-guarantors of 
the corporate loan. Finding that “exposure does not equal 
injury,” the First Circuit ruled that the injury was insuffi- 
cient to maintain a civil RICO action. Id. at *17. The 
plaintiff had not yet been forced to cover any of the 
banking venture’s debt, and even more to the point there 
was no evidence that he would have to pay more than he 
would have paid “if the defendants had never committed 
a predicate act. The injury, therefore, is speculative and 
the suit, at the very least, premature.” Id. at *8 (citing 
Bankers Trust, supra). 


Even the fraudulent reduction of the value of the 
collateral on non-recourse mortgage loans has been held 
not itself to result in an actionable RICO injury where the 
original mortgage debt was not yet “lost.” In First Nation- 
wide Bank v. Gelt Funding Corp., 27 F.3d 763 (2d Cir. 1994), 
cert. denied, ___ U.S. ___, 115 S. Ct. 728 (1995), the plaintiff 
lender alleged that the defendant mortgage broker fraud- 
ulently inflated the value of the properties pledged as 
collateral to secure non-recourse mortgages on those 
properties. The lender maintained that it had been 
injured by this scheme because the loans would not have 
been made but for the fraudulent inflation of value. The 
resulting loans were, as a consequence, undersecured, 
giving the lender an additional risk of loss. Id. at 768. The 
court found, however, that the lender had as yet suffered 
no RICO injury. It was insufficient for a civil RICO cause 
of action for the lender to claim that “it incurred addi- 
tional risk of loss as a consequence of the fraud.” Id. 


22 


Whether the lender suffered RICO injury could be deter- 
mined only once there was a foreclosure on one of the 
affected properties and the collateral proved insufficient 
to pay the debt. Id. at 769. 


Notably, the Ninth Circuit itself has recognized that a 
fraudulent attack on the underlying insurance against 
loss does not, in and of itself, constitute an actionable 
RICO injury. The defendant in Berg v. First State Insurance, 
915 F.2d 460 (9th Cir. 1990), canceled without notice the 
liability policies of six Getty Oil directors. The cancella- 
tion took place when the defendant learned that the 
directors were potentially liable for billions of dollars in 
threatened shareholder derivative suits as a result of the 
Texaco / Pennzoil battle for control of Getty Oil. Id. at 462. 
The directors brought a civil RICO action against First 
State. Subsequently, Texaco paid all of the expenses of the 
directors in the derivative lawsuits, and First State sought 
dismissal of the RICO case on the grounds that the plain- 
tiff had suffered no injury. The Ninth Circuit agreed, 
finding that the directors had “suffered no financial loss.” 
Id. at 464. Though the insurance polices may have been 
fraudulently canceled, reasoned the court of appeals, the 
plaintiffs suffered no loss since their “claims” had been 
covered by other means.

Indeed, there is no saying that Joanne will receive one

The drafters of RICO included a liberal construction

“simply unknown to common law.” Agency Holding Corp.,

presence of these unique elements. The “injury and pat-

central role played by the RICO pattern requirement.

B. General Principles Of Accrual Compel The 
Adoption Of The “Injury And Pattern Discov- 
ery” Rule 


Proponents of the “injury discovery” rule fundamer- 
tally misconstrue federal accrual principles. The case law 
governing general principles of accrual plainly confirms 
the appropriateness of an “injury and pattern” discovery 
rule for civil RICO actions. 


A leading decision of this Court on statute of limita- 
tions accrual is United States v. Kubrick, 444 US. 111 
(1979). In Kubrick, the plaintiff filed suit under the Federal 
Tort Claims Act for hearing loss suffered as a result of his 
medical treatment at a Veterans Administration hospital. 
Although aware that he had suffered hearing loss due to 
his hospital treatment in 1969, Kubrick did not realize 
that he could bring a legal action against the United 
States for negligent treatment until 1972. When Kubrick 
filed suit in 1972, the United States moved to dismiss or 
statute of limitations grounds. The United States argued 
that the two-year limitations period had accrued in 1969 
when Kubrick learned of the relevant facts relating to his 
claim. Kubrick opposed this motion, contending that 
accrual should not occur until he discovered the legal 
significance of these underlying facts. 


The Supreme Court rejected Kubrick’s argument that 
the statute of limitations should not begin to run until a 
plaintiff discovers a legal theory for recovery. In finding 
for the United States, however, the Court did not adopt 
an “injury discovery” rule. Instead, the Court held that a 
claim accrues “when the piaintiff knows both the exis- 
tence and the cause of his injury.” Id. at 113 (emphasis 
added). In 1969, Kubrick had discovered the “factual 
predicate for a claim against the VA.” Id. at 118. At that

C. An “Injury And Pattern Discovery” Accrual 
Rule Would Avoid Anomalous And Inappropri- 
ate Results 


As demonstrated above, the “injury and pattern dis- 
covery” rule best fits the language and structure of RICO 
and the established federal law accrual principles. In 
addition, the “injury and pattern discovery” rule avoids 
the inappropriate and indefensible outcomes which can 
arise when the “injury discovery” rule is applied in the 
RICO context. 


The most astounding outcome under the “injury dis- 
covery” accrual rule is that a plaintiff's RICO claim can 
be barred by the statute of limitations before her RICO 
cause of action ever existed. The loss of a RICO claim 
before it ever exists takes place in instances where the 
second predicate act occurs more than four years after the 
first predicate act. Where the plaintiff is the victim of the 
first predicate act and learns of her injury at the time of 
commission, the “injury discovery” rule would have the 
statute of limitations begin to run immediately. Under 
that rule, the limitations period will expire four years 
from the date of the first predicate act. Yet the pattern 
requirement set out in RICO provides that two predicate 
acts occurring any time within a ten-year period form the 
basis of a RICO pattern. 18 U.S.C. § 1961(5) (1994). There- 
fore, the RICO cause of action cannot exist until the 
second predicate act occurs. Yet if the second predicate 
act takes place more than four years after the first predi- 
cate act (the statute anticipates up to ten years between 
the first and second predicate acts), then the “injury 


42 


discovery” rule would preclude the victim of the first 
predicate act from bringing suit to recover for her injury. 
In these circumstances, the party injured by the first 
predicate act is barred from bringing suit before she ever 
had an opportunity to sue. 


Depriving a potential plaintiff of her right to sue 
even before a cause of action ever came into being plainly 
violates all accepted principles of accrual. See, e.g., United 
States v. Lindsay, 346 U.S. 568, 569 (1954) (“In common 
parlance a right accrues when it comes into exis- 
tence... ”); Amy v. Dubuque, 98 U.S. 470, 476 (1878) (a 
cause of action only accrues when a right to sue attaches); 
Wilcox v. Executors of Kemp Plummer, 29 U.S. (4 Pet.) 172 
(1830) (same). Furthermore, such a result cannot be rec- 
onciled with the very purpose of the RICO statute. See 
Keystone, 863 F.2d at 1134 (“Under the [injury] discovery 
rule if a plaintiff suffers a single injury as a result of a 
predicate act but the second predicate act which estab- 
lishes the necessary ‘pattern’ occurs five years after the 
injury to the plaintiff, that plaintiff’s claim is barred by the 
four year civil RICO statute of limitations. 
... In such cases the purpose of the statute is defeated by 
the simple discovery rule”);-Paul B. O’Neill, Mother of 
Mercy, Is This the Beginning of RICO? The Proper Point of 
Accrual of a Private Civil RICO Action, 65 N.Y.U. L. Rev. 
172, 235 (1990) (“To initiate the statutory period sooner 
[than all the elements exist] is potentially to restrict the 
plaintiff's cause of action in a way unauthorized by the 
statute and inconsistent with general principles of stat- 
utes of limitation”); Michael Goldsmith, Civil RICO 
Reform: The Basis for Compromise, 71 Minn. L. Rev. 827, 879 
(1987) (“Because a potential plaintiff has not been injured 
under RICO until the pattern element has been satisfied, 


43 


it is inappropriate to start the limitations period before 
the pattern is fully developed.”). 


Having the limitations period expire before the cause 
of action even exists is, on its face, absurd. Yet, this is the 
logical result of the “injury discovery” rule. Although 
this extreme outcome may not arise frequently, it under- 
scores the harsh reality of the “injury discovery” rule. 
Under this “injury discovery” rule, the victim of the first 
predicate act does not have the four-year statute of lim- 
itations established by this Court in Agency Holding Corp., 
supra. Rather, the first injured party “loses” from her four 
year limitations period the entire time period from her 
injury until the second predicate act occurs. In other 
words, the limitations period is really four years minus 
this “lost” amount of time during which a cause of action 
does not yet exist and, indeed, might never exist without 
occurrence of a second predicate act. 


For example, assume that victim A is injured by a 
predicate act in 1990 and is aware of her injury at the time 
that it occurs. Assume that the second predicate act in the 
pattern takes place in 1993 against victim B. Under the 
“injury discovery” rule, the statute of limitations period 
began to run in 1990 and expired in 1994. Yet, no RICO 
cause of action existed until the second predicate act took 
place in 1993. Thus, under the “injury discovery” rule, 
victim A did not enjoy the benefit of the four-year limita- 
tions period established by this Court in Agency Holding 
Corp. Instead, victim A has only one year: before the 
statute expires in 1994, victim A must uncover the sec- 
ond, 1993 fraud, ascertain whether it meets the pattern, 
continuity and other requirements of RICO, satisfy her 
Rule 11 obligations, and draft and file a RICO complaint 
that pleads her allegations with particularity pursuant to

simpler claims, the injury was the last act establishing the

the injury caused by the first predicate act does not 
establish the last element necessary for the cause of 
action; it will be some period of time in the future, when 
the second predicate act occurs, that all the elements of 
the RICO cause of action are satisfied. As a consequence 
of this unique quality of a RICO action, the burden that 
the “injury discovery” rule seeks to place on the victim of 
the first predicate act is unprecedented in American law. 
In the typical tort case, the tort exists at the time that the 
party is injured and the victim need only to go out and 
investigate to discover her claim. Yet, the victim of the 
first predicate act in a RICO case has a very different

underlying her claim since her cause of action does not

pattern. The “injury and pattern discovery” rule is far 
preferable since it allows an injured party sufficient time
