QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201, ef 
seq., (“FLSA”), sets a maximum hours standard for law 
enforcement at 171 hours in a 28 day period, 29 U.S.C. 
§ 207(k). The Secretary of Labor defines and delimits 
FLSA “white collar” exemptions, 29 U.S.C. § 213(a)(1), 
sO as to require compensation on a salary basis, 29 C.F.R. 
§ 541.5d and 118(a).* The questions presented involve 
the application of the salary basis test in public employ- 
ment, specifically— 


1. Are all public employees, who as a practical matter 
are subject to non-safety disciplinary suspension for less 
than a full pay period, non-exempt, or are only those who 
actually suffer deductions non-exempt? and 


2. Is a “window of correction” open to a public agency 
which simply amends one impermissible provision in a 
disciplinary code without (a) proving inadvertence, (b) 
revoking all impermissible authorization of deductions, 


and (c) restoring the lost compensation immediately upon 
discovery? 


*In 1974, the FLSA maximum hours standard was extended 
beyond the general 40 hour week by Congress to meet the special 
hours of work needs of law enforcement. In 1985 after this Court’s 
decision in Garcia v. San Antonio Transit, 469 U.S. 528 (1985), 
which overruled National League of Cities v. Usery, 426 U.S. 833 
(1976), and restored an hours of work standard to state and local 
public safety agencies, Congress and the DOL further adjusted 
the public sector rules to allow compensatory time, to provide a pro- 
tected compliance period, and otherwise to accommodate the public 
sector. See the FLSA Amendments of 1985, P.L. 99-150, 99 Stat. 
790 (1985); and DOL’s public sector rules, 52 Fed. Reg. 2032 
(1987), 29 C.F.R. Part 553. Finally, in 1992, the salary basis test 
was tailored to “principles of public accountability” by a “special 
provision applicable to employees of public agencies.” 57 Fed. Reg. 
37666 (1992), Pet. App. at 184a; 29 C_F.R. § 541.5d.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 

The FLSA, provides a “white collar” exemption for 
“executive, administrative, and professional employees” 
“as such terms are defined and delimited from time to 
time by regulations of the Secretary [of Labor].” See 
FLSA § 13(a)(1), 29 U.S.C. § 213(a)(1). (Pet. App.

employer has the burden of establishing that an employee 
fulfills the requirements of both the duties and the salary 
basis test prior to exempting that employee from the 
FLSA’s minimum wage and overtime at time and one 
half rules. See, 29 C.F.R. § 541.1 and § 541.2.? (Pet. 
App. 76a-80a).

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The decision of the Eighth Circuit that St. Louis Police 
Sergeants and Lieutenants are exempt from coverage under 
the Fair Labor Standards Act maximum hours standard 
should be reversed. The Eighth Circuit misapplied 
the salary basis disciplinary docking rule in 29 C.F.R. 
§§ 541.5d and 541.118(a)(5) and the “window of cor- 
rection” in 29 C.F.R. § 541.118(a)(6). Those rules are 
reasonable. They were developed pursuant to a Congres- 
sional directive that the Secretary of Labor “define and 
delimit” the breadth of FLSA “white collar” exemption. 


17 


29 U.S.C. §213(a)(1).% The Department of Labor’s 
rules are entitled to special deference.* This Court has 
held that all FLSA exemptions must be narrowly con- 
strued. 


The misapplication of the Department of Labor’s rules 
combines with the ambiguity of the decision itself to con- 
vert a clear objective, enforceable rule into one which is 
unclear and unenforceable. 


This Court should endorse a rule that any employee 
who as a practical matter is subject to disciplinary dock- 
ing for less than a full pay period is covered by the 
hours of work standard. Actual instances of - docking 
are relevant, but not necessary, to application of this as 
a practical matter rule. 


This Court should endorse a window of correction rule 
which requires all four of its traditional elements. The 
window is open only in cases in which an employer (1) 
meets, by clear and affirmative evidence, a burden to 
prove, (2) that any deductions were inadvertent, in the 
sense of mistaken or unintended, (3) that all deductions 
were fully rectified upon discovery, and (4) that all rules

B. The Congress Has Historically Expanded FLSA 
Coverage, Is Open To Reform, But Has Been Re- 
luctant To Broaden Exemptions. 


While the Court has played a key role in the develop- 
ment of FLSA, Congress and the Department of Labor 
have been equally clear with regard to the narrow nature 
of exemptions. Congress and the Department have been 
sensitive to the need to adjust the Act to the times. They 
have been responsive to problems. However, they have 
also been reluctant to broaden exemptions from the Act 
recognizing that the breadth of coverage is indeed “essen- 
tial to accomplish the goals of the Act.”

Congress. In 1966, Congress amended the FLSA to apply,

employees. 80 Stat. 83 (1966).

C.F.R. § 541.118(a)(6) IS AVAILABLE TO CORRECT 
IMPERMISSIBLE DEDUCTIONS ONLY IN CASES 
OF (a) ACTUAL MISTAKE OR INADVERTENCE, 
(b) RESTORATION OF LOST COMPENSATION 
UPON DISCOVERY, AND (c) THE COMPLETE 
REVOCATION OF THE IMPERMISSIBLE RULES. 


Guzy’s two day suspension clearly was not inadvertent. 
It was done consciously, while this litigation was pending, 
and after the salary basis and disciplinary docking rules 
had been briefed before the district court on summary 
judgment. Even after its discovery and extensive discus- 
sion of the disciplinary docking rule, the Police Board 
continues to withhold Sergeant Guzy’s docked pay, of- 
fering to reimburse him only if required to do so at the 
close of this litigation. 


The Eighth Circuit held that 29 C.F.R. § 541.118(a) 
(6) provides a “window of correction” to an employer 
who revises a small part of the offending code and 
promises to refund offending deductions only if ordered 
to do so by a court, even in the case of post litigation, 
non-safety disciplinary suspension for less than a pay 
period. 


44 


The Eighth Circuit fails to place the burden on the 
employer (1) to establish that actual deductions made 
were mistaken or inadvertent, (2) to revise all offending 
portions of the disciplinary code immediately, and (3) 
to seek out and refund all offending deductions upon 
discovery. This is contrary to the accepted rule; all three 
pre-conditions are necessary to the use of the window. 
See, Martin v. Malcolm Pirnie, Inc., 949 F.2d 611 (2nd 
Cir. 1991), cert. denied, 506 U.S. 905 (1992); and Klein 
v. Rush-Presbyterian-St. Luke's Medical Center, 990 F.2d 
279 (7th Cir. 1993); see, Peikes, “Tightening the White 
Collar Exemptions—the Court Breaths New Life into the 
Fair Labor Standards Act,” 10 The Labor Lawyer 121 
at 136-40 (1994); and The Thompson Group, Fair Labor 
Standards Handbook for State, Local Governments and 
Schools (Washington, D.C., 1989, supplemented monthly), 
§ 222 at 32-33; see also, DOL’s position as outlined in 
Appendix B. 


The Eighth Circuit’s ruling would permit an employer 
to intentionally act in a manner inconsistent with the 
salarly basis test with impunity. Even after being sued, 
an employer would be able to take advantage of exemp- 
tions to which the employer previously was unentitled. 
Employers could avoid the application of the FLSA hours 
of work standard since only a conditional post-litigation 
promise to reimburse deductions with a promise of limited 
future compliance would be required. 


The regulation at 29 C.F.R. § 541.118(a)(6) requires 
that an employer’s pre-litigation intention is decisive in de- 
termining whether the window of correction is available. 
Disciplinary deductions made pursuant to an established 
written policy and continued after they are discovered, 
and even after the initiation of litigation challenging them, 
necessarily indicates no intention to pay an employee on 
a salaried basis. 


The Eighth Circuit’s suggested approach would be to 
rob the salary basis regulation of any teeth. A stricter 


45 


rule is necessary to prevent the incongruous situation of 
employers cynically undertaking corporate or agency poli- 
cies which subject the compensation of all white collar 
employees to reduction for reasons other than lack of 
work, knowing that exempt status of the affected em- 
ployees could be restored through a promise cf reim- 
bursement in the event of a litigation loss and a promise 
to reform any limited portion of the rules which are es- 
tablished in litigation to have actually been applied to 
the challenging litigants. Voluntary compliance with the 
Act, absent litigation, would face a serious threat by any 
employer who engaged in the too frequent calculus of the 
cost of compliance versus the cost of non-compliance. See,
