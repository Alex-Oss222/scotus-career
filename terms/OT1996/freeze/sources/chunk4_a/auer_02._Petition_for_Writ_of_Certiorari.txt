QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Does the Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. 
§$§ 201 et seq., (“FLSA”) salary basis test of “white 
collar” FLSA exemptions apply to public employees? 


2. Do the United States Department of Labor (“DOL”) 
FLSA salary basis regulations in 29 C.F.R. §§ 541.5d 
and 541.118(a)(5), which require the loss of exempt 
status when an employee is “subject to” nonsafety dis- 
ciplinary suspension of less than a pay period apply only 
in cases of actual suspension or does DOL’s interpretation 
that a personnel rule subjecting employees to discipline 


alone result in loss of exempt status control the applica- 
tion of the rule? 


3. Do the DOL’s “window of correction” rules in 29 
C.F.R. § 541.118(a)(6) allow an employer to treat a 
group of employees, police sergeants and lieutenants in 
this case, as exempt simply by revoking, a small portion 
of an offending disciplinary rule, which it had actually 
applied after the initiation of the litigation, to a member 
of the employee group, without revoking the more general 
“subject to discipline” rule and without actually restoring 
the lost compensation to the employee involved?

2. Window of Correction: “Inadvertent” or “For Rea- 
sons Other Than Lack of Work”—Conjunctive or Dis- 
junctive. In this case, the Police Department’s Manual 
makes sergeants and lieutenants “subject to” disciplinary 
deductions for violations of more than 40 rules, and the 
record shows an actual post-litigation, two-day deduction 
from a police sergeant who violated the portion of the 
disciplinary rules requiring St. Louis residency. However,

the Eighth Circuit held that 29 C.F.R. § 541.118(a) (6) 
provides a “window of correction” to any employer who 
revised the residency requirement post-litigation to provide 
only for dismissal and announces in a post-appeal brief 
an intent to reimburse the employee for the lost wages 
but, only if required by the Appellate Court to do so. 
(Pet. App. 10a, and Pet. App. L, 219a). The general 
Police Manual rule subjecting the petitioners to disci-

repealed or revised (only its application to residency vio-

not actually reimbursed. This is a view of 29 C.F.R. 
§ 541.118(a)(6) clearly at odds with the “window of 
correction” interpretation of the Department of Labor. 


The Department of Labor’s interpretation of the “win- 
dow of correction” is that it is only available in the case 
of actual mistakes where no formal policy provides for 
non-safety deductions, where all of the rules subjecting the 
employees to non-safety discipline for less than a full work 
period are repealed, and where an actual return of any 
deductions has been made. The DOL view is best outlined 
in the Martin v. Malcom Pernie, Inc., 949 F.2d 611 (2d 
Cir. 1991), cert. denied, 113 S. Ct. 198 (1992), where 
the Second Circuit accepted and adopted the view pressed 
by the Secretary of Labor plaintiff, and in Klein v. Rush- 
Presbyterian-St. Luke’s Medical Center, 990 F.2d 279 
(7th Cir. 1993). See, Peikes, 10 The Labor Lawyers at 
136-140; and The Thompson Group, Fair Labor Stand- 
ards Handbook for State, Local Governments and Schools 
(Washington D.C., 1989, supplemented monthly), § 222, 
at 32-33. 


3. Deference To The DOL. Under this Court’s man- 
date in Chevron U.S.A. v. Natural Resources Defense 
Council, 467 U.S. 837 (1983), the Eighth Circuit erred 
when it refused to follow the Department of Labor’s inter- 
pretation of its rules on disciplinary docking and the 
window of correction. The DOL’s reasonable construction 


17 


is due deference and is controlling even if the Circuit 
Court’s own construction were equally reasonable. 

In this case, the Eighth Circuit’s interpretation ignores 
the clear wording of the regulation which requires that 
the employer actually repeal disciplinary rules calling for 
suspensions without pay and actually reimburse the com- 
pensation deductions actually made. The record here is 
clear. The St. Louis Manual provisions calling for dis- 
ciplinary suspensions has not been repealed or revoked— 
only amended to revise one of more than 40 disciplinary 
infractions by requiring that were it to re-occur, dismissal 
would result. The Court relied only on a promise, in 
Respondent's appellate pleading, of possible future re- 
imbursement conditioned on an order of the Court to 
reimburse. (Pet. App. L, 219a). The sergeant actually 
docked has not been reimbursed. 

The Eighth Circuit’s rendition of the “disciplinary dock- 
ing” and “window of correction” rules is not reasonable. 
The Secretary of Labor’s reading is entirely so. Due refer- 
ence to the agency responsible for FLSA enforcement 
requires that the Eighth Circuit’s theory does not replace 
DOL’s interpretation of its own rules.
