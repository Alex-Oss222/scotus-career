INTRODUCTION 


This reply will address the procedural issues raised by 
the City of Culver City’s Brief in Opposition to Richard 
Ehrlich’s Petition for Certiorari. The City’s arguments on 
the merits are more than adequately addressed in Ehrlich’s 
petition as well as those of amici.' Petitioner will also in this 
reply brief direct this Court’s attention to a law review article 
that was unavailable at the time that the opening brief was 
filed.

public art on his property.? In his petition for writ of 
certiorari, Ehrlich claims that forcing him to either pay for 
public art or have art placed upon his property is an 
unconstitutional physical taking without the payment of just 
compensation. Ehrlich’s Petition at 28-30. Culver City

B. The Issues Presented to This Court for 
Review Have Been Decided with Finality 


As noted in Ehrlich’s petition for certiorari’s discussion 
of jurisdiction at Page 2, Footnote 1, the importance of this 
case rests not on the amount of the public recreation fee, but 
on the fact that any fee could be imposed at all for the 
purpose of ameliorating the “loss” to the public of the 
“opportunity” to play tennis on Ehrlich’s allegedly “private” 
property. Culver City, however, devotes over seven pages 
to an argument that because the calculation of the amount of 
the fee remains undecided, this Court lacks jurisdiction. 
Culver City’s opposition at 8-15. 


The California Supreme Court, however, has decided 
conclusively and without equivocation that fees may be 
imposed on property owners when a land use restriction is 
altered. Such fees are solely for the purpose of using a 
landowner’s money to help finance public facilities that 
would allow the public to enjoy the same sort of activities 
that the landowner might have voluntarily provided under the 
previous land use restriction. Ehrlich v. City of Culver City, 
Opinion of the California Supreme Court, reproduced in 


5 


Petitioner’s Appendix A at A-34. Nothing on the remand of 
this case can change this holding one iota. 


Landowners throughout California are stuck with this 
holding. Ehrlich is stuck with this holding. That the 
ultimate amount of the fee might be more or less than the 
original $280,000 does not alter the essential loss of a 
property interest held by Ehrlich and other private 
landowners in California. 


This case is final for purposes of this Court’s review. 
This Court has recognized that state court decisions can be 
final for purposes of review even when further proceedings 
are available below. For example, in Organization for a 
Better Austin v. Keefe, 402 U.S. 415, 418 n.1 (1971), this 
Court found finality after a temporary injunction had been 
issued, noting: “Nor is there any indication that the 
injunction rests on a disputed question of fact that might be 
resolved differently upon further hearing.” Similarly, the 
existence of liability in Ehrlich’s case will not be altered by 
further hearing. 


This case is also analogous to Brady v. State of 
Maryland, 383 U.S. 83, 84 n.1 (1963), where this Court 
found a case final where a determination of guilt, but not 
punishment had been made by the state court. Here of 
course the analogy to guilt is the existence of liability for 
Owning property that experiences a change in a land use 
restriction. Only the punishment, or the amount of the fee, 
remains to be determined by the City. 


Culver City argues that Cox Broadcasting 
Corporation v. Cohn, 420 U.S. 469 (1975), should be read 
as providing for finality in these circumstances under only 
the narrowest of circumstances and if a mechanical 
application of one of four exceptions applies. See Culver 


6 


City’s Opposition Brief at 8. Commentators, however, 
consider this case to be a “major restatement of practical 
finality doctrine.” Wright and Miller, FEDERAL PRACTICE 
AND PROCEDURE, § 4010 at 586 (1977). In Cox 
Broadcasting, this Court eschewed applying finality in a 
“mechanical fashion.” 420 U.S. at 477, preferring “‘a more 
liberal, and, as we think, a more reasonable construction,’” 
Id. at 480 (citing Forgay v. Conrad, 47 U.S. (6 How.) 201, 
203 (1848)). In Cox Broadcasting this Court found that it 
often 


has taken jurisdiction without awaiting the 
completion of the additional proceedings 
anticipated in the lower state courts. In most, if 
not all, of the cases in these categories, these 
additional proceedings would not require the 
decision of other federal questions that might also 
require review by the Court at a later date, and 
immediate rather than delayed review would be 
the best way to avoid “the mischief of economic 
waste and of delayed justice.” 


420 U.S. at 477-78 (footnote and citation omitted). 


The present case easily falls within the ambit of all four 
Cox Broadcasting’s exceptions. “In the first category are 
those cases in which there are further proceedings--even 
entire trials--yet to occur in the state courts but where for 
one reason or another the federal issue is conclusive.” Jd. 
at 479. Culver City claims that because the amount of the 
exaction has not yet been determined the federal question has 
not been conclusively decided. Opposition brief at 9. 
Culver City is wrong. The California Supreme Court 
determined that the Takings Clause of the United States 
Constitution does not preclude imposition of a recreation fee 
or the arts fee. Petitioner’s Appendix at A-34, A-42, A-45- 


7 


46. The federal question has been decided. This is 
conclusive. See also Duquesne Light Co. v. Barasch, 
488 U.S. 299, 306 (1989) (finality of rulemaking found even 
in face of remand on amount of rates). 


Citing Brady, supra, this Court expiained that a second 
exception is found “in which the federal issue, finally 
decided by the highest court in the State, will survive and 
require decision regardless of the outcome of future 
state-court proceedings.” Culver City argues that because 
the amount of the fee is uncertain, and that because perhaps 
no fee would be imposed the federal issue may not survive.* 
Opposition brief at 8. However, no matter what sort of fee 
Culver City finally decides to impose, Ehrlich and other 
property owners in California will have suffered a taking of 
their property by virtue of the California Court’s determi- 
nation that land use restrictions placed upon property create 
some sort of public easement that can be altered only if the 
landowner is willing to pay a price. In Cox Broadcasting, 
citing to Radio Station WOW, Inc. v. Johnson, 326 U.S. 120 
(1945), this Court explained that “[nJothing that could 
happen in the course of the accounting, short of settlement of 
the case, would foreclose or make unnecessary decision on 
the federal question.” 420 U.S. at 480. The “accounting” 
of the amount of the fee in the present case is irrelevant to 
the issue of ultimate liability. The unlikely event of 
settlement here, as in Radio Station WOW is not enough to 
erase finality. 


The third exception is for cases where the federal 
question could not be reviewed after further state 
proceedings. 420 U.S. at 481. Upon remand the City of 
Culver City can impose some sort of fee. The calculation of

the amount of that fee may be challenged. But the 
imposition of liability will not be appealable. That issue will 
be res judicata. The California Supreme Court has decided 
the matter and no further proceedings will alter that result. 
Under the Rules of this Court, no appeal of the California 
Supreme Court’s decision will be possible after this point in 
time. Supreme Court Rule 13.1 (Petition for Certiorari must 
be filed within 90 days of final state court decision). Any 
purported challenge to the California Supreme Court’s in the 
years ahead will be untimely and unreviewable. See also 
Pennsylvania v. Richie, 480 U.S. 39 (1987) (federal ques- 
tion finally decided and later review of the federal question 
would be, at least for the most part, impossible). 


The fourth exception in Cox Broadcasting involves 
cases where Court reversal of a state court decision could 
preclude any further litigation and refusal of immediate 
review “might seriously erode federal policy.” 420 U.S. 
at 482. A decision by this Court in favor of Ehrlich would 
serve both of these ends. If this Court were to find that the 
imposition of a land use restriction does not create a virtual 
zoning easement owned by the public such that a landowner 
must pay to have it altered, then the recreation fee could not 
be sustained under any circumstance present in this case. In 
that case further state court litigation would be precluded. 
Furthermore, the policy embodied in the Fifth and Fourteenth 
Amendments proscribing takings without the payment of just 
compensation would be seriously eroded if this case is not 
brought to a timely close. There are limits to the Ehrlich’s 
resources and ability to carry on this protracted litigation. 
Thre state court opinion below will stand as binding precedent 
for the State of California no matter what happens on 
remand. Federal policy as embodied in the Constitution 
would not be well served if this case is not taken for appeal 
now. Under all the factors of Cox Broadcasting this case is 
final and ready for review.

A NEW LAW REVIEW ARTICLE DEMONSTRATES 
THE ABERRATIONAL NATURE OF THE DECISION 


Counsel for Ehrlich has just been made aware of a new 
law review article on the subject of the Ehrlich opinion. See 
Gideon Kanner, Tennis Anyone? How California Judges 
Made Land Ransom and Art Censorship Legal, 25 REAL 
ESTATE L. J. 214 (In Press 1997) (available upon request). 
The article characterizes the decision with the statement that 
“if ever a fragmented court rendered a revolutionary, yet 
muddled decision, this was it.” Jd at 217. It concludes: 
“Chief Justice Rehnquist’s Dolan admonition notwith- 
standing, property rights remain very much a constitutional 
‘poor relation’ in California.” Jd at 243.
