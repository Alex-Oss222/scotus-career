STATEMENT 
A. United States v. Brockamp 


1. Respondent Marian Brockamp is the administra- 
tor and sole beneficiary of the estate of her father, Stanley 
McGill. On or about April 16, 1984, Mr. McGill, then aged 
93, issued a personal check in favor of “Internal Revenue 
Service” in the sum of $7,000. The check did not indicate 
whether the $7,000 remittance was for income, gift or any 
other type of tax, or the period for which the remittance 
applied. Mr. McGill did not file an income tax return for 
1983 or for any of the five remaining years of his life, and 
the $7,000 remittance was the last he ever made. When 
the check was received by the Internal Revenue Service, it 
was placed in a “suspense” account, and on July 15, 1986 
was transferred into an “Excess Collection Account.” 
(Pet. App. 2a-3a.) The Service never made an assessment 
of the $7,000. (Pet. App. 22a.) 


2. Beginning in the late 1970's, Mr. McGill’s health 
began to deteriorate, and he developed the classic symp- 
toms of senile dementia. He began to look dazed and 
disoriented, and would walk about the house naked, 
unaware of his nakedness. On one occasion, he went to 
the corner mailbox in a state of undress, and when con- 
fronted with his act, was unaware of what had happened. 
He could no longer remember recent conversations. He 
would begin to dial a telemeeme, and suddenly forget

3. In response to Requests for Admissions, Peti- 
tioner admitted that Mr. McGill, who had long since 
retired, did not have an income in 1983 that could have 
resulted in a $7,000 tax liability. (Pet. App. 7a.) 


4. Following Mr. McGill’s death on November 7, 
1988, Respondent discovered the $7,000 check in Mr. 
McGill's belongings. After she was appointed administra- 
tor of his estate, she wrote the IRS, requesting that the 
$7,000 be returned. The Service refused, noting that the

B. United States v. Scott 


1. From the early 1980’s through at least early 
November, 1989, respondent Nicholas Scott (herein 
“Scott”) was a severe alcoholic. (Pet. App. 37a.) In 1984, 
Scott’s father, Gene Scott, acting under a power of attor- 
ney, made installments of Scott’s estimated tax payments 
for 1984. (Pet. App. 37a.) 


2. In 1989, Scott filed a tax return for 1984, claiming 
that he had no tax liability for 1984 and requesting a 
refund for amounts he had previously paid. The Internal 
Revenue Service denied the requested refund due to the 
expiration of the statute of limitations for filing a claim, 
based on §6511. (Pet. App. 32a.) 


3. Scott brought suit following the denial of his 
refund claim. The district court, sitting as trier of fact, 
found that Scott was mentally incompetent throughout 
the relevant period. (Pet. App. 33a.) The district court 
concluded that the doctrine of equitable tolling applies in 
tax refund suits, and that the limitations contained in 
§6511 should not be applied to a person such as Scott, 
whose alcoholism “rendered him mentally incompetent 
and unable to file a tax return from January 1985 through 
November 1989.” (Pet. App. 33a.) 


4. The court of appeals affirmed, relying on the 
decision entered the same day by the same panel in 
United States v. Brockamp, with Judge Fernandez dissent- 
ing on the same grounds as his dissent in Brockamp.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


A. In Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 
89 (1990), this Court enunciated a broad rule that, once 
Congress has waived the Government's sovereign immu- 
nity in an area, the same rebuttable presumption of equi- 
table tolling applicable in suits between private litigants 
also should apply to suits against the United States. Nei- 
ther the structure of §6511 of the Code, nor the legislative 
history of tax refund claims, nor the legislative history of 
§6511, nor the overall scheme of the administration of tax 
collection, indicates that this rebuttable presumption 
should not be applied to tax refund litigation. To the 
contrary, although express legislative intent is sparse, the 
legislative history indicates Congressional intent that 
equitable tolling be permitted with respect to tax refund 
litigation. 


B. The history of tax refund litigation indicates that 
a suit to recover overpaid taxes has been, from its incep- 
tion, a common law action for money had and received, 
which from 1836 until 1966 could be brought against 
individual Collectors. Throughout this period, such suits 
were presumptively subject to equitable tolling, just as 
any suit for money had and received against a private 
party. When Congress finally eliminated the right to sue a 
Collector individually in 1966, Congress did not manifest 
any intent to change the nature of the action or to restrict 
the amenability of such suits to equitable tolling. 


C. The §6511(b) limitations on the amount of a 
refund claim incorporate by reference the filing periods 
contained in §6511(a). If the time that a taxpayer has to 
file a refund claim is amenable to tolling, the “look-back” 


periods contained in §6511(b) are tolled to the same 
extent. Sections 6511(a) and 6511(b) should thus be read 
in the conjunctive, not in the disjunctive. The legislative 
history of §6511 supports such an interpretation. 


D. The legislative history of §6511 reveals that Con- 
gress’ intent in enacting the “amount” limitations con- 
tained in §6511(b) was to prevent a taxpayer from 
keeping the period for filing a refund claim open indefi- 
nitely by paying a de minimis amount of tax. Specifically, 
the “look-back” periods in §6511(b) were designed to be 
co-extensive with the period of time the Commissioner 
has to assess a tax against the taxpayer. Section 6511(b) 
has no application to a disabled taxpayer who has no 
ability to assess his own tax and (as was the case with Mr. 
Stanley McGill) against whom the statute of limitations 
against the Commissioner never began to run. 


E. The overall structure of tax collection is based 
upon each taxpayer assessing his or her own tax. No 
Congressional policy is violated by extending the period 
for refund claims where an overpayment is the result of 
the taxpayer's inability to assess his own tax. 


F. In the event that the period that the taxpayer has 
to file a refund claim is equitable tolled, the period that 
the Commissioner has to assess additional tax against the 
taxpayer should be tolled commensurately. Such a rule 
would be consistent with Congress’ intent to make the 
period that the taxpayer has to file a refund claim co- 
extensive with the period that the Commissioner has to 
assess the underlying tax. 


ARGUMENT 


NO GROUND EXISTS FOR NOT APPLYING THE 
REBUTTABLE PRESUMPTION IN FAVOR OF EQUITA- 
BLE TOLLING IN THE CONTEXT OF TAX REFUND 
CLAIMS. 


A. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF TAX 
REFUND LITIGATION SUPPORTS A CON- 
CLUSION THAT CONGRESS INTENDED 
EQUITABLE TOLLING TO APPLY TO SUCH 
SUITS. 


Until the creation of the Court of Claims in 1855, no 
suit could be brought directly against the United States in 
any forum. However, as early as 1836, this Court, in 
Elliott v. Swartwout, 35 U.S. (10 Pet.) 137 permitted suits 
for the refund of overpaid taxes to be brought against 
individual Collectors. Without the benefit of statutory 
authority, such suits were based upon the common law 
action for money had and received. Flora v. United States, 
362 U.S. 145 (1960). Used to recover upon rights equitable 
in nature to avoid unjust enrichment, they were the “lin- 
eal successor[s] of the common count of indebitatus 
assumpsit for money had and received.” Stone v. White 
301 U.S. 532, 534 (1937), “founded upon what the law 
terms an implied promise on the part of the defendant to 
pay what in good conscience he is bound to pay to the 
plaintiff.” Curtis's Administratrix v. Fiedler, 67 U.S. (2 
Black) 461 (1862). Such suits were equitable in nature, 


“ ... less restricted and fettered by technical 
rules and formalities than any other form of 
action. It aims at the abstract justice of the case, 
and looks solely to the inquiry, whether the 
defendant holds the money, which ex aequo et 


bono belongs to the plaintiff. . . . It approaches 
nearer a bill in equity than any other common 
law action.” United States v. Jefferson Electric, 291 
U.S. 386, 402 (1933) citing Claflin v. Godfrey, 38 
Mass. 1, 6.” : 


As such suits were legal actions brought against indi- 
viduals, they were presumptively amenable to equitable 
tolling, just as any action for money had ard received 
brought against a private individual. Beginning in 1836, 
and for the next 130 years, refund suits in the nature of 
common law actions for money had and received could 
be maintained against individual Collectors for the 
refund of overpaid taxes.* 


As a result of this Court’s holding in Elliott v. Swart- 
wout, Collectors began withholding tax collections, for 
fear that they could be personally liable for taxes wrong- 
fully collected. To thwart this, Congress, in the Act of 
March 3, 1839, 5 Stat. 339, required Collectors to remit all 
taxes collected to the Treasury. In 1845, this Court, in Cary 
v. Curtis, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 236 (1845), held that, as a result 
of the Act of March 3, 1839, individual Collectors merely 
performed the ministerial act of collecting and remitting 
for the Treasury. Consequently, no right of action against 
Collectors for refund existed. 


Congress’ reaction was swift and decisive. Within 
weeks of this Court’s decision in Cary v. Curtis, Congress 
enacted the Act of February 26, 1845, 5 Stat. 727, which 
expressly restored the taxpayer’s right to sue for refund. 


*’ The history of refund actions against Collectors was 
extensively reviewed in Plumb, Jr. Tax Refund Suits Against 
Collectors of Internal Revenue, 60 Harv. L. Rev. 685 (1947). 


Elliott v. Swartwout dealt with customs duties wrong- 
fully collected. In Elliott, this Court required that the 
taxpayer protest the exaction of the tax as a prerequisite 
to a refund suit, because a sum of money voluntarily paid 
could not form the basis of a common law action for 
money had and received.* United States v. Williams, __ 
U.S. __ (1995). The present requirement that a refund 
suit be preceded by a refund claim thus originated in the 
common law action for money had and received. In 1866, 
this Court, in City of Philadelphia v. The Collector, 72 U.S. (5 
Wall.) 720 (1867) extended the common law action for 
money had and received to actions for the recovery of 
internal revenue taxes, noting that: 


“there is no more appropriate or effectual rem- 
edy known to the common law than an action 
for assumpsit for money had and received, as in 
this case.” Id. at 733. 


In 1875, Congress acted to indemnify Collectors. Rev. 
Stat. §989; 28 U.S.C. §842 (1940). But the indemnification 
was not automatic. In order to be indemnified, the Collec- 
tor required certification by a court that there was proba- 
ble cause for the act done by the Collector. United States v. 
Kales, 314 U.S. 186 (1941). 


The truly private nature of the action against the 
Collector is evidenced by the fact that if the individual 
Collector who had exacted the tax died during the pen- 
dency of the refund action, the action could not be main- 
tained against his successor in office; the action could be

tor. Smietanka, Collector v. Indiana Steel Co., 257 U.S. 1 
(1921). 


The Government first became directly amenable to 
suits for tax refunds with the enactment of the Act of 
March 3, 1887, 24 Stat. 505, commonly known as the 
Tucker Act. But the Tucker Act did not, and was not 
intended, to impose any new conditions on the pre-exist- 
ing right to sue the Collector for tax refunds. Flora v. 
United States, 362 U.S. 145, 189 (1960) (Whittaker, J. dis- 
senting). Indeed, if the taxpayer’s claim was for an 
amount in excess of $1,000 (later increased to $10,000) the 
taxpayer's only action was a suit against the Collector. 
Even if the amount was under the jurisdictional ceiling, 
the taxpayer had the option to sue the Collector for 
refund. Significantly, the enactment of the Tucker Act did 
not change the nature of the action against the Collector, 
which continued to be a common law action for money 
had and received against a private individual. United 
States v. Kales, 314 U.S. 186, 200 (1941). Accord: Lewis v. 
Reynolds, 284 U.S. 281, 283 (1932). 


The dual nature of tax refund suits continued into the 
modern era, with the taxpayer having the choice of suing 
the Collector individually, or the United States directly, 
either in District Court up to the jurisdictional amount, or 
in the Court of Claims without regard to the jurisdictional 
amount. Not until 1954, with the enactment of the Act of 
July 30, 1954, ch. 648, 68 Stat. 589, §1, was the $10,000

ceiling on refund suits brought in District Court elimi- 
nated.* 


The right of the taxpayer to sue the individual Dis- 
trict Director (formerly the Collector) was not abrogated 
until 1966, with the enactment of §7422(f)(1) of the Code.® 
But the legislative history of this provision reveals that 
such suits were eliminated to aid the practitioner, who 
prior thereto had been caught in the trap of either suing 
the wrong Collector, or having had the right Collector die 
during the pendency of the litigation. The Senate Report 
also reveals that the right to sue the District Director was 
eliminated because the number of District Directors was 
reduced from 58 to seven, Congress fearing that these 
suits would thus be concentrated in seven districts. S. 
Rep. No. 1625, 3 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 3681 
(1966). 


Nowhere does the 1966 legislative history reveal any 
intention on the part of Congress to change the nature of 
tax refund litigation from what it had been since 1836, 
viz, a common law action on a debt.

D. THE OVERALL STRUCTURE OF OUR SYS- 
TEM OF TAX COLLECTION, BASED AS IT IS 
UPON SELF-ASSESSMENT, SUPPORTS A 
CONCLUSION THAT CONGRESS INTENDED 
EQUITABLE TOLLING TO APPLY TO TAX 
REFUND CLAIMS. 


Our “complex and comprehensive” system of tax 
collection is grounded upon a system of self-assessment. 
United States v. Arthur Young & Co., 465 U.S. 805 (1984). 


23 


Each year, every citizen or resident is required to com- 
pute his own income, determine his own tax liability, and 
forthrightly report that tax liability to the Treasury on 
forms prescribed by the Commissioner. Commissioner v. 
Dane-Wells, 321 U.S. 219 (1944). Each such form must be 
executed by the taxpayer under penalty of perjury. 


In the event that the taxpayer later discovers that he 
has overpaid his taxes and desires a refund, he is 
required to first file a claim for refund. That refund claim 
must set forth, in detail, the exact grounds upon which 
the claim is based, and it too must be executed under the 
penalty of perjury. A claim that does not comply with 
these requirements will not be considered a refund claim 
for any purpose. Treas. Regs. §301.6402-2(b) (1977). If the 
claim is denied, and the taxpayer later brings suit to 
recover the overpaid tax, any material difference between 
the “exact grounds” contained in the refund claim and 
the taxpayer's complaint will be attacked as containing a 
fatal variance. See, e.g., United States v. Felt & Tarrant Mfg. 
Co., 283 U.S. 269 (1931). 


In Irwin v. Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89 (1990), this 
Court enunciated a broad rule to the effect that once 
Congress has waived its sovereign immunity in a given 
area, then making the rule of equitable tolling applicable 
to suits against the government is “likely to be a realistic 
assessment of legislative intent as well as a practically 
useful principle of interpretation,” warranting a “rebutt- 
able presumption” in favor of tolling. Id. at 95. 


At bottom, the Petitioner maintains that in our sys- 
tem of self-assessment, Congress intended that the same 
restrictions and limitations that apply to taxpayers in 


24 


possession of their faculties must apply with equal force 
to taxpayers who have no ability to assess their own 
taxes, no ability to appreciate the penalties of perjury, and 
no ability to compose a refund claim. Moreover, the Peti- 
tioner maintains that Congress intended to create regime 
of tax collection whereunder the period of time that a 
disabled taxpayer has to file a refund claim may have 
expired while — due to the taxpayer's inability to file a tax 
return — the period of limitation on the Commissioner to 
assess the underlying tax may not yet have begun. 


E. IN THE EVENT THAT THE PERIOD THAT A 
TAXPAYER HAS TO FILE A REFUND CLAIM 
IS TOLLED, THE PERIOD THAT THE COM- 
MISSIONER HAS TO ASSESS ADDITIONAL 
TAX SHOULD BE TOLLED FOR A COMMEN- 
SURATE PERIOD. 


Respondent suggests that in the event that the period 
that the taxpayer has to file a refund claim is tolled, then 
the period that the Commissioner has to assess a tax 
against the taxpayer for that tax year should be tolled 
commensurately. Such a rule would be consistent with 
the goal of §6511(b), which is to make the period that the 
taxpayer has to file a refund claim co-extensive with the 
period that the Commissioner has to assess the underly- 
ing tax. If such a rule were adopted, a taxpayer asserting 
the doctrine of equitable tolling would do so with the full 
knowledge that if reasonable cause for invoking the doc- 
trine were found, it would have the effect of opening the 
entire tax year for review, thus limiting the availability of 
equitable tolling only to those who were truly prevented 
from filing a refund claim on a timely basis.
