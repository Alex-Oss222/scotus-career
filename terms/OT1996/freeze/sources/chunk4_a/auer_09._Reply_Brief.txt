fl. THE ELEVENTH AMENDMENT. 

This is not a case in which the Court should 
address the Eleventh Amendment. The Police Board 
asserts for the first time that it is not subject to suit in 
federal court. Contrary to the Board’s claim, Seminole 
Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 517 U.S. __, 116 S. Ct. 
1114 (1996) does not hold that federal courts have no 
jurisdiction in a case such as this. The Police Board is not 
a State Government within the meaning of the Eleventh 
Amendment. This case does not invoke the specter of 
federal court jurisdiction or control over the treasury of 
one of the 50 states. The Sergeants are not “citizens of 
another state or a foreign government" within the mez xing 
of the Eleventh Amendment. If immunity is at issue, it is 
not the developing Eleventh Amendment variety, but more 
traditional Article [I] immunity. The Sergeants seek a 
judgment solely against the Police Board. The Police 
Board waived immunity. 


The Board Is Not An Arm of the State. In 
support of their claim that the true party in interest is 
Missouri, the Police Board points to (a) the amended 
complaint’s statement that it is the “governing state agency 
authority for the St. Louis Police Department,” (b) the 
Missouri Code provision that establishes the Board, and 
(c) the Governor’s power to appoint the members of the 
Board. Resp. Brf. at 7-10. However, these are 
insufficient to confer “State” status for Eleventh 
Amendment purposes. 


This Court has consistently held that the Eleventh 
Amendment does not deprive the federal courts of 
jurisdiction over suits against political subdivisions. 
"[C]ities and counties do not enjoy Eleventh Amendment 
immunity." Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp. 


13 


115 S. Ct. 394, 404 (1994). This is true even though 
political subdivisions exercise a “slice of state power." 
Lake Country Estates v. Tahoe Planning Agency, 440 U.S. 
391, 401-02 (1979). See, also, Mt. Healthy City Board of 
Ed. v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274, 280-81 (1977); County of 
Lincoln v. Luning, 133 U.S. 529 (1890). 


The Court’s Financial Responsibility Test. 
Political subdivisions are protected by the amendment only 
if a money judgment against them would have the same 
consequence as a judgment against the State itself. This is 
the holding of Hess. In that case, this Court expressly 
rejected the two arguments made by the Board here: That 
the Board is an arm of the State because (1) it is created 
by State law, and (2) the Governor appoints its members. 
As this Court pointed out, “ultimate control of every state- 
created entity resides with the State, for the State may 
destroy or reshape any unit it creates. ‘[PJolitical 
subdivisions exist solely at the whim and behest of their 
State,’ yet cities and counties do not enjoy Eleventh 
Amendment immunity." Hess, 115 S.Ct. at 404 (citations 
omitted). "[RJendering control dispositive,” this Court 
reasoned, “does not home in on the impetus for the 
Eleventh Amendment: the prevention of federal court 
judgments that must be paid out of a State's treasury." /d.

The Minority’s State Control Test. It is not 
necessary to rest solely on the "State Treasury" factor to 
find that the Police Board is not an arm of the State. The 
Police Board fairs no better under the control test. Justice 
O’Connor, writing for the dissent, stated that the 
appropriate test is the degree of control the State retains in 
oversight of the entity. Hess, 115 S. Ct. at 411 
(O’Connor, J., dissenting; (emphasis added)). The dissent 
pointed to the fact that the Governors appointed the 
members in Hess, that the Governors had veto power over 
the decisions of the Port Authority, that the Port Authority 
was required to report annually to the State legislatures 
and that the State legislatures were required to approve 
changes in any rules or new projects. /d. at 411-12. 


In this case, the Governor of Missouri appoints 
four of five members of the Board. However, that is 
where the State control ends. The State Code mandates 
the basic authority for the Board. Neither the State 
legislature nor the Governor have control or oversight 
over the operation of the Board. Neither the Governor 
nor the State legislature have veto or approval power over 
any decisions of the Board.
