QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Does the Fair Labor Standards Act (29 U.S.C. 
§ 201 et seq.) (“FLSA”) salary basis test in 29 CFR § 541.1 
and 2, as interpreted by the Secretary of Labor, apply to 
public agency employees? 


2. Do the Secretary of Labor’s interpretations of the 
salary basis test in 29 CFR § 541.118(a) and, more partic- 
ularly § 541.118(a)(5), require the loss of salaried status 
when an employee is merely subject to the possibility of a 
disciplinary suspension of less than a week for non-safety 
related conduct, and should those interpretations apply 
to employees of a public agency engaged in law enforce- 
ment activities? 


3. Should the Secretary of Labor’s interpretation of 
the effect of an impermissible deduction from salary, 29 
CFR § 541.118(a)(6), be given a meaning other than that 
which is plainly stated and are Respondents entitled to 
restore Sergeant Michael Guzy’s salaried, exempt status 
by reimbursing him for a two (2) day suspension and 
committing to continue existing practice on disciplinary 
suspensions, if his suspension constituted an impermissi- 
ble deduction from his salary?

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 


Initially, in deciding whether or not the FLSA salary 
basis test for the executive and administrative exemp- 
tions, as interpreted by the Secretary of Labor, has appli- 
cation to public agency employees, the Court must first 
consider whether federal jurisdiction exists to hear the 
case. Because the Board of Police Commissioners, of 
which Respondents were members, is by Petitioners’ own 
description, an agency of the State of Missouri and all 
operations of the St. Louis Metropolitan Police Depart- 
ment are governed by state law, the case at bar is a suit 
commenced by Petitioners against a state. Jurisdiction of 
the federal courts was invoked under the provisions of 
the FLSA which was enacted pursuant to the Interstate 
Commerce Clause. The Eleventh Amendment bars pri- 
vate parties from bringing an action in federal court 
against a state to enforce such an Act of Congress. There- 
fore, regardless of the propriety of the FLSA salary basis 
test, this action must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. 


Notwithstanding the jurisdictional issue, that part of 
the salary basis test, as interpreted by the Secretary of 


Labor in 29 CFR § 541.118(a)(5), which would limit 
Respondents’ ability to discipline otherwise exempt 
employees by prohibiting suspensions of less than a week 
under certain circumstances, at the risk of loss of salaried 
status, is invalid in its application. Public employers, and 
more specifically law enforcement agencies, are treated 
differently than private employers under the FLSA in 
numerous respects including the overtime standards. 
More recently, the Secretary of Labor amended the regu- 
lations to allow public employers to make deductions 
from employees’ salaries for absences of less than a day 
without affecting their salaried, exempt status. This 
exception, which is not available to private employers, 
was based on the recognition of principles of public 
accountability. There is no rational basis for allowing 
deductions for a salaried employee's absences of less than 
a day while disallowing disciplinary suspensions of less 
than a full week, except for major safety rules violations. 


Furthermore, the rationale for 29 CFR § 541.118(a)(5) 
did not take into account the importance of discipline 
from a public accountability standpoint in a law enforce- 
ment agency or the fact that such an agency’s mission is 
preserving the public safety. Private employers can legit- 
imately impose disciplinary deductions on salaried, 
exempt employees for violations of safety rules of major 
significance. A public employer whose entire function is 
safety related must be allowed greater flexibility than a 
private employer in maintaining discipline within its 
organization. Thus, Respondents urge the Court to find 
the application of 29 CFR § 541.118(a)(5) invalid in this 
case or to interpret it in such a way that permits Respon- 
dents to suspend exempt officers for periods of less than 


a week for legitimate disciplinary reasons without jeopar- 
dizing their status as salaried employees. Rigid and arbi- 
trary application of salary test interpretations which were 
never designed to apply to public law enforcement agen- 
cies cannot be and has not been justified by the Secretary 
of Labor or Petitioners. 


Assuming arguendo that the Secretary of Labor’s 
interpretations are entirely valid and applicable to 
Respondents, the determination that Petitioners were 
paid on a salary basis, based on the facts of this case, was 
the correct determination and should be affirmed. 
Attempts by Petitioners to reargue the facts are inap- 
propriate. The record contains no evidence that a single 
one of the two hundred eighty-nine (289) Petitioners have 
actually suffered a deduction from salary due to varia- 
tions in quality or quantity of work. The evidence reveals 
only one Petitioner was suspended for less than a full 
week as a disciplinary measure and that occurred only 
with the Petitioner’s agreement as an alternative to the 
loss of his job, and cannot reoccur due to a change in the 
Disciplinary Code. Thus; there is no evidence of a prac- 
tice of making improper deductions for the period from 
1979 when all Sergeants and higher ranking officers were 
converted to the salary basis of payment by mandate of 
state law. 


The Secretary of Labor in the Amicus Brief filed at 
the invitation of this Court clarified the DOL interpreta- 
tion of 29 CFR § 541.118(a)(5). In so doing, the Secretary 
specifically concluded that the Eighth Circuit opinion 
correctly concluded that Petitioners, as a class, were not 
subject to reductions in pay for disciplinary suspensions 
of less than one week for violations of non-safety related 


rules. The Secretary further suggested that in the circum- 
stances of this case, one would have to ignore the realities 
of Respondents’ compensation scheme to characterize 
Petitioners as hourly-paid, rather than salaried, 
employees. 


Prior to the filing of the Amicus Brief of the United 
States, it was represented in the Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari that the Secretary of Labor’s construction and 
interpretation of his own rules on the subject of disciplin- 
ary suspensions and their effect on the salaried status of 
an employee or group of employees, were not only due 
deference but were controlling in this case. At that time 
Petitioners believed the Secretary's views to be at odds 
with the Eighth Circuit’s interpretations of 29 CFR 
§ 541.118(a)(5) and (6). However, having received the 
benefit of what the Secretary termed a “clear statement” 
on the “correct reading” of DOL regulations which is at 
odds with the positions asserted by Petitioners, they have 
apparently changed their minds and decided that the 
Secretary’s views should be controlling only when he 
shares their views. 


/ 


Respondents submit that because Petitioners commit- 
ted to a legal proposition in their Petition, they cannot 
abandon that proposition and make any credible argu- 
ment to the contrary in their Brief. Petitioners, by their 
own statement of the law, are bound by the Secretary of 
Labor’s expressions of the proper application of the sal- 
ary basis test and his interpretations thereof, on the facts 
of this case. Both the Secretary and the Eighth Circuit 


agree that the mere potential for a disciplinary suspen- 
sion which is inconsistent with the Secretary’s interpreta- 
tions of the salary test will not defeat an employee's 
salaried status. 


Not only has the Secretary concurred that two hun- 
dred eighty-eight (288) of the two hundred eighty-nine 
(289) Petitioners were not subject to improper disciplin- 
ary deductions, he has expressly endorsed the Eighth 
Circuit's fact bound holding that Respondents were and 
are entitled to correct the effects of the two (2) day 
suspension accepted by one Sergeant and preserve his 
salaried status. Clearly, that “deduction” was an isolated 
incident and not reflective of a policy or practice of 
improper deductions. It is equally clear that it was not 
made because of a lack of work and, therefore, fit 
squarely within the criteria of 29 CFR § 541.118(a)(6). 
Obviously, Respondents do not agree that the disciplin- 
ary deduction component of the interpretations of the 
salary basis test is or should be applicable to their exempt 
officers. Nor do Respondents agree that the one suspen- 
sion in evidence should be interpreted as inconsistent 
with the salary basis of payment, particularly as applied 
to a law enforcement agency. Nonetheless, if this Court 
rules to the contrary, Respondent will reimburse the Ser- 
geant in question for two (2) days of his bi-weekly salary 
and continue its existing practice by which exempt offi- 
cers are not subject to disciplinary suspensions of less 
than a week for violations of rules which are not directly 
related to the safety of the general public or their fellow 
officers. 


Petitioners are not entitled to obtain overtime com- 
pensation back to 1986 on the basis they are not salaried 


— 


employees because of one brief suspension in 1990 bar- 
gained for by the Sergeant in question, which, since 1991, 
could not be repeated. The mere existence of a disciplin- 
ary policy containing a range of penalties which include 
suspensions of less than a full week did not convert every 
Sergeant and Lieutenant, who are by state law salaried 
employees, to hourly paid employees. There must be 
evidence that it was the practice of Respondents to sub- 
ject Petitioners to improper disciplinary suspensions, 
under the Secretary's interpretations, to support a legal 
conclusion that Petitioners are not paid on a salary basis. 
The record is devoid of any such evidence. To conclude 
otherwise would result in a legal absurdity which, in 
turn, would result in a windfall for Petitioners and an 
overwhelming and unforeseen financial liability for 
Respondents.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE SALARY BASIS TEST CANNOT BE APPLIED 
BECAUSE RESPONDENTS ARE IMMUNE FROM 
SUIT BY PETITIONERS UNDER THE FAIR LABOR 
STANDARDS ACT. 


The broad question presented for review in the Peti- 
tion for Writ of Certiorari is whether or not the salary 


_basis test in 29 CFR § 541.1 and 541.2 for exemption from 


the minimum wage and overtime requirements of the Fair 
Labor Standards Act (29 U.S.C. § 213(a)(1)) (“FLSA”) is 
applicable to public employees. Without explanation, 
Petitioners omitted this question from their Brief and 


failed to present any argument in support of an affirma- 
tive resolution of the question. Respondents submit that 
strict application of the salary test to the otherwise 
exempt executive and administrative commissioned offi- 
cers of the St. Louis Metropolitan Police Department is 
invalid on two (2) separate grounds. First, the salary test 
cannot be applied at all because the facts of this case 
show that the Board of Police Commissioners on which 
Respondents served is a creation of Missouri state statute 
which grants to the Governor the power to appoint the 
members of the Board. The establishment and operation 
of the St. Louis Metropolitan Police Department 
(“Department”), including the manner in which police 
officers are compensated, is governed by state law. Sec- 
tions 84.030-100 and Section 84.160, R.S.Mo. (Jt. App. 
17-19). 


Missouri law specifically prohibits the payment of 
any additional compensation over and above the bi- 
weekly salaries authorized, or compensatory time off, for 
overtime to any officer of the rank of Sergeant or above 
(Jt. App. 18; Pet. App. 6a, 43a-44a). The trial court speci- 
fically noted in its Findings and Conclusions of Law, it 
was assumed that “Missouri’s elected representatives 
took care and gave great contemplation when designing a 
St. Louis Police Department organizational structure that 
would best serve a particular constituency.” (Pet. App. 
44a). Finally, the trial court rejected “Plaintiffs’ conten- 
tions that Mo. Rev. Stat. § 84.160 and specifically Mo. Rev. 
Stat. 84.160(5) should be preempted by the FLSA.” (Pet. 
App. 62a). Clearly, it was contemplated from the outset 
that this was a suit against the state appointed Board 
members to require them to discontinue their adherence 


ee LT 


ee eee 


to state law and, instead, comply with Petitioners’ view 
of the federal law as expressed in the FLSA and its 
implementing regulations. 


That being the case, this action is, for all intents and 
purposes, a suit against the State of Missouri within the 
meaning of the Eleventh Amendment to the United States 
Constitution.’ This Court held in Seminole Tribe of Florida 
v. Florida, 116 S.Ct. 1114 (1996), that the Eleventh Amend- 
ment bars Congress from authorizing private parties to 
bring suit in federal court against a state to enforce 
federal laws enacted pursuant to the Interstate Commerce 
Clause. The Fair Labor Standards Act was specifically 
enacted through the exercise of Congress’ power to regu- 
late commerce among the several states. 29 U.S.C. 
§ 202(a) and (b). Therefore, the FLSA salary basis test 
could not be applied to Petitioners because the trial court 
lacked jurisdiction to hear their claims for overtime com- 
pensation under the FLSA.?

Il. THE DISCIPLINARY DEDUCTION COMPONENT 
OF THE SECRETARY OF LABOR’S SALARY 
BASIS TEST IS IRRATIONAL, ARBITRARY AND 
CAPRICIOUS AS APPLIED TO A PUBLIC 
EMPLOYER ENGAGED IN LAW ENFORCEMENT 
ACTIVITY. 


If Petitioners’ FLSA claims are not dismissed, 
Respondents urge the Court to rule that possible or actual 
disciplinary suspensions of any of the Petitioners by the 
Department for periods of less than a week should not 
jeopardize their salaried status, regardless of the reason 
for the suspension. The St. Louis Metropolitan Police 
Department is a law enforcement agency which operates 
as a quasi-military organization. The trial court found it 
is structured in a way to enable police officers to respond 


Court is obligated to address it. Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp., 510 
U.S. 27, 114 S.Ct. 425 (1993). 


11 


effectively and rapidly to emergencies, to move quickly 
and decisively where force is required, and to generally 
set a tone of discipline and good order among the ranks 
(Pet. App. 41a). In the Police Manual, it is stated that the 
primary responsibility of the Department and each of its 
members is to protect the lives of the citizens they are 
sworn to serve (Pet. App. 105a). 


Members of the Department are held to a higher 
standard of conduct than members of the general public. 
See Section 7.004, Standards of Conduct (Pet. App. 
107a-113a). The Department cannot carry out its mission 
of insuring the safety of the general public in a manner 
which respects the rights of all persons without insuring 
that its officers conform to its high standards of conduct. 
To accomplish that, the Department must maintain disci- 
pline and the ability to do so is significantly infringed if it 
is effectively limited to non-salaried officers. Nor does it 
make sense that exempt officers in executive and admin- 
istrative positions who receive a predetermined amount 
of compensation each pay period regardless of how many 
hours they work, can only be suspended as a disciplinary 
measure in increments of a full week. 


The Eighth Circuit in McDonnell v. City of Omaha, 999 
F.2d 293 (8th Cir. 1993), took note of the fact that there is a 
difference between private and public sector employees. 
Congress recognized, even before this Court's decision in 
Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority, 469 
U.S. 528 (1985), that public agencies and particularly 
those engaged in law enforcement activities, warrant dif- 
ferent treatment under the FLSA from private employers. 
One of the most significant differences is that law 
enforcement agencies do not have to pay overtime com- 
pensation for hours worked in excess of forty (40) in a 


12 


week. The forty (40) hour work week requirement has 
been the standard for private employers for more than 
twenty-five (25) years but recognizing basic differences in 
law enforcement operations, the FLSA was amended to 
allow public agencies operating police departments to 
measure overtime on work periods ranging from seven 
(7) to twenty-eight (28) days, rather than work weeks, 
based on a higher range of hours worked. 29 U.S.C. 
§ 207(k); 29 CFR § 553.230. 


Law enforcement personnel are allowed to accrue up 
to twice as much compensatory time than other public 
employees. 29 U.S.C. § 207(0)(3)(A).2 All public 
employees are allowed to trade shifts with other 
employees, at their option, and the time worked is 
excluded from the calculation of hours worked for over- 
time purposes. 29 U.S.C. § 207(p)(3). All public 
employees can volunteer to do other work for a public 
agency. 29 U.S.C. § 203(e)(4)(A). These options are not 
available to private employers. Finally, as Petitioners 
point out, in 1992 the Secretary of Labor revised his 
interpretation of the salary test for public employees to 
allow them to have their pay docked for partial day 
absences, under certain circumstances, without losing 
their status as salaried employees (Pet. Br. 30). However, 
29 CFR § 541.118(a)(5) was not open for comment as a 
part of that rulemaking procedure and the Secretary of 
Labor has yet to formally consider changes to it for public 
employees, despite his continuing recognition of the need

to do so (Pet. App. 210a; Jt. App. 43-45). The different 
requirements and functions of a law enforcement agency 
necessitate different treatment. 


In 29 CFR § 541.118(a)(5), the Secretary has inter- 
preted the salary basis test to permit private employers to 
penalize, i.e., discipline, otherwise exempt employees for 
infractions of safety rules of major significance. Such 
safety rules are interpreted as those relating to the pre- 
vention of serious danger to the employer's plant, or to 
other employees. Thus, there is an explicit recognition 
that disciplinary suspensions for periods of less than a 
week where significant safety issues are involved, are not 
inconsistent with the salary basis of payment. Given the 
importance of, and special treatment granted in, matters 
of safety, it requires no great leap in logic to conclude that 
a police department, whose entire function consists of 
providing for the public safety, should have greater flex- 
ibility than a private employer in disciplining func- 
tionally exempt employees. Because of the very nature of 
their work, the Board must insure that members of the St. 
Louis Metropolitan Police Department have the trust and 
confidence of the citizens they serve. The ability to effec- 
tively maintain discipline within the Department is 
clearly essential to earning the public’s trust. 


Having already determined that public employers 
can make salary deductions for partial day absences 
based on principles of public accountability, it makes no 
sense for the Secretary or this Court to prohibit law 
enforcement agencies from using partial week suspen- 
sions of exempt officers as a means of discipline and, in 
turn, public accountability. 29 CFR § 541.5d. Maintaining 
such a distinction is irrational, arbitrary and capricious. 


14 


Forcing Respondents to suspend exempt officers for a full 
week to avoid jeopardizing their salaried status, regard- 
less of the severity of the misconduct or rules violation, 
makes no sense for either the officer involved or the 


Department. 


Federal agencies such as headed by the Secretary of 
Labor are prohibited from interpreting and applying reg- 
ulations to states which interfere with decisions typically 
left to local governing officials unless failure to do so 
would defeat the purposes of the statute being adminis- 
tered. Bowen v. American Hospital Association, 476 U.S. 610, 
643-644 (1986). Congress never intended that the Secre- 
tary of Labor regulate the internal operations of state and 
local law enforcement agencies under the guise of the 
salary test for FLSA exemption. Neither eliminating the 
application of 29 CFR § 541.118(a)(5) to public employers 
like Respondents or interpreting it in such a way that 
permits disciplinary suspensions in increments of a day 
rather than a week, will undermine the salary basis test 
or Congressional intent that exemptions from the FLSA 
be narrowly construed. Significant differences exist 
between a private, for profit, company and a publicly 
funded law enforcement agency whose mission is to pro- 
tect the public safety. Those differences have been recog- 
nized in 29 CFR Part 553 as noted herein. Therefore, rigid 
adherence to interpretations of the salary basis test 
designed strictly for private employers can no longer be 
justified.*

Ill. THERE ARE NO FACTUAL ISSUES BEFORE THE 
COURT INVOLVING THE SALARY BASIS TEST. 


In this case, Respondents asserted as affirmative

I. INTRODUCTION 


1. Plaintiffs bring this action on behalf of them- 
selves and other employees similarly situated for a 
declaratory judgment that the defendants have willfully 
and wrongfully violated their statutory obligations, and 
to recover back wages and liquidated damages in equal 
parts from the defendants for violations of the Fair Labor 
Standards Act (FLSA) of 1938, as amended, 29 U.S.C. 
Section 201, et seq. 


Defendants’ violations include: the institution and 
operation of a compensatory time system after April 15, 
1986; unilateral adjustment in the administrative regula- 
tions and application of rules controlling plaintiff's

wages or fringe benefits; a failure to keep proper records 
as required by the FLSA; the treatment of non-exempt 
employees as exempt from the FLSA on the basis of rank,

employee; and improper calculations for FLSA overtime 
entitlement.

15. The Fair Labor Standards Act was amended in 
1974 to cover virtually all state and local government 
activities. Those amendments were challenged as uncon- 
stitutional, and in National League of Cities v. Usery, 426 
U.S. 833 (1976), the United States Supreme Court held 
them to be unconstitutional. On February 19, 1985, the 
Supreme Court reversed itself, and in Garcia v. San An- 
tonio Metropolitan Transit Authority, 469 U.S. 528 (1985), 
held that the FLSA applies to all state and local govern- 
ment employees. That decision had the immediate impact 
of rendering illegal many of the standing wage and hour 
policies of defendant Board of Police Commissioners, 
including its use of compensatory time, its calculation of 
overtime entitlement, its record keeping, and its exemp- 
tion from overtime of employees on the basis of rank 
and/or salary. 


16. On November 13, 1985, the Fair Labor Standards 
Amendments of 1985 (Pub. Law 99-150) were enacted. 


App. 5 


The amendments responded to many concerns raised in 
police administration by including special provisions in 
the FLSA which apply only to state and local employees. 

17. Neither prior to nor during April 1, 1986, did 
the defendants Board of Police Commissioners take the 
necessary steps to comply with the new amendments 
before their effective date of April 15, 1986. 


18. It is currently the policy of the Board of Police
