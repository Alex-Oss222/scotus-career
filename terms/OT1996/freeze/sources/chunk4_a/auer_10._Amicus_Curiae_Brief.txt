QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


Regulations promulgated by the Secretary of Labor 
provide that employees are considered to be paid on a 
“salary basis,” and therefore may be “executive,” “admin- 
istrative,” or “professional” employees exempt from the 
overtime requirements of the Fair Labor Standards Act of 
1938, 29 U.S.C. 201 et seq., if they are paid a predeter- 
mined amount, on a weekly or less frequent basis, that “is 
not subject to reduction because of variations in the 
quality or quantity of the work performed.” 29 C.F.R. 
541.118(a). In addition, 29 C.F.R. 541.118(a)(6) pro- 
vides a “window of correction” under which an employer 
that has made a deduction from an employee’s pay that 
would remove the employee from exempt status may 
retroactively restore the employee’s exempt status by reim- 
bursing the employee and promising to comply with the 
salary basis rule in the future. The questions presented 
are: 


1. Whether the pay of a class of employees is “sub- 
ject to” reduction within the meaning of 29 C.F.R. 
541.118(a) when an employer has a written manual that 
permits disciplinary suspensions of less than one week to 
be imposed on members of the class, but no such suspen- 
sion has ever been imposed except in one unique 
circumstance. 

2. Whether an employer may take advantage of the 
window of correction to remedy a one-time disciplinary 
deduction.

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (FLSA), 29 
U.S.C. 201 et seq., contains an exemption from its over- 
time requirements for “employee[s] employed in a bona 
fide executive, administrative, or professional capacity.” 
29 U.S.C. 213(a)(1). This case raises questions con- 
cerning the interpretation of regulations issued by the 
Secretary of Labor pursuant to his authority to “define[] 
and delimit[]” (ibid.) the meaning of those terms. The 
Secretary has a substantial interest in the correct interpre- 
tation of his regulations. At the invitation of the Court, 
the United States filed a brief as amicus curiae at the peti- 
tion stage. 

STATEMENT 

1. Section 7(a)(1) of the FLSA, 29 U.S.C. 207(a)(1), 
requires an employer to pay each of its employees “at a 
rate not less than one and one-half times the regular rate 


(1) 


2 


at which he is employed” for all hours worked in excess 
of 40 hours in a week. Section 13(a)(1) of the Act, 29 
U.S.C. 213(a)(1), provides an exemption from the re- 
quirement of overtime pay for “any employee employed 
in a bona fide executive, administrative, or professional 
capacity * * * (as such terms are defined and delimited 
from time to time by regulations of the Secretary [of 
Labor]).” 

Pursuant to the authority conferred on the Secretary to 
define the scope of the exemption, the Secretary has prom- 
ulgated regulations that provide that, in order to qualify 
for executive, administrative, or professional status, an 
employee must meet both a salary test and a duties test. 
See 29 C.F.R. 541.1 (executive), 541.2 (administrative), 
541.3 (professional). The salary test consists of two ele- 
ments: (1) a requirement that the “exempt” employee 
receive no less than a specified level of compensation; and 
(2) a requirement that the compensation be paid “on a 
salary basis.” This case concerns only the latter compo- 
nent of the salary test: whether the pertinent employees 
have been paid “on a salary basis” as defined by 29 
C.F.R. 541.118(a). 

a. The salary basis test has roots that reach back to 
the enactment of the FLSA in 1938. In that year, the 
Administrator of the Department of Labor’s Wage and 
Hour Division issued regulations providing that, in order 
to come within the Section 13(a)(1) exemption, executive 
and administrative employees must perform duties of a 
specified nature and be “compensated for [their] services 
at not less than $30 * * * for a workweek.” 29 C.F.R. 
541.1 (1938 Supp.); 3 Fed. Reg. 2518 (1938). A salary 
requirement was not initially imposed for professional 
employees. 29 C.F.R. 541.2 (1938 Supp.); 3 Fed. Reg. 
2518 (1938). 

In 1940, the Wage and Hour Division conducted hear- 
ings on the regulations governing the Section 13(a)(1) 
exemption, and the presiding hearing officer issued a re- 
port recommending a salary basis test for all three classes 
of exempt employees. United States Dep’t of Labor, Wage

and Hour Division, “Executive, Administrative, Profes-

Recommendations of the Presiding Officer at Hearings 
Preliminary to Redefinition (1940) (1940 Report). In 
that report, the presiding hearing officer noted that there 
was “surprisingly wide agreement” among employers and 
employees “that a salary qualification in the definition of 
the term ‘executive’ is a valuable and easily applied index 
to the ‘bona fide’ character of the employment.” /d. at 19. 
The basis of that agreement was that “[t]he term ‘execu- 
tive’ implies a certain prestige, status, and importance” 
that is captured by a salary test. /bid. Also, because 
“executive” employees are denied the protection of the 
Act, “[ijt must be assumed that they enjoy compensatory 
privileges,” including a salary “substantially higher” than 
the minimum wages guaranteed under the Act. Ibid. The 
report noted that “[t]he shortest pay period which can 
properly be understood to be appropriate for a person em-

tives that would be satisfied if “the employee is guaranteed 
a net compensation of not less than $30 a week ‘free and 
clear.” Id. at 23 (emphasis added). The report also 
concluded that the inclusion of a salary test was vital in 
defining “administrative” and “professional” employees as 
well. Id. at 26 (“[A] salary criterion constitutes the best 
and most easily applied test of the employer's good faith 
in claiming that the person whose exemption is desired 
is actually of such importance to the firm that he is prop-

administrative capacity.”); id. at 36 (“[I)n order to avoid 
disputes, to assist in the effective enforcement of the act 
and to prevent abuse, it appears essential * * * to include 
a salary test in the definition [of professional].”). 

Based on the presiding officer’s recommendation, the 
Wage and Hour Division Administrator promulgated reg- 
ulations providing that an exempt executive must be “com- 
pensated for his services on a salary basis at not less than 
$30 per week.” 29 C.F.R. 541.1(e) (1940 Supp.); 5

541.3(b) (professional) (1940 Supp.); 5 Fed. Reg. 4077 
(1940) (emphasis added). 

b. In 1944, the Wage and Hour Division issued Re- 
lease No. A-9, which addressed the meaning of “salary 
basis.” The Release stated that an employee will be con- 
sidered to be paid on a salary basis if 


under his employment agreement he regularly re- 
ceives each pay period, on a weekly, biweekly, semi- 
monthly, monthly or annual basis, a predetermined 
amount constituting all or part of his compensation, 
which amount is not subject to reduction because of 
variations in the number of hours worked or in the 
quantity or quality of the work performed during the

Release No. A-9 (Aug. 24, 1944), reprinted in Wage &

a deduction in pay. /bid. Due to particular war-time 
conditions, however, “disciplinary deductions * * * made 
for unreasonable absences” were permissible. /bid.

c. In 1954, the Administrator issued a revised Section 
541.118(a) that remains in force today. That regulation 
was adopted after being proposed for comment, see 19 
Fed. Reg. 1321 (1954), and was issued pursuant to the 
Administrator’s authority under Section 13(a)(1) of the 
Act, see id. at 4405-4406. As in the 1949 regulation, an 
employee is deemed to be paid on a salary basis if, un- 
der the employment agreement, the employee “regularly 
receives * * * a predetermined amount” of compensation 
that “is not subject to reduction because of variations 
‘4m the quality or quantity of the work performed.” 29 
C.F.R. 541.118(a). A “salary basis” employee “must 
receive his full salary for any week in which he performs 
any work without regard to the number of days or hours 
worked,” but need not be paid for any week in which he 
performs no work. /bid. The new version of Section 
541.118(a), however, added exceptions to that rule. One 
exception is that an employer may impose penalties for 
infractions of safety rules of major significance without 
affecting an employee's salaried status. 29 C.F.R. 541.118 
(a)(5). The proposed version of the new regulation 
would have permitted deductions for “other major dis- 
ciplinary reasons” as well. 19 Fed. Reg. 1321, 1322 
(1954) (proposed Section 541.118(a)(5)). “On the 
basis of the comments” received, however, the Administra- 
tor deleted from the final regulation the authorization of 
deductions for other major disciplinary reasons. Jd. at 
4406. Penalties for infractions of work rules that are not 
major safety rules are therefore governed by the general 
salary basis test set forth in paragraph (a). Thus, an 
employee suspended without pay for less than one week 
for a violation of a rule that is not a major safety rule 
would be classified as nonsalaried. On the other hand, 
an otherwise salaried employee who is suspended without

would remain a salaried employee.

b. In light of its experience, the Department of Labor 
has taken a series of steps that address the application 
of the salary basis test to public employers. In January 
1987, the Department issued an enforcement policy, stat- 
ing that the Wage and Hour Division would not generally 
deny a Section 13(a)(1) exemption for an otherwise 


9 


exempt public employee whose pay was reduced for ab- 
sences of less than a day, if such reductions were required 
by state or local law. See 57 Fed. Reg. 37,666, 37,668 
(1992) (quoting policy). In September 1991, the De- 
partment issued an interim final rule providing that pub- 
lic employees could qualify as exempt under Section 13 
(a)(1) even if they are subject to pay deductions for 
periods of less than a day due to personal reasons, ill- 
ness or injury when no leave is available. 56 Fed. Reg. 
45,824-45,826 (1991). The rule was prompted by the 
Department’s recognition that public employers are often 
required by principles of public accountability to reduce 
pay for partial<day absences. /d. at 45,825. That interim 
rule became effective immediately, without notice and 
comment, in order to spare public employers from “po- 
tentially enormous and generally unexpected back wage 
liabilities” to employees who would otherwise be exempt. 
Ibid. 

In August 1992, after notice and comment, the Depart- 
ment issued a final regulation, 29 C.F.R. 541.5d, which 
provides that a public employee who otherwise meets the 
salary basis test shall not be disqualified from exemption 
under 29 C.F.R. 541.1, 541.2 or 541.3 on the ground 
that the employee “is paid according to a pay system 
established by statute, ordinance, or regulation, or by a 
policy or practice established pursuant to principles of 
public accountability * * * which requires the public 
agency employee’s pay to be reduced or such employee 
to be placed on leave without pay for absences for per- 
sonal reasons or because of illness or injury of less than 
one work-day” under specified circumstances. See 57 
Fed. Reg. 37,677 (1992). The effect of the regulation 
is to preserve the exemption in most circumstances in 
which the salary of state and local executive, professional, 
or administrative employees is required to be reduced for 
partial-day absences. In issuing that new regulation, the 
Secretary noted that, while executive, administrative, and 
professional employees in the private sector are nearly 
always paid on a salary basis, id. at 37,672, public em- 
ployers are normally subject to “principles of public ac-

countability” that prohibit paying employees for time not 
worked, id. at 37,667. The Secretary also recognized that, 
if the partial-day absence rule were applied to the public

executive, administrative, and professional employees, a 
result that would be inconsistent with Congress’s intent. 
Id. at 37,672. The Secretary therefore exempted public 
employees from the partial-day absence rule.
