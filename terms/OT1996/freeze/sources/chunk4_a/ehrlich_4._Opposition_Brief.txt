QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 


The questions as framed by the petitioner seriously 


misstate the opinion of the California Supreme Court and 
the issues before this Court. The questions presented by 


the petition are more properly stated as follows: 


Whether a claim that the imposition of a 
recreation fee constitutes a “taking without a 
payment of just compensation” under the Fifth 
Amendment presents a “final judgment” within 
the meaning of 28 USC § 1257, where the Cali- 
fornia Supreme Court has remanded the claim 
to the respondent public agency and the lower 
courts of the State to determine “what, if any, 
recreational fee the evidence might justify.” 


Can the issue of whether an ordinance 
requiring a developer to commit one percent 
(1%) of the value of his proposed improvements 
to public art constitutes a “physical taking” 
under the Fifth Amendment, which was not 
pressed or passed upon in the lower courts, be 
raised for the first time in the petition? 


If the first question presented is answered in 
the affirmative, then may a public agency under 
any circumstances condition the grant of a discre- 
tionary upzoning of property on a requirement 
that a developer pay a fee as a partial mitigation of 
the impacts caused by the upzoning? 


If the second question presented is 
answered in the affirmative, then should this 
Court reverse its unanimous decision in Yee v. 
City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519 (1992), and 
instead hold that regulation designed to pre- 
serve a community’s-aesthetics is a per se taking 
under the Fifth Amendment?

REASONS FOR DENYING THE PETITION. 
I. 


THE CASE IS NOT PROPERLY BEFORE THIS HON- 
ORABLE COURT 


A. The Entire Matter Regarding the Recreation Fee 
Is Still Pending in the Trial Court. 


The California Supreme Court has remanded this 
matter to the lower courts with directions to remand the 
matter to Culver City for reconsideration. That recon- 
sideration will include an application of the test required 
by the Just Compensation Clause of the Fifth Amendment 
as interpreted by this Honorable Court in Dolan v. City of 
Tigard, 512 U.S. __, 114 S.Ct. 2309 (1994) and the Califor- 
nia Supreme Court in Ehrlich, supra. Until that recon- 
sideration is completed and the highest California court 
has ruled on the appropriateness of any fee Culver City 
may impose, no final judgment will have been entered in 
this case and the federal question whether Culver City 
has taken private property without payment of just com- 
pensation, will remain unresolved. Without a final judg- 
ment, this Honorable Court has no jurisdiction to hear 
this matter. 28 USC § 1257. 


In Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 U.S. 469, 477 
(1975), this Honorable Court reiterated that there are but 
a few exceptions which permit this Court to grant cer- 
tiorari when a final judgment has not been rendered 
below. Cox discusses the four categories of exceptions. 


The first category deals with those situations where 
the federal issue has been conclusively determined or 
that conclusion is preordained. Cox, supra, at 479. As 


described previously, that factor is not found in the 
instant case. The California Supreme Court has directed 
Culver City to reevaluate the mitigation fee based upon 
the principles articulated by that court. Ehrlich, supra, at 
p. 885; Petition Appendix A, p. A44. 


Following remand, the city must determine 
whether and to what extent approval of plain- 
tiff’s requested land use changes justify the 
imposition of a recreation fee as a means of 
compensating it for the additional costs of 
attracting the development of comparable recre- 
ational facilities for its residents. The determina- 
tion of such a fee will, of course, require the city 
to make specific findings supported by substan- 
tial evidence — that is, the city “must make some 
effort to quantify its findings” supporting any 
fee, beyond “conclusory statements,” although 
“{no] precise mathematical calculation is 
required” ... . (Dolan, supra, 512 at p. __, 114 
S.Ct. at p. 2322.) 


Ehrlich, supra, at p. 885; Petition Appendix A, p. A44. 


Based on the foregoing, it is clear that the first Cox 
exception to 28 USC § 1257 does not apply. The federal 
issue in this matter has not been conclusively determined 
nor is such conclusion preordained. 


The second category of exceptions are those in which 
no matter what the outcome of the lower courts, the 
federal issues survive and require decision by this Court. 
Cox, supra, at p. 480. After remand to Culver City, one of 
three things will happen. 


First, Culver City may determine that no fee could be 
supported, therefore, no fee would be imposed. If that 


10 


happens, then no further judicial determination as to 
whether a regulatory taking has occurred would be nec- 
essary and this matter would be moot. 


Second, Culver City could impose a fee which the 
petitioner may accept. If that occurs, then there would be 
no justiciable controversy remaining and this matter 
would again be moot. 


Finally, Culver City could impose a fee which the 
petitioner might resist. If that happens, then the matter 
would be returned to the trial court for a determination 
of whether the fee constitutes a taking of private property 
without payment of just compensation. Based on the fore- 
going, there are two possible outcomes after remand 
which will not require any review by this Honorable 
Court. Therefore, the second Cox exception does not 


apply. 


The third category of exceptions are those cases 
where the federal claim has been finally decided but the 
further proceedings on the merits in the state courts will 
preclude review of the federal claim no matter what the 
outcome of the case. Id. at p. 481. As stated previously, 
the federal claim has not been finally decided. However, 
even assuming it had been, this exception still does not 
apply. As shown above, one of the possible outcomes in 
the matter below would permit the petitioner to retain his 
rights to seek this Honorable Court’s review. Therefore, 
exception category number three does not apply. 


Finally, the fourth category of exceptions relates to 
those cases where the federal issue has again been finally 
resolved but the further state court proceedings could be 
precluded by a different outcome on the federal issue. Id. 


11 


at p. 482. Here the only matter left to be resolved is the 
federal question. The petitioner’s claims are based on the 
Just Compensation Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The 
California Supreme Court's remand directs Culver City to 
reconsider its imposition of the recreation fee based on 
principles required under the Fifth Amendment as it 
relates to claims of a regulatory taking. If the matter is 
returned to the trial court after Culver City’s reconsidera- 
tion, then that Fifth Amendment claim will likewise be 
reviewed and the petitioner will retain his rights to seek 
review from this Court after pursuing his case through 
the appellate system in California. Therefore, the fourth 
category of exception also is inapplicable. 


Further support for the inapplicability of the four 
recognized exceptions to the jurisdictional requirement of 
28 USC § 1257 can be found in the reasons for that 
requirement. 


The finality requirement of 28 USC § 1257 which 
limits our review of state court judgments, 
serves several ends: (1) it avoids piecemeal 
review by federal courts of state court decisions; 
(2) it avoids giving advisory opinions in cases 
where there may be no real “case” or “contro- 
versy” in the sense of Art III; (3) it limits federal 
review of state court determinations of federal 
constitutional issues to leave at a minimum fed- 
eral intrusion in state affairs. 


North Dakota State Board of Pharmacy v. Snyder’s Drug 
Stores, Inc., 414 U.S. 156, 159 (1973). In the instant case, all 
three reasons for 28 USC § 1257 will be met by denial of 
the petition. 


12 


First, this Honorable Court is being requested to 
conduct a piecemeal review of the relevant issues. The 
petition asks for review of one-half of the issues involved 
in this case. This Court is being asked to review the state 
court’s determination regarding the Nollan v. California 
Coastal Commission, 483 U.S. 825 (1987) half of the Nollan/ 
Dolan test before the state courts have reached a final 
decision on the Dolan half of the test. This request is 
clearly a “piecemeal” approach. 


Second, this Court is also being asked to give what 
could very well be an advisory decision. As stated earlier, 
two of the three possible outcomes which may occur after 
the remand of the matter to Culver City will result in “no 
real case or controversy”. With both of those outcomes, 
no further judicial review of the subject issues will be 
required. 


Finally, if this Court grants review at this time, then it 
would intrude on the pending state proceeding. As noted 
before, the California Supreme Court has directed the 
trial court to issue an order directing Culver City to 
review the amount of the fee. Upon that review, if Culver 
City imposes a fee, then the petitioner might continue his 
challenge. That challenge would first be brought in the 
trial court and then proceed through the state appellate 
system. Those state court proceedings should be permit- 
ted to continue to final judgment without intrusion from 
the federal bench at this time. 


In addition to the foregoing policy reasons, this 
Court should deny the petition in order to give full effect 
to its previous order in this matter. For the petitioner to 
prevail on his argument that this matter has reached a 


13 


final judgment in the courts below, the two-prong test 
established by Nollan and Dolan would be meaningless. 
The petitioner’s argument would mean that no federal 
issue remains in dispute in this matter because the Cali- 
fornia Supreme Court has reached a conclusion about 
only the first prong of the Nollan/Dolan test. Petitioner’s 
argument, in fact, even makes this Court’s remand of the 
instant case “for further reconsideration in light of Dolan 
[]” ineffectual. Ehrlich v. City of Culver City, 512 U.S. __, 
114 S.Ct. 2731 (1994). Clearly, this Court required the 
lower courts to reconsider both prongs of the test applica- 
ble to this type of regulatory taking claim. 


Upon that reconsideration, the California Supreme 
Court has determined that the “essential nexus” prong of 
the Nollan/Dolan test has been met. Additionally, based on 
the facts it had before it, that court determined that 
Culver City had not met the “rough proportionality” 
prong of Nollan/Dolan. On that latter issue, the matter will 
be returned to Culver City for further proceedings, simi- 
lar to what happened in Dolan itself after the case was 
returned to the Oregon Supreme Court. See, Dolan v. City 
of Tigard, 319 Or. 567 (1994). 


Given that the second prong of the Nollan/Dolan test 
is not yet final either administratively or in the State’s 
courts, accepting the petition would constitute a gross 
federal interference by this Court with ongoing state 
administrative and judicial functions. As the Chief Justice 
has written, the “final judgment” requirement serves an 
important role in preserving harmonious State-federal 
relations. 


14 


I consider § 1257 finality to be but one of a 
number of congressional provisions reflecting 
concern that uncontrolled federal judicial inter- 
ference with state administrative and judicial 
functions would have untoward consequences 
for our federal system. [Footnote omitted.] 


Cox, supra, at p. 503 (Rehnquist, J., dissenting.) 


The final judgment requirement has been a cor- 
nerstone of this Court’s jurisprudence since 1789. As 
Justice Frankfurter has stated: “This requirement is not 
one of those technicalities to be easily scorned. 't is an 
important factor in the smooth working of our federal 
system.” Radio Station WOW, Inc. v. Johnson, 326 U.S. 120, 
124 (1945). 


Moreover, even in Cox, the Court explicitly recog- 
nized that in cases involving the Fifth Amendment’s tak- 
ings clause, such as the case at bar, there is often an initial 
interlocutory decision involving a federal question with 
another federal question to be decided later by the state 
courts. But this Court held in Cox that: 


A state judgment is not final unless it covers 
both aspects of that integral problem. [Citations 
omitted. | 


Cox, supra, at 479 n. 6. 


In the case at bar, the petitioner has not yet received a 
final state judgment on the second aspect of the integral 
Fifth Amendment problem, i.e., the amount of fee that 
may be constitutionally imposed on his development. As 
is discussed above, under several logical scenarios that 
issue may never become a “case or controversy.” This is 
not the case to warrant further expansion of the “final 


15 


judgment” requirement and a reversal of long-standing 
authorities. 


Based on all of the foregoing, at this time this Honor-

intrusions into private property. 


A land use regulation does not effect a taking if 
it “substantially advances(s) legitimate state 
interests” and does not “den(y) an owner eco-

Tiburon, 447 U.S. 255, 260 (1980). [Modifications

interference with property can be characterized 
as a physical invasion by government, [citation

some public program adjusting the benefits and 
burdens of economic life to promote the com- 
mon good. 


Penn Central Transp. Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104, 124 
(1978). 


Culver City has clear authority to plan its community 
for various uses, including recreational facilities which 
the city council has determined are necessary for the

Based on the foregoing long-recognized authority, the

This is true even if, as in this case, the property

THE ART PROGRAM IS NOT A PER SE PHYSICAL 
TAKING OF PRIVATE PROPERTY 


A physical taking occurs only when a governmental 
agency physically occupies private property. 


Because the Escondido rent control ordinance 
does not compel a landowner to suffer the phys- 
ical occupation of his property, it does not effect 
a per se taking under Loretto. 


Yee v. City of Escondido, supra, at p. 539. Prior to that 
holding, this Court decided Loretto v. Teleprompter Man- 
hattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419, 441 (1982): 


Our holding today is very narrow. We affirm the 
traditional rule that a permanent physical occu- 
pation of property is a taking. In such a case, the 
property owner entertains a historically rooted 
expectation of compensation, and the character 
of the invasion is qualitatively more intrusive 
than perhaps any other category of property 
regulation. We do not, however, question the 
equally substantial authority upholding a State’s 
broad authority to impose appropriate restric- 
tions upon an owner’s use of his property. 
{Emphasis original.] 


As stated earlier, pursuant to Culver City’s art pro- 
gram a developer may place art work on a subject project

site and retain all ownership interest in that art work and 
the site. No publicly owned art work would be placed on 
the developer’s property nor is any property given to the 
city. Alternatively, the developer may pay an in lieu fee. If 
that fee is paid, then no art work, either private or public, 
is placed on the developer’s property. As discussed 
below, the program is more akin to aesthetic use regula- 
tions which require certain types of architectural design. 
Based on the foregoing, it is clear that no physical occu- 
pation of private property is required or even implicated

NO HEIGHTENED SCRUTINY NEED BE APPLIED TO 
CULVER CITY’S ART PROGRAM 


Culver City’s art program requires neither a dedica- 
tion of land or other property, nor the payment of any 
fees. Instead, it provides the owner of a large develop- 
ment with at least two options.? An art work can be 
incorporated into the development project or an in lieu 
fee can be paid to the city which is deposited into a 
special fund for the purchase of art work. In the first 
instance, the developer retains all right, title and interest 
in the art work. In the latter, Culver City could use the 
money to purchase art work to be placed on public prop- 
erty. The choice is left to the absolute discretion of the 
developer. The art requirement was adopted by ordinance 
through a legislative process and applies throughout the
