A. Procedural History 


Petitioner Gregg Uhlrig, as the executor of the estate of 
Stephanie Uhirig, and for himself and the couple’s two minor 
children as individuals, filed this suit against respondents, 
various Kansas officials responsible for the administration of 
state mental hospitals, in the United States District Court for the 
District of Kansas in 1993. In the complaint, petitioners alleged a 
substantive due process claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and 
pendent state law tort claims. The District Court dismissed the 
state law claims, dismissed as plaintiffs all but Gregg Uhlrig in 
his capacity as executor, and granted summary judgment to 
respondents on the § 1983 claim. 


Petitioners appealed only the District Court’s ruling on the 
§ 1983 claim. Ina published opinion, Uhirig, et al. v. Harder, et

judgment in respondents’ favor. App. C. Petitioners filed a timely 
petition for rehearing and rehearing en banc which the Tenth 
Circuit denied on September 29, 1995.

2. The Tenth Circuit's Disposition of the Appeal 


Petitioners appealed only the District Court’s ruling on the 
§ 1983 claim to the Tenth Circuit. In Uhlrig, et al. v. Harder, et al., 
64 F.3d 567 (10th Cir. 1995), the Tenth Circuit affirmed the 
judgment in respondents’ favor. The Tenth Circuit began its 
analysis of the substantive due process claim by declaring that in 
order to state a substantive due process claim “Plaintiff must 
demonstrate that the state acted in a manner that ‘shock([s] the 
conscience.’"” App. C, 20a (citing Collins v. City of Harker 
Heights, 503 U.S. 115, 126 (1992) (quoting Rochin v. California, 
342 U.S. 165, 172) (1952)). 


The Tenth Circuit observed that state actors generally are not 
responsible for harms caused by private actors, deriving that 
proposition from this Court’s decision in DeShaney v. Winnebago 
County Dept. of Social Services, 489 U.S. 189 (1989). Id. at 21a. 
The Tenth Circuit declared, however, that this Court in DeShaney 


5 


recognized the two exceptions to the rule: (1) the special 
relationship doctrine and (2) the “danger creation” theory. Jd. at 
2ia-22a. The Tenth Circuit dismissed the notion that the 
respondents, as Stephanie Uhirig’s employer, in this case had a 
special relationship to her which would give rise to liability under 
the first DeShaney exception, id. at 22a, and, indeed, petitioners 
never made such a contention. Instead, the Tenth Circuit focused 
on the “danger creation” exception Id. Pointing out that “the 
conduct complained of in the instant case was committed by a 
private third party,” id. at 20a, the Tenth Circuit declared that 
“Plaintiff must demonstrate . . . that the state recklessly created 
the danger that caused the constitutional violation.” Id. at 20a- 
21a (citing Graham v. Independent School Dist. No. I- 89,22 F.3d 
991, 994-95 (10th Cir. 1994), which in turn relied upon 
DeShaney). The Tenth Circuit stated that the “classic case” of a 
state official incurring liability under a danger creation theory 
was Wood v. Ostrander, 879 F.2d 583 (9th Cir. 1989), cert. 
denied, 498 U.S. 938 (1990), in which police officers were held 
liable under § 1983 on a substantive due process claim when they 
impounded the plaintiff’s car and abandoned her in a high crime 
area at 2:30 a.m., resulting in her being assaulted by third parties. 
Id. at 22a. 


The Tenth Circuit opined that, because many state activities 
have the potential to create some danger, liability under §1983 
arises only when the danger rises to the level of being “a special 
danger.” Jd. at 22a-23a. Moreover, in the Tenth Circuit’s view, 
petitioners were required to establish not only that respondents 
recklessly or intentionally created a special danger to Stephanie 
Uhirig, but also that respondents’ conduct “shocks the 
conscience.” Jd. at 23a. The Tenth Circuit derived its “shocks the 
conscience” requirement from this Court’s decision in Collins v. 
City of Harker Heights, 503 U.S. 115 (1992). App. C, 23a. 


Ultimately, the Tenth Circuit phrased the standard for a 


6 


“danger creation” claim as follows: “Did Defendants Recklessly 
Create A Special Danger To Uhlrig That Shocks the 
Conscience?” Jd. at 26a. Attempting to merge this Court’s recent 
substantive due process decisions, the Tenth Circuit declared that 
petitioners had to demonstrate the following five elements in 
order to state a cognizable, substantive due process claim based 
on the “danger creation” theory in a §1983 suit against state 
officials: 


(1) [that] Uhlrig was a member of a limited 
and specifically definable group; (2) 
Defendants’ conduct put Uhirig and the other 
members of that group at substantial risk of 
serious, immediate and proximate harm; (3) 
the risk was obvious or known; (4) 
Defendants acted recklessly in conscious 
disregard of that risk; and (5) such conduct, 
when viewed in total, is conscience shocking. 


Id. at 26a. 


Based on its reading of the evidence before it, the Tenth 
Circuit opined that petitioners had failed to raise a genuine 
dispute of fact on the second, fourth and fifth elements. /d. at 27a. 
In the Tenth Circuit’s view, the record established that the 
respondents did not put Uhlrig at substantial risk of serious harm, 
did not act recklessly and did not act in a “conscience shocking” 
manner. Therefore, the court affirmed the District Court’s grant 


of summary judgment.

A. Introduction 


In DeShaney v. Winnebago County Dept. of Social Services, 
489 U.S. 189, 196-97 (1989), this Court affirmed the general 
Proposition that state actors ordinarily have no liability for harms 
caused by private actors. The plaintiffs in DeShaney were a 
young boy and his mother who sought to hold state officials liable 
in a $1983 suit after the boy’s father brutally beat him, severely 
and permanently injuring the child. State social services officials 
were aware of problems in the boy’s home and had investigated 
the situation but did not remove the boy from his father’s custody 
until after the tragic beating. The plaintiffs’ theory was that the 
State had a constitutional duty to protect the boy, at least once the 
State became aware of the situation. 


This Court rejected that claim, concluding that substantive 
due process principles ordinarily do not impose upon state 
officials a constitutional duty to protect persons from harm that 
may be caused by private parties. 489 U.S. at 194-97. The Court 
observed that there are €xceptions to the general “no duty” rule, 
the primary one being the existence of a “special relationship” 
between the victim and the state Officials, for example, when state 


8 


officials have an inmate or mental patient in their custody. Jd. at 
197-201. More importantly for purposes of this case and the 
issues it raises, the Court emphasized that the state officials in 
DeShaney “played no part in creating” the danger to the boy. /d. 
at 197 (emphasis added). The Court observed near the end of its 
opinion that “[wJhile the State may have been aware of the 
dangers that Joshua faced in the free world, it played no part in 
their creation, nor did it do anything to render him any more 
vulnerable to them.” Jd. at 201 (emphasis added). The Court in 
DeShaney did not expand upon the “danger creation” notion 
beyond the two statements quoted above, perhaps because the 
Court easily concluded that, even if such an exception exists, it 
would not have applied in DeShaney, or perhaps because the 
Court did not intend to endorse or recognize such an exception. 


Many lower federal courts have interpreted the foregoing 
statements as acknowledging and endorsing a “danger creation” 
exception to the general “no duty” rule for state actors. See, e.g., 
L.W. v. Grubbs, 974 F.2d 119, 121 (9th Cir. 1992), cert. denied, 
113 S. Ct. 2442 (1993). As one respected federal judge has 
articulated the theory behind the “danger creation” exception, 
“{iJf a state puts a man in a position of danger from private 
persons and then fails to protect him .. . it is as much an active 
tortfeasor as if it had thrown him into a snake pit.” Bowers v. 
DeVito, 686 F.2d 616 (7th Cir. 1982) (Posner, J.). A few lower 
federal courts, however, have questioned whether the exception 
actually exists. See, e.g., Leffall v. Dallas Indep. School Dist., 28 
F.3d 521, 530 (Sth Cir. 1994) (questioning whether the Court 
intended in DeShaney to recognize a separate and independent 
“danger creation” exception to the “no duty” rule or was merely 
describing the nature and scope of the “special relationship” 
exception). 


This case squarely raises the “danger creation” exception 
and required the lower courts to grapple with the nature and scope

B. The Lower Courts And The Practicing Bar Require This 
Court’s Guidance Regarding the Nature and Scope of the 
“Danger Creation” Exception to the “No Duty” Rule. 


Although many lower courts have confronted and grappled 
with the “danger creation” exception to the general “no duty” 
rule, including the Tenth Circuit on several occasions, no other 
circuit has adopted the five element test that the Tenth Circuit 
relied upon in this case. In particular, no other Court of Appeals 
has engrafted the general substantive due process “shocks the 
conscience” standard, as articulated in cases such as Rochin v. 
California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952) (where police officers 
forcibly and against the plaintiff's will pumped his stomach), and 
Collins v. City of Harker Heights, 503 U.S. 115, 126 (1992) 
(where a decedent’s widow sued her husband’s employer, the 
city, in a §1983 suit alleging failure to maintain a safe work 
environment), onto the “danger creation” exception to the “no 
duty” rule. In essence, the Tenth Circuit's decision requires a 
plaintiff in petitioners’ position to prove that (1) the defendant 
acted recklessly (2) in creating a special danger which (3) shocks 
the conscience. 


There are at least two potential faults with the Tenth Circuit's 
standard. First, nothing in this Court’s DeShaney decision 
suggests that the Court envisioned or intended the merging of 
substantive due process concepts that have been developed in two 
very different contexts. Rather, DeShaney reads as though, and it 
would seem logical to assume that, when state officials through 
their affirmative actions recklessly or intentionally create a 
significant danger to others, their conduct goes beyond the 
bounds of the constitutionally acceptable. In other words, 
reckless or intentional affirmative conduct that creates a 
significant danger to others “shocks the conscience.” The Tenth 
Circuit, however, suggests that even if state officials create a 
danger to others and do so recklessly or even intentionally, there

that the resulting situation and harm “shocks the conscience.”

involve harm caused by third parties. Danger creation cases such

very different types of substantive due process cases. At bottom,

The second problem with the Tenth Circuit's test is that, even 
assuming there is merit in the Tenth Circuit’s coupling of the 
“danger creation” theory and the “shocks the conscience” 
standard, it provides virtually no meaningful guidance to lawyers 
and clients trying to decide whether to file and litigate such 
claims or to the lower federal courts which ultimately must rule 
on these claims. Defining “recklessness” is difficult enough for 
the practicing bar and the lower courts, but add the undefined 
notions of “special” danger and “shocks the conscience” and the 
result is an indecipherable judicial test that provides no clarity 
and no guidance. From the standpoint of the practicing bar and 
the lower federal courts, it might be better to eliminate the 
“danger creation” exception altogether, rather than to create 
standards that provide no guidance and make it impossible to 
identify the cases which might actually satisfy the standard. 


Perhaps that is why no other Court of Appeals has adopted 
the Tenth Circuit’s standard and, in fact, have reached results 
directly contrary to Uhirig on virtually identical facts. The most 
striking contrast to the Tenth Circuit’s approach in this case is 
provided by the Ninth Circuit’s decision affirming potential 
liability in L.W. v. Grubbs, 974 F.2d 119 (9th Cir. 1992), cert. 
denied, 113 S. Ct. 2442 (1993). In Grubbs, a registered female 
nurse employed by the state of Oregon to work in a medium 
security prison was raped and terrorized by a male inmate 
assigned to be her assistant in the prison’s medical clinic. The 
defendants, state employees who were the nurse’s supervisors at 
the prison, led her to believe that she would not work alone \. ith 
violent sex offenders. Nonetheless, the assistant they chose for 
her was a violent sex offender who had failed all treatment

programs at the prison and was viewed as posing a significant risk 
to any female. Once alone with the nurse in the clinic, the inmate 
raped and terrorized her. The nurse sued the defendants under 
§1983 on the theory that they intentionally or recklessly placed 
her in a position of known danger, i.e., the “danger creation” 
theory. The District Court dismissed the suit but the Ninth Circuit 
reversed. 


Relying on this Court's statements in DeShaney that the state 
officials in DeShaney played no part in creating the danger to the 
young boy, the Ninth Circuit concluded that “DeShaney did not 
rule that custody was required where the state affirmatively 
causes the harm.” 974 F.2d at 121. According to the Ninth 
Circuit, “DeShaney thus Suggests that had the state created the 
danger, Joshua might have recovered even though he was not in 
custody.” Jd. Applying the danger creation exception to the case 
before it, the Ninth Circuit easily concluded that the 
defendants—based on the allegations in the complaint — “used 
their authority as state correctional Officers to create an 
opportunity for [the inmate] to assault [the nurse] that would not 
otherwise have existed.” Jd. 


The Ninth Circuit then discussed Collins v. City of Harker 
Heights, which had been decided more than four months prior to 
the argument and six months prior to the decision in Grubbs. But, 
unlike the Tenth Circuit, the Ninth Circuit did not inquire whether 
the defendants’ affirmative conduct “shocked the conscience.” 
Indeed, the Ninth Circuit distinguished Collins on the ground that 
the plaintiff in Grubbs, unlike the widow in Collins, “alleges that 
the Defendants took affirmative steps to place her at significant 
risk, and that they knew of the risks. Contrary to Defendants’ 
arguments, Collins actually supports [the nurse’s} claim.” /d. at 
122. Ultimately, the Ninth Circuit’s standard is a relatively 
simple one: Did the defendants recklessly or intentionally engage 
in affirmative acts which created a significant danger to the 
plaintiff?

compel reversal of the grant of summary judgment in 
respondents’ favor. Moreover, all other circuits to consider the 
“danger creation” exception have adopted a standard identical or 
similar to the Grubbs standard. See, ¢.g., Wood v. Ostrander, 879

affirmative act of preventing private individuals from attempting

814 F.2d 277 (6th Cir. 1987) (en banc) (county officials who 
permitted inmate to drive police car had potential liability for 
substantive due process violation when inmate used car to pull

such claims on the merits, the Courts of Appeal have looked

MEMORANDUM AND ORDER 


The present action has been brought by the heirs of 
Stephanie Uhlrig and by the administrator of her estate against 
five administrators of the mental health system of the State of 
Kansas. After extensive discovery, the defendants have moved 
for summary judgment, and a hearing on the arguments relating 
to these motions was held May 2, 1994. 


Summary judgment is proper where the pleadings, 
depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, 
together with affidavits, if any, show there is no genuine issue as 
to any material fact, and that the moving party is entitled to 
judgment as a matter of law. Fed.R.Civ.P. 56(c). In considering a 
motion for summary judgment, the court must examine all 
evidence in a light most favorable to the opposing party. 
McKenzie v. Mercy Hospital, 854 F.2d 365, 367 (10th Cir. 1988). 
The party moving for summary judgment must demonstrate its 
entitlement to summary judgment beyond a reasonable doubt. 
Ellis v. El Paso Natural Gas Co., 754 F.2d 884, 885 (10th Cir. 
1985). The moving party need not disprove plaintiff’s claim; it 
need only establish that the factual allegations have no legal 
significance. Dayton Hudson Corp. v. Macerich Real Estate Co., 
812 F.2d 1319, 1323 (10th Cir. 1987). 


In resisting a motion for summary judgment, the opposing 
party may not rely upon mere allegations or denials contained in 
its pleadings or briefs. Rather, the nonmoving party must come 
forward with specific facts showing the presence of a genuine 
issue of material fact for trial and significant probative evidence 
supporting the allegation. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 
U.S. 242, 256 (1986). Once the moving party has carried its 
burden under Rule 56(c), the party opposing summary judgment 


Sa 
Appendix B 


must do more than simply show there is some metaphysical doubt 
as to the material facts. “In the language of the Rule, the 
nonmoving party must come forward with ‘specific facts 
showing that there is a genuine issue for trial.’ ” Matsushita Elec. 
Indus. Co., Ltd. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986) 
(quoting Fed.R.Civ.P. 56(e)) (emphasis in Matsushita). One of 
the principal purposes of the summary judgment rule is to isolate 
and dispose of factually unsupported claims or defenses, and the 
rule should be interpreted in a way that allows it to accomplish 
this purpose. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett,477 U.S. 317 (1986).

All defendants argue that the plaintiffs’ claims are barred by 
the Eleventh Amendment, and that the present action — which 
includes claims by Stephanie Uhirig’s heirs — has not been 
brought by the proper parties. The plaintiffs concede that under 
the Eleventh Amendment, their federal claims against the

the plaintiffs stress that Gregg Uhlrig properly appears in his

capacity as administrator of Stephanie Uhirig’s estate. Berry v.

action also names as plaintiffs, however, Gregg Uhlrig and the

Uhirig. The court finds that these are not proper parties to the 
present action. The plaintiffs’ reliance on Trujillo v. Board of

the claims of the heirs will be dismissed. Apodaca v. Rio Arriba 
County Sheriff's Dept., 905 F.2d 1445 (10th Cir. 1990). 


The next issue is whether the plaintiffs have presented a 
colorable claim of deprivation of due process under Section 
1983. It has been held that there can be no due process violation 
for failing to protect a person against private violence. DeShaney 
v. Winnebago County Dept. of Soc. Serv., 489 U.S. 189, 196 
(1989). The Supreme Court has held that the constitution does not 
impose a duty upon the state to provide employees with minimal 
levels of workplace safety. Collins v, City of Harker Heights, 503 
U.S. _, 112 S.Ct. 1061, 117 L.Ed.2d 261 (1992). See Walker v. 
Rowe, 791 F.2d 507 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 479 U.S. 994 (1986). 


The plaintiffs argue that liability may exist where there is 
evidence that the defendants acted recklessly with regard to 
public safety, citing decisions such as Medina v. City and County 
of Denver, 960 F.2d 1493, 1496 (10th Cir. 1992); Archuleta v. 
McShan, 897 F.2d 495, 499 (10th Cir. 1990); Harris v. Maynard, 
843 F.2d 414, 416 (10th Cir. 1988). However, the court finds no 
evidence of recklessness on the part of the administrators named 
as defendants herein. There is no evidence of any knowledge by 


12a 


Appendix B 


the defendants of the dangerous propensities of Waddell. There is 
no evidence that the defendants willfully concealed the presence 
of a known danger from Stephanie Uhirig. Cf Cornelius v. Town 
of Highland Blake, 880 F.2d 348 (11th Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 
494 U.S. 1066 (1990); Nishiyama v. Dickson County, 814 F.2d 
277 (6th Cir. 1987). 


In signing a copy of her job description, Uhirig had 
acknowledged the potential danger inherent in her job. The 
plaintiffs emphasize that portion of Uhirig’s job description 
which states that her position was one of “minimal hazards.” As a 
general matter, this may be true, but the description also clearly 
and explicitly warns that the danger faced by workers may 
increase in some cases. The description indicates the potential for 
serious hazards, acknowledging the possibility that “a patient 
may become aggressive or combative.” The description states, 
under the heading “Consequences of Actions and Decisions,” 
that the erroneous handling of a patient “could result in injury to 
client/patient and staff.” 


There is no evidence in the present case that the defendants 
recklessly placed Stephanie Uhirig’s life at risk by ignoring a 
known danger. The defendants are administrators charged with 
the operation of the state mental health system. They are not 
treating physicians with knowledge of individual patient 
histories. The court finds that the plaintiffs’ claims pursuant to 42 
U.S.C. § 1983 are without merit. 


The court further finds that the defendants in any event 
would be entitled to the protections of qualified immunity. See 
Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520, 547 (1979). The Seventh Circuit has 
observed that there is “no constitutional right to be protected by 
the state against being murdered by criminals or madmen.”

Bowers v. DeVito, 686 F.2d 616, 618 (7th Cir. 1982). The 
plaintiffs have presented nothing to indicate that any of the 
defendants violated any clearly established law in deciding, in the 
face of significant spending cuts, to close a psychiatric ward of a 
state hospital. To the contrary, it would appear that broad 
discretion is necessary in determining how to meet such 
administrative and financial decisions. Accordingly, the court 
finds that the plaintiffs should be entitled to qualified immunity.

court will grant summary judgment on plaintiffs’ state law

II. DISCUSSION 


In granting Defendants’ motion for summary judgment 
based on qualified immunity, the district court correctly followed 
the approach set forth in Siegert v. Gilley, 500 U.S. 226, 232 
(1991). That is, the court first determined that, under the facts of 
the instant case, Plaintiffs failed to state an actionable claim, and 
then concluded that, even if Plaintiff’s claim was actionable, it 
was not clearly established under the law of the Tenth Circuit. In 
reviewing the district court’s rulings, we also follow the approach 
set forth in Siegert, and affirm. 


We review the district court’s grant of summary judgment by 
applying the same legal standard used by the district court 
pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c). Universal Money Crtrs., Inc. v. 
AT&T, 22 F.3d 1527, 1529 (10th Cir.), cert. denied, 1158S. Ct. 655 
(1994); Applied Genetics Int'l Inc. v. First Affiliated Sec., Inc., 
912 F.2d 1238, 1241 (10th Cir. 1990). That is, we first consider 
whether there is a genuine issue of material fact in dispute; if not, 
then we must determine if the district court correctly applied the 
substantive law. Applied Genetics, 912 F.2d at 1241. In 
determining whether there is a genuine dispute of material fact, 
“we examine the factual record and reasonable inferences 
therefrom in the light most favorable to the party opposing 
summary judgment.” Applied Genetics, 912 F.2d at 1241. 


In order to prevail on their substantive due process claim, 
Plaintiff must demonstrate that the state acted in a manner that

U.S. 165, 172 (1952)). Moreover, as the conduct complained of in 
the instant case was committed by a private third party (i.e. 
Waddell) rather than by a state actor, Plaintiff must demonstrate

D. Qualified Immunity 


In accordance with the approach set forth in Siegert v. Gilley, 
500 U.S. 226, 232 (1991), we first concluded that Plaintiff clearly 
failed to satisfy the substantive requirements for stating a § 1983 
claim against Defendants based on a substantive due process 
violation arising from the acts of a third party — i.e. a non-

governmental actor. Second, to the extent that Plaintiff is asking 
us to extend the scope of § 1983 liability, we still would not assign 
§ 1983 liability to Defendants because such an extension is not 
directly supported by any controlling precedent (and indeed, is 
undercut by Medina). We conclude that Defendants acted in an 
objectively reasonable manner based on what “a reasonably 
competent public official [would] know [of] the law governing 
his conduct.” Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 819 (1982); 
Medina, 960 F.2d at 1498 (in order to overcome a defense of 
qualified immunity, a plaintiff must demonstrate that either a 
Tenth Circuit or Supreme Court case controls the issue or that the 
clearly established weight of authority supports plaintiff’s 
claim). Thus, we also affirm the district court’s judgment that 
Defendants are protected from liability by their shield of 
qualified immunity. 


CONCLUSION 


While Uhlrig’s murder was undeniably tragic, it was not the 
result of reckless and “conscience shocking” conduct by the state 
mental health administrators sued in the instant case. Section 
1983 does not convert all harms into substantive due process 
violations, but rather only provides a cause of action for those 
reckless government actions which “shock the conscience.” 
Here, we conclude that the record establishes that Defendants did 
not (1) put Uhlrig at substantial risk of serious harm; (2) act 
recklessly in conscious disregard of such risk; or (3) act in a 
“conscience shocking” manner. Thus, the district court properly 
granted summary judgment for Defendants both on the merits of 
Plaintiff’s claim as well as based on Defendants’ qualified 
immunity defense. Therefore, we AFFIRM the district court’s 
grant of Defendants’ motion for summary judgment.
