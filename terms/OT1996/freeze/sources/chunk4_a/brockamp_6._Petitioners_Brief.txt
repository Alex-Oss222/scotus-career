STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


1. Section 6511 of the Internal Revenue Code, 26 
U.S.C. 6511, provides in relevant part: 


(a) Period of limitation on filing claim 


Claim for credit or refund of an overpayment of 
any tax imposed by this title in respect of which 
tax the taxpayer is required to file a return shall 
be filed by the taxpayer within 3 years from the 
time the return was filed or 2 years from the time 
the tax was paid, whichever of such periods 
expires the later, or if no return was filed by the 
taxpayer, within 2 years from the time the tax was 
paid. Claim for credit or refund of an overpayment 
of any tax imposed by this title which is required 
to be paid by means of a stamp shall be filed by the 
taxpayer within 3 years from the time the tax was 


paid. 


(b) Limitation on allowance of credits and 
refunds 


(1) Filing of claim within prescribed period 


No credit or refund shall be allowed or made 
after the expiration of the period of limitation 
prescribed in subsection (a) for the filing of a 
claim for credit or refund, unless a claim for 
credit or refund is filed by the taxpayer 
within such period. 


(2) Limit on amount of credit or refund 


(A) Limit where claim filed within 3- 
year period 


If the claim was filed by the taxpayer 
during the 3-year period prescribed in 
subsection (a), the amount of the credit or 
refund shall not exceed the portion of the 
tax paid within the period, immediately 
preceding the filing of the claim, equal to 
3 years plus the period of any extension 
of time for filing the return. If the tax 
was required to be paid by means of a 
stamp, the amount of the credit or refund 
shall not exceed the portion of the tax 
paid within the 3 years immediately 
preceding the filing of the claim. 


(B) Limit where claim not filed 
within 3-year period 


If the claim was not filed within such 
3-year period, the amount of the credit or 
refund shall not exceed the portion of the 
tax paid during the 2 years immediately 
preceding the filing of the claim. 


(C) Limit if no claim filed 


If no claim was filed, the credit or 
refund shall not exceed the amount which 
would be allowable under subparagraph 
(A) or (B), as the case may be, if claim 
was filed on the date the credit or refund 
is allowed. 


2. Section 7422(a) of the Internal Revenue Code, 26 
U.S.C. 7422(a), provides: 


(a) No suit prior to filing claim for refund 


No suit or proceeding shall be maintained in any 
court for the recovery of any internal revenue tax 
alleged to have been erroneously or illegally as- 
sessed or collected, or of any penalty claimed to 
have been collected without authority, or of any 
sum alleged to have been excessive or in any 
manner wrongfully collected, until a claim for 
refund or credit has been duly filed with the 
Secretary, according to the provisions of law in 
that regard, and the regulations of the Secretary 
established in pursuance thereof. 


STATEMENT 


A. United States v. Brockamp 

1. Respondent Marian Brockamp is the adminis- 
trator and sole beneficiary of the estate of her father, 
Stanley McGill. On April 15, 1984, Mr. McGill trans- 
mitted $7000 to the Internal Revenue Service in 
payment of income taxes for 1983. Accompanying the 
check was an application for an extension of time in 
which to file his return for that year. Mr. McGill did 
not, however, thereafter file either a return or a claim 
for refund for the 1983 tax year. In 1984, Mr. McGill 


5 


was 93 years old. According to respondent, he was 
“senile” and “mentally deranged” at that time (Pet. 
App. 18a-19a). 

Mr. McGill died in 1988. After respondent was ap- 
pointed administrator of his estate, she wrote the IRS 
to request a refund of the taxes that he paid for 1983. 
She stated that her father “had mistakenly sent a 
check for $7000 instead of $700” to the IRS (Pet. App. 
19a).' Respondent thereafter submitted an income tax 
return for Mr. McGill for the 1983 tax year which 
indicated that his liability for taxes for that year was 
$427 (id. at 19a, 24a). 

The IRS denied the requested refund. The Service 
noted that the taxes were paid “more than three years 
prior to her claim” (Pet. App. 19a) and that the re- 
quested refund was therefore barred by Section 6511 
of the Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. 6511. 

2. Mrs. Brockamp thereafter commenced this tax 
refund action in federal district court on behalf of the 
estate. She contended (i) that the doctrine of equita- 
bie tolling applies to the limitation periods for income 
tax refund claims under Section 6511 and (ii) that the 
claimed mental incompetency of Mr. McGill is a suffi- 
cient basis for applying equitable tolling.” 


' The contention that Mr. McGill was senile was supported 
only by Mrs. Brockamp’s affidavit, which was submitted in 
support of her motion for summary judgment. The court of 
appeals concluded that equitable tolling was applicable to tax 
refund suits and remanded the case for the district court to 
determine whether Mr. McGill was competent when he paid 
his taxes in 1984 (Pet. App. 8a). 


* Respondent also contended that the $7000 payment made 
by Mr. McGill was only a “deposit”—and not a payment of 
taxes—and was therefore not subject to the limitation periods 
of Section 6511 (Pet. App. 22a). The district court rejected

The district court concluded that the doctrine of 
equitable tolling does not apply to the statutory limi- 
tations on tax refund claims set forth in Section 6511 
of the Code. The court first noted (Pet. App. 20a) that 
Section 7422(a) of the Code specifies that courts may 
not exercise jurisdiction over a tax refund suit unless 
“a claim for refund or credit has been duly filed with 
the Secretary, according to the provisions of law in 
that regard” (26 U.S.C. 7422(a)). The court observed 
that the applicable “provisions of law” are contained 
in Section 6511(a), which provides that a claim for 
refund must be filed (26 U.S.C. 6511(a)) 


within 3 years from the time the return was filed 
or 2 years from the time the tax was paid, 
whichever of such periods expires the later, or if 
no return was filed by the taxpayer, within 2 years 
from the time the tax was paid. 


Because the refund claim submitted by respondent 
fell outside these statutory periods, the district court 
concluded that it lacked jurisdiction over the suit 
under Section 7422(a) (Pet. App. 21a, 29a-30a). 

The court further noted that, “[iJn addition to the 
time limitations of Section 6511(a), Section 6511(b) 
limits the amount of a refund recoverable by a 
claimant” (Pet. App. 21a-22a (emphasis added)): 


Specifically, if a person files a timely claim 
within the three-year period mentioned in

therefore did not address respondent's contention that the

dissenting).

The court explained that the provisions of Section 
6511(b) that establish substantive limitations on the 
amount of any refund—even on a timely filed claim— 
are not subject to the doctrine of equitable tolling 
(Pet. App. 21a-22a, 27a-28a). 

The district court noted that in Jrwin v. Depart- 
ment of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89 (1990), this 
Court “recognized that the courts have traditionally 
declined to apply equitable principles in cases against 
the government because waivers of sovereign im- 
munity are to be strictly construed” (Pet. App. 25a, 
citing 498 U.S. at 96-99). In Irwin, however, the 
Court “changed its approach to the issue of equitable 
tolling against the government” and held that (Pet. 
App. 25a, quoting 498 U.S. at 95-96) 


the same rebuttable presumption of equitable toll- 
ing applicable to suits against private defendants 
should also apply to suits against the United 
States. 


8 


Several lower courts thereafter relied on Jrwin to 
“extend[] the doctrine of equitable tolling to tax 
refund cases” (id. at 25a-27a, citing cases). The 
district court concluded, however, that those deci- 
sions were not correct. The court noted that in 
Oropallo v. United States, 994 F.2d 25 (1993), cert. 
denied, 114 S. Ct. 705 (1994), the First Circuit (in a 
per curiam opinion joined by then-Judge Breyer) con- 
cluded that the “rebuttable presumption” that equita- 
ble tolling applies to suits against the government is 
inapplicable to tax refund suits because the “limita- 
tions imposed by Sections 6511(a) and (b) are funda- 
mentally inconsistent with the doctrine of equitable 
tolling” (Pet. App. 27a, citing 994 F.2d at 30-31). The 
district court also noted that in United States v. 
Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (1990), this Court held that an 
equitable claim for recovery of a tax overpayment was 
not exempt from the limitation periods of Section 6511 
(Pet. App. 28a-29a, citing 494 U.S. at 610). 

Applying the reasoning of Oropallo and Dalm, the 
district court held that the periods of limitations on 
refund claims and refund recoveries under Sections 
6511(a) and (b) are not subject to the doctrine of 
equitable tolling. Because the refund claim in this 
case was filed outside those periods of limitations, the 
court entered judgment for the government (Pet. App. 
30a). 

3. The court of appeals reversed (Pet. App. la-8a), 
with Judge Fernandez dissenting (id. at 8a-16a). The 
majority opinion stated that this Court’s decision in 
Irwin “effectively limited the Dalm decision” and 
“requires that, in the absence of congressional action, 
statutes of limitations are, as a general rule, pre- 
sumed to be subject to equitable tolling in suits 
against the United States” (id. at 5a). The court 


9 


found nothing in the text or history of Section 6511 to 
indicate “any intention [by Congress] that equitable 
tolling should not apply to § 6511” (ibid.). The court 
therefore concluded that “the statute of limitations 
provided in § 6511 may be equitably tolled” (ibid.). 

The court noted that, in contexts not involving tax 
refund claims, “several post-Jrwin courts have held 
that mental incompetence will toll statutes of limita- 
tion in suits against the government” (Pet. App. 7a, 
citing cases). The court stated that it agreed with 
those decisions because (id. at 8a) 


[p)rinciples of equity mandate that when mental 
incompetence precludes a person from asserting 
his rights during the proper time period, he should 
not be precluded from later seeking redress for his 
injuries. 
Because a “triable issue of fact [exists] as to Mr. 
McGill’s mental incompetence,” the court of appeals 
reversed the decision of the district court and 
remanded for further proceedings (ibid.). See note 1, 
supra.

court of appeals because “the principles of equitable 
tolling cannot be applied to § 6511” (Pet. App. 9a, 10a):

The Court stated that “the same rebuttable presump-

private defendants should also apply to suits against 
the United States” (id. at 95-96). The Court reasoned 
that a presumption of equitable tolling is, in general, 
“likely to be a realistic assessment of legislative

presumption is rebuttable and that particular legisla-

As four other courts of appeals have concluded, the 
Ninth Circuit erred in the present case in concluding 
that the “rebuttable presumption of equitable tolling” 
applies to the statutes governing tax refund claims. 
These detailed statutes do more than merely limit the 
time for commencing suit—they also expressly and 
unequivocally prohibit the payment of claims that 
have not been filed within the defined statutory 
periods (26 U.S.C. 6511(b)(1)) and further provide that 
any payment of such a claim would be “erroneous” and 
“void” as a matter of law (26 U.S.C. 6514(a)).

represent a “statute of limitations” for the com- 
mencement of a suit or the filing of a claim. Instead, 
they impose substantive limitations on the amount of 
recovery that (i) preclude any payment on a claim 
filed outside the express statutory periods estab- 
lished in Section 6511(a) and (ii) limit the amount of 
any refund to the “portion of the tax paid within” 
those express statutory periods—and thereby pro- 
hibit any refund of taxes for which an untimely claim 
was made. 26 U.S.C. 6511(b)(1), (b)(2)(A)-(C). Because 
Section 6511(b) imposes substantive, rather than 
procedural, limitations on tax refund recoveries, the 
requirements of that statute are not subject to 
modification under the doctrine of equitable tolling. 

These substantive limitations on tax refund claims 
confirm that Congress has insisted upon a definite 
and unyielding point of repose for such claims. 
Especially in light of the fact that approximately

which could potentially be the subject of an untimely 
refund claim—the only “realistic assessment of 
legislative intent” manifested by these statutes is 
that Congress did not intend the detailed limitations

“equitable” grounds. In this case, as in Lampf,

501 U.S. 350 (1991), the presumption of equitable 
tolling described in /rwin is inapplicable because the 
statutory scheme manifests a legislative policy of 
repose that cannot be reconciled with equitable 
tolling.

1. The Evolution of the Refund Claim Require- 
ment. 


For the first one hundred years of our Nation’s 
history, only two methods were available for obtain- 
ing a refund of taxes that had been improperly or 
unlawfully collected. First, a taxpayer could apply to 
Congress for a private bill authorizing an appro- 
priation to pay monies from the Treasury to refund 
the taxes. Such private bills were necessary because, 
in the absence of any general legislation authorizing 
such recoveries, suits seeking money judgments 
directly against the sovereign were not permitted 
both as a matter of constitutional tradition and 
constitutional text. See U.S. Const. Art. I, § 9, Cl. 7 
(“No money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in 
Consequence of Appropriations made by Law.”). The 
only judicial remedy available to a taxpayer prior to 
1887 was a suit directly against the Collector who had 
exacted and received the allegedly erroneous payment 
of taxes. The theory of such an action was that the

suit was against the Collector in his personal capac- 
ity and was not against the sovereign.” 

From the beginning of this history, the United 
States has insisted upon a prompt claim for refund as 
a condition of refunding taxes that have been improp- 
erly collected. In the judicial action against the 
Collector, it was a prerequisite to any relief that the 
taxpayer make a protest and demand for refund at the 
time the taxes were paid. The rationale for this 
requirement was that, if the Collector had paid the 
taxes over to the Treasury before receiving notice of 
the taxpayer’s protest, (i) the Collector was then 
exonerated from personal liability and (ii) the monies 
could not be obtained directly from the Treasury 
because no statute had then authorized suit against 
the United States. Elliott v. Swartwout, 35 U.S. (10 
Pet.) 137, 158 (1836). See also Bend v. Hoyt, 38 U.S.

such an action against the government. This

there is no reason to assume that Congress would 
intend to make it inherently more difficult to
