INTRODUCTION 
Respondent requests that the Court deny the Petition for 
Writ of Certiorari seeking review of an opinion of the United 
States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. The Court of 
Appeals opinion is reported at 102 F.3d 433 (and was appended 
to the Petition for Writ of Certiorari). Petitioner, in that Petition, 
seeks certiorari from the final judgment of the United States 
Court of Appeals for the Central Ninth Circuit entered on 
December 13, 1996 (an order granting Respondent Watson’s 
appeal of a denial of her Petition for Writ of Habeas Corpus in 
the United States District Court). Petitioner, Judge William 
Pounders of the California Superior Court, had sentenced 
Respondent (a criminal defense attorney) to two days in custody 
and she has served her two day sentence in the custody of the 
Sheriff of the County of Los Angeles, on September 16 through 
September 17, 1994, and is currently not in custody. She received 
a notice from the State Bar of California indicating that an 
investigation has begun with possible disciplinary consequences 
in regard to her ability to practice law related to her having been 
found in contempt by the Los Angeles County Superior Court. 
The alleged contempt arose in the context of a murder trial 
in which Respondent’s client had previously faced the death 
penalty, and now faced a potential sentence of life without the 
possibility of parole. Respondent’s client was testifying that he 
had been the individual who has called “911” to report the 
burning of the victim. The client was revealing this information 
for the first time to anyone except his attorneys during the 
questioning by Respondent-Attorney, who was attempting to 
establish the client’s motivation for having withheld these facts. 
Respondent was one of two attorneys representing her client in 
that trial. 
Respondent, in her appeal to the Ninth Circuit Court of 
Appeals raised the United States constitutional issue of notice 
2 
and due process in that Respondent received no actual notice 
nor had no actual knowledge of the alleged admonition(s) by 
Los Angeles Superior Court Judge Pounders (which was/were 
made outside of her presence and to other attorneys on the case) 
and thus, the judgment and conviction of contempt for violation 
of said admonition(s) violated Respondent’s right to due process 
pursuant to the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United 
States Constitution. Picard v. Connor, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971). 
STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION 
The Supreme Court could assert jurisdiction pursuant to 28 
U.S.C. § 1254(1). 
STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Respondent Penelope Watson, an attorney, was charged with 
direct contempt by a judge of the Los Angeles County Superior 
Court. Respondent respectfully opposes the Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari for numerous reasons, not the least of which is that 
the unanimous decision by the United States Court of Appeals 
for the Ninth Circuit was so narrowly construed, without even 
reaching the full import of Respondent’s due process argument, 
as to leave little or no room for reasonable minds to differ. 
Unfortunately, Petitioner fails to include highly material and 
relevant facts in his statement of the case bearing on the issues 
decided below. 
; 
For example, Petitioner’s summary of the case includes this 
incomplete, and thus misleading rendering of the facts: 
On April 7, 1994, an attorney for one of 
the co-defendant’s in open court repeatedly 
raised the issue of the punishment the 
defendants faced. The trial judge stated 
categorically that possible punishment “is not 
3 
a subject open to discussion. It should not 
be explored.” On April 20, the attorney for a 
different co-defendant again raised the issue 
of punishment, and the trial judge stated 
again that such questioning is prejudicial and 
is not to be discussed. On April 21, Ms. 
Watson’s co-counsel asked a series of seven 
questions in which he stated that the 
defendants were “looking at life in prison.” 
At side bar, the trial judge told Ms. Watson’s 
co-counsel that the matter was not to be 
discussed again. 
On June 21, 1994, Ms. Watson asked a 
question of her client that referred to the 
penalty he faced (life without parole). The 
trial judge warned her to follow the court’s 
prior admonishments that the subject of prior 
punishments was not to be explored. Ms. 
Watson’s next question of her client was, 
“You're facing life without the possibility of 
parole?” 
(Petition at 2-3). Ms. Watson was then held in contempt by the 
court for failing to follow the court’s previous admonishments 
to other counsel regarding possible punishment of the 
defendants. The question she had asked her client dealt with the 
effect of, having faced the death penalty until the beginning of 
trial, why her client had not revealed this information 
incriminating other defendants until that testimony. 
What Petitioner fails to inform this Court is that Ms. Watson 
had no knowledge of the previous admonishments to other 
counsel. Ms. Watson had been ordered not to duplicate any 
efforts of her co-counsel, including reading transcripts of his 
4 
examinations (and thus reported bench conferences). She was 
not present at bench conferences which her co-counsel attended, 
and was not present at bench conferences in which any other 
attorney ;or co-defendants had been warned, admonished, or 
ordered regarding the use of information regarding possible 
punishment. Respondent had no knowledge of the court’s orders. 
Further, Petitioner neglects to tell this Court the trial judge 
convicting Respondent of direct summary contempt had no facts 
before him demonstrating any knowledge by Respondent of the 
court’s relevant orders. 
Respondent was held in direct summary contempt for asking 
two questions, in the course of a three-month long murder trial. 
After the first question, Respondent was told to follow the court’s 
previous admonitions (at which the giving of said admonitions 
Respondent was not present; Petitioner could not and did not 
demonstrate any facts to the contrary, although the issue was 
raised at the hearing after the finding of contempt), and after 
the second and rephrased question Respondent was held in 
contempt. The court did not ask if Respondent was aware of the 
admonitions given to others outside her presence. The questions 
were not disruptive, violent, abusive, nor threatening the dignity 
nor control of the court. 
What then, was the urgent necessity for summary contempt? 
Could not a less onerous procedure be employed when no threat 
to the court was imminent? This is exactly what the Court of 
Appeals found; there was no reason, let alone necessity, for direct 
summary contempt given these facts. The Court of Appeals 
decision squarely found that summary contempt procedure in 
the circumstances of this case violate due process (Court of 
Appeals decision at App. 14 of Petitioner’s brief). 
While Petitioner attempts to capture the Court's attention 
with entreaties to the specter of federal! court interference with 
5 
state court authority, half-truths and distortions are not 
appropriate. 
REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 
What the unanimous decision of the Court of Appeals held 
was that the conduct of Respondent (without deciding whether 
Respondent willfully disobeyed a court order) in two isolated 
questions over a three-month long trial “did not constitute such 
an open, serious threat to orderly procedure that instant and 
summary punishment was necessary, given the principle that 
only [t]he least possible power adequate to the end proposed 
should be used in contempt cases.” United States v. Wilson, 421 
U.S. 309, 39 (1975); In re Gustafson, 650 F.2d 1017, 1022 
(9th Cir. 1981) (en banc); Harris v. United States, 382 U.S. 162, 
165 (1965). Not only was it unnecessary, the summary procedure 
was held violative of Respondent’s due process rights given these 
facts. In re Oliver, 333 U.S. 257, 275-277 (1948). 
Such a holding is not controversial nor does it fly in the 
face of authority, let alone California state court rulings, contrary 
to Petitioner’s assertion. 
The words of the California Supreme Court in /n re 
Hallinan, 71 Cal. 2d 1179, 1183-1185, 81 Cal. Rptr. 1 (1969) 
may be instructive: 
It appears appropriate 
to once more 
emphasize as was done in Gallagher v. 
Municipal Court, supra, 31 Cal.2d 784, 789, 
that “The heat of courtroom debate, 
particularly where liberty is concerned, often 
gives rise to persistence on the part of 
counsel. If the words used by counsel are 
respectful and pertinent to the matter before 
6 
the court, it is not unnecessarily burdensome 
to require the judge first to warn the attorney 
... [that his actions] tend to interrupt the due 
course of the proceeding... .” More recently 
this court again commented that the summary 
contempt power “must be exercised with 
great caution, lest it stifle the freedom of 
thought and speech so necessary to a fair trial 
under our adversary system.” 
In re Hallinan, supra, at 1185. 
Attempting to waive a red “Ninth Circuit” flag at this Court 
by claiming that sufficient deference to state court findings had 
not been given by the Court of Appeals is inappropriate and 
unappealing. (See Petition at 5). 
There is no issue that calls to this Court to review. 
CONCLUSION 
The writ of certiorari should be denied.
