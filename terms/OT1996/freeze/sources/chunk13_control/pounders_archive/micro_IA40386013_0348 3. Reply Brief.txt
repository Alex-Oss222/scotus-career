RESPONDENT’S CONTENTION THAT SHE WAS NOT 
AWARE OF THE ORDERS SHE VIOLATED WAS 
REJECTED BY ALL CALIFORNIA COURTS AND BY 
THE U.S. DISTRICT COURT, AND IS IRRELEVANT 
TO THE NINTH CIRCUIT OPINION AND TO THE 
REASONS FOR GRANTING REVIEW. 
Respondent in her reply to the petition contends that 
the petition is misleading because it does not discuss in 
detail respondent’s claim that she was unaware of the 
trial court’s order that she violated. Respondent's conten- 
tion was not only rejected by the trial judge and not 
found to be a basis for review in any California appellate 
court, but was also rejected by the United States District 
Court Judge who stated: 
“The record makes it quite clear that multi- 
ple statements made in open court gave Peti- 
tioner adequate warning 
to put a person 
of 
reasonable intelligence on notice as to what con- 
duct Judge Pounders had prohibited, satisfying 
due process notice requirements.” (Sept. 8, 1994, 
District Court Order denying petition for habeas 
corpus, App. 
15). 
Most importantly, the reasons for granting review are 
in no way affected by respondent's rejected claim that she 
was unaware of the court’s order despite its repeated 
restatement 
in open court and despite the court’s 
repeated warnings. The Ninth Circuit decision for which 
review is sought never reached this contention of peti- 
tioner, and its decision is not in any way dependent upon 
that contention. (Ninth Circuit opinion, App. 11.) 
Review should be granted because, contrary to long- 
standing California authority, the Ninth Circuit in this case 
has held that even the willful disobedience of an order not 
to ask certain prejudicial questions after warnings not to 
do so cannot be summarily punished by contempt unless a 
reviewing court independently concludes that the attorney 
would continue to disobey the order unless she were 
immediately punished. California authority, including In re 
Hallinan, 71 Cal.2d 1179, 1183-1185, 81 Cal.Rptr. 1 (1969), 
relied upon by respondent in her opposition to the petition 
for certiorari, has consistently held that if warnings have 
been given that the attorney’s conduct is disruptive to the 
proceedings, a trial court may thereafter summarily hold 
the attorney in contempt for willful violation of the order 
and warnings to cease the conduct, because “disobedience 
of court orders tends to lessen the effect of those orders.” 
Gallagher v. Municipal Court, 31 Cal.2d 784, 797, 192 P.2d 
905, 914 (1948). 
Without authority to impose immediate sanction when 
an attorney persists in violating a court’s orders during a 
jury trial despite repeated warnings not to do so, trial 
courts will lack effective tools to ensure the orderly con- 
duct of proceedings before them.
