QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
Whether a state court judge during the course of a 
multi-defendant felony criminal jury trial may consti- 
tutionally summarily find an attorney in contempt for 
twice willfully violating in open court orders not to 
ask certain prejudicial questions if the statute under 
which the contempt is imposed limits punishment to 
no more than a $1,000 fine and imprisonment in jail 
for no more than 5 days or both. 
Whether the provisions of 28 U.S.C. §2254(d), provid- 
ing that state court factual findings are presumed to 
be correct in federal habeas corpus proceedings, is 
violated when a federal appeals court accepts the 
state court’s findings that an attorney twice willfully 
disobeyed a court order not to ask certain prejudicial 
questions during a felony criminal jury trial after 
numerous prior warnings to other counsel in the case, 
but finds that summary contempt procedures were 
not necessary to preclude further violations and dis- 
ruption of the trial.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Respondent attorney Penelope Watson represented 
one defendant in a multi-defendant criminal murder jury 
trial that began on March 28, 1994, and lasted for three 
months. On April 7, 1994, an attorney for one of the co- 
defendants in open court repeatedly raised the issue of 
the punishment the defendants faced. The trial judge 
stated categorically that possible punishment “is not a 
subject open to discussion. It should not be explored.” On 
April 20, the attorney for a different co-defendant again 
raised the issue of punishment, and the trial judge stated 
again that such questioning is prejudicial and is not to be 
discussed. On April 21, Ms. Watson’s co-counsel asked a 
series of seven questions in which he stated that the 
defendants were “looking at life in prison.” At side bar, 
|
| 
the trial judge told Ms. Watson’s co-counsel that the 
matter was not to be discussed again. 
On June 21, 1994, Ms. Watson asked a question of her 
client that referred to the penalty he faced (life without 
parole). The trial judge warned her to follow the court's 
prior admonishments that the subject of prior punish- 
ments was not to be explored. Ms. Watson’s next question 
of her client was, “You're facing life without possibility of 
parole?” 
At the side bar which followed immediately, Judge 
Pounders asked Ms. Watson why she should not be held 
in contempt, rejected her contention that she didn’t think 
the question was in violation of the court’s orders, and 
found her in contempt of court for violating California 
Code of Civil Procedure §1209(a)(5), which provides that 
disobedience of any lawful order ic a contempt of court, 
and for which a $1,000 fine or five days in jail, or both is 
the maximum permissible punishment. California Code 
of Civil Procedure §1218(a). The next day on June 22, 
1994, the trial judge issued a written order of contempt, 
finding that “the questions asked by contemnor 
.. 
. in the 
presence of the jury had as [their] sole purpose improp- 
erly advising the jury of the potential penalty for the 
defendants in violation of the court order” and that “the 
contemnor was aware of the order,” and imposed a 2-day 
jail sentence to be served after trial. 
On July 8, 1994, two days after the case was submit- 
ted to the jury, the trial judge gave Ms. Watson a further 
opportunity to explain her conduct. Ms. Watson repre- 
sented by counsel provided a further explanation which 
the trial judge found unconvincing. 
Ms. Watson’s habeas petitions to the California Court 
of Appeal and Supreme Court were denied summarily. 
Her habeas petition to the United States District 
Court for the Central District of California was denied on 
September 8, 1994, without an evidentiary hearing on the 
grounds that “[t]he record makes it quite clear that multi- 
ple statements made in open court gave Petitioner ade- 
quate warning to put a person of reasonable intelligence 
on notice as to what conduct Judge Pounders had prohib- 
ited, satisfying the due process notice requirements.” 
On appeal from the district court’s denial of her 
habeas petition, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals on 
December 
13, 1996, reversed the districi court’s order 
denying the habeas petition and remanded for entry of 
judgment granting her petition. Accepting Judge 
Pounder’s findings that Ms. Watson willfully disobeyed a 
court order, and acknowledging Judge Pounder’s concern 
that Ms. Watson’s questions might prejudice jurors in 
favor of her client, the Ninth Circuit concluded that Ms. 
Watson’s questions did not pose a sufficient threat to the 
orderly administration of justice +> justify summary con- 
tempt procedure. 
This petition for certiorari to the United States Court 
of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit is timely filed within 
ninety days of the filing of the Ninth Circuit opinion on 
December 
13, 1996. Jurisdiction 
is conferred upon the 
Supreme Court pursuant to 28 U.S.C. §1254(1).

REASONS FOR GRANTING CERTIORARI 
1. 
The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals has decided an 
important question in a way that conflicts with a 
decision by a state court of last resort. 
The California Supreme Court has long held that 
when a contempt order is based on the statements of an 
attorney made in open court in language that is not itself 
insolent, contemptuous, or disorderly, a warning is 
required before an attorney may be cited for contempt. 
Gallagher v. Municipal Court, 31 Cal.2d 784, 797, 192 P.2d 
905, 914 (1948). However, if such warnings have been 
given, a trial court may thereafter summarily hold an 
attorney in contempt for willful violation of the order and 
warning, because “[d]Jisobedience of court orders tends to 
lessen the effect of those orders.” Id. 
Contrary to this longstanding California authority, 
the Ninth Circuit in this case has held that even the 
willful disobedience of an order not to ask certain preju- 
dicial questions after warnings not to do so cannot be 
summarily punished by contempt unless a reviewing 
court independently concludes that the attorney would 
continue to disobey the order unless she were imme- 
diately punished. 
2. The Ninth Circuit has so far departed from the 
accepted and usual standard of review of state court 
contempt proceedings as to call for an exercise of 
this Court’s supervisory power. 
Sufficient deference was not given by the Ninth Cir- 
cuit to the state trial court findings as required by 28 
U.S.C. §2254(d). Although accepting the state trial court’s 
findings that attorney Watson willfully disobeyed a court 
order after repeated warnings to other counsel in the 
case, and purporting to understand the trial court’s con- 
cern that her questions might prejudice jurors in favor of 
her client, the Ninth Circuit, on the basis of its limited 
review of the record and without evidentiary hearing, 
simply disagreed that the trial judge’s actions were 
required to prevent demoralization of the court’s author- 
ity or to prevent further violations. The conclusion sim- 
ply ignores the fact that Ms. Watson’s violations followed 
the obvious insufficiency of earlier warnings rather than 
immediate punishment of the prior violations by the 
other attorneys in the case, including her own co-counsel. 
Exercise of this Court’s supervisory power is war- 
ranted because of the historical recalcitrance of the Ninth 
Circuit to give sufficient deference to state court findings. 
See Sumner v. Mata, 449 U.S. 539, 101 S.Ct. 764, 66 L.Ed.2d 
722 (1981), where this Court took the Ninth Circuit to task 
for not giving sufficient deference to state court findings; 
and Sumner v. Mata (1982) 455 U.S. 591, 71 L.Ed.2d 480, 
102 S.Ct. 1303, sending the same case back again for the 
same failure to give sufficient deference to the state court 
findings.

SUMMARY 
Criminal Law and Procedure/Contempt 
The court of appeals reversed a judgment of the 
district court. The court held that a defense attorney’s 
isolated reference during a months-long trial to the pen- 
alty faced by the defendant does not warrant the trial 
court’s use of summary contempt procedure. 
During a multi-defendant capital murder trial that 
lasted three months, appellant defense attorney Penelope 
Watson asked two questions of her client that referred to 
the penalty he faced (life without parole). Watson’s 
asserted reason for the questions was to show her client’s 
state of mind, rather than placing the question of punish- 
ment before the jury. 
Without a hearing, the trial court determined that 
Watson has disobeyed an order of the court for counsel to 
refrain from making references to penalty and found her 
in contempt. The court sentenced Watson to two days in 
jail, to be served after conclusion of the trial. 
The California appellate courts and the district court 
denied Watson’s habeas corpus petitions. On appeal, Wat- 
son contended that her due process rights were violated 
because she did not have notice of the conduct prohibited 
by the trial court, and that she was entitled to a hearing 
to determine whether her conduct was willful. 
[1] Due process requires that a person charged with 
contempt usually be given a hearing on the charges. 
Summary contempt proceedings are appropriate only for 
+t oe
App. 3 
a narrowly limited category of contempts. This category 
includes only charges of misconduct, 
in open court, 
where immediate punishment 
is essential 
to prevent 
demoralization of the court’s authority before the public. 
[2] Watson’s two questions did not pose a threat to 
the orderly administratin of justice sufficient to justify 
summary contempt procedure. When viewed in the con- 
text of a three-month long trial, the two questions did not 
constitute such an open, serious threat to orderly pro- 
cedure that instant and summary punishment was neces- 
"sary. [3] The magnitude of the threat posed to the orderly 
administration of justice by Watson’s questions paled in 
comparison with that posed by the conduct of attorneys 
whose summary contempt citations have been upheld by 
the court of appeals. 
[4] Watson did not engage in a pattern of repeated 
violations that pervaded the courtroom and threatened 
the dignity of the court She was held in contempt for 
asking two questions during a three-month trial. Nothing 
indicated that she showed disrespect for the court’s 
authority at any other time, or that she would have 
repeated the references to punishment or engaged in 
other improper conduct unless she were immediately 
punished. The use of summary contempt procedure vio- 
lated due process.

Ms. Watson’s habeas petitions to the California Court 
of Appeal and the California Supreme Court were denied. 
She now appeals the denial of her federal habeas peti- 
tion. Ms. Watson claims that her due process rights were 
violated because she did not have notice of the prohibited 
conduct. In addition, she argues that summary contempt 
procedure violated due process because the trial judge 
could not have known without a hearing whether or not 
her conduct was willful. See, e.g., United States v. Arm- 
strong, 781 F.2d 700, 706 (9th Cir. 1986) (“willfullness [sic] 
is an essential element of criminal contempt”). We do not 
address either of Ms. Watson’s arguments because we 
hold that her conduct was not so disruptive as to justify 
the use of summary contempt procedure. 
3 Although Ms. Watson has served her two-day sentence, 
her appeal is not moot because she remains at risk of being 
disciplined by the California State Bar, in part because of her 
contempt conviction. Zal v. Steppe, 968 F.2d 924, 926 (9th 
Cir.1992). 
App. 12 
[1] Due process requires that a person charged with 
contempt usually be given a hearing on the charges 
against her. In re Oliver, 333 U.S. 257, 275 (1948) (“due 
process of law 
. 
. 
. requires that one charged with con- 
tempt of court be advised of the charges against him, 
have a reasonable opportunity to meet them by way of 
defense or explanation, have the right to be represented 
by counsel, and have a chance to testify and call other 
witnesses in his behalf”). Summary contempt proceed- 
ings are appropriate only for a “narrowly limited cate- 
gory of contempts.” Id. This narrow category “includes 
only charges of misconduct, in open court, 
. 
. 
. where 
immediate punishment is essential to prevent ‘demoraliz- 
ation of the court’s authority’ before the public.” Id.; see 
also United States v. Wilson, 421 U.S. 309, 316 (1975) (sum- 
mary contempt appropriate when a refusal to comply 
with a court order “disrupts and frustrates an ongoing 
proceeding”) (discussing Fed. R. Crim. P. 42(a)); Harris v. 
United States, 382 U.S. 162, 167 (1965) (summary contempt 
proceedings under Rule 42(a) only permissible in “those 
unusual situations 
. 
. 
. where instant action is necessary to 
protect the judicial institution itself”); United States 
v. 
Engstrom, 16 F.3d 1006, 1011 (9th Cir. 1994) (“Rule 42(a) 
may only be used when contempt poses a threat to the 
orderliness of ongoing proceedings, that is, when prompt 
action is required”); United States v. Flynt, 756 F.2d 1352, 
1363 (9th Cir. 1985) (summary contempt proceedings 
under Rule 42(a) are justified when there is a need to 
“remedy a breakdown in the orderly operation of the 
judicial system”); In re Gustafson, 650 F.2d 1017, 1022 (9th 
Cir. 1981) (en banc) (trial court has discretion to invoke 
summary contempt procedures “when the contemnor’s 
App. 13 
conduct is ‘such an open, serious threat to orderly pro- 
cedure that instant and summary punishment, as distin- 
guished from due and deliberate procedures. 
. 
. 
. 
, [is] 
necessary’ ”) (quoting Harris, 382 U.S. at 165 (alteration in 
original); United States v. Abascal, 509 F.2d 752, 756 (9th 
Cir. 1975) (“the ‘need for immediate penal vindication of 
the dignity of the court’ is the basis for Rule 42(a)”). 
[2] Although we do not decide the issue whether 
Ms. Watson willfully disobeyed a court order, we can 
understand Judge Pounders’ concern that her two ques- 
tions might prejudice jurors in favor of her client by 
conveying the message that the life of a gang member 
was not “worth” life imprisonment for her young client. 
Nonetheless, we are not persuaded that these two ques- 
tions posed a threat to the orderly administration of 
justice sufficient to justify summary contempt procedure. 
These two questions, when viewed in the context of a 
three-month long trial, did not constitute “ ‘such an open, 
serious threat to orderly procedure that instant and sum- 
mary punishment was necessary,’ ” Gustafson, 650 F.2d at 
1022 (quoting Harris, 382 U.S. at 165), given the “principle 
that only ‘[t]he least possible power adequate to the end 
proposed’ should be used in contempt cases.” Wilson, 421 
U.S. at 319 (citation omitted). 
[3] The magnitude of the threat posed to the orderly 
administration of justice by Ms. Watson’s two questions 
pales in comparison with the magnitude of the threat 
posed by the conduct of attorneys whose summary con- 
tempt citations we have upheld. For instance, 
in 
Gustafson, the defense attorney “openly disregarded the 
Court’s rulings and directives” during closing argument, 
650 F.2d at 1023, ignoring at least nineteen admonitions to 
App. 14 
slow down, id. at 1018, and engaging in “persistent argu- 
ment on prohibited topics.” Id. at 1020. In Hawk v. Car- 
doza, 575 F.2d 732 (9th Cir. 1978) (per curiam), we upheld 
summary contempt citations issued against defense coun- 
sel nineteen times. We held that “the necessary evidence 
of . 
. 
. actual obstruction” was established by “the trial 
judge’s warnings to [the attorney], his special orders 
concerning conduct at trial issued before the trial began 
and [the attorney]’s continuing conduct and repeated 
violations of those orders.” Id. at 735. 
[4] Unlike the attorneys held in summary contempt 
in Gustafson and Hawk, Ms. Watson did not engage in a 
pattern of repeated violations that pervaded the court- 
room and threatene? the dignity of the court. She was 
held in summary contempt for asking two questions dur- 
ing the course of a three-month 
trial. Nothing in the 
record indicates that she showed any disrespect for the 
court’s authority at any other time. Nor does anything in 
the record indicate that Ms. Watson would have repeated 
the references to punishment or engaged in any other 
improper conduct unless she were immediately punished. 
Accordingly, we hold that the use of summary contempt 
procedure in the circumstances of this case violated due 
process. Cf. Abascal, 509 F.2d at 754, 756 (holding that 
summary contempt procedure violated due process 
because, although “[a]ppellant defied 
a clear order 
directed to him personally by the court, 
. 
. 
. it does not 
appear that it was necessary for the court to order imme- 
diate incarceration”).

habeas 
petition 
is REVERSED, 
and the case 
is 
REMANDED for entry of judgment granting her petition.
