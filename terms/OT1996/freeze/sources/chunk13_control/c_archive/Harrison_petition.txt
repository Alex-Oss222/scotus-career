QUESTIONS PRESENTED 
I. What are the standards for imposing liability upon an 
employer for a hostile work environment created by the sexual 
harassment of an employee by a supervisor? 
A. Does the Restatement (Second) of Agency § 219(2)(d) 
provide two separate standards for imposing liability 
on the employer under Title VII or must both provisions 
of § 219(2)(d) be present to establish liability of the 
employer? 
B. Can an employer escape liability where it has an 
established written sexual harassment policy, makes 
that policy known to its employees, vigorously enforces 
that policy, and has an established grievance process 
available for employees experiencing problems in their 
employment situation? 
C. Are employers strictly liable for a hostile work 
environment created by sexual harassment committed 
by a supervisor against an employee? 
II. Can a Collective Bargaining Agreement compel an 
employee to arbitrate a claim that she may have under one of 
the federal statutes, such as Title VII of the Civil Rights Act?

x Introduction 
Over the past several years, the issue as to when an employer 
may be held liable under Title VII for a lower level supervisor's 
sexual harassment which allegedly created a hostile working 
environment has been the subject of a massive amount of litigation 
and heated debate. The federal courts have found themselves in 
conflict and disarray as to what standards are applicable. The courts 
are unsure as to the proper standards to apply. The Eighth Circuit 
recently recognized the confusion by noting the issue is the subject 
of less than clear precedent. Davis v. City of Sioux City, 115 F.3d 
1365, 70 Empl. Prac. Dec. P. 44,778 (8th Cir. (Iowa), Jun. 18, 1997) 
(No. 96-2194). As aresult, employers across this nation are unsure 
as to the standards to which they will be held. The facts of this case 
clearly present that issue. 
Also, with arbitration of employees’ statutory employment 
discrimination claims gaining widespread acceptance from the 
courts, the question of whether a union employee must first invoke 
the grievance process in a Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA) 
before that union member may seek redress in court for the statutory 
employment discrimination claim has likewise become the center 
of litigation and debate. National labor policy is an arena which 
“(pjeculiarly ... calls for uniform law.” Teamsters, Chauffeurs, 
Warehousemen & Helpers v. Lucas Flour Co., 369 U.S. 95, 104 
(1962) (quoting Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Public Service Comm'n, 
250 U.S. 566, 569 (1919). The courts are in disagreement on this 
issue and have failed to achieve uniformity. This issue also involves 
a major question regarding judicial economy in courts with ever 
more crowded dockets. The facts of this case distinctly and clearly 
present both issues.

B. Facts 
EPI operates an underground potash mine near Carlsbad, Eddy 
County, New Mexico. The Defendant, R. L. Brown (Brown), was 
a low-level supervisor with the responsibilities of a shift foreman 
and face boss. The position of face boss is the lowest supervisory 
level at EPI. EPI had combined the duties of an underground shift 
foreman and face boss. Brown, as the shift foreman, had no authority 
to hire, no authority to fire, and no authority to disciplive. The 
extent of Brown’s authority as an underground shift foreman/face 
boss was to make work assignments for members of his crew and 
pair members of his crew to accomplish those work assignments. 
At all times material to this case, EPI had a written policy 
against sexual harassment in effect. The sexual harassment policy 
was drafted and adopted in 1989. It had been disseminated 
throughout the mine. It was posted on all bulletin boards where 
any employee wishing to read it had the opportunity to read such 
policy. Itis undisputed EPI’s sexual harassment policy was actively 
enforced by management. 
In addition to the written sexual harassment policy, there 
existed a CBA between EPI and the United Steel Workers of 
America (USWA) Local 177. The CBA outlined the terms and 
conditions of employment as well as the grievance procedure the 
employees were to follow when problems arose in their employment 
situation. A policy against sexual discrimination was contained in 
the CBA at Article 18.8. (See Appendix E, p. 68a). The CBA also 
contained a mandatory grievance and arbitration process for 
aggrieved employees to follow. (See Appendix E, pp. 65a-67a). 
When management found it necessary to discipline an 
employee, EPI was required to adhere to the following process: 
The employee was initially given an oral warning. Brown had 
no authority to issue an oral warning. Brown’s only authority was 
4 
to report an infraction to upper-level management and await upper- 
level management’s response as to the appropriate action to be taken 
regarding the employee’s infraction. The second level in the 
disciplinary process was to issue a written warning to the employee. 
Brown’s participation at the second level was identical to his 
participation in the first level oral warning stage, which was to 
simply advise upper-level management of the infraction. Upper- 
level management then made the decision to issue the written 
warning and its contents. The third level in the disciplinary process 
was suspension of the employee from work. Again, Brown had no 
involvement other than reporting the infraction to upper-level 
management. EPI’s decision as to the appropriate level of 
disciplinary action to be invoked was made by a committee or group 
of upper-level management personnel. Brown, as an underground 
shift foreman/face boss, was not a member of, nor participant in, 
the group. More importantly, because of the existence of the CBA, 
there was very ‘ittle discretion regarding the action to be taken as 
to the various levels of discipline as they were primarily controlled 
by the CBA. Because of the CBA, even upper-level management 
exercised limited discretion in the disciplinary process. Brown, as 
an underground face boss/shift foreman, had no authority to 
determine who was assigned to his crew or what work was to be 
done. Those decisions were controlled 
by upper level management.

accomplished.

was one in which each miner was assigned another laborer.

C. Proceedings 
On September 16, 1994, Harrison filed a complaint against 
EPI and Brown in the United States District Court for the District 
of New Mexicc alleging sexual harassment in violation of Title 
VII and a number of supplemental state law tort claims. Subject 
matter jurisdiction in the District Court over these claims was 
alleged to be under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 28 U.S.C. § 1367. Prior 
to trial, Defendants EPI and Brown filed a Motion to Dismiss for 
Lack of Subject Matter Jurisdiction arguing that Harrison’s failure 
to invoke the grievance procedure as contained in the CBA resulted 
in a denial of subject matter jurisdiction to the District Court. (See 
Appendix B). The Motion to Dismiss (see Appendix B) was denied 
and a week-long jury trial was conducted with a verdict being 
returned in favor of EPI and against Harrison on February 7, 1996. 
(See Appendix D). The jury returned a verdict in favor of Plaintiff 
Harrison against Defendant Brown on state common law tort claims

In Meritor Savings Bank, F-S.B. v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57 (1986), 
this Court held that under Title VII employers were not strictly 
liable for a hostile work environment created as a result of sexual 
harassment by a supervisor upon an employee. The instruction from 
this Court was that the principles of agency would be used to 
determine when an employer might be liable for a hostile work 
environment created by a supervisor. In this case, the Tenth Circuit’s 
purported application of agency principles to employer liability 
for acts of a supervisor under Title VII presents the following reasons 
for this Court granting a writ: 
1. The Tenth Circuit’s decision is in contradiction to Meritor 
Savings Bank, F.S.B. v. Vinson, supra., because, while 
purporting not to have adopted strict liability, in reality, 
the Tenth Circuit has, for all practical purposes, adopted a 
standard of strict liability. Decisions by a Circuit Court of 
Appeals failing to follow, or in contradiction to, a United 
2. 
9 
States Supreme Court decision constitute a basis for 
granting a petition for writ of certiorari. 
There exists extreme conflict between the Circuit Courts 
of Appeal as to the standards to be applied in determining 
when an employer will be liable under Title VII for hostile 
work environment in the form of sexual harassment when 
committed by a lower level supervisor. The vast differences 
among the various circuits have led to confusion and 
uncertainty as to what should be the proper standard for 
determining liability. 
The issue in this case as to what standards are to be used 
when determining whether or not an employer will be liable 
for the actions of a lower-level supervisor in a hostile work 
environment situation presents a matter of nation-wide 
importance which affects millions of workers and their 
employers. Employers carrying on business throughout the 
United States in the various circuits are now presented with 
inconsistency where there is an obvious necessity for 
consistency. A uniform rule of law is necessary to allow a 
consistent application of work rules and policies by 
employers for theiz employees throughout the nation. 
Presently, an employer doing business in one circuit may, 
irrespective of that employer’s policies against sexual 
harassment, face liability for the actions of a supervisor, 
when, in another circuit that employer will not face liability 
for those same actions. Also, one employer’s response to 
the actions of a supervisor will, in one circuit, insulate the 
employer from liability for the supervisor’s actions while 
in another circuit, such as the Tenth Circuit, an employer’s 
response will not. In addition, the decision of the Tenth 
Circuit, in adopting a rule that is tantamount to strict 
liability, defeats a major goal of Title VII, which is stopping 
sexual harassment, because it tends to eliminate the 
incentive on the part of employers to adopt rules against 
sexual harassment and to see that those rules are enforced.

The Tenth Circuit Court’s opinion in this case directly conflicts

In Meritor, this Court expressly held that

Rather, the Courts were directed to look to

In this case, the Tenth Circuit has stretched its analysis of

In

still be liable. While one might argue strict liability might have the

was enacted and certainly not what this Court thought in Meritor.

employers to take action to ensure a harassment-free work

Title VII affects every person in the work force today,

An issue pertaining to Title VII and its application in the work 
place is, therefore, an issue of interest to millions of people. An 
issue affecting a substantial segment of our society is a basis for 
granting a writ of certiorari. Patterson v. Lamb, 329 U.S. 539, 541 
(1947). This Court must grant certiorari to decide the crucial issue 
of the standard to which employers are to be held for sexual 
harassment in violation of Title VII committed by supervisors acting 
outside the scope of their employment.

A. Federal Labor Policy 
For many years, federal labor law has expressed approval of 
arbitration to settle disputes in the work place that arise under the 
context of aCBA. This was first established under the Steel Workers 
Trilogy: United Steel Workers of America v. Enterprise Wheel and 
Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593 (1960); United Steel Workers of America 
v. Warrior and Gulf Navigator Co., 363 U.S. 574 (1960); United 
Steel Workers of America v. American Manufacturing Co., 363 U.S. 
564 (1960). As a part of that policy, employees have to exhaust the 
grievance and arbitration process contained in the CBA before they 
Can initiate a law suit. Republic Steel Corp. v. Maddox, 379 U.S. 
650, 652-53 (1965). Concurrently, the union has a duty to insure 
its members’ grievances are adequately pursued. If the union fails 
in its duty to fairly represent the employee in the grievance/ 
arbitration process, that employee has redress by means of suit 
21 
under 29 U.S.C. § 185. Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171, 177 (1967), 
also see Hines v. Anchor Motor Freight, Inc., 424 U.S. 554 (1976). 
Also, where a union intentionally fails to pursue a Title VII claim 
because of the possible difficulty in pursuing the claim and the 
possible resentment pursuit of that claim might cause, the aggrieved 
employee also has a cause of action against the union under Title 
VII and 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Goodman v. Lukens Steel Co., 482 U.S. 
656, 669 (1987). With the well-established body of federal labor 
law favoring arbitration, the question which has arisen is whether 
an employee’s federal statutory claims involving discrimination 
are subject to having to be exhausted through the CBA’s grievance/ 
arbitration provisions before the aggrieved employee may seek 
redress in the courts. Federal law, as it has developed, reinforces 
deferral to arbitration to settle employees’ work-related disputes. 
Under traditional federal labor law, as contained in the National 
Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 159, the unions have the authority 
to negotiate the terms of employment for their members. The 
authority vested in the unions acts to bar the individual employee 
from bargaining with the employer over that employee’s individual 
rights. General Electric Co., 150 NLRB 192, 194 (1964), enforced, 
418 F.2d 736 (2d Cir. 1969), cert. denied, 397 U.S. 965 (1970). 
The authority granted the union encompasses an expansive number 
of issues involving the employee’s terms of employment. In that 
bargaining process, the union may waive individual rights of its 
members to gain other benefits for those members. A classic 
example is bargaining away certain rights, such as the right to strike, 
for the benefit of a right to arbitrate disputes arising under the terms 
of the CBA. The Fourth Circuit in Austin v. Owens-Brockway Glass 
Container, Inc., 78 F.3d 875 (4th Cir. 1996), cert. denied, 117 S. 
Ct. 432 (1996), saw no difference between bargaining away the 
right to strike and bargaining for the right to arbitrate. The Austin 
court, quoting Gilmer, said: 
[B]y agreeing to arbitrate a statutory 
claim, a party does not forgo the 
substantive rights afforded by the 
22 
Statute; it only submits to their 
resolution in an arbitral, rather than a 
judicial forum. . . . [ciiations omitted]

Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co., 415 U.S. 36 (1974), first

grievance/arbitration process in CBAs as to an individual

The Alexander Court held an employee who submitted a Title VII

discrimination clause in a CBA was not bound by the arbitrator’s 
adverse decision, but was entitled to a de novo hearing in federal 
court on his Title VII claim. To a major extent, the decision was 
based upon the then long standing suspicion of arbitration as a 
proper and viable alternative to settle individuals’ statutory claims, 
which had traditionally been-settled in the courtroom. In the two 
decades following Alexander v. Gardner—Denver, the law has 
continued to develop and change regarding the public policy of 
allowing arbitration as an alternative to settle disputes involving

than that individual only being able to seek relief in the courts. 
This Court’s steps in that evolutionary process are reflected by

directly dealt with a CBA, they initiated a trend by this Court to

Section 219(2)(d) contains two separate clauses, each of 
which can provide a basis for imposing liability on an employer. 
The first clause provides an employer will be liable if “the 
servant purported to act or to speak on behalf of the principal 
and there was reliance upon apparent authority.” To recover, a 
plaintiff “must show that the employer manifested in [the 
supervisor] the authority to act on its behalf, that such 
manifestation resulted in harm to the plaintiff, and that the 
plaintiff acted or relied on the apparent authority in some way.” 
48 Vand. Rev. at 1087. The first element is established 
“[w]henever an employer vests its supervisor with the authority 
to control significant aspects of the work environment.” /d. at 
1089. The second element is satisfied by demonstrating the 
supervisor subjected plaintiff to sexual harassment. The third 
element (that plaintiff acted or relied on the apparent authority 
of her supervisor) is the most difficult to prove and will often 
hinge upon whether the employer has a formal policy against 
sexual harassment. Jd. “When an employer lacks a formal written 
grievance policy, a victim of sexual harassment will reasonably 
perceive her only available options to be silently acquiescing in 
the harassment or leaving her job.” Jd. at 1090. In contrast, if an 
employer has taken steps “to remove any possible inference that 
a supervisor has authority to sexually harass his subordinates,” 
the victim is likely aware the harassment is not authorized and 
reliance on apparent authority will be difficult to establish. Jd.

The central question posed by the above-quoted language 
is whether, as the district court in this case apparently concluded, 
it imposes an additional requirement that must be met before an 
employer can be held liable under Title VII for hostile work 
environment sexual harassment. Specifically, the question is

under §§ 219(1), (2)(b), or (2)(d)) demonstrate the individual

control over the plaintiff’s hiring, firing or conditions of

conclude Sauers should not be interpreted as imposing such a 
requirement. 
First, the above-quoted language from Sauers arose solely 
in the context of deciding whether the employer (Salt Lake 
County) had been timely sued. Thus, the “requirement” that the 
harasser “serve[ ] in a supervisory position and exercise[ ] 
significant control over the plaintiff’s hiring, firing or conditions 
of employment,” applies only when deciding whether the filing 
of a suit against a harasser (in his official capacity) can operate 
as a Suit against the employer. 
Second, there is nothing in the language of Title VII or 
Restatement of Agency that limits the liability of an employer 
to harassment perpetrated by an employee who “serve[d] a 
supervisory position and and significant control over the plaintiff 
plaintiff’s hiring, firing or conditions of employment.” Notably, 
we have never imposed such requirements in any of our post- 
Meritor, pre-Sauers opinions (i.e., Hicks and Hirschfeld). As 
Sauers itself acknowledges, the question of employer liability 
for the conduct of its employees is determined according to the 
agency principles referred to in Meritor and fleshed out in 
Hirschfeld. 
Third, application of the Savers definition of “agent,” taken 
to its extreme, would have the illogical result of shielding 
employers from liability for harassment committed by low-level

Appendix A 
employees. In particular, under the Sauers definition an employer 
would never be liable for harassment committed by low-level 
supervisors (or any employees who commit peer harassment) 
because the harasser would not meet the Sauers definition of 
“agent.” Clearly, this was not the result intended by Title VII, 
Meritor, Hicks, or Hirschfeld. 
For all of these reasons, we conclude the discussion in 
Sauers of who constitutes an “agent” for Title VII purposes is 
strictly limited to situations in which a court is determining 
whether a claim against a named individual defendant (in his 
official capacity) can operate as a claim against the employer 
itself. Where, as here, plaintiff has properly sought Title VII 
relief directly against her employer, the “agent” definition in 
Sauers simply has no applicability in deciding whether the 
employer is liable for the conduct of its employees.

On appeal, plaintiff argues “apparent authority” is a 
confusing technical term that was not adequately explained to 
the jury by the district court’s instruction. Plaintiff further argues 
the instruction confused actual and apparent authority by telling 
the jury they should “consider what knowledge the plaintiff had 
regarding the authority of the agent to commit such acts in 
question.” According to plaintiff, this language directed the jury 
to examine plaintiff’s knowledge concerning Brown’s actual 
authority to assault her. At the very least, plaintiff argues, this 
language was confusing and did not properly guide the jury in 
its deliberations. Finally, plaintiff argues the instruction could 
be construed to mean the existence of a policy against sexual 
harassment is a complete defense for the employer, which, 
according to plaintiff, is not an accurate statement of Tenth 
Circuit law. 
We conclude the instruction is flawed in two major respects. 
First, and most significantly, the instruction informed the jury 
that Potash could be liable for the acts of Brown only if Brown 
had authority, apparent or otherwise, to harass plaintiff. This is 
simply not an accurate statement of the law. Although apparent 
authority to harass is a necessary requirement for imposing 
liability on an employer under the first clause of § 219(2)(d), 
no such authority is needed to establish liability under the second 
clause of § 219(2)(d). Rather, the fact that the supervisor has 
actual or apparent authority to control the victim’s working 
environment, and is aided in harassing the victim by that 
authority, is sufficient to establish employer liability under the 
second clause of § 219(2)(d). 
30a 
Appendix A 
Second, the instruction did not provide the jury with an 
accurate definition of the term “apparent authority.” Restatement 
(Second) of Agency § 27 indicates apparent authority “to do an 
act is created as to a third person by written or spoken words or 
any other conduct of the principal which, reasonably interpreted, 
causes the third person to believe that the principal consents to 
have the act done on his behalf by the person purporting to act 
for him.” In the comment to that section, it is further noted “there 
is the basic requirement that the principal be responsible for the 
information which comes to the mind of the third person.” 
Accordingly, the comment provides that “the principal must 
intend to cause the third person to believe that the agent is 
authorized to act for him, or he should realize that his conduct 
is likely to create such belief.” Jd. Here, the challenged 
instruction wholly failed to inform the jury that any “apparent 
authority” on the part of Brown would have been created by the 
“written or spoken words or any other conduct” of Potash. 
Instead, the jury was led to believe the sole focus of apparent 
authority was the “knowledge” of plaintiff. The references to 
plaintiff’s “knowledge” should have been deleted; instead, the 
instruction should have referred to what plaintiff reasonably 
“believed” based upon the employer’s words or conduct. 
Although plaintiff complains about the last sentence of the 
instruction (which discussed whether Potash had implemented 
a sexual harassment policy), we find nothing wrong with it. As 
noted in the general discussion of employer liability, if an 
employer has taken steps “to remove any possible inference that 
a supervisor has authority to sexually harass his subordinates,” 
48 Vand. L. Rev. at 1087, the victim is lh.ely aware the 
harc :sment is not authorized, and reliance on apparent authority 
will be difficult to establish. Accordingly, the question of 
whether the employer established and implemented a policy

Appendix A 
against sexual harassment is an important factor in deciding 
whether apparent authority existed. 
Viewing the instructions as a whole, we conclude the flaws 
in the apparent authority instruction were prejudicial to plaintiff. 
Although it was uncontroverted that Potash had in place a policy 
against sexual harassment, there were genuine questions of 
material fact concerning whether plaintiff had prior knowledge 
of this policy. Thus, there was at least some basis upon which 
the jury could have concluded, had they been properly instructed, 
that Brown had apparent authority to commit the harassment. 
Moreover, the challenged instruction incorrectly informed the 
jury that Potash could be liable only if Brown had actual or 
apparent authority to commit the harassment. Under the second 
clause of § 219(2)(d), no such authority was necessary.

During the period of plaintiff’s employment, Potash 
allegedly operated under a Collective Bargaining Agreement 
(CBA) with Steelworkers Union, Local #177, of which plaintiff 
was a member. According to Potash, the CBA contained a

The seminal decision concerning the “proper relationship 
between federal courts and the grievance-arbitration machinery 
of collective-bargaining agreements in the resolution and 
enforcement of an individual’s rights to equal employment 
opportunities under Title VII” is Alexander v. Gardner-Denver 
Co., 415 U.S. 36, 38 (1974). Harrell Alexander was a black, 
union-represented employee who had been fired by Gardner- 
Denver for allegedly producing too many defective or unusable 
parts. Claiming his discharge was racially motivated, Alexander 
filed a grievance alleging a violation of the nondiscrimination 
clause in the collective bargaining agreement between his union 
and Gardner-Denver. Following the arbitration hearing, the 
arbitrator denied the grievance without addressing the racial 
discrimination claim. Alexander then filed a Title VII action in 
federal district court based upon the same facts alleged in his

grievance. Gardner-Denver moved to dismiss Alexander's

The district court, later affirmed by the court of appeals, granted

statutory right to trial under Title VII was not foreclosed by his 
earlier submission of a discrimination claim to arbitration. “The 
[Gardner-Denver] Court found that in enacting Title VII, 
Congress had granted individual employees a nonwaivable

v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, 450 U.S. 728, 737-38 (1981). 
In addition, “because Congress had granted aggrieved employees 
access to the courts, and because contractual grievances and 
arbitration procedures provided an inadequate forum for 
enforcement of Title VII rights, the Court concluded that Title 
VII claims should be resolved by the courts de novo.” /d. at 
738. 
Seventeen years later, the Court issued its opinion in Gilmer 
v. Interstate/Johnson Lane Corp., 500 U.S. 20 (1991). Robert 
Gilmer was hired by defendant as a manager of financial 
services. As a condition of his employment, Gilmer was required 
to register as a securities representative with several stock 
exchanges, including the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). 
The NYSE registration application included a provision by 
which Gilmer agreed to arbitrate any claim which arose between 
him and defendant and which was required to be arbitrated under

Appendix A 
employment. Defendant terminated Gilmer’s employment in

charge of discrimination with the EEOC, Gilmer filed suit 
against defendant in federal court alleging violations of the Age 
Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA). Defendant moved 
to compel arbitration of Gilmer’s claim and the district court, 
citing Alexander, denied the motion. On appeal, the Fourth 
Circuit reversed. The Supreme Court then granted certiorari “to 
resolve a conflict among the Courts of Appeals regarding the 
arbitrability of ADEA claims.” 500 U.S. at 24. 
Relying on the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA), 9 U.S.C. § 1 
et seq., the Supreme Court held that Gilmer was obligated to 
arbitrate his claim that his discharge violated the ADEA. In so 
holding, the Court rejected Gilmer’s arguments that compulsory 
arbitration of ADEA claims was inconsistent with the statutory 
framework and purposes of the ADEA. that arbitration would 
undermine the role of the EEOC in enforcing the ADEA, and 
that compulsory arbitration deprived claimants of the judicial 
forum provided by the ADEA. Further, the Court rejected various 
challenges raised by Gilmer to the adequacy of arbitration 
procedures. The Court rejected Gilmer’s reliance on Alexander, 
specifically concluding Alexander and its progeny were factually 
distinguishable from Gilmer’s situation: 
First, those cases did not involve the issue of 
the enforceability of an agreement to arbitrate 
statutory claims. Rather? they involved the 
quite different issue whether arbitration of 
contract-based claims precluded subsequent 
judicial resolution of statutory claims. Since 
the employees there had not agreed to 
arbitrate their statutory claims, and the labor

Appendix A 
arbitrators were not authorized to resolve 
such claims. the arbitration in those cases 
understandably was held not to preclude 
subsequent statutory actions. Second, 
because the arbitration in those cases 
occurred in the context of a collective- 
bargaining agreement, the claimants there 
were represented by their unions in the 
arbitration proceedings. An important 
concern therefore was the tension between 
collective representation and individual 
statutory rights, a concern not applicable to 
the present case. Finally. those cases were 
not decided under the FAA, which, as 
discussed above, reflects a “liberal federal 
policy favoring arbitration agreements.” 
Therefore. those cases provide no basis for 
refusing to enforce Gilmer’s agreement to 
arbitrate his ADEA claim. 
Id. at 35 (internal citation omitted). 
Since Gilmer, employer/defendants have argued that Gilmer 
effectively overruled Alexander and now requires plaintiffs to 
comply with a collective bargaining agreement’s grievance and 
arbitration procedures prior to filing suit in federal court. To 
date, only the Fourth Circuit has accepted this argument, Austin 
v. Owens-Brockway Glass Container, 78 F.3d 875 (4th Cir.) 
(affirming grant of summary judgment to employer because 
plaintiff failed to submit Title VII and ADA claims to mandatory 
arbitration under collective bargaining agreement), cert. denied, 
117 S. Ct. 432 (1996), while the majority view is that Alexander 
and its progeny “remain good law and that statutory employment 
39a 
Appendix A 
claims are independent of a collective bargaining agreement’s 
grievance and arbitration procedures.” Malin, Arbitrating 
Statutory Employment Claims in the Aftermath of Gilmer, 40 
St. Louis U.L.J. 77, 84 (1996); see, e.g., Varner v. National Super 
Markets, 94 F.3d 1209, 1213 (8th Cir. 1996) (holding employee 
not required to exhaust grievance and arbitration remedies under 
collective bargaining agreement before bringing suit under Title 
VII)? cert. denied, 117 S.Ct. 946 (1997); Tran v. Tran, 54 F.3d 
115, 117 (2d Cir. 1995) (“There is nothing in Gilmer which 
appears to throw anything but favorable light upon the continuing 
authority of [Alexander and its progeny].”), cert. denied, 116 
S. Ct. 1417 (1996); Bintner v. Burlington Northern, 857 F. Supp. 
1484, 1488 (D. Wyo. 1994) (“There is nothing in Gilmer to 
suggest that the Court abandoned or even reconsidered its efforts 
to protect individual statutory rights from the give-and-take of 
the collective-bargaining process.”). Although the Tenth Circuit 
has interpreted Gilmer as authorizing compulsory arbitration 
of Title VII claims, Metz v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce. Fenner & 
Smith, 39 F. 3d 1482, 1487 (10th Cir. 1994) (plaintiff completed 
securities registration application which contained compulsory 
arbitration clause), it has never addressed the precise question 
at issue here (i.e., whether a Title VII plaintiff must comply 
with a grievance procedure outlined in union’s collective 
bargaining agreement prior to filing suit in federal court against 
employer). 
For the following reasons, we adopt the majority view. The 
context in which an arbitration clause arises cannot be ignored. 
In Alexander, the clause was contained in a collective bargaining 
agreement; in Gilmer, it was contained in an individual 
application for registration as a securities dealer. “Nothing in 
Gilmer suggests that the Court abandoned its concern about the 
inherent conflicts between group goals and individual rights that 
40a 
Appendix A 
exist in the give-and-take of the collective bargaining process.” 
Randolph v. Cooper Industries, 879 F. Supp. 518, 521 (W.D. 
Pa. 1994). Although plaintiffs like the one in Gilmer at least 
have “the theoretical possibility of negotiating a separate deal 
with their employers” that does not require arbitration, unionized 
employees have no such choice. 40 St. Louis. U.L.J. at 87. “Any 
separate deal [they] might negotiate would be void because the 
union is [their] exclusive bargaining representative.” /d. 
“Consequently, . . . the union is restricted to waiving collective 
rights.” Jd. 
An arbitration clause in a collective bargaining agreement 
(at least historically) implicates only contractual rights under 
that agreement. Alexander, 415 U.S. at 49. Thus, by submitting 
a grievance, a union employee “seeks to vindicate his contractual 
right[s]” under the collective bargaining agreement, but does 
not assert his “independent statutory rights accorded by 
Congress.” Id. at 49-50. In contrast, the arbitration clause at 
issue in Gilmer encompassed the employee’s statutory rights.’ 
500 U.S. at 23. 
There are significant differences between labor arbitration 
and commercial arbitration. In labor arbitration, the arbitrator 
is “the proctor of the bargain,” and his “task is to effectuate the 
intent of the parties.” Alexander, 415 U.S. at 53. Consequently, 
“(h]Jis source of authority is the collective bargaining agreement,” 
and he “has no general authority to invoke public laws that 
conflict with the bargain between the parties.” Jd. In short, he 
2. Here, we do not have access to the CBA between Potash and 
plaintiff's union and therefore do not know what the arbitration clause

4la

“is confined to a system of private law, totally outside the system 
of public law of which employment statutes are a part.” 40 St. 
Louis U.L.J. at 87-88. “In contrast, the employment arbitrator 
{in commercial arbitration], as envisioned in Gilmer, provides a 
forum for the vindication of public law rights.” Jd. at 88. 
Finally, we question whether Gilmer can be applied where, 
as here, the arbitration clause at issue is not encompassed by 
the FAA. Section 1 of the FAA specifically excludes from the 
FAA’s reach “contracts of employment of seamen, railroad 
employees, or any other class of workers engaged in foreign or 
interstate commerce.” 9 U.S.C. § 1. We have previously 
concluded this language encompasses collective bargaining 
agreements, and have thus held the FAA “is generally 
inapplicable to labor arbitration.” United Food & Commercial 
Workers. Local Union 7R v. Safeway Stores, 889 F. 2d 940, 944 
(10th Cir. 1989). Because the arbitration clause at issue in Gilmer 
was not contained in a collective bargaining agreement, the Court 
was free to rely on the FAA for support. Here, in contrast, the 
arbitration clause at issue is contained in a collective bargaining 
agreement that is not covered by the FAA. Thus, Alexander 
provides the better reasoned authority for this situation. See 
Turner, Compulsory Arbitration of Employment Discrimination 
Claims with Special Reference to the Three A’s — Access, 
Adjudication, and Acceptability, 31 Wake Forest L. Rev. 231, 
262 (1996) (discussing FAA exemption issue). 
In conclusion, we reject Potash’s cross-appeal, and refuse 
to require plaintiff to exhaust the grievance procedures provided 
in the CBA.

ARTICLE 13 
GRIEVANCE PROCEDURE 
13.1 The term “grievance” as used in this Agreement means 
any dispute between the Company and an employee or between 
the Company and the Union over the interpretation or application 
of terms of this Agreement where a discussion between the 
employee and his supervisor or the steward and the supervisor 
has not resulted in a resolution. A grievance will not include 
any desired or proposed changes in the terms of this Agreement 
or discharge of any employee occurring prior to the completion 
of his probationary period. 
13.2 Step One. To be considered by the Company, a 
grievance must be reduced to writing on forms provided by the 
Company, dated, signed by the grievant(s), specify the section(s) 
of the Agreement alleged to have been violated, the remedy 
requested and presented by the steward, with or without the 
employee, to the grievant’s supervisor within five (5) workdays 
of the time when the cause of the grievance is known or 
reasonably should be known to the grievant. After it is filed, an 
attempt will be made to settle the grievance at a meeting between 
the grievant, the steward and the grievant’s supervisor. 
13.3 Step Two. If no satisfactory settlement of the grievance 
is reached within five (5) workdays after submission under Step 
One above, the grievance may be appealed to Step Two, which 
shall be a meeting between no more than two Union 
representatives and the appropriate department head. The 
department head shall answer the Step Two appeal within five 
(5) workdays following the grievance meeting. If the written 
answer of the department head is not satisfactory to the Union, 
it may, within five (5) working days after the answer is received, 
refer the grievance to Step Three. 
66a 
Appendix E 
13.4 Step Three. Grievances referred to Step Three shall 
be discussed between the Human Resources Manager and the 
Union Grievance Committee. Union Staff Representatives may 
attend. The Human Resources Manager shall provide the Union 
with a written decision within five (5) working days following 
the Step Three meeting. Final disposition of the matter at the 
third step shall be noted on all copies of the grievance form and 
signed by the Human Resource Manager and the appropriate 
designated Union representative. If the Company and the Union 
are unable to resolve their differences, the Union may appeal 
the matter to arbitration as provided in Article 14. 
13.5 The Union or the grievant may at any time drop or 
rescind the grievance by notice to the Company representative 
involved, in which event the dispute shall be considered settled 
and all procedures relative thereto terminated. 
13.6 Delays in grievance and arbitration time tables may 
be provided for only by written mutual agreement between the 
Company and the Union. 
13.7 Investigating of grievances and interviewing of 
employees regarding grievances shall be done in such a manner 
and at such time and place as will result in the least interference 
with Company operations. When attending scheduled grievance 
meetings, no employee shall be deprived of his regular earnings. 
13.8 If Management’s decision in any step of this procedure 
is not appealed by the Union to the next step within the time 
limits provided for such appeal, the particular grievance shall 
be deemed closed. Should Management fail to respond to a 
grievance within the time limits specified, the grievance will be 
resolved in favor of the grievant. Any grievance resclved by 
67a 
Appendix E 
failure of either party to meet a time requirment shall be 
considered resolved with no precedent being set. 
ARTICLE 14 
ARBITRATION 
14.1 If a grievance is not settled by the procedure set forth 
in the Grievance Procedure, the aggrieved party may, upon 
written notice to the other party, refer the grievance to arbitration. 
This shall be done not later than ten (10) working days following 
receipt of the Step Three answer. No employee may compel the 
arbitration of a grievance without the written consent of the 
Union. 
14.2 A single impartial arbitrator may be mutually agreed 
upon by the parties. In case of failure to agree upon an arbitrator 
within ten (10) days from the date of receipt of the written notice 
specified in Section One of this Article, the aggrieved party may, 
within five (5) days thereafter, request the Federal Mediation 
and Conciliation Service to submit a panel of seven names from 
which an arbitrator may be selected. The arbitrator so selected 
or agreed upon shall have the authority to interpret and apply 
the provisions of this Agreement but shall not have authority to 
alter, add to or disregard any of its provisions or decide any 
matter not covered by a specific provision of this Agreement. 
No question of a change in the wage scale or a change in 
differentials of pay shall be the subject of arbitration unless as 
specifically mutually agreed by the parties. 
14.3 The decision of the arbitrator shall be final and binding 
on the parties concerned. The decision and opinion shall be in 
writing and shall be rendered within thirty (30) days of the last 
hearing on the matter conducted by the arbitrator, or within thirty 
68a 
Appendix E 
(30) days of the receipt of post-hearing briefs, whichever is later. 
It is understood and agreed that no more than one written 
grievance shall be the subject matter of a single arbitration. 
However, grievances arising out of the same facts and 
circumstances and relating to the same issue shall be submitted 
to a single arbitrator. 
14.4 The necessary expenses of the arbitrator shall be borne 
equally by the parties.
