## Cohen v. de la Cruz

No. 96-1923; argued January 20, 1998; decided March 24, 1998; certiorari to the Third Circuit. O'Connor delivered a unanimous opinion: Rehnquist, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer joined her. No separate writing. The Third Circuit judgment was affirmed.

The historical holding is that once money or property is established to have been obtained through qualifying fraud, §523(a)(2)(A) excludes all enforceable liability arising from that acquisition from discharge, not merely restitution of the amount obtained. Debt means liability on a claim, and a claim includes an enforceable right to payment. The acquisition phrase modifies the money/property/etc., distinguishing fraudulent from nonfraudulent receipts; it does not cap resulting liability. Parallel statutory uses of debt-for, prior bankruptcy practice, the stylistic nature of the 1984 amendment, and the exception's protection of fraud victims reinforce that construction.

Here $31,382.50 in fraudulently collected rent produced $94,147.50 in treble damages, plus reasonable attorney's fees and costs. The whole resulting debt was nondischargeable; the opinion does not provide a separate dollar figure for fees/costs. Fraud findings were sustained below. Footnote 1 leaves undecided whether the New Jersey treble award is punitive: that classification does not affect the construction. Do not turn the opinion's shorthand about all liability arising from fraud into an adjudication of every fraud scenario lacking the qualifying acquisition addressed here.

## Faragher v. City of Boca Raton

No. 97-282; argued March 25, 1998; decided June 26, 1998; certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit. Souter wrote for Rehnquist, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, and Breyer, a seven-Justice Court. Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia. No other writing. The Court reversed and remanded specifically for reinstatement of the district court judgment, including the one-dollar nominal Title VII award; this was not an open-ended retrial on liability.

**Historical standard:** An employer is vicariously liable for an actionable hostile environment created by a supervisor with immediate or successively higher authority over the employee. Without a tangible employment action, the employer may prove an affirmative defense to liability or damages by a preponderance: both reasonable preventive and prompt corrective care, and the employee's unreasonable failure to use preventive/corrective opportunities or otherwise avoid harm. A harassment policy with a complaint procedure is not invariably necessary as a matter of law, though circumstances may require one. Unreasonable failure to use a complaint procedure normally satisfies the second element, but is not its exclusive form. The defense is unavailable when harassment culminates in tangible employment action, such as discharge, demotion, or undesirable reassignment.

**Agency and precedent:** Meritor supplies agency guidance and rejects both automatic employer liability and automatic immunity from a grievance procedure or lack of actual notice. Faragher preserves a distinction between scope-of-employment torts and a supervisor's personal conduct outside that scope; misuse of supervisory authority alone is not a reason to classify harassment within the scope. Instead it adapts the aided-by-agency principle to Title VII, rejecting a rigid demand that supervisory authority be expressly invoked. The defense reconciles vicarious responsibility with prevention, prompt correction, and reasonable avoidance of harm. Footnote 3 expressly disclaims a general pronouncement of agency law or wholesale transplantation of the Restatement. Footnote 4 emphasizes statutory stare decisis and Congress's retention of Meritor while expanding remedies in 1991.

The actionable-environment standard remains severe or pervasive misconduct, both subjectively and objectively hostile; ordinary teasing or isolated incidents ordinarily do not suffice unless extremely serious. The opinion's use of historical Harris and later Oncale is comparator reasoning, not permission to import those historical formulations over any controlling simulated law.

**Application and limits:** The City had entirely failed to communicate its policy to the isolated beach workforce, did not monitor the supervisors, and supplied no assurance that complaints could bypass them. Given its dispersed operations, the City could not establish reasonable care as a matter of law; the opinion contrasts a small workforce where informal precautions might suffice. The record foreclosed its proposed excuses about incomplete evidence or lack of guidance before 1990. With only nominal damages awarded, employee mitigation supplied no reason for further litigation. Imputed knowledge and negligence remained unadjudicated alternative grounds because resolving them would require unnecessary factfinding. The Court did not transform the statutory command against discrimination into a statutory text expressly prescribing this particular complaint procedure.

**Thomas/Scalia:** They reject vicarious liability absent an adverse employment consequence and would remand only for the plaintiff to prove negligence. The City should be allowed to show a reasonably available route to an official supervising both harassers, or that distributing its policy would not have revealed the harassment. Their three footnotes discuss the timing and advisory status of EEOC guidance, the handbook route to the Personnel Director and his response, and Faragher's failure to complain to City officials even after reading the policy.

The incorporated Ellerth dissent grounds their position in parallel treatment of racial and sexual harassment and the employer's own fault. Actual adverse employment acts are company acts; a hostile environment alone calls for knowledge, actual or reasonably discoverable, plus failure to remedy. They read Restatement §219(2)(d) as depending on apparent ordinary business authority, question the majority's extension and the uncertain defense, and object that a careful employer can remain liable when the employee also acted reasonably. Its discussion of Burlington's facts is not a finding about Boca Raton. Its footnote 3 treats an unfulfilled sexual threat as environmental harassment and a carried-out employment detriment as a possible disparate-treatment claim.
