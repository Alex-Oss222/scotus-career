### United States v. Fordice; Ayers v. Fordice, Nos. 90-1205 and 90-6588

Consolidated plenary merits decision, June 26, 1992

The Court vacates the Fifth Circuit's judgment in both consolidated dockets and remands for further proceedings under Mississippi's continuing duty to dismantle its former legally segregated university system. Argued November 13, 1991. On writs of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fifth Circuit sitting en banc, [914 F.2d 676 (1990)](https://static.case.law/f2d/914/cases/0676-01.json), which affirmed the District Court's compliance judgment at 674 F.Supp. 1523 (N.D.Miss. 1987). The United States and private plaintiffs challenge the standard for discharging the State's established duty and seek further remedial proceedings; private plaintiffs also seek relief concerning historically black institutions. The Court reviews Equal Protection and the related Title VI duty. The consolidated certiorari grant limits Ayers to its first two presented questions. No entitlement to a particular closure, merger, funding amount or racial quota is established by the questions under review.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision. Thomas took his oath on October 23, 1991, before argument. The full policy-specific and system-wide remand is 8–1: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support it; Scalia opposes that breadth. All nine agree that the ACT admissions requirements require further review, but Scalia supports only a narrower admission-barrier remand on his own ground.

White writes the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. O'Connor and Thomas each file a separate concurrence without additional joiners. Scalia concurs in the judgment as to further review of discriminatory ACT admissions requirements and dissents from the broader remand; he joins no part of White's opinion. His agreement on admissions supplies no ninth join in the systemic standard.

A State that maintained a legally segregated university system does not discharge its affirmative dismantlement duty merely by adopting good-faith, facially neutral policies. It must eliminate policies traceable to that system that continue to foster segregation when their effects can practicably be removed consistently with sound educational practices; the established duty does not require fresh discriminatory intent for each such remnant, and current racial imbalance alone is insufficient.

White's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, eight of nine Justices, controls the Equal Protection rule and the related Title VI disposition in these cases.

The Court holds that opening a university's doors does not necessarily remove the State's old barriers to educational choice. Brown establishes the duty to end legally imposed educational separation. Green requires effective dismantlement rather than reliance on the name given a freedom-of-choice policy. Dowell and the Court's decision in Freeman preserve practical limits and require identification of effects attributable to the established violation. University attendance is voluntary, and differing institutional missions may serve legitimate educational purposes. But the alternatives among which a student chooses remain shaped by admissions requirements, available programs and State-assigned institutional roles. Bazemore does not make those arrangements private choices: its voluntary clubs presented findings that discriminatory practices had ended and choice was unfettered. Mississippi's good faith therefore does not answer whether its inherited machinery still fosters segregation. The Title VI claim follows the same established discrimination duty here. The Court leaves new policies without roots in the dual system to ordinary equal-protection analysis and does not require proportional enrollment or decide unrelated statutory theories.

The Court applies Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, and Brown II, 349 U.S. 294; university systems share the affirmative duty to dismantle established legal segregation.

The Court applies Green v. School Board of New Kent County, 391 U.S. 430, to effective dismantlement, with voluntary university attendance and legitimate institutional differences preserved.

The Court applies Board of Education v. Dowell, 498 U.S. 237; good faith and practicable removal of vestiges remain distinct requirements.

The Court applies Freeman v. Pitts, the decision of this Court of March 31, 1992, within its actual causal and practicability limits; its school-decree holding is not replaced or mechanically transplanted.

The Court distinguishes Bazemore v. Friday, 478 U.S. 385; genuinely unfettered voluntary club choice does not establish removal of inherited university constraints.

Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp., 429 U.S. 252, remains in force for a new violation involving policies not rooted in the established dual system.

The State bears the burden of demonstrating that it has dismantled its established dual system; a court may not shift that burden to plaintiffs by demanding that they disprove discharge. The court must examine the current effects and practical alternatives of challenged inherited policies, including their interaction, and must tie any remedy to the violation rather than infer liability or relief from racial identifiability alone.

The same eight-Justice opinion of the Court adopts the burden, combined-effects inquiry and violation-linked remedial boundary.

The Court holds that a finding of current good intentions cannot substitute for proof of completed dismantlement. Brown II places responsibility for overcoming the established unlawful system on the State. Green requires examination of whether the chosen measures actually remove its operation, and Swann ties remedial authority to the constitutional wrong. Here the District Court identified substantial unnecessary duplication yet relied on an asserted absence of plaintiffs' proof that changing it would affect enrollment. That approach reverses the burden and isolates a policy that may reinforce admissions and mission differences. The Court's Freeman decision likewise requires attention to causal and remedial connections rather than treating a finding on one question as an answer to all others. Undisturbed historical findings remain evidence; applying the wrong compliance standard does not itself require retrying every fact. On remand the courts must examine the affected policies together where their effects interact, assess educational justification and practical change, and state the findings supporting relief. Neither general racial balance nor unlimited judicial management follows.

The Court applies Brown II, 349 U.S. 294; the State retains the burden of showing discharge of its established duty.

The Court applies Green, 391 U.S. 430; effectiveness is assessed in actual operation, not by formal policy labels.

The Court applies Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1; the scope of the violation bounds equitable relief.

The Court applies Freeman v. Pitts, the decision of this Court of March 31, 1992; causal burdens, practicable compliance and remedial interdependence are retained.

The Milliken v. Bradley decisions, 418 U.S. 717 and 433 U.S. 267, remain in force; remediation must address the proven violation and its consequences.

Where present admissions requirements derive from deliberately exclusionary criteria and continue segregative effects, the State cannot establish dismantlement merely by attributing their later retention to neutral educational purposes. On this record it must adequately justify the differential thresholds and ACT-only method under the continuing-duty standard or replace the unjustified, practicably removable remnants; the Court does not invalidate every use of the ACT or prescribe a substitute score.

White's eight-Justice Court opinion controls this application. Scalia supplies a ninth vote for further admissions review only on his distinct intentional-admission-barrier ground; he does not join the broader rule.

The Court holds that changing a policy's stated purpose does not by itself remove its discriminatory inheritance. Brown and Green require the State to dismantle the exclusionary system actually established. The ACT requirements began as a means of excluding black applicants from historically white institutions, and the lower findings show continuing differences in automatic admission opportunities. The asserted concern about unprepared students does not adequately explain all differences between institutions with comparable undergraduate missions or establish why different missions require these precise thresholds. The refusal to consider school performance alongside test scores also requires more than the general invocation of uneven grading. The record presents an available predictor of success and materially different effects that the courts below did not adequately assess under the State's burden. This conclusion does not replace university admissions expertise with a judicial formula. It requires an explanation adequate to the established remedial duty and examination of practicable alternatives. The State may maintain sound admissions standards that satisfy that duty; no universal ACT prohibition is announced.

The Court applies Brown and Green; inherited discriminatory admission barriers require effective dismantlement rather than a new neutral label.

The Court applies Dowell and the Court's Freeman decision; practical removal and causal attribution govern the continuing compliance inquiry.

The Court applies Florida ex rel. Hawkins v. Board of Control, 350 U.S. 413, to equal access to higher education; no new test-score formula follows.

The corrected inquiry must examine unnecessary program duplication, inherited mission assignments, continued institutional organization, and other properly challenged features including affected funding, with their combined segregative effects and educational justifications. Neither racial identifiability nor fiscal inefficiency alone requires closing or merging a university, and improved funding alone cannot establish dismantlement while unlawful barriers remain.

White's opinion, joined by the same seven Justices, controls the full remand in both dockets; Scalia dissents from this breadth.

The Court holds that the State must examine the structure through which its universities offer educational opportunity. Green supplies the demand for effective removal of the former system, and Swann and Milliken limit remedies to its established consequences. Unnecessary duplication can preserve separate pathways, and mission assignments that carry forward the former institutions' unequal roles may reinforce admissions barriers. Maintaining all eight institutions must also be examined where that organization perpetuates the dual system; expense alone does not resolve the constitutional issue. The present record does not establish that closing any school is required, and changes to admissions or programs may make closure unnecessary. Historically black institutions' educational value and students' interests remain relevant to sound remedial design. The Court rejects an entitlement to additional funding merely to maintain publicly financed separate enclaves, but leaves funding needed for actual dismantlement for remand. The Alabama State Teachers Association summary affirmance concerned an educationally justified new campus; it did not approve every inherited policy. Concrete remedies must follow the corrected findings, not precede them.

The Court applies Green and Swann to effective, violation-linked structural relief rather than a numerical enrollment command.

The Court applies Milliken, 418 U.S. 717, and 433 U.S. 267, to connect practical educational relief to proven consequences.

The Court confines Alabama State Teachers Association v. Alabama Public School and College Authority, 393 U.S. 400, to the issues necessarily affirmed concerning the justified new campus; no blanket university-compliance safe harbor is recognized.

Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, the decision of this Court of January 15, 1992, remains intact in its consent-decree-modification setting; it supplies no different liability or automatic termination standard here.

No precedent is overruled. The Court creates neither a general Title VI disparate-impact cause of action nor a heightened standard of proof. Ordinary intent requirements remain for a new policy outside the established dual system. Freeman's separate disposition concerning school supervision remains intact.

Effective June 26, 1992, Mississippi must establish actual dismantlement by addressing the challenged policies' roots in the former dual system, present segregative effects, educational justification and practicable cure. Neutral wording and good faith alone do not discharge the established affirmative duty in higher education. The State's existing burden continues. Neither racial identifiability nor every difference in funding, programs or admissions establishes a violation. Properly challenged inherited policies must be reassessed with their relevant interactions, subject to causation and educational practicality. Equal Protection and the related Title VI disposition share that duty; the Court establishes no specific closure, merger, funding award or racial assignment rule.

O'Connor joins the Court and separately emphasizes careful examination of the State's claimed educational reasons. On her view, where an essential remnant must remain, the State must counteract and minimize its segregative effects as far as possible. Her additional formulation does not replace the Court's stated rule.

Thomas joins the Court and separately stresses policy-specific liability and sound education. He views tainted origins, continued adverse effect and lack of adequate justification as supporting the inference that the prior discriminatory purpose has not been overcome. He stresses that historically black institutions may have independent educational value and traditions; the decision neither commands their destruction nor guarantees their continued racial identifiability. His intent formulation is not an additional Court test.

Scalia agrees that discriminatory admissions barriers must be removed and that Mississippi has not adequately justified continued ACT requirements. He opposes the broader remand, reading Bazemore to make truly nondiscriminatory access sufficient in voluntary higher education. He objects that the wider effects inquiry gives uncertain guidance and may pressure the State to reduce institutional diversity. He rejects a constitutional entitlement to funding equality while preserving the State's freedom to support lawful institutions.

The cases return through the Fifth Circuit for further proceedings. The courts below must examine traceable policies' current segregative effects, educational justification, practical elimination and interaction, including differential admissions, unnecessary duplication, mission assignments, continued institutional organization, and affected funding and other challenged features. Existing factual findings are not set aside merely because the governing legal standard was wrong. If the corrected inquiry establishes an undischarged obligation, the State must be adjudged in violation of Equal Protection and the related Title VI duty, followed by appropriate remedial proceedings. The Court orders no campus closure, merger, fixed funding award, compulsory assignment or numerical racial balance. Supreme Court merits review is complete in both dockets; the Court retains no supervisory docket and sets no special mandate date or further hearing. No separate Ayers event remains unresolved.

**End of entry: United States v. Fordice; Ayers v. Fordice, consolidated plenary merits decision, June 26, 1992.**

### Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña, No. 93-1841

Merits decision, June 12, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 17, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, 16 F.3d 1537 (1994), affirming summary judgment for federal respondents. The admitted question is what equal-protection scrutiny governs the federal subcontractor compensation arrangement and whether this record permits final judgment. The prime selected a certified firm instead of Adarand's low bid; no particular certification route is found.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Tenth Circuit judgment is affirmed, 5–4.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Equal competitive opportunity and prospective standing

**Controlling proposition:** A bidder able and ready to compete suffers a personal injury when a governmental racial preference denies equal competitive opportunity; it need not prove that it would have received the contract. Prospective relief also requires a real, sufficiently imminent renewed exposure to that preference, which Adarand's repeated bidding and continuing opportunity to encounter the challenged incentive establish here.

**Authority:** Threshold portion of Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the proposition. Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas independently agree with this threshold conclusion in their dissenting positions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that unequal opportunity to compete is an injury even when the award remains uncertain. Northeastern Florida identifies the governmental barrier, rather than proof of the award that would follow its removal, as the relevant competitive injury. Adarand submitted the low bid, but the prime contractor selected a certified disadvantaged business in response to the financial incentive. The contractor could decline that incentive; its optional character does not remove its effect on competitive opportunity. Lujan requires the connection to be concrete and the requested relief appropriate to a continuing or threatened injury. Adarand's recurring bids and the likelihood of encountering the challenged clause again satisfy that prospective requirement on this record. The Court does not infer a guaranteed future award, an entitlement to damages, or a waiver of sovereign immunity. Nor does accepting this competitive injury establish that the challenged preference is unconstitutional; justification remains a separate question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Northeastern Florida Chapter, AGC v. Jacksonville (June 14, 1993): applies its equal-opportunity injury rule; no guaranteed contract award is required.
- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (June 12, 1992): retains concrete injury, causal connection and stage-appropriate proof; prospective relief requires sufficiently imminent renewed exposure.

##### The inherited federal remedial standard sustains this challenge

**Controlling proposition:** Under Metro Broadcasting, a benign federal racial classification supported by Congress must serve an important governmental objective and be substantially related to that objective; Fullilove retains its supported judgment recognizing Congress's remedial authority at the scope actually sustained there. Applying that meaningful review of purpose, fit and burdens, the Court affirms the judgment rejecting this preserved challenge to the subcontractor compensation arrangement, without deciding every certification route or conferring a general exemption on measures called benign.

**Authority:** Merits portion of Justice Stevens's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this rule and bounded application, not merely the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that supported efforts to overcome exclusion differ constitutionally from measures maintaining racial subordination. Metro Broadcasting supplies the inherited federal standard, and Fullilove recognizes Congress's distinct authority and competence to identify and remedy barriers to economic participation. Those decisions do not authorize preferences on goodwill alone. The lower court examined the statutory evidence, delegation, eligibility and flexibility, rather than simply accepting a remedial label. The incentive remains optional, qualifying firms outside the presumptive groups may obtain certification, and the challenged presumptions are subject to safeguards and rebuttal. Those features support the judgment within the challenge presented. The Court does not treat the lower court's description of concessions as an unconditional waiver: the actual appellate statement accepted evidence only for discussion and continued to contest tailoring, and the merits brief contests the delegation characterization. Nor does the Court find that Gonzales used a particular certification route. Croson's treatment of state and local classifications does not require abandonment of the governing federal precedents here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Metro Broadcasting, Inc. v. FCC, 497 U.S. 547 (1990): retains the important-objective/substantial-relationship standard for benign federal classifications at its actual scope.
- Fullilove v. Klutznick, 448 U.S. 448 (1980): retains its supported remedial judgment and congressional-competence recognition; no single universal rationale is attributed to the fractured decision.
- City of Richmond v. J. A. Croson Co., 488 U.S. 469 (1989): distinguished as state/local remedial classification law; its standard is not displaced or extended to erase Metro here.
- Wygant v. Jackson Board of Education, 476 U.S. 267 (1986): retains attention to evidence, remedial fit and burdens; remedial purpose alone supplies no exemption.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Metro and Fullilove remain effective at the scope stated above; Croson and Wygant retain their separate settings and burdens. Shaw remains territorial-design law at its adopted scope; it establishes no universal contracting scrutiny tier. No precedent is overruled.

The inherited federal remedial framework remains controlling; the preserved challenge is rejected. The decision does not decide every certification route, all future applications, sovereign immunity, damages, or any program-wide injunction. The compensation clause is an optional incentive, distinct from STURAA's expenditure command or a mandatory subcontracting duty. DBE subcontracts must total more than 10% of the original prime contract; compensation is 10% of approved final DBE subcontract amounts, capped at 1.5% of the original contract for one DBE and 2% for two or more. It compensates the stated assistance and documentation duties and requires certification evidence and a certified executed subcontract copy. Compensation is confined to DBE-performed work; adjustment may apply to non-DBE second-tier work, with full compensation permitted where only insignificant portions are subcontracted and the DBE performs the major portion. No finding is made that every duty was performed or every payment is constitutional.

SBA §8(a) presumes social disadvantage but requires individual proof of economic disadvantage. DOT state certification rebuttably presumes both, and third parties may challenge actual disadvantage. The §8(d) regulations remain in tension: 13 CFR §124.106(b) appears to require individual economic proof while 48 CFR §19.703(a)(2) appears to presume both. Nonminority applicants may establish disadvantage. Other governmental, county or city certification is accepted individually only on the contracting officer's finding of an acceptable and viable certification program. Mandatory §8(a) review, urged but nonmandatory DOT state review, and the absence of an identified separate §8(d) review provision remain distinct. Gonzales's actual route is unknown. The Court does not find irrebuttability, lack of actual disadvantage, or resolve the conflicting §8(d) rules.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor dissents, joined by Justice Kennedy throughout and by Justices Scalia and Thomas in the uniform-scrutiny and vacatur/remand portions. She would require a compelling interest and narrow tailoring for federal explicit racial classifications, replace Metro's relaxed tier, and remand for actual application. Congress's institutional competence may inform evidence and fit, but does not replace individual judicial protection. Strict scrutiny need not invalidate every racial remedy. O'Connor and Kennedy support the dissent's particular stare-decisis explanation; it is not joined by Scalia or Thomas and is not the Court's ground.

Justice Scalia separately dissents. He supports strict review and vacatur/remand but rejects compensation or entitlement based merely on shared racial ancestry, and does not join an affirmative endorsement of group catchup remedies. Justice Thomas separately dissents, rejecting the benign/paternalistic exception and racial entitlement by group ancestry. His broader equivalence and paternalism reasoning is his own; neither separate position makes the unknown certification route invalid or issues an injunction.

Justice Souter concurs, joined by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer, emphasizing the actual scope of Fullilove and Metro, constitutional stare decisis, and the need to distinguish preserved challenges from disputed concession characterizations. Justice Ginsburg also concurs, joined by Justice Breyer, emphasizing continuing exclusion, meaningful access and Congress's remedial competence while preserving review of outsiders' burdens. These concurrences add no unrestricted quota entitlement or universal benign-program exemption.

The joint appendix pp. 1–36, including the subcontractor compensation clause at pp. 24–26 and prime contractor’s declaration at pp. 30–31, petitioner’s merits brief pp. 16–24, and respondents’ merits brief pp. 12–19 and 29–35 supply the arrangement and competing arguments. Constitutional and statutory sources include the Fifth Amendment, Small Business Act and operative certification regulations. Fullilove, Metro, Croson and Wygant supply precedent within the limits stated above. The §8(d) regulatory conflict and actual certification route remain unresolved.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Tenth Circuit judgment remains effective. No remand is ordered by this decision, and no administrative program redesign or continuing judicial supervision is directed.

### Missouri v. Jenkins, No. 93-1823

Merits decision, June 12, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 11, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, reviewing both 11 F.3d 755 and 13 F.3d 1170. The questions concern the challenged instructional and noninstructional salary orders and the continuing quality-education obligation and partial-release standard. Review does not reopen the broader foundation of the magnet remedy, settled liability, the capital plan, voluntary transfers or every earlier funding order.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Both Eighth Circuit judgments are affirmed, 5–4, on the challenged salary and quality-education components.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Challenged salary orders, both reviewed judgments | Affirm, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Existing orders sustained on independent educational repair and staff necessity |
| Quality education and denial of partial release | Affirm, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Continuing jurisdiction and present funding preserved; later properly supported release motion remains available |

No reimbursement, immediate salary clawback, rollback or new taxation is ordered. The actual fifty/fifty allocation and joint-and-several obligations within the salary orders’ terms are not rewritten. A prior salary settlement is not reopened. No new supervisory body or review condition is imposed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Violation-linked salary support is permissible on the preserved findings

**Controlling proposition:** A desegregation court may fund instructional and noninstructional salary support reasonably necessary to deliver educational measures repairing established segregation-caused injury, without treating low salaries as a separate constitutional wrong. These challenged orders are sustained on their independently sufficient intradistrict educational-repair and personnel-necessity findings; incidental voluntary attraction does not negate that ground, and the broader magnet foundation is not reopened.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this holding and affirm both reviewed salary judgments.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these salary orders rest on a sufficient lawful educational-repair basis. Milliken II permits measures repairing educational effects of state-imposed segregation, while Swann makes equitable flexibility serve the established wrong. The preserved findings identify inferior education, reduced achievement and the staff necessary to implement remedial instruction and programs. Critical support functions include maintenance, warehouse, operating, mailroom and food-service work. The State's argument that each salary shortfall must itself be unconstitutional mistakes implementation costs for the original injury. Nor does voluntary attraction outside the district defeat an independently sufficient repair basis within it. Milliken I still bars a remedy for an unproved interdistrict violation; the Court neither authorizes external racial balancing nor reopens the magnet foundation without adequate notice and briefing. The November salary findings were not challenged as clearly erroneous; distinct December labor-market and support-staff challenges were reviewed under that standard. The Court preserves those distinctions, approves no automatic future increase and orders no general educational improvement guarantee.

**Precedent treatment:** Milliken v. Bradley (Milliken II), 433 U.S. 267, is applied to violation-linked educational repair and necessary implementation. Swann is applied to equitable means bounded by the adjudicated injury. Milliken I, 418 U.S. 717, retains the interdistrict-violation limit; it is not extended into a categorical support-staff exclusion.

##### Partial release requires the complete Freeman showing

**Controlling proposition:** Partial withdrawal may occur only after the defendant seeking release proves full and satisfactory compliance and practicable elimination of the relevant vestiges, whole-decree good faith shown by conduct, and the necessary causal and remedial connections; retained control necessary or practicable for compliance elsewhere and nonobstruction of remaining relief must be assessed. Missouri’s proof of installing programs does not make that complete showing, so continued funding and denial of partial release are affirmed; low scores alone establish neither continuing constitutional injury nor completed compliance, and national parity is not an indispensable release condition.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the complete existing Freeman rule and its present-record application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Missouri did not establish entitlement to the requested partial release. Dowell identifies the temporary purpose of desegregation relief, and the Court's Freeman decision permits withdrawal in stages only on its complete showing. Installing programs and spending funds do not alone prove practicable elimination of the effects those measures repair. Missouri's submission also did not establish its own whole-decree good faith, the remaining remedial connections or causal independence. Achievement evidence can illuminate those questions, but below-national scores are neither an automatic finding of constitutional injury nor a command to continue every beneficial program permanently. The contrary argument assumes that implementation itself ends the obligation; it omits the effects and connections Freeman requires. Conversely, the Court does not adopt maximum educational potential or satisfaction of every program goal as the constitutional standard. A later motion remains available on proper evidence. Existing findings receive clear-error review, and a finding on one issue cannot resolve unexamined causal or remedial relationships. No additional consensus, reviewer endorsement or changed-circumstances requirement is imposed.

**Precedent treatment:** Freeman v. Pitts, 503 U.S. 467, is applied at its operative scope: permission, release and causal burdens, whole-decree conduct, retained control and nonobstruction. Dowell is applied to temporary relief and dissolution. Rufo retains its consent-modification scope and supplies no changed-circumstances condition for fulfilled school obligations.

The State and district’s respective responsibilities must be assessed separately; one defendant’s conduct does not establish the other’s compliance. The defendant bears the release showing and the showing that imbalance for which it disclaims responsibility is not traceable to the violation. Genuinely independent demographic change imposes no perpetual balancing duty once the required work is completed. Racial imbalance alone proves neither liability nor completed remedy. Partial release may, rather than must, follow a satisfactory showing. The court must examine whether retaining control is necessary or practicable for compliance elsewhere, avoid obstructing remaining relief, and define released duties, remaining obligations and retained supervision. Existing factual findings receive clear-error review; findings on one issue do not decide unexamined connections. There is no all-components-at-once restriction or good-intentions substitute.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holdings apply Milliken II, Swann, Milliken I, Dowell and Freeman as stated. Rufo is kept within its distinct modification scope. No precedent is overruled. Ex parte Peterson and La Buy appear only in the concurrence and establish no new majority procedural holding here.

Violation-linked educational repair includes supported instructional and critical support-staff implementation. The complete Freeman rule remains controlling without a national-score-parity requirement. The broad magnet foundation is reserved on this review. The judgment does not guarantee general improvement, perpetual funding or immediate release. Shaw’s racial-design-only districting holding neither removes the independently established segregation injury nor decides school-remedy scope.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs. He would recognize joint development with independent review as an available method for revising the continuing remedy within the court’s discretion, rather than a prescribed new supervisory institution. State and district officials and class representatives and counsel should develop proposals with meaningful opportunities for families and educators to review information, offer alternatives and identify material disagreement. Participation supplements legal representation; it does not displace administrators, create a compliance veto or substitute agreement for the governing obligation. If disagreements remain, the judge decides.

A qualified neutral reviewer may assess implementation, evidence reliability and alternatives only under applicable procedural authority, after the parties can address qualifications and conflicts. Duties, access and scope must be defined; the parties must be able to challenge the report and its evidence. The reviewer advises and the judge retains findings, enforcement and release decisions. Ex parte Peterson supports defined judicial assistance; La Buy forbids displacement of the judicial function, and neither supplies unrestricted appointment authority. Public reports and reasons should identify remaining injury, actual remedial benefits, burdens and alternatives while protecting student information. Review must remain proportionate to unresolved work.

Programs, staffing, schedules and progress measures may be revised with explained connections to the same remedial question and intelligible comparisons over time. Recruitment and retention evidence can inform salary necessity; better retention alone does not establish elimination of educational injury. An ineffective measure need not remain simply because it was in the initial decree. These are possible evidence sources, not a compulsory checklist or a general-improvement mission. Release requires the governing defendant-specific showing; neither plaintiff agreement, consensus nor reviewer endorsement is necessary. A motion cannot be indefinitely delayed by participation or review. Material evidentiary deficiencies should be identified, monitoring should narrow with remaining work, and further relief must rest on proper authority and record support. This method remains noncontrolling and adds no substantive release condition.

Justice O’Connor dissents, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. They would remand the challenged salary orders to identify an independently sufficient intradistrict repair ground because the adopted external-recruitment purpose cannot obtain authority merely from personnel necessity to implement that objective. They acknowledge the actual educational and necessity findings, lawful support-staff funding and clear-error constraints; they would not dismantle all magnet remedies, reverse every funding order, claw back salaries or impose new taxation. For quality education, they accept complete Freeman burdens, whole-decree conduct, retained-control necessity or practicability, nonobstruction and defined obligations. They would require explicit separation of residual injury and practicable repair from contextual maximum-potential and goals-in-entirety language. Correct abstract factors and inadequate implementation proof do not, in their view, identify the independently lawful application. They find no earned entitlement to release and impose no plaintiff causation burden, national score threshold or mandatory supervisor. Their scope permits examination of the salary purpose actually presented without reopening all settled remedial foundations.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit judgments remain in force and the district court retains remedial jurisdiction under the existing orders. Missouri may seek partial or full release on a proper motion with the complete governing showing. Further changes require their own legal and factual basis. No new supervisory arrangement is ordered and no mandatory next release date is set.
