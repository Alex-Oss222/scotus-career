## Material record and the lower judgments

The four plaintiffs—Cheryl Hopwood, Douglas Carvell, Kenneth Elliott and David Rogers—are white Texas residents who applied to the 1992 entering class and were not admitted through the communicated admissions process. The case is not a class action. The complaint sought declaratory and injunctive relief, admission, compensatory damages and punitive damages under the Equal Protection Clause and derivative claims under §§1981 and 1983 and Title VI. The named defendants are Texas, the university/regents/law school and the identified officials in their official capacities; no individual-capacity defendant is established by the lower opinion's footnote 13.

The Texas Index combined LSAT and undergraduate GPA; the approximation was sixty percent LSAT and forty percent GPA, with conversion for two historical LSAT scales. The index sorted files into presumptive admission, discretionary review and presumptive denial, but the admissions office also considered undergraduate rigor, major, grade trends, background, experience and other individual characteristics. Approximately five to ten percent of presumptive-admit files could be moved into discretionary review. Files in the presumptive-deny range could also be upgraded when the score understated potential. Those exceptions matter: an index category was consequential but not an exceptionless mechanical decision.

Black American and Mexican-American applicants received separate, lower index thresholds, separate review and separate waiting lists. In March 1992 the resident presumptive-admit threshold was 199 for white and other nonpreferred applicants and 189 for Black and Mexican-American applicants; presumptive-deny thresholds were 192 and 179, respectively. Earlier thresholds for the two preferred groups differed and were adjusted during the admissions season. Within the 189–192 range, the recorded admission rates were 100 percent for Black applicants, 90 percent for Mexican-American applicants and six percent for white applicants. These figures describe that pool, not every applicant or an adjudicated lawful counterfactual.

Applications were coded by race. Preferred-group discretionary files received minority-subcommittee consideration; other discretionary files were distributed to three-member subcommittees whose limited votes determined offers, waiting-list status or rejection, subject to the chairman's veto. The law school pursued aspirations of roughly ten percent Mexican-American and five percent Black enrollment. In 1992 the resulting entering proportions were about 10.7 and eight percent. The record does not clearly establish how the different waiting-list classes were compared for final openings, although race was monitored. The plaintiffs' assertion that hundreds of higher-scoring white residents were passed over is not a specific finding relied on by the lower court.

Hopwood's index was 199; the chairman moved her into discretionary review because he assessed her educational background as overstating the GPA's strength. The other three had indexes of 197. Each received one or no subcommittee vote. Footnote 57 identifies a material individualized-remedy point: the District Court found that a late offer to Elliott had not been communicated, while also finding that all four probably would not have been admitted under a constitutional process. The appellate court directed reconsideration of that tension. Carvell testified to the tuition difference between Texas and Southern Methodist, which he attended. The truth and legally compensable amount of each claimed loss remain for proper findings.

After a bench trial, the District Court applied strict scrutiny. It accepted educational diversity and remediation as compelling interests, found defects in separate discretionary-review committees, accepted a racial plus in the index component and discussed presumptive denial in a footnote rather than entering a separate categorical holding about it. It awarded each plaintiff one dollar nominal damages, declaratory relief and fee-free reapplication. It placed ultimate nonadmission/damages persuasion on the plaintiffs using a Title VII analogy, found they had not shown admission under a lawful process, and refused compensatory damages, punitive damages, admission and a broad prospective injunction.

For remediation, the District Court found Texas's educational institutions connected and identified the law school's reputation as a white institution, perceived hostility and minority underrepresentation as present effects of past discrimination; it also said there was strong evidence even using the University of Texas as the unit. It found no recent overt officially sanctioned discrimination at the university. The Fifth Circuit rejected a statewide or university-wide remedial unit, limited the relevant actor to the law school, and rejected the identified effects as insufficiently connected or remedied by the preferences. Texas invoked its history, the Texas Plan/OCR process and continuing educational effects; the appellate panel found no direct Texas Plan command imposing this particular law-school admissions program. These are attributed findings and rulings, not a new Supreme Court resolution of Fordice's scope.

The challenged separate-committee process was discontinued. Contrary to the earlier packet's statement that nothing is known about a later design, the lower opinion recites a small administrative admissions group and no presumptive admit/deny scores in the new procedure. The law school continued to defend race-conscious consideration. That limited description establishes neither the complete later program nor its constitutional validity. A ruling on the 1992 system cannot silently adjudicate all subsequent admissions processes.

The Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded the merits judgment. It held diversity an impermissible justification for the racial admissions treatment and rejected the offered remedial grounds. It directed that the law school bear a preponderance burden of proving that each plaintiff would not have been admitted under a constitutional admissions system; appropriate equitable or monetary relief would remain for the District Court if the school did not establish the same result. It held that the intentional differential treatment qualified as intentional discrimination for the Title VI analysis despite good faith. It expressly did **not** decide Eleventh Amendment immunity. It upheld refusal of punitive damages in view of the good-faith finding under Smith v. Wade. It left the District Court's refusal to issue an injunction intact, expecting compliance with its legal directions and leaving any later injunction to that court. It did not enter an injunction itself.

Judge Wiener agreed with the result and the causation-burden correction but would assume diversity compelling and invalidate the 1992 process for insufficient tailoring. He considered the panel's categorical diversity resolution unnecessarily broad and opposed its prospective remedial directions concerning programs beyond these four individuals' 1992 claims. He agreed that neither the law school nor the university system had shown sufficient present effects on this record, while rejecting a statewide public-school basis. These separate grounds are material alternatives, not a Supreme Court writing or vote.

On denial of rehearing, Chief Judge Politz and Judges King, Wiener, Benavides, Stewart, Parker and Dennis dissented. They argued that the panel's categorical prohibition both exceeded what these individual 1992 claims required and disregarded Bakke's continuing authority under Rodriguez de Quijas's instruction that lower courts follow directly controlling Supreme Court precedent until the Court itself changes it. Their footnote 10 identifies Powell's Part V-C as expressly joined by Brennan, White, Marshall and Blackmun, rejecting an injunction against every consideration of race. Judge Stewart separately stressed the continuing institutional history recited in the District Court, including post-Sweatt discrimination and the absence of a determination that Texas's system had completed compliance; his disagreement bears on the inference that the 1950 decision or an absence of recent overt discrimination necessarily ended every traceable effect. These are lower-court dissent positions, not new facts proved by their force of expression or controlling Supreme Court construction. The groups had offered a Title VI attack on the index and a claim that affirmative action was required; the court did not adjudicate that substantive independent Title VI challenge, and expressly did not preclude a separate suit.
