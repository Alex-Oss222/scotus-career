## Threshold comparison and adopted construction

**Preserved-review path adopted.** Section 309(a) sets April 1, 1997 as Title III-A's commencement, expressly excepting §309 and §§303(b)(2), 306(c), 308(d)(2)(D), and 308(d)(5). Corrected §306(c)(1) makes the §306(a)/(b) amendments applicable as provided in §309, with a separate instruction that §242(g) extends to claims from past, pending and future proceedings. The latter instruction overrides the limitation of §242(g) to newly commenced proceedings; it does not state a different date on which the new §242(g) jurisdictional rule itself begins operating. Section 306(c) is an applicability instruction already operative as an instruction about the new system. Its exception has real work after April 1: §242(g), unlike the generally saved changes, can reach old proceedings. Reading the cross-reference and exception this way preserves both the general commencement command and §242(g)'s distinct temporal range. The corrected phrase 'except that' separates the latter's coverage from §309's ordinary grandfathering; it is not itself an enactment-date command.

**Strongest contrary construction tested.** Section 309(a) expressly excepts §306(c); §306(c)(1) then uses mandatory language and reaches pending claims without limitation. Those words support immediate jurisdictional operation, particularly because Congress could withdraw jurisdiction from pending review without reclassifying the underlying fraud. The adopted reading does not invoke a presumption that jurisdiction can never change during litigation. It answers the text: Congress specified a commencement date for the new amendments, while the exceptional sentence specifies the proceedings reached but supplies no replacement commencement date. Giving it immediate effect as an applicability instruction does not render its old-proceeding coverage redundant. Section 309(c)(1)'s explicit preservation of judicial review reinforces the need to distinguish these jobs. None of the eight Associates treats a general reference to delay as sufficient without this construction.

**Independent scope and review-saving questions considered, not silently decided.** If §242(g) commenced immediately, its three subjects—decisions/actions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases, and execute orders—would require a separate construction. The Government could characterize the waiver denial as part of adjudicating the deportation case, and 'arising from' cannot be ignored. Yang could distinguish a claim about the legal scope of waiver discretion in ordinary review of a completed final order from a claim against the decision to institute, conduct or execute enforcement. Section 242(g)'s exception for review provided in §242 and the corrected saving rules would then have to operate together; a construction eliminating ordinary saved judicial review of every legal error would create substantial tension with §309(c)(1). Conversely, §309(c)(1) cannot simply erase §306(c)(1)'s special treatment of §242(g). Section 306(c)(2) protects existing §106 judgments/orders against invalidation or compelled reconsideration by paragraph (1); it does not by itself conclusively confer continued appellate jurisdiction. The temporal holding makes choosing between these scope constructions unnecessary at the November event. No Associate commits to a holding governing §242(g)'s post-April scope, a free-standing constitutional exception, or all removal litigation.

The permanent discretionary bar in §242(a)(2)(B)(i) has its own list—§§212(h), 212(i), 240A, 240B and 245—and clause (ii) separately addresses other statutorily discretionary authority, except §208(a) grants. It is not the transitional list. Corrected §309(c)(4) instead turns on a final exclusion/deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30 and lists §§212(c), 212(h), 212(i), 244 and 245. Former §241(a)(1)(H) is absent. The 1995 order and review supply no newly triggered order; the Supreme Court's scheduled date cannot be substituted. ## Merits and remedy comparison

**Adopted rule.** Statutory eligibility for this discretionary waiver does not prohibit consideration of a continuing immigration-fraud pattern merely because particular acts were connected with the initial entry. The Ninth Circuit's categorical 'inextricably intertwined' exclusion is therefore erroneous. This conclusion does not authorize an automatic denial to every eligible applicant on the bare fact establishing eligibility, dispense with the agency's actual balance, or displace binding agency rules or policies.

The statutory eligibility and consequential-waiver clauses define who may receive relief and what an actually granted waiver accomplishes. Neither commands evidentiary amnesia at the selection stage. *Jay* supplies the eligibility/discretion distinction. *Errico*'s mandatory predecessor and family purpose cannot convert the amended discretion into entitlement or eliminate the naturalization and repeated-fraud distinctions in this record. *NCIR* supports discretion structured within the statute, including genuinely available individual consideration. The BIA actually balanced favorable circumstances; the Court may not replace that balance with its own assessment.

**Strongest competing merits path.** The waiver would be emptied of practical content if every applicant necessarily lost for the very fraud Congress made waivable. A narrow disregard rule could distinguish unavoidable entry fraud from independent dishonesty; an affirmance could then require reweighing without entry-related acts. The objection succeeds against an automatic universal denial, but it does not justify the Ninth Circuit's rule excluding later naturalization fraud and collaboration simply because they share a factual history. Those acts can distinguish the seriousness and duration of misconduct among otherwise eligible applicants. The BIA's express disregard of initial entry fraud independently narrows what must be decided here. The Court need not decide whether the unavoidable minimum fraud alone would sustain an individualized denial.

**Policy path.** A lawful statutory factor and compliance with agency policy are separate. An unexplained departure from a binding rule or genuinely settled policy may be arbitrary even where the statute allows that factor. *Smiley* and *Good Samaritan* require actual source and reason review; they supply no finding that the BIA changed policy here. The recovered BIA decision expressly retains its entry-fraud distinction and explains why other conduct matters. A judicial remand premised on a newly found policy reversal would lack that necessary finding. Conversely, statutory authority does not immunize any independently preserved, substantiated policy-consistency objection.

**Disposition committed:** Reverse the Ninth Circuit's categorical legal restriction and remand for proceedings consistent with the permissible-factor ruling. Do not order a waiver, redo the balance, or find an unrecovered policy reversal. The mandate removes the compelled reweighing requirement resting on the categorical exclusion. It does not require the BIA to reopen or reconsider on a newly invented ground; any genuinely remaining, preserved objection is considered only to the extent the lower court's ordinary mandate and record permit. No new deportation execution, stay, deadline, or personal consequence is ordered.
