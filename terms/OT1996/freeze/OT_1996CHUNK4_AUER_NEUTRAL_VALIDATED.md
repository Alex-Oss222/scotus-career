## 1. Route, judgment below, and preserved questions

The petition seeks review of an Eighth Circuit judgment treating the petitioner St. Louis police sergeants and lieutenant as exempt executive or administrative employees under the Fair Labor Standards Act. The officers seek reversal and further proceedings on overtime; the police commissioners defend exemption. The route is review of a federal court of appeals under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), not review of a state judgment under §1257. The approved event and argument dates do not settle a disputed immunity defense or establish an overtime award. No amount of unpaid overtime, final damages calculation, or completed correction payment is established in the supplied source.

The source-supported questions are whether the salary-basis rule validly applies to these public employees, what it means to be practically subject to impermissible short disciplinary deductions, and when correction can preserve the exemption. The petition presents those three subjects; the two merits questions reproduced below pursue salary basis and correction. Salary amount, salary basis, and duties are separate exemption requirements. Respondents report a stipulation that every petitioner earned at least $250 weekly, making the executive and administrative short tests applicable, and both respondents and the United States expressly describe the appellate duties holding as unchallenged. The duties ruling therefore is an uncontested predicate, not an independent preserved error on this source record. A salary-basis ruling does not authorize a new duties adjudication or itself establish the number or value of compensable overtime hours.

Additional deduction examples and employee-understanding testimony present a narrower preservation dispute. The United States says those particular materials were not advanced in the court of appeals or petition; the officers say they are supporting evidence for an already preserved salary-basis issue. Neither position is an adjudicated forfeiture. Keep the legal issue, particular evidence, and any necessary additional factual finding distinct.

## 2. Supported operative record and competing positions

The departmental manual governed exempt and nonexempt personnel and permitted disciplinary sanctions including unpaid suspensions shorter than a full workweek. Sergeant Guzy accepted a negotiated two-day unpaid suspension for a residency violation in place of proposed termination. The appellate court treated that event as unique. The residency rule later made termination the sole penalty. These facts establish neither that all disciplinary authorizations were eliminated nor that every officer faced an effective prospect of a short deduction. They also do not establish an adjudicated pattern of recurring deductions.

The officers contend that practical exposure to impermissible deductions can defeat salaried status without an actual deduction from each claimant. They rely on the manual, disciplinary practice, and employee understanding. The United States proposes actual practice or a communicated policy creating a significant likelihood of such deductions. On that view, a merely theoretical possibility in an undifferentiated range of penalties is insufficient, while effective communication may matter before a deduction occurs. Its brief expressly leaves the precise frequency of deductions and extent of notice needed unresolved. Do not convert this proposed interpretation into an actual-deductions-only rule, numerical minimum, or conclusively established finding about this department.

Correction presents three separate questions: eligibility to use paragraph (a)(6), satisfaction of its conditions, and disposition while conditions remain unsatisfied. The officers argue that the intentional deduction, remaining disciplinary authorizations, and failure promptly to restore pay preclude correction. The United States reads inadvertence and reasons other than lack of work as alternative grounds, allowing an isolated intentional disciplinary deduction to qualify while distinguishing a settled policy of regular or recurring deductions. It relies in part on a December 24, 1991 Department opinion letter. The supplied source reports that the correction conditions have not all been fulfilled. The United States proposes reimbursement of Guzy and a promise of future compliance on remand, with no dismissal until satisfaction, describing that as apparently the course contemplated below. This is an attributed proposed implementation, not proof of completed reimbursement, a fulfilled promise, or the precise terms of an independently reviewed mandate.

Respondents challenge applying the salary test to paramilitary public-safety departments. They invoke the need for short disciplinary suspensions, congressional differentiation of public law-enforcement work periods, and the major-safety exception; they dispute adequate agency consideration of discipline during the 1992 revision. The officers answer that Congress expressly delegated definition of the exemption, tailored other public-sector requirements without displacing it, and left the policy judgment to the Secretary. The United States recounts a 1954 proposal for broader major-disciplinary deductions that was omitted from the final rule, and the 1991 interim and 1992 final public-accountability changes that altered partial-day-absence treatment without creating a general disciplinary exception. This is the source-supported dispute over validity and agency reasoning; the packet does not decide it.

The officers support the Secretary's authority and validity position but dispute the United States' favorable factual application to an employer the Department had not investigated or subjected to an enforcement proceeding. Their objection is not an unqualified rejection of interpretive deference. It requires separating statutory delegation, the rule's validity, interpretation of regulatory text, and application to established facts. No supplied passage establishes whether the employer filed an APA §553(e) rulemaking petition. Neither filing nor failure to file, exhaustion, forfeiture, or agency refusal may be inferred from that silence.

## 3. Operative text and distinctions that must survive modeling

29 U.S.C. §213(a)(1) delegates definition and delimitation of the bona fide executive, administrative, and professional exemption to the Secretary. The general overtime requirement and the special law-enforcement work period are distinct; petitioners identify §207(k)'s 171 hours in 28 days. This packet does not derive every threshold or damages condition from that statement.

The complete source text of §541.118(a)(1)–(6) and final 1992 §541.5d appears below. Use that text for all operative exceptions, rather than a modern amendment or an isolated phrase:

- The predetermined salary must not be subject to reduction for variations in quality or quantity of work. Subject to the enumerated exceptions, an employee receives the full salary for a week with any work; salary need not be paid for a week with no work.
- Paragraph (a)(1) bars deductions caused by the employer or business operations when the employee is ready, willing, and able to work. Paragraph (a)(2) separately permits deductions for personal absences of a day or more, other than sickness or accident.
- Paragraph (a)(3)'s day-or-more sickness/disability treatment depends on its bona fide compensation-plan or insurance conditions, including its before-qualification and exhausted-leave qualifications. Paragraph (a)(4) bars salary deductions for jury service, witness attendance, and temporary military leave, while allowing its specified fee/pay offsets.
- Paragraph (a)(5) permits good-faith penalties for safety rules of major significance, expressly confined to prevention of serious danger to the plant or other employees. A police disciplinary label does not itself establish that exception. No source-supported determination here classifies every challenged rule as a major-safety rule.
- Paragraph (a)(6) makes the effect of an improper deduction depend on the particular facts. Deductions generally made when work is unavailable indicate no intention to pay on a salary basis, defeating the exemption for the relevant period. Inadvertence **or** reasons other than lack of work are alternative correction grounds; reimbursement **and** a promise of future compliance are cumulative conditions. Eligibility alone is not fulfillment.
- Section 541.5d(a) protects an otherwise qualifying public employee under the specified statute, ordinance, regulation, or public-accountability pay system despite specified partial-day personal/illness/injury absences when accrued leave is not used because permission was not sought or was denied, leave was exhausted, or the employee chose unpaid leave. Section 541.5d(b) separately addresses budget-required furloughs, with an exception for the workweek in which the furlough occurs and pay is reduced. Neither paragraph enacts a general public-sector short-disciplinary-suspension exception.

## 4. Immunity and preservation are threshold legal questions

Respondents assert that the Board is an arm of Missouri because state law creates and regulates it, the governor appoints commissioners, and statutes constrain operations and compensation. The officers counter that the Board is municipally financed and city funds bear salary and claims; they quote the trial decision and invoke Missouri §§84.160, 84.210, and 84.190. They identify city budget certification and appropriations, restrictions on inter-item transfers without municipal approval, four gubernatorial appointees plus the mayor under §84.020, Board selection of officers, no asserted state operational veto, and city approval for certain additional personnel. These are competing statutory and record representations. The clean extraction has not independently established every possible judgment-payment source or the legal consequences of every cited state provision.

The officers also assert waiver. Their reply quotes an initial motion seeking dismissal of individual-capacity claims while conceding official-capacity FLSA jurisdiction, says answers admitted jurisdiction, and invokes an August 1994 consent judgment and conditional reimbursement conduct. Those assertions were verified in the reply; the original initial written motion, complete consent judgment, and counsel's state-law authority to bind the State were not independently reviewed. A general jurisdictional admission and lawful waiver of a sovereign's immunity are not interchangeable without legal analysis.

The actual September 21, 1993 trial-motion passage at JA94–95 was reviewed by the source extractor. It preserved the contention that extending the FLSA to governmental agencies under *Garcia* was unconstitutional while acknowledging the contrary existing Supreme Court rule. It did not expressly assert an arm-of-state or Eleventh Amendment theory. Do not redescribe that constitutional-coverage objection as an express Eleventh Amendment objection. Conversely, the absence of that express objection at trial does not itself establish forfeiture of immunity: *Edelman v. Jordan*, 415 U.S. 651, 677–678 (1974), permits an Eleventh Amendment defense to be considered despite failure to present it below. Actual waiver, the authority to waive, and entity status remain separate questions. *Ford Motor Co. v. Department of Treasury*, 323 U.S. 459, 464–469 (1945), requires attention to authorized consent rather than treating participation alone as conclusive. These decisions supply threshold rules, not findings about this Board. [Edelman primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/415/651); [Ford primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/323/459).

Canonical *Hess* requires examination of the entity's actual legal, financial, and institutional relationship to the State. State-treasury exposure is highly probative but not an indispensable condition of immunity; state creation or appointment is not conclusive either. If the Board is not a state arm, there is no need to decide state sovereign immunity or congressional abrogation for this defendant. If it is a state arm and has not validly waived immunity, identify the exact source of any congressional authorization and the governing constitutional rule before reaching a monetary remedy. An unresolved indispensable predicate may require further proceedings; it may not be filled by an inferred guarantee of state funds or an assumed waiver.

Canonical *Seminole Tribe* did **not** produce a majority rule forbidding Article I abrogation and did **not** overrule *Union Gas*. It produced no majority State-amenability rationale. Its constrained prospective-officer holding concerned IGRA's remedial sequence. That ruling cannot be treated as a holding that all official-capacity defendants must answer an FLSA backpay claim, or that naming commissioners makes retroactive monetary relief prospective. The lack of a broad abrogation holding is also not a new majority holding that every Article I abrogation is valid. *Garcia*'s public-employer coverage question, state-arm classification, authorized waiver, statutory abrogation, constitutional authority, and permissible remedy must remain distinct.

## 5. Date-eligible interpretive law and individual Justice anchors

The linked entering-law slice and exact public entries control the account of simulated decisions. Historical descriptions in party filings cannot displace them.

In *Thomas Jefferson University* (June 24, 1994), Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, and Souter, applies the rule that an agency's interpretation of its own regulation controls unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation. It sustains the particular anti-redistribution interpretation on the text and administrative basis before the Court. A considered change does not automatically destroy deference, but the actual agency ground matters; a court cannot supply missing findings or a substitute agency rationale. This is not permission to defer to a rule contrary to its text or to appellate counsel's invented policy.

Thomas's dissent, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, and Ginsburg, regards the asserted exclusion as lacking the regulatory adoption and textual support necessary to do that work. Their view does not guarantee payment: remaining allowability questions would require agency proceedings. It supplies a concrete text-and-authority objection, not categorical opposition to all agency interpretation. Breyer had not yet joined the Court.

*Stinson* (May 3, 1993), Kennedy's opinion joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas, treats interpretive guideline commentary as authoritative unless unconstitutional, contrary to statute or guideline text, or plainly erroneous. Commentary cannot amend contradictory operative text. A prior judicial interpretation does not automatically prevent a later permissible administrative interpretation; the temporal applicability question remains distinct. Its Sentencing Commission setting and source of authority must be accounted for rather than importing all consequences into a Department of Labor amicus submission.

Predivergence *Martin v. OSHRC*, 499 U.S. 144 (1991), is additional common law available to all sitting Justices. Marshall's unanimous opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter among the current Court, locates interpretive authority in the Secretary under OSHA's allocation of responsibility. Interpretation embodied in the Secretary's administrative enforcement action was agency action, with notice, consistency, and actual exercise of delegated responsibility relevant to reasonableness. The Court distinguished review of agency legal interpretation from the commission's factual role. That statutory allocation does not itself establish the authoritative status of every proposition in this FLSA amicus brief. *Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204, 212–213 (1988), supplies the limit on a wholly unsupported litigation position, including one inconsistent with the agency's prior position. Neither case makes the label “litigation” a substitute for examining the interpretation's regulatory and administrative basis. [Martin primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/499/144), [parallel primary text](https://openjurist.org/499/us/144); [Bowen primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/488/204).

§ 541.118 Salary basis. 


(a) An employee will be considered to be “on a 
salary basis” within the meaning of the regulations if 
under his employment agreement he regularly receives 
each pay period on a weekly, or less frequent basis, a 
predetermined amount constituting all or part of his com- 
pensation, which amount is not subject to reduction be- 
cause of variations in the quality or quantity of the work 


performed. Subject to the exceptions provided below, the

which he performs any work without regard to the number

the general rule that an employee need not be paid for

pensation are made for absences occasioned by the em- 
ployer or by the operating requirements of the business.

to work, deductions may not be made for time when 
work is not available. 


(2) Deductions may be made, however, when the em- 
ployee absents himself from work for a day or more for 
personal reasons, other than sickness or accident. Thus, 
if an employee is absent for a day or longer to handle 
personal affairs, his salaried status will not be affected if 
deductions are made from his salary for such absences. 


(3) Deductions may also be made for absences of a 
day or more occasioned by sickness or disability (includ-

cordance with a bona fide plan, policy or practice of 
providing compensation for loss of salary occasioned by 
both sickness and disability. Thus, if the employer’s par-

such absences, deductions for absences of a day or longer 
because of sickness or disability may be made before an 
employee has qualified under such plan, policy or practice, 
and after he has exhausted his leave allowance thereunder. 
It is not required that the employee be paid any portion 
of his salary for such days or days for which he receives 
compensation for leave under such plan, policy or prac- 
tice. Similarly, if the employer operates under a State 
sickness and disability insurance law, or a private sickness 
and disability insurance plan, deductions may be made 
for absences of a working day or longer if benefits are

provided in accordance with the particular law or plan.
