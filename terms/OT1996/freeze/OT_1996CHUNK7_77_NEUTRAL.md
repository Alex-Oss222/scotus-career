## Event, scope, participation, and chronology

- **Case and docket:** Strate v. A-1 Contractors, No. 95-1872; Eighth Circuit No. 92-3359; A-1 Contractors v. Strate, 76 F.3d 930 (1996) (en banc).
- **Event:** Authorized argued appellate merits consideration, April 28, 1997. Argument was January 7, 1997. Review concerns the Tribal Court of the Three Affiliated Tribes' subject-matter jurisdiction over Gisela Fredericks's personal-injury claim against the non-Indian contractors arising from this Highway 8 collision.
- **Route:** Certiorari to the Eighth Circuit. This is review of a federal action challenging the outer federal-law limit on tribal jurisdiction, not removal of the negligence suit or a federal trial of liability. Section 1331 supplies the established review channel under National Farmers Union. Completion of tribal exhaustion is undisputed in this appellate posture.
- **Judgment below and requested relief:** The en banc court reversed the District Court's judgment sustaining tribal jurisdiction. Petitioners seek reversal; A-1 and Stockert seek affirmance and remand for declaratory and injunctive relief. The former panel opinion was vacated on rehearing and is not a second subsisting judgment. No Court action in this packet enters an injunction, awards damages, or disposes of a tribal claim.
- **Scope limits:** The appeal addresses subject-matter jurisdiction over Fredericks's claim. Personal jurisdiction and her adult sons' unresolved consortium claims are outside this appeal. Their membership and interests may be arguments bearing on tribal welfare; they do not supply an already adjudicated jurisdictional component. The insurer was dismissed without prejudice by stipulation; no judgment against it is under review.
- **Participation:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participate at argument and decision. All nine were seated; no case-specific exclusion is supplied or invented. Quorum and ordinary majority requirements are satisfied. Stone's position is outside this packet.

The identified April 21 public holdings in Blessing and Joseph A. do not decide tribal nonmember jurisdiction. ## Supported record and material distinctions

**Collision and identities.** On November 9, 1990, Fredericks's vehicle and a gravel truck driven by Lyle Stockert for A-1 Contractors collided on State Highway 8 north of Twin Buttes within the Fort Berthold Reservation. Fredericks and Stockert were both non-Indians and nonmembers of the Three Affiliated Tribes. A-1 was a non-Indian business based outside the reservation; Stockert was an employee and part owner. Fredericks was the widow of a member and the mother of five adult tribal-member sons. Her injuries required twenty-four days' hospitalization. Neither kinship nor residence changes her membership. Nonmember and non-Indian are distinct classifications even though both describe these two drivers. JA 20–21, 42–43, 55, 92–94.

**Fault is untried.** A-1's federal complaint alleges Fredericks drove southbound in the northbound lane and Stockert braked and swerved; petitioners explain her account as a left turn toward a driveway. Those are competing allegations. The jurisdiction litigation supplies no negligence, causation, comparative-fault, or damages finding.

**Business relationship versus trip purpose.** A-1 had a $12,490 subcontract with LCM Corporation, wholly owned by the Tribe, for excavation, berming, and recompacting at a reservation community-center project. All contracted work was on the reservation. The existence of this voluntary business relationship is established; it is not the same proposition as Stockert's performing that work at the instant of collision. The tribal complaint alleged work and hauling on November 9. The tribal trial court expressly had no affidavits and treated pleadings/brief facts as undisputed for its dismissal motion. JA 20–24. The tribal appellate court and District Court recited a work connection, but the en banc majority said the record did not clearly show it and identified pleadings rather than proof. JA 55, 92–93 n.1. Judge Gibson's inference that no other plausible explanation existed for the truck's presence is a dissenting inference, not an evidentiary finding. Petitioners' reply acknowledges that a jurisdictional test tied to tribal interests could make the uncertainty material. No load, assignment, delivery, or route is invented. Do not turn lack of proof into an affirmative finding that the trip was unrelated.

**Contract terms.** The en banc court noted a claimed Utah-law/forum clause but expressly gave it no controlling weight because the subcontract was outside the appellate record. JA 111 n.5. Respondents say a copy was submitted in tribal opposition and invoke it against forum consent. Petitioners answer that the clause, even if considered, concerns contract disputes and not this independent tort. The District Court also recounts obligations respecting tribal building, employment-rights, and other applicable laws. JA 61. None of those accounts establishes a general waiver or consent to tribal adjudication of all third-party torts. Fredericks was not a party to the subcontract.

**Residence.** The contractors' federal complaint affirmatively alleges reservation residence, and the tribal defendants' amended answer admits it. JA 42, 48. The tribal trial court assumed residence for its motion; the tribal appellate court described forty years' residence. The District Court later acknowledged a residence dispute, held it immaterial under the competing theories, struck exhibits, and entered no new evidentiary finding resolving it. JA 55–58, 64–66. Respondents call it unresolved; petitioners rely on their opponents' pleading. Neither a never-contested historical residence finding nor established nonresidence follows. A path that makes residence decisive must confront the admission, later dispute, procedural rulings, and possible need for further proceedings.

### The actual grant and the public highway

The United States, through the Fort Berthold Agency Superintendent, executed the May 8, 1970 grant to the North Dakota State Highway Department under the stated delegation, 25 U.S.C. §§323–328, and then-Part 161 regulations. The acknowledged payment was $5,546.50 plus other good consideration. Its purpose is a right-of-way for realignment and improvement of Highway 8 over the described Dunn County lands. The complete signed instrument text, including its acknowledgments, is mechanically copied in the source annex below.

The grant expressly applies to trust lands only and excludes non-trust property. It identifies tracts/allotments and an approximately 6.59-mile route with widths defined in incorporated maps/plats. Its header lists zero tribal acres, 87.48 individually owned acres, and zero government-owned acres. Those entries must not be converted into a finding identifying the exact accident parcel's title, or a premise that all affected land is collectively owned tribal land. The incorporated maps/plats are absent from the reproduced appendix; their absence establishes no grant defect.

Tenure is unlimited **only while the easement is actually used for its specified purpose**, and it remains subject to valid existing rights or adverse claims. The United States grantor may terminate in whole or part on thirty days' written notice if the grantee fails during that period to correct the basis for termination. The four listed bases are breach of a grant term or applicable regulation; two consecutive years of nonuse for the granted purpose; abandonment; and failure to file the required completion affidavit when construction ends. None is found to have occurred. The Indian landowners, lessees, successors, and assigns retain crossings reasonably necessary to undisturbed use and occupancy; the owners or lawful occupants/users construct and maintain them at their own risk and assume responsibility for avoiding or repairing resulting right-of-way damage. Those reserved rights are distinct from the federal grantor's termination power. The conditions bind and benefit the grantee's successors as stated.

Highway 8 was publicly used and state-maintained/controlled, as the briefs and opinions recite; respondents also describe access to Corps-controlled Lake Sakakawea. The instrument's purpose clause does not itself contain an express transfer of exclusive legislative or adjudicatory jurisdiction. Whether lawful public passage displaces an exclusion predicate and warrants a Montana fee-land analogy is a legal consequence to be decided from the grant, authorization, use, and retained interests, not an express conveyance of every governmental power. Conversely, the word easement and continued trust title do not themselves establish a continuing tribal power to exclude public motorists.

Both sides discuss statutory consent and compensation; petitioners do not allege invalid issuance for noncompliance. A separate signed consent document is not reproduced in the recovered core set. The instrument establishes acknowledged consideration; absence of the separate document is not proof that consent was absent. Do not collapse §324's tribal-land and individually owned-land conditions into a single universal rule or infer a statutory violation. No missing map, consent, or exact-tract document is necessary merely to consider the grant's stated public-road consequence; if a chosen rationale turns on a missing parcel-specific fact, obtain that precise source or retain the narrower supported ground.

## Procedure, preservation, and available remedies

Fredericks sued in tribal court in May 1991. The complaint included the sons' consortium claims, insurer claims, and punitive relief. The trial court denied jurisdictional dismissal September 4, 1991; the tribal appellate court affirmed January 8, 1992 and returned remaining matters, including insurer and consortium issues. It rejected the asserted timeliness/interlocutory barrier to the appeal. Stipulation on February 3 and an order on February 4 dismissed the insurer without prejudice. Petitioners say punitive relief was dismissed with it; the dismissal order itself must not be characterized as an express adjudication of every punitive theory against all defendants.

A-1 and Stockert filed the federal action February 5, 1992 for a declaration against tribal jurisdiction and injunctions against continuation and assertion of that jurisdiction, plus costs and fees. The District Court granted opposing summary-judgment motions, denied theirs, and dismissed the federal suit with prejudice September 16, 1992. The en banc judgment of February 16, 1996 reversed, following vacatur of the November 29, 1994 panel decision. The appeal did not adjudicate the sons' consortium jurisdiction or personal jurisdiction. The underlying tort remains untried.

**Limited sovereign-immunity consent.** The amended answer consents to remaining in federal court to defend declaratory relief concerning tribal civil jurisdiction, expressly withholding consent to other causes, claims, issues, relief, or liability now or later. Resolution 92-025-JJR, adopted March 20, 1992, confines waiver to protecting, presenting, and asserting tribal jurisdiction in this federal case and excludes other claims/issues. JA 49–53. The District Court and en banc opinion describe limited defense of the injunctive claims, JA 55, 95. Preserve the actual instruments and that difference in description. Neither establishes a general surrender of immunity or a new adjudicated entitlement to all coercive relief. Any remand relief must remain within the lawful case and its established parties/predicates; do not manufacture an independent broad immunity holding.

**Treaty theory.** Respondents invoke the 1825 treaties' safe-conduct and redress provisions, 1851 road/depredation provisions, and the unratified 1866 agreement (while acknowledging nonratification and later appropriations) as evidence against the claimed authority. Petitioners object that this theory first appeared in the merits brief, was not presented below or exhausted, and mistakes criminal/redress arrangements for withdrawal of concurrent civil adjudication. The objection is preserved as a submission, not an already adjudicated bar. No historical agreement is converted into a ratified treaty or an exclusive-state-jurisdiction clause. A disposition independently resting on a particular treaty must verify its exact official operative text, relevant scope, and the procedural basis for reaching it; this packet makes no such holding necessary.

**Forum and remedy.** A protective North Dakota state action, No. 96-C-41, was filed; petitioners describe a six-year limitations concern. Neither no available state filing nor expiration of all state relief is established. State-forum availability is argued as relevant to tribal necessity, but does not logically disprove concurrent tribal power without the governing jurisdiction rule. Reversal could restore rejection of the federal challenge, or a properly explained vacatur/remand could allow necessary factual or jurisdictional consideration. Affirmance could lead to conforming declaratory/injunctive relief on remand within lawful limits. No route decides fault, a fixed award, claims outside review, automatic state liability, or a general loss of tribal authority on all reservation roads.

## Law entering the case and genuinely open questions

1. **Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), Part III:** The Court recognizes exclusion/conditioning of nonmember hunting and fishing on tribal or trust land and rejects the asserted power over non-Indian fee land absent a sufficient source. Its general limitation on nonmember authority coexists with two exceptions: activities of nonmembers who enter consensual relationships with the Tribe or members through commercial dealings, contracts, leases, or other arrangements; and non-Indian conduct on fee lands threatening or directly affecting tribal political integrity, economic security, or health or welfare. It applies those exceptions to the particular hunting/fishing record. The reach of that framework to a limited public-road easement and civil adjudication is the present question; neither an automatic total territorial rule nor a universal ban is supplied.
2. **National Farmers Union, 471 U.S. 845 (1985):** Stevens's unanimous opinion recognizes §1331 review of the outer federal limit, refuses automatic application of Oliphant's criminal bar to all non-Indian civil cases, and requires first opportunity for tribal evaluation, record development, and appellate correction. It leaves ultimate tribal jurisdiction for examination of law and circumstances. Its exhaustion exceptions concern bad faith/harassment, patent violation of express prohibitions, and futility from no adequate opportunity to challenge jurisdiction. None supplies a new default here, where exhaustion is complete.
3. **Iowa Mutual, 480 U.S. 9 (1987):** Marshall's opinion, joined by O'Connor and Scalia among others, extends prudential tribal exhaustion to diversity cases and requires opportunity for tribal appellate review. It describes presumptive authority over reservation activities while citing Montana and recognizes later federal review of the jurisdiction limit. It does not finally decide the underlying tribunal's power simply by enforcing exhaustion. Stevens agrees federal jurisdiction exists but separately rejects extending the exhaustion rationale to require withholding a distinct diversity merits action. His separate position is not a denial of the National Farmers jurisdictional-review rule.
4. **Williams v. Lee, 358 U.S. 217 (1959):** State adjudication of an on-reservation trader's debt claim against resident tribal members impermissibly infringed tribal self-government. The transaction and Indian defendants matter. The decision provides a concrete adjudicatory and internal-affairs example, not a holding that every non-Indian tort belongs exclusively in tribal court.
5. **Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe, 455 U.S. 130 (1982), Part II:** The severance-tax rationale treats taxing reservation business as a retained governmental power, not solely a revocable permission by a private landowner; a commercial lease's silence does not surrender sovereign taxing authority. Its alternative exclusion rationale preserves later conditions on entry in that setting. It remains a strong answer to an assertion that every unreserved power automatically disappears on granting a use right. Whether the highway grant establishes a different federal public-access limitation and whether tort adjudication has the required tribal relationship remain distinct questions.
6. **Controlling Bourland, June 14, 1993:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, and Kennedy, holds the particular acquisition/public-recreation legislation displaced exclusive occupation and hunting/fishing regulation derived from it, while preserving a separate Montana-exception and properly established federal-authorization inquiry. It does not declare all state power exclusive, all Indian-country boundaries altered, or every easement an acquisition of sovereignty. Blackmun's public dissent, joined by Souter, insists that loss of absolute exclusion does not without more prove loss of distinct compatible regulation and reads the legislation as insufficiently abrogating that authority. That dissent is a current public position, not controlling law.
7. **Chickasaw Nation, June 14, 1995:** Ginsburg's controlling income portion reads the surviving treaty in its geographic context and separates governmental protection from off-country employee-income immunity; Breyer's dissent, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, and Souter, gives the treaty/history/canon and actual member/employer/work nexus greater force. All nine join Ginsburg's fuel-incidence ruling and its refusal to decide late Hayden-Cartwright authorization. These are actual public positions on source, nexus, and preservation; they create no direct highway-jurisdiction rule. Sac and Fox's §1151 residence/source holding includes qualifying rights-of-way in Indian country, but Indian-country classification and this particular nonmember jurisdiction are distinct inquiries.

No historical post-divergence Supreme Court citation in the filings or lower opinions replaces the canonical rule. Brendale's fracture supplies no universal formula by an unexplained combination of rationales. Duro's criminal classification and subsequent legislation cannot be casually imported as the civil rule; no present result depends on an unverified extension of either.
