## Result and exact ground

All eight Associates provisionally support **reversing the denial of appellate habeas relief and remanding for further proceedings** on the erroneous administrative-purpose ground. The reviewed Eleventh Circuit order denied a certificate of probable cause without explanatory merits reasoning; the district court adopted the magistrate's categorical treatment of overcrowding credits as administrative. No reasoned appellate merits opinion is invented.

The Court should hold that the population-management purpose of a statutory credit does not exclude it from the Ex Post Facto Clause when its operation shortens punishment and retrospective withdrawal lengthens custody. A prisoner must still establish the legally available offense-date release framework, the adverse retrospective change, and the resulting punishment increase. If later enactments merely implemented or continued that framework, the State cannot avoid the constitutional inquiry by calling the later credits administrative. Conversely, the Clause does not automatically freeze every new benefit first granted after the offense. The Court should return the material statutory-continuity and protected-credit question to the lower courts under that rule.

This commitment does **not** establish that all 1,860 provisional days are protected, award an unconditional refund of days, reverse the separate 335-day cancellation, or command immediate release. The Court decides the categorical legal ground, not an invented final credit calculation. The lower court must determine which challenged credits implement the protected offense-date opportunity and then address lawful custody and appropriate habeas relief. If the corrected lawful sentence has expired, release follows through the proper habeas disposition; no current expiry is found here.

## Correct factual and statutory predicates

The offense occurred October 27, 1985; sentence was imposed April 14, 1986. Lynce was released October 1, 1992, ordered retaken May 17, 1993, and returned to custody June 8, 1993. The 5,668-day calculation is an aggregate offset, not a count of provisional credits. The sworn affidavit and the briefs identify 1,860 provisional days credited July 1988–January 1991; the isolated 1,360 figure is reported as a typographical error. Separate administrative, basic, additional and jail credits and forfeitures retain their identity.

The offense-date 1983 emergency-gain-time system under §944.598, the 1987 administrative system under §944.276 and the 1988 provisional system under §944.277 are distinct enactments. The earlier emergency trigger was 98 percent; the later provisional threshold described in the packet is 97.5 percent. The State disputes equivalence and says the emergency mechanism was never implemented. A later easier trigger cannot be presumed to have existed in 1985; neither may implementation through a later mechanism automatically erase an earlier legally available release opportunity. The present record does not establish how many of these days would fall within a materially continuous offense-date entitlement. That inquiry remains open rather than being answered by a reconstructed count.

The initial tentative date before provisional credits was November 4, 1997; provisional credits produced October 1, 1992. The subsequent May 19, 1998 calculation also reflects other credit and forfeiture changes, including the separate administrative-credit cancellation. It is not the isolated effect of the 1,860-day cancellation. Characterizing the initial release as mistaken assumes the constitutional applicability of the disputed retrospective exclusion; that label cannot answer the constitutional issue.

## Enhanced alternative paths

**Reject categorical purpose immunity and remand:** Weaver examines the practical increase in punishment and does not make the legislature's initial fiscal motivation dispositive. Collins v. Youngblood retains the punishment boundary. Canonical Morales requires an established offense-date opportunity, actual withdrawal and a concrete connection to legally possible earlier release; it neither requires certainty about a lost discretionary release decision nor protects every procedural preference. Here actual credits produced release and their cancellation produced rearrest, a direct custodial effect materially different from an asserted administrative inconvenience. The strongest counterargument is that these were entirely new post-offense benefits. That remains material, but was not resolved by the district court's administrative-purpose rationale and cannot be replaced by an assumption of statutory identity.

**Affirm based on post-offense benefits:** If all challenged credits arose solely from a new benefit unavailable within the 1985 framework, removing them would not necessarily increase punishment relative to the offense-date law. The State's different triggers and restrictions make this a serious legal alternative. Its strongest obstacle is that a later administrative label or implementation mechanism does not alone establish substantive discontinuity. The competing statutory accounts and unresolved protected portion require adjudication under the correct punishment rule rather than blanket affirmance on a factual/legal premise the reviewed dismissal did not settle.

**Restore all credits immediately:** Actual release and rearrest strongly demonstrate custodial consequences; this path offers direct relief for an obvious loss. Its decisive defect is the unresolved offense-date source and the distinct accounting adjustments. The Clause's baseline cannot be replaced by the mere fact that every post-offense award once existed. No Associate's present commitment supports that unconditional remedy.

No new burden of proving an actually foregone release decision is added. At the same time, Morales's State-substitute substantiation rule is not transplanted into a generalized burden on Florida to disprove statutory continuity. Lynce retains the ultimate constitutional showing. If the State invokes a particular substitute for a demonstrated protected opportunity, the existing rule applies at its own scope.
