### Rules and controlling explanations supported by six

**No last-predicate revival.** A predicate committed within four years of suit does not restart the period for an earlier actionable injury or permit recovery of stale damages merely because it belongs to the same RICO pattern. This holds whether the later act injures someone else or merely continues the pattern; petitioners' proposed last-act rule, which they distinguish from the Third Circuit's last-act-or-injury/discovery formula, fails insofar as it has that revival consequence.

Agency Holding supplies the four-year period and the close Clayton Act analogy. A pattern may continue indefinitely, with predicates years apart; measuring recovery of already actionable harm from the pattern's latest act would undermine repose and permit a knowledgeable claimant to accumulate treble damages. The criminal last-act analogy does not overcome the distinct civil limitation that Agency Holding adopted. Section 1964(c)'s own injury requirement, preserved by Holmes and NOW, keeps the loss legally connected to the act that caused it. None of those predecessors already decided the universal civil-RICO accrual trigger, and today's six do not decide it either.

**Separate accrual and later-provable damages remain distinct.** A genuinely new, directly caused injury from a later wrongful act can support its own timely recovery without reviving damages caused by earlier acts. Separately, where damages from an earlier act were genuinely too speculative or incapable of proof to be recovered earlier, Zenith permits their later accrual when they occur; inability to discover the claim through inadequate diligence, continuing accumulation of calculable loss, and a merely later calculation of an old loss are different.

Zenith and Hanover Shoe explain why a continuing violation may yield new recovery for a new injurious transaction while leaving old damages barred. Common type, defendant, product or pattern does not by itself disqualify a genuinely new injury; nor do subsequent advertisements or a later dated repair by themselves establish that injury. Here the allegations and reviewed adjudication concern continuing harm to the dairy operation from the original failed unit. The late acts have not been identified in the reviewed showing as causing additional harm distinct from that continuing loss. The asserted farm losses are concrete and calculable, rather than the previously unprovable future injury addressed in Zenith. That conclusion rejects these supplied alternatives at their actual scope; it neither establishes a universal same-source bar nor silently decides the contents of the unrecovered lodging. No amount calculation or present finding that the fall-1990 repair was a fraudulent predicate is necessary.

**Concealment requires reasonable diligence.** A civil-RICO claimant invoking fraudulent concealment must show that concealment prevented timely discovery despite reasonable diligence. Affirmative concealment does not dispense with the claimant's diligence obligation.

Bailey and Holmberg supply the equitable framework. The antitrust borrowing context and civil RICO's encouragement of diligent private investigation reinforce that concealment tolling is not solely punishment of a defendant's misconduct. The granted legal challenge fails; the separate fact-specific diligence challenge is not reopened. This is a civil-RICO holding, not a new universal disposition of every equitable-estoppel doctrine. Brockamp's tax-specific ruling supplies no categorical federal anti-tolling rule.

**Remedy and reservations:** Affirm the reviewed judgment. No trial, new discovery, allocation hearing or remand is directed. The state-law dispositions remain at their existing scope. The six reserve injury versus injury-discovery versus injury-and-pattern-discovery accrual. Grimmett's earlier unexplained dismissal supplied no answer. **Nominal-award rule supported by five.** Where a work injury causes no present wage-earning loss under existing conditions but creates a significant potential that future conditions will reduce earning capacity below preinjury earnings, the Act recognizes a presently nominal economic disability and authorizes nominal compensation. A claimant who establishes that qualifying disability and the ordinary statutory predicates is entitled to the appropriate nominal award; permanent physical impairment or an imagined possibility alone is not enough. Availability on this legal standard is distinct from a finding that Rambo has proved entitlement.

Sections 2(10), 8(c)(21) and 8(h) treat disability as economic capacity, not bodily impairment or actual wages alone. Section 8(h) expressly requires attention to natural future effects. Sections 13(a) and 22 limit when a claim or modification can be pursued, making it inadequate to tell a worker with a proved significant injury-related vulnerability simply to wait until economic loss occurs after the reopening window closes. The five read the provisions together to permit a nominal award corresponding to that present vulnerability, with later modification based on actual developments. The deadline is context, not an independent source of benefits. This does not create compensation for every permanent impairment or an indefinitely preserved case without the statutory risk finding.

The principal objection is that §8(h) requires an immediate monetary fixing of complete earning capacity and that a nominal amount evades Congress's finality choice. The five answer that the Act ordinarily permits adjustment as economic circumstances develop, avoiding speculative lump-sum forecasts, and its future-effects direction also must operate where current wages are undiminished. The supported risk is the economic predicate; the nominal amount is not an arbitrary exception for physical injury alone. Potomac Electric preserves the different scheduled-injury branch; this ruling concerns an unscheduled award.

**Proof and existing mandate.** Under Greenwich Collieries, the employer seeking modification must prove the qualifying change; current Rambo I specifically rejects treating increased wages alone as an automatic recovery presumption or persuasion shift. Actual wages determine capacity only when they fairly and reasonably represent it, with the stated future-effects qualifications. The claimant requesting nominal compensation must prove by a preponderance the work injury and significant potential of future capacity loss, subject to genuinely applicable statutory presumptions. Confidence that a significant risk exists is distinct from requiring proof that the future loss itself has probability greater than one-half. No numerical risk threshold or actuarial formula is selected.

The existing crane training, sustained market work, wage and physical-demand findings support the conclusion that present economic loss has ended; that is an evaluation of the demonstrated capacity change, not an automatic inference from a larger paycheck. For the five, the ALJ's existing discussion of current employment and relative job-loss risk did not resolve the distinct significant-future-risk inquiry under the newly adopted standard. The appellate court must return that application to the statutory factfinder. A new evidentiary hearing is not unconditionally ordered; lawful adequate findings on the existing record and ordinary substantial-evidence review remain possible. No amount or final nominal entitlement is fixed. Section 8(i) settlements and §22's actual grounds and deadlines remain intact.

**Three-Justice objection.** O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas read §8(h) to require a present dollar assessment of earning capacity incorporating predictable future loss, proved under the APA standard. A worker may therefore have a compensable present disability despite high wages, but a significant possibility of future loss is not itself the statutorily required reduced capacity. A merely nominal payment to preserve reopening sacrifices the enacted one-year limit. They also read the existing ALJ findings concerning job prospects, training and steady work as an adequate, substantially supported rejection of the claimed future economic loss. Their proposed reversal does not ignore future effects or make current wages conclusive. It remains subject to the actual Rambo I mandate on any genuinely preserved independent issue not resolved in this review.

**Old-rule holding supported by five.** Gardner's opportunity to deny or explain material capital-sentencing information and Skipper's express future-danger rebuttal protection dictated an effective opportunity to give the jury an accurate explanation of legally fixed ordinary parole ineligibility when future dangerousness is the State's reason for death and life without ordinary parole is the only lawful noncapital sentence. The principle does not require a fixed instruction, entitle every defendant to release predictions, or prevent the State from proving danger within prison.

Skipper's due-process ground was accepted beyond the Justices joining its separate Eighth Amendment rationale; it concerns the defendant's opportunity to answer the case for death. A legally fixed confinement alternative supplies information responsive to the State's danger argument just as the good-conduct evidence in Skipper did. The precise function, rather than a general appeal to fairness, makes the application dictated. Ramos permitted truthful information about commutation and preserved state choices within constitutional limits; it did not authorize withholding this accurate response after the State made danger material. Caldwell's prohibition on misleading diminution of the jury's responsibility likewise does not turn truthful rebuttal into forbidden speculation about postsentence proceedings.

The strongest contrary contention distinguishes historical facts about the defendant from information about the governing sentencing law. The five reject that difference for this limited purpose: a legal fact fixing the only alternative to death bears directly on the asserted danger, without requiring a general program of sentencing-law instruction. Simmons supplies the present rule and its actual limits. Graham, Gray and Lambrix govern disciplined functional comparison but are not inserted into the body of cases existing in 1988. Gilmore and Caspari still permit a genuinely new extension after that comparison; no broad principle that all capital protections are old is adopted.

**Three-Justice new-rule ground.** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept that accurate parole information may rebut danger, but regard the required opportunity to describe the postsentencing legal regime as an extension beyond Gardner's undisclosed material and Skipper's excluded historical evidence. ### Supported complete propositions and explanations

**Normal baseline.** Alaska's statutory three-mile submerged-land grant is measured from the Convention's normal baseline, including the ordinary low-water line and its applicable river/bay closing rules and separate island belts. The supplied historical materials establish no firm and continuing general ten-mile island-fringe rule requiring enclosure of Stefansson Sound; the 1985 Alabama/Mississippi judgment does not preclude that conclusion.

California's adoption of Convention definitions supplies the baseline framework. Article 4 permits straight baselines in an appropriate island-fringe setting; it does not command the United States to choose them, and the national choice was not made here. The international twelve-mile sea does not enlarge the statutory three-mile State grant. Alaska's separate general-practice theory is considered on its own terms, not rejected for failure to prove a specific historic-title claim it does not make. The 1930 four-mile assimilation proposal, six-mile high-seas strait rule and ten-mile inland-sea/bay rule were materially different propositions. The Chapman Line, Webb letter, Gulf submissions and 1961 letter do not establish the proposed uniform rule. Oil-leasing convenience and avoidance of federal enclaves cannot supply it.

The 1985 Alabama/Mississippi decision resolved Mississippi Sound's particular historic inland-water status on specific assertions and foreign acquiescence; it did not necessarily decide the universal formulation Alaska proposes. Issue preclusion therefore fails even assuming it could apply here. Mendoza's general rule against nonmutual estoppel against the United States need not be expanded or given a new original-jurisdiction exception. The later Alabama/Mississippi and Massachusetts decrees retain their actual geographic force, and the Nome artificial-extension permit holding remains distinct. No general authority to contract existing state territory is decided.

**Dinkum Sands.** A naturally formed feature must ordinarily remain above the agreed mean-high-water datum to qualify as an island under Article 10(1). Dinkum Sands' recurring seasonal loss of elevation below that datum defeats island status and a separate three-mile grant here; isolated emergence does not create periodically appearing and disappearing island entitlement.

The Convention's drafting history distinguishes exceptional inundation of an otherwise enduring island from an ordinarily changing gravel-and-ice formation. The approximately nineteen-year mean-high-water datum already accounts for ordinary tidal variation. The joint monitoring study and subsequent measurements support the Master's finding of frequent below-datum conditions; the 1971 chart and 1979 map depended on the earlier survey, and visual emergence does not displace calibrated elevation evidence. Borax and the California decree support the datum, not an absolute physical-permanence condition. Ordinary accretion or erosion of an established island remains distinct. Article 11 supplies no alternate entitlement at these distances.

**Petroleum Reserve — six-Justice rule.** Congress may retain submerged lands in federal ownership for an appropriate public purpose if the governing instruments clearly include them and clearly establish intent to defeat future state title. The same demanding retention inquiry governs the disputed equal-footing and statutory territorial-sea lands; §1313(a)'s express-retention requirement cannot be replaced by a presumption favoring the Federal Government simply because the Submerged Lands Act is a federal grant.

Shively supplies Congress's Property Clause power for appropriate territorial public purposes; Utah Division and Montana require separate clear inclusion and state-title defeat, not geography alone. The 1923 order's ocean-side barrier description, small-lagoon closure and express three-mile qualification include the disputed submerged estate. Its purpose of securing petroleum resources for national defense would be defeated by excluding the submerged portions of the same deposits. Unlike Montana's unneeded riverbed or Utah Division's reservoir-control rationale, federal ownership of this estate performs the reservation's central work.

Section 11(b) preserves exclusive federal legislative authority over federally owned military lands at admission and specifically names the petroleum reserve. Read with the already defined estate, that congressional treatment confirms continued federal ownership and ratifies inclusion of its submerged lands. The provision is not rewritten as an express generic submerged-title clause, and federal regulatory jurisdiction alone would not establish ownership. The precise named-reserve recognition and petroleum ownership purpose supply the title conclusion. Because Congress could retain that estate at admission and confirmed it, the result does not require deciding the disputed independent reach of the Pickett Act. No broad Executive power to defeat future state title is announced.

**Petroleum objection of Scalia and Thomas.** The Submerged Lands Act is the operative grant for this post-1953 State, and its exception demands an express retention of submerged lands, in terms embracing the land under water. In their view, necessary inclusion inferred from an exterior boundary and resource purpose cannot replace that requirement; congressional ratification cannot cure an instrument lacking it. Section 11(b)'s legislative-jurisdiction and existing-ownership language does not expressly retain the submerged estate. They would sustain Alaska's exception. They do not extend this statutory analysis to States admitted before the Act or announce that Congress lacks constitutional power to retain submerged lands with adequate language.

**Arctic Range — six-Justice rule.** The 1957 application and the operative temporary-segregation regulation set the specifically described lands apart for the proposed wildlife refuge before Alaska entered the Union; §6(e)'s exclusion for lands withdrawn or otherwise set apart as wildlife refuges therefore retained those submerged lands at statehood. The application was not a completed final reservation, but the statute's broader alternative reaches this legally effective, purpose-specific segregation.

The low-water boundary, submerged coastal features and habitat justification establish inclusion separately from retention. Federal protection of the identified aquatic habitat supports that inclusion under Utah Division/Montana's demanding test. The regulation segregates the lands from the enumerated forms of disposal only to the extent the proposed reservation, if effected, would bar them; it also preserves administrative jurisdiction. Those qualifications are retained. They do not remove the presently effective segregation pending the Secretary's decision. Section 6(e), not the application's mere existence or the 1960 order retroactively, supplies congressional retention. Requiring a completed reservation would underread the broader phrase 'otherwise set apart'; the six reject the contention that 'as' makes final establishment indispensable in this setting.

Section 6(m)'s incorporation of the Submerged Lands Act remains distinct from §6(e)'s wildlife exception. The latter supplies clear title-defeating intent for the specific lands already set apart; neither general federal ownership nor wildlife purpose alone would suffice. The 1980 expansion does not determine the original statehood boundary. Alaska's defensive inclusion argument is addressed because it overlaps with the legal effect of the application, notwithstanding its lack of a separate exception to the Master's geographic discussion.

**Range objection of Scalia and Thomas.** An application preserving a prospective decision from inconsistent disposal is not land already set apart as a refuge. The applicant expressly left all or part of the acreage for later determination, administrative jurisdiction remained unchanged, and the decision on actual refuge creation was substantive rather than ministerial. 'Otherwise set apart' can cover effective refuge creation by means other than formal withdrawal without encompassing every pending application. The two would overrule the United States' exception. They do not join the six's review and resolution of the unexcepted geographic Question 10; they express no contrary geographic finding and regard the timing/legal-effect issue as sufficient. Their refusal must not be counted as assent to clear submerged inclusion.

**Disposition and stage:** For the six, overrule Alaska's three exceptions, sustain the United States' Range exception, adopt the Master's recommendations only as consistent with these rulings, direct submission of a proposed implementing decree, retain jurisdiction to effectuate and supplement it, and have each party bear its own costs. The two join that procedural mechanism only with their different reservation-title dispositions. No appellate affirmance, final metes-and-bounds decree, invented survey, oil-accounting award or damages determination is entered.
