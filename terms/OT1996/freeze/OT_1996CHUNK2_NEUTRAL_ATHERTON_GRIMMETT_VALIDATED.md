## 21. Atherton v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation — corrected packet

### Event, posture, questions, and record

The scheduled merits review concerns No. 95-928 and the Third Circuit's interlocutory decision under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b), Resolution Trust Corp. v. CityFed Financial Corp., 57 F.3d 1231 (1995). City Federal was federally chartered. Its assets and claims passed through successor institutions to City Savings, F.S.B.; RTC sued in its receiver capacity, and FDIC succeeded RTC when RTC terminated on December 31, 1995. The Third Circuit opinion also dealt with the distinct, state-chartered United Savings litigation. That consolidation matters to understanding the lower opinion but does not add a Supreme Court docket or a companion disposition to this packet.

The receiver alleged losses exceeding $100 million arising from consideration, approval, and oversight of acquisition, development, and construction loans in the mid-to-late 1980s. City Federal was declared insolvent December 7, 1989. These are allegations and procedural facts, not findings that any defendant breached a duty or caused a recoverable loss. The supplied lower-opinion recitation identifies no allegation of self-dealing, conflict of interest, bad faith, or fraud in the City Federal case; that recitation does not establish the absence of material from an unread original pleading.

The original and first amended complaints invoked both state and federal negligence, gross-negligence, and fiduciary-duty theories. The District Court dismissed claims beyond §1821(k), treated the state-law claims as withdrawn, and allowed an amended statutory gross-negligence pleading. The receiver appealed the dismissal of federal-common-law theories. Petitioner asserts that the withdrawal ruling was not appealed. Thus a rejection of federal common law does not itself restore the withdrawn state claims, decide whether they may now be pursued, or adjudicate New Jersey law or liability. Preservation and the permissible scope of any remand require their own treatment.

The original packet's statement that the Third Circuit applied an ordinary-negligence federal standard is incomplete. It restored the availability of federal-common-law negligence and fiduciary-duty theories but reserved the content of the federal standard, including ordinary versus gross negligence (appendix A32 n.16). It also held §1821(k) inapplicable to federally chartered institutions (appendix A37 n.17). Both sides challenge that exclusion. Their agreement that the text covers both federal and state charters does not resolve the survival or content of federal common law, the savings sentence, or the availability of a particular state claim. Judge Mansmann accepted preservation of stricter state-law claims for state institutions but would treat the statute as the exclusive gross-negligence standard in receiver litigation over a federally chartered institution.

The petition primarily asks whether §1821(k) supplants federal common law and establishes an exclusive receiver standard, and whether the Third Circuit could exclude federal institutions from the section. Petitioner's merits argument additionally denies that an independent federal-common-law duty rule existed and urges state law. FDIC disputes whether that antecedent question was presented in the petition while also briefing it. The reply contests the scope objection. The broader question labeled "Question Granted" in the copied block cannot resolve this live dispute. The independent model must distinguish a new supporting legal argument, a fairly included subsidiary question, and a related independent question; this packet does not choose between them. No independent dispute over receiver standing or federal subject-matter jurisdiction has been identified, but that fact does not erase question scope or the state-claim withdrawal.

### Exact statutory provision

The following is copied mechanically from the official 1994 United States Code source, paragraph (k) in full. The text is authorization for liability, not a finding that its factual predicates are satisfied:

> (k) Liability of directors and officers
> A director or officer of an insured depository institution may be held personally liable for monetary damages in any civil action by, on behalf of, or at the request or direction of the Corporation, which action is prosecuted wholly or partially for the benefit of the Corporation—
> (1) acting as conservator or receiver of such institution,
> (2) acting based upon a suit, claim, or cause of action purchased from, assigned by, or otherwise conveyed by such receiver or conservator, or
> (3) acting based upon a suit, claim, or cause of action purchased from, assigned by, or otherwise conveyed in whole or in part by an insured depository institution or its affiliate in connection with assistance provided under section 1823 of this title,
> for gross negligence, including any similar conduct or conduct that demonstrates a greater disregard of a duty of care (than gross negligence) including intentional tortious conduct, as such terms are defined and determined under applicable State law. Nothing in this paragraph shall impair or affect any right of the Corporation under other applicable law.

The qualifying action, Corporation-benefit requirement, three capacity/acquisition routes, state-law definition of the culpability terms, and complete savings sentence remain distinct. "May be held" does not compel a liability judgment. The meaning and effect of "other applicable law," including whether it preserves a more demanding state-law duty, must be construed; neither the original packet's "federal minimum" formulation nor a claim of exclusive federal gross-negligence liability is treated here as a holding already established by the register.

### Law entering the event and the material alternatives

**O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC (June 13, 1994):** The actual acquired-California-claim holding rejects a federal anti-imputation rule on an independently sufficient pre-FIRREA ground because fund protection, greater recovery, and general uniformity do not show a particular significant conflict with identified federal policy. Its separate FIRREA construction is conditional: if that Act applies, succession under §1821(d)(2)(A)(i) carries the acquired rights' legal incidents, subject to Congress's enacted exceptions, and supplies no additional anti-imputation rule. It does not decide FIRREA's temporal application, the present directors' duty, §1821(k)'s floor-versus-exclusive-standard question, or whether federal chartering can ever support valid federal displacement. It also does not direct automatic restoration of a withdrawn or dismissed claim.

**Operative register provision:** "State-law incidents of claims acquired by the FDIC," present operation effective O'Melveny, June 13, 1994, states that state law ordinarily supplies imputation and defenses to acquired state tort claims; ordinary federal financial or uniformity interests alone do not justify a new rule. Its pre-FIRREA and conditional FIRREA components and temporal reservation must travel together. No distinct current-wording authority is supplied in the selected register entry.

**Supporting law and scope:** Erie excludes general federal common law; Wallis and Boyle require the identified significant conflict necessary for exceptional displacement; Kimbell Foods distinguishes federal program rights and rejects automatic national uniformity even in that setting. Those propositions enter through O'Melveny's controlling treatment, not through a historical Atherton opinion. Yee's argument/claim distinction and independent question-presented limit, Izumi's strong prudential presumption with exceptional circumstances retained, Williams's bounded review of a question actually passed upon, and Lebron's discretionary consideration of an included legal argument inform the scope dispute. Williams does not excuse affirmative waiver; Lebron does not erase distinct claims, requested relief, or missing facts. Mere merits briefing does not automatically enlarge a question.

The supported alternatives for later legal analysis include: (1) applying the state-law starting point because a sufficient conflict is absent, while giving §1821(k)'s express text and savings clause their own operation; (2) addressing statutory displacement of federal common law and the asserted exclusive gross-negligence standard on the questions actually presented, with the savings-clause objection squarely answered; and (3) assessing the claimed basis for surviving federal common law despite the substantial constraints imposed by O'Melveny, without presuming that charter status, insurance, receiver status, or a wish for greater recoveries alone supplies the necessary conflict. Each is an analytical path to test, not a conclusion that its legal predicates are proved. A scope-limited resolution or a discretionary refusal to reach the contested antecedent question also remains available under the applicable review rules. The lower court's reserved content question prevents treating its decision as a final adjudication of ordinary-negligence liability.

For any selected merits path, separate the federal-charter coverage of §1821(k), the source and content of the duty, the savings sentence, the procedural availability of the state claims, and the resulting remand. The withdrawal and amendment history can require a bounded remand even if a state-law rule is selected. No state claim, state standard, liability finding, damages amount, or defense is established simply by reversing one federal-common-law premise. If a proposed result depends on applying FIRREA to earlier conduct, Landgraf's provision-specific temporal-direction and new-consequences inquiry governs; O'Melveny's conditional discussion is not an across-the-board retroactivity answer. A narrower resolution that does not require deciding temporal reach must say why.

**Corrected filing date:** The RICO complaint was filed **November 24, 1993**, in civil action CV-S-93-1155-PMP (RJJ). May 1989 to November 24, 1993 still exceeds four years; that arithmetic alone does not identify the governing accrual trigger or defeat every asserted injury or tolling theory.

Joanne Siragusa and the bankruptcy trustee alleged fraudulent reorganization of the debtor's medical practice and related deception. The May 1989 bankruptcy complaint is evidence of what injury and facts were then alleged or known, not an automatic determination of every RICO element or every claimant's injury. Distinguish alleged lien impairment, asserted inability to collect a debt, the estate's injury, and uncertainty about the amount ultimately recoverable. Their legal and evidentiary consequences must be evaluated under the selected accrual rule.

The petition asks (1) about discovery of injury, causation, and a pattern; and (2) whether accrual occurs before damages are clear where an ongoing bankruptcy or other proceeding may make the claimant whole. The merits brief reverses their order and emphasizes its contention that no concrete debt loss arose before final discharge. Those related but different formulations require a scope assessment; the model may not replace both questions with a bare universal choice between injury discovery and pattern discovery.

The Ninth Circuit treated the limitations dismissal as summary judgment because it considered material outside the pleadings. It applied discovery of injury while recognizing that a complete RICO claim, including a pattern, must exist before time can run. The existence of a completed pattern and discovery of that pattern are separate questions. It rejected, on distinct grounds: (a) the asserted later independent injury; (b) fraudulent concealment for deficient pleading and, alternatively, insufficient supported active concealment and diligence; (c) automatic tolling during bankruptcy because the nondebtor lawyer could be sued separately; and (d) deferred accrual merely because later bankruptcy recovery could mitigate a known injury. Do not collapse accrual, later injury, concealment, and bankruptcy into one trigger or one burden. Footnote 4 reports that plaintiffs did not ask the Ninth Circuit to adopt the last-predicate-act rule; the later model cannot present that rule as an independently preserved request without further source support.

### Settlement, remaining relief, and vehicle

Beckley contends that the settlement with the debtor and revocation of discharge defeat the asserted injury and make the case moot. That contention depends in part on reading petitioners as having abandoned lien impairment and as requiring final discharge for all RICO injury. It also asserts full compensation from settlement proceeds and prior receipts. Petitioners contest both the asserted abandonment and full compensation. Their reply identifies $160,000 toward alimony arrearages and $40,000 to the estate, argues that estate expenses, other creditors, allocation among causes of action, and trebling affect remaining recovery, and says the release preserved claims against these lawyers and the estate. Their alternative contention that the 1994 personal release first crystallized loss also remains an argument, not an established accrual or causation finding.

The agreement's documentary limits are material. Paragraph 3 disclaims admissions of wrongdoing, lack of merit, and the adequacy of consideration fully to compensate plaintiffs, and reserves claims against nonparties. Paragraph 4A excludes Patricia L. Brown, her firm, Beckley, and identified related persons from the releases. Paragraph 4B preserves Joanne's claims against the bankruptcy estate, including her proof of claim. The subsequent stipulation calls for revocation of discharge and dismissal with prejudice of the other claims against the debtor defendants, while retaining the nonadmission terms. These terms do not establish full satisfaction or release of the present respondents. Conversely, a nonadmission clause does not itself prove an uncompensated injury. The date and entry of an implementing order remain unverified here; a disposition that makes actual revocation or its timing decisive needs that specific source.

Mootness requires an independent inquiry into whether any effectual relief remains on the claims before the Court. A settlement document, uncertain damages, difficulty selecting an accrual rule, or a deficient vehicle does not alone establish the absence of a live controversy. If the dispute remains live, the Court may separately consider whether the questions and record justify exercising its discretionary review. Dismissal as improvidently granted is a discretionary vehicle disposition, not an Article III mootness judgment; it supplies no merits holding on civil RICO accrual. No poll or vote is proposed here.

### Entering law, distinct triggers, and supported alternatives

Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff, 483 U.S. 143 (1987), supplies the four-year period while leaving accrual undecided. Sedima and H.J. Inc. concern RICO's elements and pattern; requiring an actual complete claim is analytically different from requiring discovery of the pattern. Zenith concerns distinct new injury and speculative future damages, not automatic revival of an old injury whenever another act occurs. Kubrick distinguishes factual knowledge of injury and cause from knowledge of the legal theory. These pre-divergence authorities are identified in the source extraction; their exact scope must be sourced further if a proposed holding or Justice-specific fork turns on a passage not supplied in the packet. The lower opinion's quotation of then-Judge Kennedy's State Farm v. Ammann concurrence supports identifying separate accrual as a relevant alternative; obtain the direct concurrence before using its exact reasoning to support an individual modeled commitment. Later RICO opinions, including the later scheduled Klehr matter, supply neither law nor a disposition for this event.

The authorized entering-law slice's Holmes holding (March 24, 1992) requires factual and proximate causation, including an appropriate direct relation between the claimed injury and the §1962 violation. It is not a universal third-party-injury bar or a new accrual rule. Reves (March 3, 1993) concerns operation or management under §1962(c); it does not decide injury, causation, accrual, or liability under the other distinct §1962 provisions. Neither should be used to introduce an unpreserved new merits ground merely because the defendants supplied professional services.

Church of Scientology (November 16, 1992) establishes that meaningful partial relief can keep a controversy live. Calderon v. Moore (June 17, 1996) similarly requires attention to actual remaining effectual relief rather than a label attached to later events. Current-term Murray (December 2, 1996) holds that an enduring legal disagreement cannot preserve an obsolete claim when no redressable residue or supported recurrence remains; its actual removal of the requested placement barrier does not decide whether this settlement leaves uncompensated RICO claims. The [Current-Term Neutral Projection](../workspace/neutral-projection.md) therefore supersedes the copied packet's stale statement that no earlier completed OT1996 decision exists.

If a live controversy remains, supported paths include resolving a genuinely preserved accrual question and applying it to this summary-judgment record; remanding for application if a material legal error leaves factual or alternative-ground work; deciding a sufficiently preserved narrow issue that actually resolves the judgment while addressing surviving alternative grounds; or dismissing the writ as improvidently granted if the question/record mismatch prevents appropriate review. Pattern discovery, a distinct later injury, and suspension of a clock already running are not interchangeable rules. Selecting one legal rule does not itself supply a finding that the other grounds fail. A new injury may support its own timely recovery without reviving stale injury; the claimed later act must be linked to an actual distinct injury, not merely the continuing effect of the earlier loss. Concealment must receive its separate pleading, affirmative-conduct, and diligence treatment, and bankruptcy's asserted tolling effect must be distinguished from the contention that no concrete injury yet existed.

If the controversy is actually moot, merits review is unavailable, but the disposition of the judgments remains a separate equitable question under §2106. U.S. Bancorp (November 8, 1994) ordinarily denies vacatur to a party whose voluntary settlement caused the loss of review unless a further sufficient equitable reason is established; a settlement term requesting vacatur is not itself exceptional. Munsingwear's happenstance rationale and Murray's ordinary educational-reassessment setting do not make vacatur automatic after settlement. Identify whose voluntary act caused the mootness, the judgment properly before the Court, and the particular equitable reason before selecting vacatur or leaving the judgment undisturbed. A mootness determination cannot silently be recast as a DIG, and a DIG cannot supply a merits affirmance or erase the lower judgment.
