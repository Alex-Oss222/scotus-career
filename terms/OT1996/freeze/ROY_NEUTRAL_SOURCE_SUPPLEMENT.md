# California v. Roy — neutral primary-source supplement

**Scope and cutoff:** Matter 2 only, No. 95-2025; November 4, 1996. This is a proposed supplement for fresh neutral validation, not an adjudication or a replacement commitment. It contains no current-matter Supreme Court comparator, proposed Justice votes, or Stone material. The existing validated neutral packet and entering law remain applicable except where the source account below adds precision. No historical filing's description of *Brecht* or *O'Neal* displaces the simulation's governing law.

## 1. What the lower courts actually decided

The full en banc opinion, *Roy v. Gomez*, 81 F.3d 863, 868–869 (9th Cir. 1996), does more than say that its necessary-findings inquiry creates grave doubt. It expressly concludes that the error would fail harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt on direct review and that reversal would follow there. It then asks the separate historical collateral-review question and grants relief under its reading of *Brecht/O'Neal*. Thus there is an express adverse *Chapman* assessment in the lower opinion. Whether that assessment employed the legally correct method remains a separate question; it must not be described as an inquiry the lower court simply never performed.

The en banc majority treats the omission as trial error, not universally structural error. It reasons that knowledge of McHargue's intent, together with conduct helping him, did not necessarily establish Roy's own purpose to facilitate the robbery: help intended for a companion is its proposed counterexample. The dissent agrees about trial-error classification but disputes both the necessity restriction and the majority's assessment of the record. The opinion contains seven footnotes; the complete majority, dissent and footnotes have been read. The State's certiorari petition itself reproduces and disputes the majority's reasoning. Source: [full en banc opinion](https://openjurist.org/81/f3d/863); State Appendix A, pp14–17, in the local source below.

The State's Appendix J supplies the complete California Court of Appeal opinion of January 27, 1989, including its unpublished discussion of the underlying *Beeman* error. Its majority, pp37–47, found that error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt and affirmed the underlying Mannix convictions. It reasoned that the circumstances supporting knowing assistance left no evidentiary basis for assistance without the relevant purpose. It separated the account denying assistance, the prosecution's prior-plan account, and the remaining circumstances of the encounter. At p45 it says the jury disbelieved Hudspeth. That statement is an appellate inference from the verdict, not an independently returned jury finding identifying every part of testimony the jury rejected. The state court's legal harmlessness conclusion is also not a historical fact binding this Court on the constitutional question.

The same state opinion reversed the special-circumstance findings for instructional error concerning the intent required for those findings. Its discussion, pp18–36, addresses the original oral instruction, the written revision deleting the willful/deliberate/premeditated qualification, the felony-murder alternative, and the possible meanings of aiding the first-degree murder. The 17-page Evans dissent would also uphold the special circumstances. The distinction between purpose to facilitate robbery and intent to kill must be preserved when assessing either side's argument. Reversal of the special-circumstance findings does not itself decide whether some aspect of the jury's findings has legitimate relevance to the distinct underlying-intent inquiry; neither does it make the reversed findings operative punishments again.

Its quotations and analysis of the written modification to CALJIC 8.80 are part of the recovered record. The separate Clark conviction remains outside the present federal petition. These reports do not establish an extra express jury finding of Roy's purpose beyond what the actual verdicts and instructions legally support.

The State argues that the special-circumstance language and other instructions supply actual findings that cure the omission, and separately relies on the trial evidence. The Opposition contests both uses. Its pp2–4 challenge the State's description of Hudspeth, Hall, the physical evidence and Roy's statements. Those particular factual criticisms remain respondent's assertions unless independently supported by the reproduced lower findings or trial material; they are not adopted wholesale as findings here. The lower judicial opinions already supply substantial accounts of the evidence, so the evidence cannot fairly be characterized as wholly unavailable.

## 3. Two preserved Opposition objections that require attention

**Special-circumstance argument.** Opposition pp14–15 says the State did not present its proposed special-circumstance cure argument in any lower court, and that counsel acknowledged this at the en banc argument. It invokes Rule 15.2 and also contests the argument on its merits because the findings were instructionally defective and reversed. The argument and claimed concession are established as assertions in the filed Opposition; an en banc argument transcript has not been independently recovered and read here. Do not treat the concession as verified or turn the assertion automatically into a litigant default. Distinguish a new question, a subsidiary argument within the preserved harmlessness issue, and the possible limits on resolving that argument in the first instance.

**Petitioner identity.** Opposition pp1–2 and 5 objects under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) and the Court's Rules that the petition names the People of California and Attorney General Daniel Lungren while the amended habeas petition names James Gomez and Robert Borg, later replaced by William Merkle. Its attached amended-petition caption, verified-answer caption and party identification, and substitution stipulation support that account of the district-court papers. However, the en banc opinion's own caption also names John Van de Kamp as a respondent, alongside Gomez and Merkle. Those sources must be reconciled; this supplement makes no finding that the named Supreme Court petitioners lacked party status or that a merely formal caption problem is fatal. A public-officer succession or misnomer analysis requires its actual predicate rather than assumption.

The official 1995 Rules supply the relevant procedural framework: Rule 12.4 addresses who may petition and prevents a party not joined in a petition at filing from joining it later; Rule 12.6 addresses parties below and their status in this Court; Rule 14.1(a) confines consideration to presented or fairly included questions, and 14.1(b) requires the party list; Rule 15.2 requires an opposition to identify relevant misstatements and permits waiver of certain unraised nonjurisdictional objections; Rule 35.3 provides automatic substitution for a public officer in the stated circumstances and disregards misnomers not affecting substantial rights. Rule 35 does not establish that an officer was a party in the first place. Source: [official 1995 Rules](https://www.supremecourt.gov/ctrules/rules/Rules_1995.pdf), corresponding text at `sources/Rules_1995.txt` relative to this freeze directory. The pertinent Rule 12 provisions, Rule 14.1(a)–(b), Rule 15.2 and all of Rule 35 were checked.
