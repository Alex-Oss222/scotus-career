## Components and legal premises

1. **Standing and review scope.** The hearing-based account concerns the plaintiffs' children in the affected public-school system and impending implementation at school events; the superintendent and principal supplied testimony before the opening of school. It supports threatened personal exposure rather than an abstract objection to a statute. Neither completed prayer nor punishment is required before prospective relief. Lujan's actual-or-imminent, personal-injury, traceability and redressability requirements remain. No taxpayer-standing expansion, statewide injury to every pupil, or finding of completed coercion is made. Review under the established appellate grant includes the cross-appeal about graduation and the separate intervention denials.
2. **Section 1(2) and official religious activity.** The enacted provision says qualifying invocations, benedictions or student-initiated voluntary prayer shall be permitted at compulsory and noncompulsory school-related assemblies, sporting events, graduations and other events. It is not merely a general forum-opening enactment. Its words do not themselves establish that an official has composed, led or screened an actual prayer. On the preliminary record, the event mandate and anticipated school administration support a substantial likelihood of unconstitutional official religious activity and, in the compulsory setting, religious pressure. Relief may prevent officials from organizing, adopting or directing worship as part of their own program, including choosing a devotional slot or a speaker to deliver prayer, and from applying the nonsectarian/nonproselytizing limits through official religious-content screening. Calling that activity student-initiated does not conclusively make it private.
3. **Distinct safeguards and private expression.** Section 1(3) preserves constitutionally protected expression at other times and events. Section 1(4)'s disclaimer does not decide attribution contrary to actual conduct. Section 1(5) directs severability of Section 1's provisions. Section 2 permits voluntary participation and denies teachers or school authorities authority to prescribe prayer's form or content. That denial is real and cuts against reading the Act as commanding official authorship; it does not erase Section 1(2)'s distinct eligibility filters or prove that an official event devotional exercise is private. Genuinely private prayer and actual neutral private-expression forums remain governed by Lamb's Chapel, Chabad and Good News/Good Sports. Neither the statute nor relief requires a school to establish a new forum. An injunction must not become a prohibition of private religious viewpoints.
4. **Graduation.** The lower exception is limited to Jones II's student-choice, student-delivery and nonsectarian/nonproselytizing conditions. Those conditions do not categorically establish a private forum or eliminate official adoption and pressure. A graduation may therefore receive no automatic exemption from the same constitutional inquiry. Conversely, no conclusion follows that every religious reference by a privately selected student speaker is governmental prayer. Lee's two controlling grounds remain distinct.
5. **Interim relief and severability.** The Court reviews a preliminary injunction, not a final declaration. Likely infringement of the plaintiffs' religious freedom supports irreparable injury; a tailored restraint preserves that freedom without suppressing independent private prayer. The State has no legitimate interest in official unconstitutional worship but retains an interest in lawful accommodations and expression. The blanket restraint on the Act apart from graduation must be narrowed by actual unconstitutional operation and by its separately functioning provisions; graduation's categorical safe harbor must be removed. Do not excise religious-content qualifiers and thereby invent a broader statutory access entitlement. Remand should permit application-sensitive and provision-sensitive relief under state severability law, preserving lawful independent provisions and applications, without rewriting Mississippi's program or issuing a final universal prayer code.
6. **Rule 24.** The final orders denied intervention as of right and permissively; initial abeyance is not the disposition on review. Rule 24(a)(2) requires timely application, a relevant interest and practical impairment unless existing parties adequately represent that interest. Trbovich makes the showing that representation may be inadequate minimal and rejects categorical sufficiency based on a public representative's official duty. Here the published account says the Attorney General actually pressed the students' speech and exercise interests, and applicants identified no omitted right in the statutory-validity litigation. That is affirmative record support for adequacy at this stage, not an irrebuttable presumption. Rule 24(b) makes commonality a basis for permission, not an entitlement; the court must consider undue delay or prejudice. The reported duplication and delay finding, together with allowed amicus presentation and the bounded scope of the action, supports the discretionary denial. Amicus status would not substitute for intervention if Rule 24(a)'s conditions were satisfied.

## Enhanced comparison of the material forks

### Standing

**Reach path:** Current students and parents facing imminent administration of the event statute have personal exposure supported by the hearing, and restraint of defendants' administration redresses it. **Competing path:** Vacate for insufficient imminence because no actual prayer has yet occurred and the statute permits lawful private speech. The latter correctly rejects abstract statutory disagreement, but improperly demands completed infringement if made a categorical ground. Lujan permits a supported imminent injury. None of the eight makes a merits finding of inevitable illegality a jurisdictional prerequisite. Scalia and Thomas preserve their narrower assessment of unconstitutional implementation on the merits; accepting actual threatened application does not decide the application's legality. The other six find Lee's prospective-exposure principle and the school-record context sufficient. No separate taxpayer holding is needed.

### Official activity and private speech

**Restraint path:** Enforce Lee against officials' use of the mandatory event permission to organize worship, choose devotional presentations, or screen religious content, while preserving real private expression. **Strongest objection:** Section 2 expressly denies content-prescription power; neutral accommodation, voluntary participation and student initiation may result in entirely private prayer. Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer answer that the injunction must attach to official operation and religious supervision, not to the existence of religious speech, and that a ban on prescribing content is not a ban on organizing the devotional exercise. Their own Lee/Chabad/Good News positions support that distinction. Kennedy accepts restraint where the State directs the exercise in a compulsory or comparably pressured setting; he does not join sponsorship without pressure as the sufficient ground.

**Vacatur path:** Remove the prospective restraint because the text can operate constitutionally and unlawful implementation is not yet established. **Strongest objection:** The statute commands event permission, religious eligibility must be administered, and the witnesses support imminent official involvement; waiting for punishment would deprive the children of meaningful protection. Scalia and Thomas do not require actual punishment to sue. Their dissent instead rejects treating this enactment as already selecting governmental worship, stresses the no-prescription command and voluntary/private possibilities, and would require an adequately demonstrated unconstitutional application before the present restraint. Their prior Lee and private-forum positions support this legal distinction. Their ground is neither a new factual finding about how Mississippi has implemented the Act nor an assertion that all official worship is permissible.

### Graduation

**Remove safe harbor:** Lee does not let the State avoid scrutiny by substituting a student for clergy or a student proposal for the official decision to incorporate prayer. The school still may organize the ceremony, select a devotional slot, supervise content and press students to participate. **Strongest objection:** A genuinely private student speaker may include a religious perspective in a voluntary ceremony. The six Justices supporting vacatur of the exemption preserve that possibility; they reject only Jones II's criteria as a conclusive safe harbor. Stevens, O'Connor and Souter rely on their two Lee joins; Ginsburg and Breyer on their contextual private-expression positions plus governing Lee; Kennedy on his own pressure ground and express private-expression reservations.

**Preserve present allowance:** Scalia and Thomas regard voluntary student initiation and delivery, the no-prescription duty and the lack of demonstrated implementation as sufficient to resist extending an injunction at this stage. **Strongest objection:** Lee is governing law and concerns graduation despite formally optional attendance. Their answer distinguishes the authorized private student conduct from Lee's established official clergy-selection practice; they do not purport to overrule Lee through this interlocutory disposition. They do not join a universal rule that student election or delivery always insulates school-directed prayer.

### Severability and interim remedy

**Tailored continuing restraint:** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer preserve the supported protection against official worship, vacate the categorical graduation carveout, and require narrowing of the decree's broader reach. The actual intrusion, not a belief that every accommodation is invalid, sustains likelihood of success and irreparable injury; protection of private expression addresses the claimed countervailing harm. **Strongest objection:** A federal court may rewrite a law by inventing a new neutral program or by striking only the religious qualifiers. The six reject that move. They preserve independently functioning provisions and lawful applications under the severability direction, leaving state-law construction and actual decree formulation below. The Court need not declare a final application of every phrase lawful or unlawful to correct this interlocutory decree.

**Vacate present restraint altogether:** Scalia and Thomas support removing the broad injunction for the more fundamental insufficiency of the predicted merits showing. They share the proposition that Section 1(3), Section 2 and actual independent expression cannot be enjoined merely because Section 1(2) can operate unlawfully, but they do not join the majority's retained interim restraint. Thus agreement that relief is overbroad is a shared proposition; the two remedial judgments are not identical. No total invalidation of the Act, compulsory new forum or substitute prayer policy is adopted by any row.

### Intervention as of right and permissively

**Affirm both denials:** All eight accept the published lower account that the Attorney General actually argued the proposed students' constitutional interests and that their proposed participation identified no unrepresented interest in the present statutory-validity controversy. The narrow prospective restraint leaves private prayer rights intact. Trbovich's minimal burden remains real, but it does not make every distinction between public and private identity sufficient without a relevant potential representational difference. Shared desired outcome alone would not sustain denial; the actual representation and limited dispute do. Stevens's Lamb's Chapel join, O'Connor/Souter/Ginsburg/Breyer's contextual equal-access joins, and Scalia/Kennedy/Thomas's private-expression writings all preserve the asserted private interests rather than treating them as nonexistent.

**Vacate for reconsideration or direct intervention:** The strongest alternative is that a government's defense of an enactment may diverge from private students' protection of their own expression, especially if content filters are severed or new private-speech restraints are added. Trbovich supports that possible divergence and rejects conclusively equating the two interests. On the present record, however, the AG's actual argument addresses those expression rights and the decree must protect private speech; no new adverse construction or surrendered student claim is established. The alternative therefore does not justify mandatory intervention now. Materially changed remedial interests may be assessed on a proper later application under ordinary Rule 24; no new hearing or automatic renewed intervention is ordered.

Permissive intervention is separately discretionary. A common question establishes eligibility, not an obligation. The reported duplication/delay assessment is within that discretion on this narrow proceeding, and the ability to present amicus argument helps explain the absence of prejudice without substituting for a right to intervene. The Court does not approve an absolute rule that absence of a novel legal question always warrants denial, adopt the circuit's historical assertion that it never reverses such denials, or replace abuse-of-discretion review with a universal plain-error requirement. Each Justice supports this same bounded rule; no separate constitutional merits preference changes that procedural conclusion.
