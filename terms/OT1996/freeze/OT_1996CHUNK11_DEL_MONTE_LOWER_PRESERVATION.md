## Objective source findings

1. The City challenged **both** liability claims below. Paragraph5 says its post-trial motions sought judgment as a matter of law and a new trial on both equal protection and inverse condemnation. Paragraph6 says the City appealed jury submission of both claims: equal protection because liability presented a mixed issue of law and fact for the court; inverse condemnation because no jury right existed. Paragraph6 separately reports a request for judgment as a matter of law on both claims. The published opinion does not give a complete account of each substantive equal-protection objection or its supporting evidence.

2. The Ninth Circuit intentionally did **not reach** the City's equal-protection arguments after sustaining the taking claim and damages. Paragraph10 says one supported liability claim could sustain the single award and expressly states that if inverse condemnation and its damages were affirmed, the court need not consider the equal-protection arguments. The remainder of the opinion addresses inverse-condemnation jury allocation and evidentiary sufficiency, followed by damages. Thus this published lower decision supplies neither an appellate merits rejection nor a finding of abandonment of the City's equal-protection challenges.

3. There are two distinct verdict relationships. Between **claims**, paragraphs8?10 describe separate takings and equal-protection instructions and liability determinations with a common $1,450,000 award for the delay proximately caused by the City's action. The court distinguished that arrangement from alternative theories under one general verdict and stated that substantial evidence supporting either claim could support the judgment. Within the **takings claim**, paragraph22 says the verdict did not differentiate its two liability theories; the court therefore required substantial evidence for each theory before upholding that taking verdict. The first proposition does not erase the second.

4. Paragraph27's express absence of objection concerns **how the substantial-advancement taking instruction was framed**. It is not a declaration that the City abandoned its jury-allocation objections, its motion on equal protection, or every legal objection. Paragraph21 also states that erroneous submission of a legal issue would not necessarily require a new trial: factual findings and conclusions by the district court could be an available procedural response. These are descriptions of the lower opinion's posture and reasoning, not selection of this Court's remedy.

5. The exact supplied grant is copied below. Its first question expressly identifies disputed factual issues underlying the **takings claim**; its second addresses regulatory taking; its third concerns ?1983 damages for an unconstitutional land-use decision. It contains no separately stated equal-protection merits question. The source establishes the breadth of objections below, while the authorized grant independently supplies the Supreme Court issue boundary. This extraction does not decide fair inclusion or expand that grant.

6. Paragraph7 makes the owner's waiver of its additional-damages cross-appeal conditional on affirmance. Paragraph4 identifies the adverse substantive-due-process determination as undisputed. Paragraphs58?61 separately discuss the City's damages objections and reject them; no second damages sum is allocated to equal protection.

## Exact authorized grant ? mechanically copied from the final neutral June16 packet

## A. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Whether a property owner bringing a damages action under 42 U.S.C. §1983 for an alleged unconstitutional regulatory taking has a Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial on disputed factual issues underlying the takings claim.

2. Whether a municipality's repeated rejection of development proposals may constitute a regulatory taking where the restrictions deny economically viable use or, under the then-governing formulation, fail substantially to advance a legitimate governmental interest.

3. Whether a federal court may award damages under §1983 for an unconstitutional land-use decision rather than limiting the property owner to prospective or administrative relief.

See id. at 1499-1506, 1509.

L69: The district court held that the City did not violate Del Monte's substantive due process rights because the City asserted valid regulatory reasons for denying Del Monte's development application. L84: At the outset, we consider whether reversal of either the inverse condemnation claim or the equal protection claim would require a new trial. The district court instructed the jury on two separate claims.
L85: First, the district court addressed Del Monte's takings claim, instructing the jury that it should find for Del Monte if (1) all economically viable use of the property had been denied; or (2) the City's decision to reject Del Monte's development application did not substantially advance a legitimate public purpose. L86: ¶9

L88: "If you find that each of these elements has been proved by a preponderance of the evidence, your verdict should be for the plaintiff on the equal protection claim." Thus, the jury was charged separately on the taking and equal protection claims.

L90: This case does not present the interplay of a general verdict and alternative theories of liability. See Landes Constr. Co. v. L91: American Multicinema , 793 F.2d 990, 1001 -02 (9th Cir.1986) (Syufy Enterprises ) (general verdict usually upheld only if substantial evidence supports each and every theory of liability submitted to the jury), cert. denied, 479 U.S. 1031 , 107 S.Ct. 876 , 93 L.Ed.2d 830 (1987)).
L92: The district court instructed the jury that if it found the City liable for any constitutional violation, it should award Del Monte damages "in an amount that will compensate [Del Monte] for the delay [proximately] caused by the City's action." We therefore can affirm the judgment and verdict if we determine that substantial evidence supports either the inverse condemnation or equal protection claims. See Landes, 833 F.2d at 1373 .
L93: If we hold that the inverse condemnation claim and resultant damages can be affirmed, we need not consider the City's arguments concerning the equal protection claim.

L274: Finally, the City argues that the district court abused its discretion by denying its motion for a new trial because the jury awarded Del Monte excessive damages. Not only do we review the district court's denial of a motion for a new trial for an abuse of discretion, California Sansome, 55 F.3d at 1405, we allow substantial deference to a jury's finding of the appropriate amount of damages. Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum Comm'n v. NFL , 791 F.2d 1356, 1360 (9th Cir.1986), cert.
L275: denied, 484 U.S. 826 , 108 S.Ct. 92 , 98 L.Ed.2d 53 (1987). We must uphold the jury's finding unless the amount is grossly excessive or monstrous, clearly not supported by the evidence, or based only on speculation or guesswork. Id.
L276: ¶58
L277: The district court instructed the jury to determine damages in an amount that would compensate Del Monte for the delay the City caused in Del Monte's efforts to develop the Dunes. The jury awarded Del Monte $1,450,000.
L278: The City contends that these damages are excessive because (1) the jury was permitted to consider delay damages for a period extended up to the date of trial; (2) Del Monte's experts' opinions on the property's value both before and after the City's actions were contrary to the weight of the evidence; and (3) Del Monte's experts were erroneously allowed to assume that there was a reasonable probability that the California Coastal Commission (Coastal Commission) would have approved Del Monte's proposed development.
L279: ¶59
L280: The record refutes the City's arguments. First, the district court refused to decide the relevant length of time for determining delay damages, concluding that this was an issue for the jury. The City has no factual basis upon which to argue what length of the time the jury found relevant. Second, Del Monte's experts testified that the more progress Del Monte made in the application process the more valuable the Dunes became.
L281: The jury was entitled to infer from this evidence that Del Monte's progress toward permit approval, up until 1986 when the City conclusively denied the application, could account for an appreciation rate higher than the eight to ten percent Del Monte's experts said was an "average" rate of return.
L282: ¶60
L283: 
L284: Finally, the City's contention that Del Monte's experts erroneously assumed that the Coastal Commission would approve the development was refuted by expert testimony. There is ample evidence upon which the jury could conclude that approval by the Commission was likely. See Oviatt, 954 F.2d at 1473 (within the jury's province to determine credibility of witnesses).
