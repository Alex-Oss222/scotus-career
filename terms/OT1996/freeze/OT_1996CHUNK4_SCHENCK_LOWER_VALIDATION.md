## 1. Exact posture and scope of the lower judgment

The preliminary injunction was issued February 14, 1992, after a March 6 to April 1, 1991 preliminary-injunction hearing and contempt hearings extending from February 6, 1991 through January 30, 1992. Only Paul Schenck and Dwight Saunders appealed, although the underlying defendants included three organizations and approximately fifty individuals. The original appeal was under 28 U.S.C. section 1292(a)(1).

The complaint pleaded one federal claim under section 1985(3) and six state theories: New York Civil Rights Law section 40-c/Executive Law section 296, tortious interference with business, trespass, intentional infliction of emotional harm, tortious harassment, and false imprisonment. The district court initially found likely success on the federal claim, the section 40-c claim by its relationship to the federal claim, and trespass; it did not then decide whether the other state theories independently warranted relief. Following historical Bray, the district court dismissed the section 1985(3) claim with leave to amend, retained the remaining state claims on judicial-economy, convenience, fairness, and comity grounds, and refused to vacate the injunction. This procedural account does not establish the present sufficiency of every cause of action under simulated law.

A divided panel on September 6, 1994 upheld the refusal to vacate and the other provisions but invalidated the buffer-zone and cease-and-desist provisions. Rehearing, ordered December 27, 1994, was confined to those two provisions. The en banc court's September 28, 1995 judgment vacated the panel's invalidation and affirmed the two challenged provisions **as modified**; the other provisions remained affirmed under the panel decision. The en banc court expressly did not decide whether these two provisions remained supportable under the state claims after dismissal of the federal claim, saying the district court had not yet considered that question. It resolved the First Amendment question only.

The en banc opinion expressly limits its record to facts before the district court at the initial injunction. It excludes the later "Spring of Life" protests considered in the separate refusal-to-vacate proceeding. Do not import those later events as justification for the original challenged decree.

## 2. Operative provisions, qualifications, and the appellate modification

The mechanically copied full decree appears in section 6. The legally material distinctions are:

- Paragraph 1(a) already separately prohibits trespass, sitting in, blocking, impeding, or obstructing ingress or egress, including parking lots, parking-lot entrances, driveways, and driveway entrances. Its breadth matters to the claim that the added geographic restrictions are unnecessary.
- Paragraph 1(b) prohibits **demonstrating**, not mere presence, within fifteen feet of the specified edges/front of doorways, doorway entrances, parking-lot entrances, driveways, and driveway entrances. It also prohibits demonstrating within fifteen feet of any person **or vehicle** seeking access to or leaving a covered facility. The fixed and mobile restrictions are distinct applications requiring distinct examination.
- Covered facilities are any facilities where abortions are performed in the Western District of New York, not just the named plaintiffs' facilities. The mobile zone is not limited to patients or women; it also protects other persons or vehicles seeking access or leaving.
- The exception allows nonthreatening conversational sidewalk counseling by no more than **two people with each person or group** they seek to counsel. It is not a cap of two counselors in an entire fixed zone. Paragraph 1(b) incorporates this exception into the fixed and floating restrictions. It allows an initial approach without affirmative advance consent.
- Paragraph 1(c) separately prohibits physically abusing, grabbing, touching, pushing, shoving, or crowding persons entering, leaving, working at, or using services at covered facilities. The counseling exception does not authorize these acts.
- The original decree says that an unwilling recipient, or one wishing to leave or walk away, may do so; counselors must cease "such counseling" and then comply with paragraph 1(b)'s personal-distance provision. The appellate court directed a textual modification on remand to make explicit the district court's construction: the person or group must **indicate, verbally or nonverbally, that they do not wish to be counseled**. Mere uncommunicated unwillingness is not the trigger as construed.
- The appellate construction of cessation is retreat from the personal fifteen-foot zone. It does **not** require ending all communication with that listener: advocacy from fifteen feet remains allowed. Approaches to other persons, distribution of leaflets to passersby, and other demonstration within the decree's bounds remain possible.
- Footnote 5 records the appellees' oral-argument concession that the decree as written does not prevent entering a rebuffing person's zone to counsel another person who has not refused. The court did not decide whether such a prohibition could validly be imposed and reported no occurrence of this scenario. This is an important limit, not a general permission to resume counseling the unwilling person.
- The same footnote leaves defendants free to seek modification if escorts drown out their message. It does not find that this allegation has been disproved or grant relief on it.
- Footnote 4 leaves the geographic reach of a floating zone away from a clinic unresolved, anticipating case-by-case district-court adjudication. Do not invent a maximum travel distance or represent a precise endpoint as established.
- Paragraph 1(c) preserves police authority to maintain public order and reasonably necessary rules at a particular site. The decree separately preserves legitimate First Amendment rights, requires good-faith instructions against prohibited activities, preserves local/state law-enforcement obligations, and lasts until modification or final resolution of permanent-injunction claims.
- The decree binds defendants, specified representatives, and persons acting on their behalf or in concert who receive actual or constructive notice; it does not purport to bind every person sharing their views. This reproduces the lower decree's wording, not an adjudication here of Rule 65's permissible notice standard.
- Enforcement is by appropriate motion, with stated civil damages of $10,000 per day for a first violation and/or criminal-contempt proceedings, doubling civil-contempt fines for successive violations, and joint/several fee responsibility. These contempt consequences make precision material. The exact terms, recipients of sums, and marshal-reading requirement are preserved below.

## 3. Findings, evidentiary limits, and remedial facts

The Oakes opinion reports the district court's findings that the demonstrations were usually peaceful but often became emotionally charged. It recounts yelling, intimidating and obstructive crowding, grabbing, pushing and shoving patients, escorts, and staff; congestion at vehicle entrances; and counselors who, frustrated by refusal, persisted, raised their voices, crowded patients, and continued after indications that counseling was unwanted. These are published recitations of findings, not independent review of the underlying testimony by this validator. This opinion does not verify the packet's additional assertions of spitting or harassment of police; those require another authorized source if material.

The district court found stress, physical injury in some instances, interference with the clinical environment, delays caused by frightened or confused patients leaving, and ineffective local enforcement because continuous protests exceeded available resources and arrests did not deter repeated conduct. The medical testimony recited includes elevated blood pressure, hyperventilation, sedation and additional counseling, inability to remain still during procedures, increased risks from later-term delay, and risks from delayed removal of pre-abortion laminaria or missed follow-up care. Women traveling substantial distances could have difficulty rescheduling. These support arguments about safety and access, but do not erase the distinct tailoring inquiry.

The defendants stipulated that a physical blockade could be enjoined. A threatened September 28, 1990 blockade did not occur after issuance of the TRO; defendants held a peaceful protest instead. No full physical blockade occurred after the TRO, according to the district-court statement quoted by the dissent. The TRO already contained personal fifteen-foot protection and a two-person counseling exception and cessation requirement, but not fixed fifteen-foot zones around clinic entrances. Its exact text is copied with the majority footnotes below. It is inaccurate to describe the original TRO as a prohibition on obstruction alone.

The judges disputed what the record established about the need for expanded relief. The majority accepted general TRO compliance with notable exceptions and held that a preliminary injunction may take account of pre-TRO misconduct; failure of a prior order was not treated as an indispensable prerequisite. Winter agreed that this record showed less disruption than the Madsen record, but regarded specific separation as justified by the coercive acts that did occur and as clearer to enforce than a generic prohibition.

Meskill emphasized that only two of the five contempt findings involved obstruction: a March 26, 1991 doorway incident involving Robert and Paul Schenck, and a January 1, 1992 driveway incident involving Paul Schenck and Daren Drzymala. His footnotes state that six contempt motions produced five findings, with one allegation unproved, and that the other contempt incidents involved the TRO's cessation condition. Treat this as the dissent's supported record account, not as five established obstruction incidents. The majority's initial description says hearings on only five of the six motions had occurred before the preliminary injunction, a procedural statement which should not silently be equated with disposition of all six motions.

The dissent also identified location-specific burdens: testimony placed protesters outside an entirely different building at 1241 Main Street after retreat, and suburban layouts could make a nominal fifteen-foot separation larger in practical effect unless protesters stood in the street. These facts were advanced against treating any fifteen-foot zone as automatically modest. The district court's quoted findings also criticize patient escorts for raising their voices to drown out counselors and blocking counselors, aggravating confrontations; that conduct bears on the parties' competing neutrality and remedy arguments.

## 4. Lower rationales and material disagreements

**Oakes opinion, joined by Newman, Kearse, Miner, Walker, Leval, Calabresi, Cabranes, and Parker.** The injunction was treated as directed at harmful conduct rather than viewpoint, and assessed under the historical Madsen requirement of no more speech burden than necessary for significant interests. The identified interests were medical safety, public safety, and lawful access. The majority viewed the fifteen-foot limits as smaller and more permeable than Madsen's thirty-six-foot entry zone because two counselors could enter. It nevertheless acknowledged that these zones also move with persons, unlike that earlier fixed zone. It regarded alternative demonstration nearby, available personal counseling, the clinical consequences of confrontations, and the district court's record familiarity as supporting fit. It did not deem post-TRO failure necessary to justify an injunction following a fuller evidentiary hearing.

For cessation, Oakes distinguished a bar on all uninvited approaches from a rule allowing an approach until an affirmative verbal/nonverbal refusal. It upheld retreat as protection against unwanted physical proximity and persistent pursuit of medically vulnerable listeners, rather than a ban on the message. It used captive-audience reasoning for persons who could avoid the confrontation only by forgoing needed care, and allowed continued advocacy from fifteen feet. The construction and open questions in section 2 are part of this rationale, not optional details.

**Winter concurrence in the result, joined by Newman, Kearse, Mahoney, McLaughlin, Jacobs, Walker, Leval, Calabresi, and Cabranes.** Winter proposed a broader, generally applicable principle: coercive or obstructionist conduct is unprotected in any forum, and persuasion rights do not authorize forcing an audience to receive advocacy. He expressly preserved breathing space for insulting, strong, but nonthreatening expression. Targeting particular people at places they cannot easily avoid increases the opportunity for coercion. On the documented obstruction and intimidation, physical separation was a permissible and comparatively precise response, even without the more extensive record in Madsen. His rationale did not depend on captive-audience or nonpublic-forum doctrine, and he rejected the view that general compliance with a TRO erased the significance of earlier misconduct.

**Jacobs concurrence in the result, joined by Mahoney.** Jacobs joined Winter fully and cautioned that an abortion-patient-health-only rationale was too narrow and potentially message-specific. Because the decree protects any person or vehicle entering or leaving, including a male staff member walking alone, its support must rest on general public safety, traffic, property, and freedom from coercion as well as care of patients. This is a material distinction between rationales supporting the same lower judgment.

**Meskill dissent, joined by Altimari.** The dissent accepted that some injunction was proper and did not oppose restraints on obstruction or physical harassment. It challenged these additional provisions as cumulative restraints on protected signs, leaflets, peaceful picketing, and speech, lacking a demonstrated need beyond existing paragraphs 1(a), 1(c), and 1(d). It emphasized substantial TRO compliance, limited subsequent obstruction, differing physical sites, and the duty of independent First Amendment review rather than deference to a court applying the older time/place/manner test. It argued that isolated coercive acts do not justify banning peaceful expression collectively, relying on Claiborne Hardware and Milk Wagon Drivers to distinguish protected advocacy from pervasive intimidation.

On cessation, Meskill considered the initial-approach distinction insufficient: a listener still gained control over otherwise protected public-forum expression. He disputed the extension of captive-audience protection from patients receiving care inside a clinic to persons walking toward it, and maintained that emotional offense is not itself grounds for suppression. He distinguished prohibited threats, fighting words, or violent coercion from protected expression; cited the independent prohibitions on physical interference; and questioned viewpoint neutrality where escorts could engage in loud close conduct. He reported that the ACLU amicus generally supported the clinics but regarded cessation as too restrictive in this respect. This is the opinion's description of that amicus position; the brief itself was not read.

**Altimari separate dissent.** Altimari joined Meskill and particularly stressed the floating zone's invisible, moving boundary, uncertain travel distance, and notice/chilling problems. He accepted that location and medical interests may inform the public-forum analysis but denied that they change its stringent standard. He considered the existing anti-crowding and access provisions material alternatives, distinguished Madsen's constrained physical access and more extensive misconduct, and objected to an unwilling listener's power to move protected expression fifteen feet away. He retained his earlier view that the injunction itself was content neutral while criticizing the Oakes rationale as content based.

## 5. Necessary neutral-packet refinements and readiness

The supplied packet's statement that fixed and floating zones were affirmed needs "as modified," including the explicit refusal trigger and permissible continued advocacy. Its statement that two counselors are allowed in fixed zones is incomplete: the exception is two per approached person/group and operates within the personal zones as well. The packet's statement that federal and state claims "remain sufficient for review" must not become a finding that the en banc court decided state-law entitlement; it expressly reserved that issue.

"Failed lesser measures" must identify the particular record and judicial disagreement, rather than assume continuous failure of an otherwise effective TRO. The packet's assertion that mobile uncertainty and breadth "establish overbreadth" states a conclusion on a live merits issue. For neutral framing, the source supports competing positions: the moving zones' reach, notice, and speech burdens may exceed the need shown, while their supporters regard them as precise safeguards against persistent pursuit and coercion; the material limits and exceptions above remain part of both analyses. This addendum makes no resolution of that dispute.

```text
APPENDIX
PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION
Upon consideration of the evidence introduced at a hearing held on plaintiffs’ motion for a preliminary injunction, and at hearings on plaintiffs’ motions to find defendants Nancy Walker, Bonnie Behn, Carla Rainero, Rev. Paul Schenck, Rev. Robert Schenck and Project Rescue in civil contempt for violations of this Court’s Temporary Restraining Order, and upon consideration of the complaint and supporting documents, and upon consideration of defendants’ stipulations pertaining to the entry of a preliminary injunction against certain conduct and to the binding nature of any preliminary injunction on all named individual and organizational defendants, it is hereby
ORDERED that defendants, the officers, directors, agents, and representatives of defendants, and all other persons whomsoever, known or unknown, acting in their behalf or in concert with them, and receiving actual or constructive notice of this Order, are:
1. Enjoined and restrained in any manner or by any means from:
(a)trespassing on, sitting in, blocking, impeding, or obstructing access to, ingress into or egress from any facility, including, but not limited to, the parking lots, parking lot entrances, driveways, and driveway entrances, at which abortions are performed in the Western District of New York;
(b) demonstrating within fifteen feet from either side or edge of, or in front of, doorways or doorway entrances, parking lot entrances, driveways and driveway entrances of such facilities, or within fifteen feet of any person or vehicle seeking access to or leaving such facilities, except that the form of demonstrating known as sidewalk counseling by no more than two persons as specified in paragraph (c) shall be allowed;
(c) physically abusing, grabbing, touching, pushing, shoving, or crowding persons entering or leaving, working at or using any services at any facility at which abortions are performed; provided, however, that sidewalk counseling consisting of a conversation of a non-threatening nature by not more than two people with each person or group of persons they are seeking to counsel shall not be prohibited. Also provided that no one is required to accept or listen to sidewalk counseling, and that if anyone or any group of persons who is sought to be counseled wants to not have counseling, wants to leave, or walk away, they shall have the absolute right to do that, and in such event all persons seeking to counsel that person or group of persons shall cease and desist from such counseling, and shall thereafter be governed by the provisions of paragraph (b) pertaining to not demonstrating within fifteen feet of persons seeking access to or leaving a facility. In addition, it is further provided that this right to sidewalk counseling as defined herein shall not limit the right of the Police Department to maintain public order or such reasonably necessary rules and regulations as they decide are necessary at each particular demonstration site;
(d) using any mechanical loudspeaker or sound amplification device or making any excessively loud sound which injures, disturbs, or endangers the health or safety of any patient or employee of a health care facility at which abortions are performed, nor shall any person make such sounds which interfere with the rights of anyone not in violation of this Order;
(e) attempting, or inducing, directing, aiding, or abetting in any manner, others to take any of the actions described in paragraphs (a) through (d) above; and it is further
ORDERED that nothing in this Order shall be construed to limit defendants and those acting in concert with them from exercising their legitimate First Amendment rights; and it is further
ORDERED that the defendant organizations and their officers and agents, and all individual defendants and those acting in concert with them, shall make a good faith effort to instruct all organizations and individuals they believe to be planning to participate in any of the activities enumerated above not to engage or participate in the activities enjoined in paragraphs (a) through (e) above;
2. IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that the failure to comply with this Order by any defendant or anyone acting in their behalf or in concert with them shall be subject to civil damages of $10,000 per day for the first violation of this Order and/or may subject them to criminal contempt proceedings; and it is further
ORDERED that each successive violation of this Order shall subject the eontemnor to a civil contempt fine double that of the previous fine and/or criminal contempt proceedings; and it is further
ORDERED that any amounts collected thereunder shall be paid to the Plaintiff health care facility at which the violation occurred, or to the Registry of the Court if the targeted health care facility is not a Plaintiff; and it is further
ORDERED that each eontemnor shall be jointly and severally liable for all attorney’s fees and related costs incurred by plaintiffs in relation to the enforcement of this Order.
3. Violations of this Order shall be enforced by appropriate motion.
4. IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that the United States Marshal for the Western District of New York shall read this Order as set forth above in Paragraphs 1 and 2 in their entirety at the site of a demonstration or protest at a facility at which abortions are performed in the Western District of New York. Plaintiffs’ counsel shall be responsible for notifying the Marshal in a timely manner of the location of such demonstration or protest activity.
5. It is the Court’s intention that nothing contained in this Order shall supersede or diminish the obligation of local and state law enforcement authorities to fulfill their duties and responsibilities in enforcing state laws and local ordinances.
6. This Order shall remain in full force and effect until modified by further Order, or until final resolution by this Court of the claims for permanent injunctive relief in the above captioned matter.
It is so ordered.
```
