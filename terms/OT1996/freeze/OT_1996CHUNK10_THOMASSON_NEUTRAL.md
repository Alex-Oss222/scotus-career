## Questions, parties, record, and posture

The supplied grant presents the Fifth Amendment equal-protection validity of the statement provision; the First Amendment validity of using the statement as a discharge basis; and the extent of congressional and military deference. The requested relief is reversal, declaratory relief, reinstatement, and record correction. The Government seeks affirmance. The lower court affirmed summary judgment for the Government.

The named defendants in the lower report are William J. Perry, Secretary of Defense, and John H. Dalton, Secretary of the Navy. The case concerns official discharge action and prospective/corrective relief. This packet creates no damages claim, new defendant, jurisdictional statute, exhaustion default, immunity ruling, or additional procedural history. The supplied appellate merits grant and continuing discharge injury define the present vehicle. APA and due-process objections discussed below in the lower report do not automatically expand the three questions granted.

Thomasson served approximately ten years with an excellent performance record. In March 1994 he sent four admirals a letter stating that he was gay and opposing the new policy. The Navy convened a Board of Inquiry in May 1994. The published record describes a two-day proceeding with his service record, live and written supporting testimony, and expert evidence. The Board unanimously found the statutory presumption unrebutted. A Board of Review agreed, and the discharge process continued. The published appeal concerns the resulting honorable discharge. An exact final discharge date is unnecessary to these questions and is not invented.

No identified past homosexual act, performance failure, security breach, or operational impairment was proved against him. That is a limitation on the individualized evidence, not an adjudicated finding that his statement could have no predictive relevance or that the statutory class could have no rational military justification. Conversely, receipt of excellent service evidence does not establish its legal irrelevance to every possible question.

The Board received the submitted evidence and found it insufficient to rebut conduct, propensity, or intent; Thomasson declined to supply further specific rebuttal of the presumed predicate. The present sources do **not** establish that the Board excluded his service record, ordered him to deny his identity, required proof of a past act, or denied a hearing. Those propositions cannot be used as factual shortcuts to reversal. A conclusion that the Board applied an unlawful meaning of propensity or an inadequate rebuttal standard must identify the legal error in the published decision and explain its material effect.

The lower majority describes the lawful purpose of the statement inference as identifying conduct, propensity, or intent. Hall's dissent disputes both its rationality and its practical operation. Neither characterization is a substitute for examining the statute, the directive, and this record.

## Statutory and regulatory terms

The complete statutory text is supplied in the exact copy above. These distinct provisions must remain distinct:

* Section 654(a) contains Congress's findings concerning the military's mission, discipline, morale, cohesion, privacy, and living conditions. These are legislative judgments; they are not adjudicated findings about Thomasson's conduct or his unit.
* Section 654(b) directs separation under approved procedures when one or more specified findings is made. Paragraph (b)(1) concerns an actual, attempted, or solicited homosexual act. Its retention exception requires **all five** additional findings: departure from usual behavior; unlikely recurrence; no force, coercion, or intimidation; continued presence consistent with discipline, good order, and morale; and no propensity or intent to engage in homosexual acts.
* Paragraph (b)(2), the challenged route here, concerns a statement that the member is homosexual or bisexual, or words to that effect, **unless there is a further finding that the member has demonstrated that he or she does not engage in, attempt to engage in, have a propensity to engage in, or intend to engage in homosexual acts**. The five findings in (b)(1) are not the rebuttal rule in (b)(2).
* Paragraph (b)(3) separately concerns marriage or attempted marriage to a person known to be of the same biological sex. No such finding is supplied here.
* Section 654(e) does not require processing for separation when the military determines both that statements or conduct were made or undertaken to avoid or terminate service and that separation would not serve the military's best interest. The record here does not establish the avoidance premise; this qualification cannot simply be omitted from a general description of statutory separation obligations.
* Section 654(f) defines homosexual and bisexual by conduct, attempted conduct, propensity, or intent and includes gay and lesbian within homosexual. Its definition of a homosexual act includes specified same-sex bodily contact for sexual satisfaction and bodily contact reasonably understood to demonstrate propensity or intent. The statutory definitions do not themselves resolve the constitutional relevance of ordinary identity language.

The lower report quotes Directive 1332.30's definition of propensity as more than an abstract preference or desire: it indicates a likelihood that the person engages in or will engage in homosexual acts. The directive distinguishes private orientation from conduct, propensity, and intent. The published rebuttal factors include acts, credibility, others' accounts of past conduct, the circumstances of the statement, and other relevant evidence. These terms supply a possible construction inquiry; they do not establish that the Board actually refused to consider a listed factor.

The statute and directive must also be kept distinct. Luttig's concurrence argues that the directive's likelihood requirement is narrower than the statute permits. The majority does not invalidate the directive. The lower writings reveal a dispute about the presentation and preservation of that issue. A disposition invalidating the directive or granting independent APA relief must establish its place within the present review; it cannot be treated as an uncontested threshold.

## Entering law and the limits of analogy

**Walmer, January 13, 1997, is intervening law.** O'Connor's controlling rational-basis analysis, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Breyer, sustained denial of preliminary relief under the earlier Army regulation where homosexual acts were admitted and the retention exceptions were stipulated not to be met. It permits conceivable legitimate reasons, reasonable military predictions, imperfect classifications, and predictive nonindividualized judgments. It does not require formal findings, empirical certainty, proof of individualized impairment, or the least restrictive personnel rule. It also requires a rational relation to the actual disadvantage and does not authorize bare harm or irrational prejudice. Military deference is substantial, not constitutional immunity.

Walmer expressly reserves an orientation-only inference and the later statutory statements rule. Thomasson's final discharge under §654(b)(2), without an identified prohibited act, therefore requires a fresh application. The differences prevent treating Walmer as an automatic affirmance; they also prevent treating its ordinary rationality and deference rules as inapplicable.

Souter's Walmer dissent, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, would require examination of the actual classification rather than treating earlier circuit precedent as automatically conclusive. It does not adopt heightened scrutiny, a governmental actual-purpose proof burden, or an individualized-impairment prerequisite. Scalia's concurrence, joined by Thomas, gives additional weight to permissible judgments about specified conduct; it does not convert all aversion into a valid justification or erase Romer. These public positions are relevant Justice-specific evidence for the independent modeler.

**Romer** invalidates Amendment 2's broad withdrawal and foreclosure of legal protection under ordinary rational basis. The controlling opinion preserves conceivable grounds and reasonable prediction, does not impose a universal actual-purpose burden, and does not assign orientation classifications a new scrutiny tier. Its breadth and relation-to-the-disadvantage inquiry matter here. Its express treatment of narrower military conditions prevents a mechanical application of its result. The public opinion and separate positions, including the controlling joins of Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer and the opposing position of Scalia and Thomas, supply evidence without predetermining a narrower statement case.

**Virginia** supplies demanding intermediate scrutiny for the sex classification actually adjudicated: an actual important objective, a substantial relationship, and governmental persuasion without overbroad sex stereotypes. It does not transfer that test automatically to sexual orientation or make the military issue a sex-classification case by assertion. A proposed new tier, a distinct sex-discrimination theory, or an extension of Virginia requires its own legal justification and an explanation of scope and preservation.

**Heller and Beach Communications** preserve ordinary rationality's latitude while asking whether a permissible distinction connects to the particular disadvantage. Heller's separate proof-burden and family-participation holdings must not be collapsed into one heightened test. Neither requires formal findings or empirical certainty for every rational prediction.

**Dawson and Mitchell** distinguish punishment of abstract belief or association from evidentiary use with a constitutionally relevant connection. Mitchell sustained an enhancement requiring an independently criminal act and intentional selection; it did not hold that every identity statement proves prohibited conduct. Dawson did not create an absolute bar to speech or association evidence. Their relevance inquiry leaves room both for a lawful inference and for a challenge that this inference penalizes expression without the required connection.

**Weiss** supports substantial consideration of the military context while retaining independent constitutional judgment. Its fixed-term and appointment holdings do not resolve personnel classifications. **Waters** concerns public-employee speech, employer belief, reasonable investigation, and motive under its recorded fractured grounds. The disputed identity words here are not an uncertain report of what an employee said. No automatic actual-disruption requirement, mandatory investigation checklist, or least-severe-discipline rule may be imported.

Bowers retains its specified-conduct holding within the entered law; it does not alone resolve an equal-protection classification, the legal meaning of propensity, the validity of evidence drawn from identity language, or every asserted substantive-liberty question. No independent substantive-liberty claim is silently added to the grant.

## What the lower writings establish

Wilkinson's majority sustains the law and this discharge under rational-basis review and substantial military deference. It treats the statement as a rebuttable indicator of conduct, propensity, or intent, regards general service excellence as insufficient rebuttal of that predicate, and distinguishes suppression of opposition from evidentiary use. Its account permits policy criticism, association, and other advocacy without an identity statement being necessary. It rejects the lower due-process and APA arguments on the record it describes.

Murnaghan agrees with the rationality of the unrebutted presumption without adopting the majority's broadest deference rhetoric. This supplies a narrower affirmance route.

Luttig would read the statute more broadly than the directive, question the directive's narrowing definition, and sustain a status-based military restriction. That is a separate construction and constitutional route, not a holding of the majority. His legislative-history discussion does not establish disputed individual facts.

Hall, joined by Ervin, Michael, and Motz, argues that the presumption irrationally equates orientation with disobedience and punishes the disclosure of what the policy otherwise describes as private orientation. He relies on independent judicial review, ordinary rationality, the absence of proved misconduct, the service evidence, and limits on speech used as evidence. His conclusion that the evidence disproves the relevant inference is an adjudicative disagreement, not an undisputed Board finding.

The report's descriptions of other members' rebuttals and other lower cases may support analysis of the rule's operation. They do not establish a rebuttal by Thomasson or import a later Supreme or simulated disposition into this case.
