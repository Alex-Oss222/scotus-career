## Judgment and vehicle

All eight Associate Justices participate and commit to affirm the Tenth Circuit's judgment vacating termination and remanding for adequate findings under the decree's own substantial-and-continuous-compliance condition. They do not determine actual compliance or noncompliance. The existing decree remains operative unless and until lawfully terminated or modified; this judgment sets no new twelve-month clock, expiration date, supervision mechanism or fixed release date.

The appeal challenges the district court's final termination order adopting both reports. It is not defeated by treating the children's challenge as an appeal of only the 1993 report: the 1989 report had been held inadequate alone and the final judgment rested on both. The separate 1994 monitoring-fee judgment, 28 F.3d 1056, is outside this grant; no §1988 holding is made. The later State Auditor report and proposed supplemental study were not before the Special Master and are not used to establish the decree's failure. Timely quality-assurance material actually considered below remains distinct from those excluded materials.

## Common propositions available for joinder

**J1 — The agreed release condition governs.** The Department may obtain termination by establishing twelve consecutive months of substantial and continuous compliance with the 1983 decree. Substantial compliance concerns the decree's actual essential purposes—fair, reasonable and timely adoption decisionmaking, and fair, reasonable and adequate procedures and practices securing access to permanent homes—and the specified obligations through which the parties agreed to achieve them. An unmet criterion does not invariably defeat release if its deviation is immaterial to those purposes; conversely, a general impression of improvement cannot erase material unfulfilled requirements. No universal numerical compliance percentage supplies a safe harbor.

**J2 — Findings must permit review.** Rule 52(a) requires enough subsidiary factual findings and explanation to disclose the basis for the ultimate compliance conclusion; it applies to a master's findings to the extent adopted by the district court. Neither a long evidentiary summary nor repetition of the legal conclusion substitutes for material findings. The court need not recite every item or discuss irrelevant details, and reviewing courts must respect supported findings under clear-error review. They cannot weigh disputed evidence themselves to supply the material findings the trial tribunal omitted.

**J3 — These findings are inadequate.** The adopted report does not adequately connect its methodology, competing quantitative and qualitative evidence, and stated compliance conclusions to the decree's obligations. It does not explain the relationship between reported low conference-compliance measures and the compliance finding; nor between the attorney-notification evidence and that finding. It discounts adverse individual case examples as selected instances without explaining why they are unrepresentative rather than evidence of wider defects. It does not adequately resolve the timely challenge to ADAPT data or explain/discredit testimony that time in custody increased. These are gaps in explanation and factfinding, not Supreme Court findings accepting every adverse measure or declaring systemwide failure.

For clarity, the report describes conference measures ranging from 6.2–63 percent, other content measures from 31–97 percent, timely attorney notification of 26 percent and no notification for 8 percent, and testimony that custody length increased from 1.5 to 2.1 years. The Court identifies those as examples of evidence requiring an intelligible account in the findings. It selects no percentage, computes no overall score, credits no disputed system automatically, and does not decide whether the Department's opposing evidence is more persuasive. A reasoned explanation may establish why a particular shortfall was immaterial or why a contrary measure is reliable; it must actually be supplied by the tribunal charged with finding facts.

**J4 — Performance and modification are distinct.** The Department need not establish a significant change under Rufo merely to prove performance of the decree's existing release condition. If it instead seeks to reduce still-binding obligations because law or facts changed, Rufo supplies that different threshold and tailoring inquiry. Freeman and Jenkins reinforce the need to match findings to the actual remedy within their school-desegregation settings; they neither replace this decree's twelve-month condition with their full school-release inquiry nor create a universal constitutional-minimum exit. No PLRA prison-conditions predicate is established here.

**J5 — Remand is bounded.** The district court must apply the stated contractual condition, address material deviations and their effect on the decree's essential purposes, explain relevant methodology and resolve the material factual conflicts needed for review. It may then enter the lawful judgment the supported findings warrant. The Court requires no plaintiff consent, consensus, neutral-reviewer endorsement, guarantee against every future lapse or new institutional arrangement. Procedures contemplated by the decree must be genuinely in place; that requirement does not extend the bargained twelve-month duration or impose an independent indefinite prediction requirement. No automatic retrial of every settled fact or acceptance of newly tendered material follows from remand; further proceedings are governed by ordinary authority.

## Enhanced alternative-path comparison

**Reinstate termination on the reports read as a whole.** The strongest case is that the experienced district judge adopted lengthy reports, substantial compliance tolerates immaterial defects, and Rule 52 does not require every evidentiary detail. O'Connor's Rufo emphasis and the institutional-restraint concerns in the Jenkins dissent make that a real alternative. The reports nevertheless omit material—not merely repetitive—connections between contested evidence and essential compliance findings. The unexplained methodology and disregard of adverse cases cannot be repaired by assuming that some unarticulated weighing occurred. All eight reject reinstatement on that record without demanding a transcript of every evidentiary choice.

**Affirm because the data conclusively establish noncompliance.** Low compliance measures and prolonged custody provide substantial grounds for concern, but the State offered opposing evidence and the appellate tribunal did not resolve credibility or measurement reliability. Rule 52 prevents an appellate substitute for missing factual findings in either direction. All eight refuse a definitive noncompliance holding or permanent enforcement judgment.

**Affirm remand under the decree and Rule 52.** This selected path preserves meaningful review, the State's contractual route to release and the children's right to performance. Its strongest objection is the cost of more supervision despite years of proceedings. Neither age nor administrative burden creates the missing findings; the remand must nevertheless remain confined and may lead to termination upon the requisite supported showing. No newly created procedure may indefinitely postpone a properly established right to release.

**Require Rufo change, Freeman's school checklist or constitutional-minimum proof as the release test.** Each substitutes a different legal question for performance of this decree. All eight reject these transpositions. Rufo applies only to changed-duty relief; school-desegregation precedents illuminate review and tailoring at their actual scope.
