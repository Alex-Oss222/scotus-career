## Questions, supported record and lower judgment

The authorized questions concern whether Nollan's nexus and canonical Dolan's rough-proportionality inquiry extend to monetary conditions attached to a particular development approval; whether the recreation charge can be sustained without the requisite relationship to this project's effects; and the boundary between such individualized conditions and generally applicable taxes, assessments and regulatory fees. The art condition is separately presented within that controversy. These questions do not waive jurisdiction or expand the record into an unpresented compensation valuation, separate park-fee challenge or new factual claim.

Ehrlich's redevelopment of a defunct private recreation facility for 30 townhouses carried a $280,000 recreation charge and an art-program allocation generally identified as $33,200. A separate $30,000 park fee is unchallenged. The initial recreation use supplied a private benefit; the City treats redesignation of the parcel as loss of a land-use inducement for comparable recreational opportunity. The source does not establish a public entitlement to the former private operation. The California Supreme Court found sufficient nexus to the change of permitted land use but an inadequate basis for the recreation amount. It sustained the art condition.

The state court's operative judgment reverses the Court of Appeal and remands to it with directions to return the case to the City. It does not conclusively assess a new recreation fee. The remand directs the City to determine whether any fee is justified and, if so, its extent. The allowable theory described in the lead opinion concerns additional costs of attracting comparable **private** recreation after removing this parcel's recreational-use designation, such as appropriately supported inducements or redesignation costs. It rejects using the value of replacement **public** recreation facilities. Net cost must account for relative benefits the project contributes to the same public interest. Specific findings supported by substantial evidence and some quantification beyond conclusory statements are required, but no exact mathematical calculation. The current record does not fix the resulting amount, supply new local findings or establish that the only remaining task is arithmetic.

Arabian, Lucas and George join the lead opinion. ### Art-program terms material to the challenge

The ordinance applies to new residential projects of more than four units and commercial, industrial and public building projects above $500,000 valuation. For residential valuation it excludes units covenanted for low- or moderate-income households or senior citizens. Remodeling of the specified building categories comes within the ordinance above $250,000 valuation, excluding required seismic-safety earthquake rehabilitation and preserving the residential covenant exclusions.

The program allocation is one percent of total building valuation, excluding land acquisition and offsite improvements. It ordinarily uses the latest International Conference of Building Officials valuation data, unless the Building Official considers a different measure appropriate. The applicant may place approved art onsite, pay the allocation to the City Art Fund, or donate approved art accepted by the City Council. A work costing less than the allocation is permitted if the balance is paid to the fund. Final approval or a certificate of occupancy requires satisfaction of the entire allocation through approved placement, in-lieu payment, accepted donation, or approved financial security equal to acquisition and installation cost. Unauthorized removal of private-site art permits revocation of the certificate.

The onsite option has an express accessibility qualification. Section 33H-26(d) requires a narrative showing that the work will be displayed in an area open and freely available to the general public at least ten hours daily, **or** that it otherwise supplies equivalent public accessibility based on the work's characteristics or placement. Approval evaluates aesthetic quality, harmony, location and public accessibility. For a project otherwise subject to Planning Commission approval, the Arts Committee recommends and the Commission considers that recommendation; significant later design revisions return to the Committee. The ordinary Arts Committee decision is appealable under the specified code procedure. This is not an unconditional option to place art anywhere on a closed private parcel, and the equivalent-access alternative must not be omitted.

Under §33H-31, onsite art remains the applicant's property. The site owner must preserve and maintain it, protect against defacement, mutilation or alteration, and provide required fire, extended and vandalism insurance in an amount determined by the City Attorney. Applicant and owner must record an approved maintenance covenant before placement. Failure to maintain is declared a public nuisance; on reasonable notice the City may undertake necessary maintenance or insure the art and impose the resulting costs as a lien, in addition to other lawful remedies. Donated art becomes City property only upon Council acceptance. The donation application separately calls for an artist's waiver under the California Art Preservation Act or other applicable law. None of these distinct provisions may be transposed onto a different compliance option.

## Law entering the distinct issues

### State-court review

Section 1257(a), as enacted by Pub. L. 100-352 §3, 102 Stat. 662 (1988), authorizes certiorari review of final judgments or decrees of the highest state court in which a decision could be had when the specified federal issue or federal right is presented. Section 7 made the amendment effective ninety days after June 27, 1988, with exceptions for then-pending Supreme Court cases and earlier judgments. Those transition exceptions do not govern this 1996 state judgment. The modern certiorari channel must not be confused with the former mandatory-appeal subsection used in older opinions. [Official enactment](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-102/pdf/STATUTE-102-Pg662.pdf).

*Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469, 477–487 (1975), recognizes four settings in which a finally decided federal issue can support review before completion of the state proceedings: (1) the federal determination is conclusive or further proceedings are preordained, leaving the case practically concluded; (2) the federal issue necessarily survives whatever happens next; (3) later federal review will be unavailable whatever the ultimate outcome; or (4) the applicant might prevail later on nonfederal grounds, federal reversal would end litigation on the relevant cause of action, and postponement might seriously erode federal policy. Final decision of a legal issue alone does not supply these further conditions. [Opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/420/469), [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/420/us/469).

*Flynt v. Ohio*, 451 U.S. 619, 620–623 (1981), applies the fourth category's separate policy requirement and warns against piecemeal federal review. Even litigation-ending reversal of a constitutional issue does not automatically make an interlocutory judgment reviewable. *Goodyear Atomic Corp. v. Miller*, 486 U.S. 174, 178–180 (1988), applies category four to federal nuclear-production policy and an agency remand; the exception is not limited to speech cases. Neither decision makes the importance of a constitutional right, standing alone, a substitute for the statutory conditions. [Flynt](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/451/619), [Goodyear Atomic](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/486/174), [Goodyear cross-check](https://openjurist.org/486/us/174).

*Fort Wayne Books, Inc. v. Indiana*, 489 U.S. 46, 54–57 (1989), applies category four to a facial First Amendment challenge that would terminate the relevant RICO charges. Justice O'Connor's separate opinion, 489 U.S. at 68–70, instead insists that the anticipated trial and possible later federal issues defeat jurisdiction in that companion matter; she distinguishes the other case's already-imposed seizure affecting protected expression. This is a source of competing legal approaches, not a decision about development charges. [Opinion and writings](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/489/46), [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/489/us/46).

*Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1037–1044 (1983), permits federal review when the decision appears primarily federal or interwoven with federal law and the independence and adequacy of a state ground are unclear. A clear, bona fide, independently sufficient state ground prevents review that could not alter the judgment. The inquiry concerns the actual basis of the judgment, not an available state argument that no controlling state coalition adopted. Justice O'Connor wrote the Court's opinion; Justice Stevens's dissent favored a presumption against jurisdiction where an adequate state ground's independence remained uncertain. The holding governs despite that separate position. [Opinion and writings](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/463/1032), [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/463/us/1032).

### Preserved claim and supporting argument

*Dewey v. Des Moines*, 173 U.S. 193, 197–201 (1899), distinguishes a new reason supporting the federal question raised below from a different federal question: parties may develop additional arguments within the preserved federal question, but common facts do not make distinct constitutional challenges the same claim. *Cardinale v. ### Exaction, regulation and monetary charge

The Fifth Amendment's compensation guarantee applies to state action through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Nollan v. California Coastal Commission*, 483 U.S. 825, 831–842 (1987), requires an essential nexus between the permit condition and the regulatory interest that could justify the restriction. The demanded property interest itself matters: legitimate land-use regulation does not by itself justify obtaining an unrelated public-access easement without compensation. Scalia wrote the opinion; O'Connor joined. Stevens's dissent objected to the uncertain burdens placed on legitimate land-use planning and favored the contrary legal arguments. [Nollan](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/483/825).

The exact operative register entry is **Nexus and rough proportionality for individualized land dedications**, present operation effective *Dolan*, June 24, 1994. Canonical *Dolan* requires the government, for an individualized development permit demanding a real-property dedication, to establish essential nexus and rough proportionality in both nature and extent to the project's effects. Some individualized determination is required; reasonable estimates may suffice without exact mathematics or proof that the property is uniquely suitable. A generalized possible benefit is inadequate. The right to exclude requires separate justification when an ordinary restriction could address the asserted impact. O'Connor's controlling opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

Canonical *Dolan* expressly leaves monetary exactions, generally applicable legislative conditions and ordinary zoning unresolved. It finds no completed taking and orders no compensation or unconditional permit. Its reserved questions must be analyzed here; the California court's historical discussion cannot enlarge the controlling Supreme Court rule. The full canonical text and authority limits are in the entering-law slice under **Canonical Holdings selection: Dolan v. City of Tigard, 512 U.S. 374**.

The earlier current-term *Babbitt v. Youpee*, January 21, 1997, applies *Hodel v. Irving* and *Penn Central* to the particular destruction of succession interests. O'Connor's opinion is joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer as well as Stone-Zsela; Stevens dissents on the amended options and transition period. It expressly leaves the *Dolan* permit-exaction rule unchanged and does not create a categorical taking whenever a separate property attribute is limited. Its reasoning and limits, including Stevens's published position, are supplied in the entering-law slice.

*United States v. Sperry Corp.*, 493 U.S. 52, 60–63 & n.9 (1989), upholds a reasonable charge for governmental services, permits fair approximation rather than exact service-by-service accounting, and rejects treating the deduction of fungible money as a permanent physical occupation. Its legislative cost-reimbursement setting does not decide every individualized permit demand. *Connolly v. PBGC*, 475 U.S. 211, 223–228 (1986), rejects a facial taking challenge to pension withdrawal liability using economic impact, expectations and governmental character; requiring expenditure for another's benefit is not sufficient by itself. O'Connor's concurrence reserves materially different as-applied takings and arbitrary-liability questions. [Sperry](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/493/52), [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/493/us/52); [Connolly](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/475/211), [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/475/us/211).

*Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies, Inc. v. Beckwith*, 449 U.S. 155, 160–165 (1980), prevents a State from relabeling privately owned fund earnings as public property and retaining them where a separate service fee already compensates its services. Its narrow holding preserves the distinct possibility of a genuine service charge. It defeats an automatic inference that every monetary demand lies outside property protection, without deciding the exaction test for these fees. [Opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/449/155).
