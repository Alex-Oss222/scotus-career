## Review objects, chronology and jurisdiction

The petition expressly seeks review of BOTH the August 6, 1996 merits judgment and the December 16, 1996 refusal to recall/stay the mandate, renew rehearing, or allow a second rehearing petition. It was filed December 27. First rehearing was denied September 3; mandate issued September 11; the second motion was filed September 20. The October 1 clerk letter required a response to the September 20 petition for rehearing and en banc suggestion; the respondent filed its response October 8. The December 16 order says the panel considered the motions and found them all without merit, and that no active judge requested an en banc poll. It gives no further substantive explanation. Treating this as only an unpresented request or a motion the court never considered is inaccurate. Treating it as conclusively a merits rehearing is also a legal conclusion requiring analysis.

The December order says Sheets did not come to petitioners' attention before August 30. That is not a finding that they learned of it on August 30, and does not contradict the original packet's September 17 date. The exact September 17 assertion is not independently established by the clean order; if an equitable disposition depends on that precise date rather than the established sequence, obtain the relevant clean petition narrative. Sheets was decided July 26, eleven days before the merits judgment, not after it.

The 1995 Rule 13, copied from the official rules, uses a 90-day judgment/rehearing period, not the date of mandate. Its text expressly addresses timely rehearing and does not contain the additional wording found in later rules about appropriately entertained untimely rehearing. The older judicial doctrine remains relevant: Bowman v. Loperena, 311 U.S. 262, 266 (1940), distinguishes an untimely petition or denied request for leave that is not entertained on the merits from a permitted petition that the court actually considers on the merits; only the latter postpones finality. FPC v. Idaho Power Co., 344 U.S. 17, 21 n.1 (1952), applies the entertained-and-considered principle to Supreme Court review, while its main judgment analysis separately requires a real alteration rather than a formal repetition. Neither case makes the bare filing or any unexplained denial sufficient.

The model must decide whether this court's requested adversarial response and December consideration constituted actual entertainment of rehearing, despite the form of denying all motions including leave. If so, the December 27 petition is within 90 days of December 16 and review of the underlying judgment has a supported legal route. If not, the September 3 denial ordinarily yields a December 2 deadline, and the original-judgment petition is late unless an authorized extension or other legally effective timing premise exists. None is established in the clean record. Section 2106 supplies relief after jurisdiction; it cannot itself cure lateness. The minimum missing factual premise on that latter path is a timely petition or valid extension applicable to the original judgment; do not invent one.

The December 16 refusal is expressly a review object and is itself challenged promptly. Its scope must remain distinct: review of a refusal to recall or reopen, if independently cognizable, addresses that refusal and its lawful remedial consequences; it does not automatically reopen expired direct review of the August judgment. A mere denial of rehearing is not converted into an independent vehicle to evade the original deadline. Any model relying exclusively on the separate-recall route must identify the basis and standard for that review and limit relief to the properly reviewed order. The presently supported route to ordinary original-judgment review is the actual-entertainment analysis above. This is a legal issue for independent assessment, not a presumed source defect and not a reason to substitute a fictional filing date.

## Sheets, the verdict and competing GVR paths

Sheets concerns an insurer's duty to defend when the allegations create potential coverage, including property damage caused by an occurrence. It holds that negligently made misrepresentations may be accidents when the resulting damage was unforeseen or unexpected by the insured. Intending a statement and expecting reliance do not alone establish intent or expectation of the resulting property damage. The majority rejects a natural-and-probable-consequences test that would eliminate accidents caused by ordinary negligence, disapproves Ed Winkler to the extent inconsistent, and identifies I.A. Construction's reliance on that reasoning. It does not award indemnity: settlement left the actual basis of liability unresolved. Its dissent disputes the treatment of the volitional misrepresentation and the need to decide the broader accident question. The full official opinion, including that dissent, has been reviewed.

Here Continental supplied a defense under a reservation of rights; the live claim seeks indemnity after a jury verdict. Negligence counts had been dismissed under Maryland's economic-loss rule. That procedural history does not by itself prove that the acts supporting remaining statutory or warranty liability were intentional, nor does it make a defense-potentiality holding dispositive of indemnity. The petitioner's argument concerns whether the occurrence rationale remains valid, while the insurer points to actual liability, causation, policy terms and exclusions, and the difference between duties. Neither party's characterization is a finding.

The jury found for the association on express and implied warranty counts 1-3 and statutory description/quality misrepresentation count 5, and against it on counts 4, 6 and 7. The saved instructions expressly say knowledge of falsity or intent to deceive is unnecessary for BOTH count 5 and count 6. The reply's suggestion that both counts 6 and 7 necessarily required scienter is not adopted. The adverse count 6/7 verdicts are not affirmative findings that the insured lacked an expectation of property damage; equally, count 5 does not require intent to cause that damage. Keep the verdict, instructions and inferences separate.

The copied Maryland provisions preserve express-warranty formation and modification requirements; implied-warranty inspection and waiver qualifications; condominium unit/common-element warranty periods, notice and suit conditions and exclusions; and the selected consumer-protection definitions actually reproduced. They do not establish coverage independently of the policy. The record supports assessing whether Sheets undermines an outcome-material accident premise; any ultimate coverage determination still requires the applicable policy terms, exclusions, liability basis, causation and damage categories.

Controlling simulated Lawrence requires a reasonable probability of a changed outcome-material premise plus an independent equitable assessment. A possible effect or thematic similarity is insufficient. The requested response and considered denial matter to the value of another remand; the lack of explanation does not establish either full substantive reconsideration or a purely procedural refusal. Finality, the decision's pre-judgment availability, diligence on the supported chronology, and independent coverage grounds all remain live. One lawful path, after establishing review jurisdiction, is a limited GVR for state-law reconsideration without holding coverage. Another is denial because the probability or equities are insufficient. No path assumes the ultimate Maryland-law answer.
