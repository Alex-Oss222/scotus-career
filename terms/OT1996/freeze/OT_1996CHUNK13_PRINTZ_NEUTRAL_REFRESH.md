## Additive neutral record

1. **Budget and expenditure assertions.** The petitioners asserted that their budgets contained no provision for the federal work and that they faced liability for unauthorized expenditures. These are reported litigation assertions. They are not findings of actual cost, expenditure, personal liability, unavailable resources in a specified amount, damages, a taking or a legally sufficient compensation claim. They strengthen the need to address the officers' actual fiscal and legal objections when evaluating the Government's limited-burden position. The asserted cost or absence of funding does not alone decide the structural question, and no automatic reimbursement entitlement follows.

2. **State-law assertion.** Both officers asserted that state law prohibited their undertaking the federal responsibilities. This is stronger than saying that state law did not require the work, but its asserted character must be retained. The supplied excerpt establishes no authoritative construction of either State's law and does not establish that the two States' restrictions have identical language, scope or clarity. It supplies no new ground to invalidate a federal command merely because state law differs. The constitutional source and proper means question remains distinct from the content and effect of an actual state-law constraint.

3. **Reported nonparticipation.** The supplied objective recitation states that Montana and Arizona did not require their chief law-enforcement officers to implement the Brady Act and that Printz and Mack chose not to do so. That is a limited description of program participation. It does not establish that neither officer received a notice, received a purchaser statement, possessed derived information, made a particular eligibility determination, sent a notice, received a request for reasons or acted under a separately established state authority. Those transaction-specific facts cannot be inferred either way from the refusal to implement the program.

These propositions do not create a new filing, concession, adjudication, claim or source of relief. The reported absence of a state requirement and the asserted state prohibition remain distinct. No verified cost or coercion threshold is added to the law.

## Consequences at the actual statutory triggers

The exact text in `../entering-law/OT_1996CHUNK13_PRINTZ_STATUTE.md` continues to control the distinctions:

- **Section 922(s)(2):** The reasonable-effort command is addressed to an officer to whom the transferor has provided the specified notice under (s)(1)(A)(i)(III). Reported refusal to participate does not textually convert that command into a voluntary request, establish receipt of a particular notice, or decide its constitutional validity. Its five-business-day inquiry remains separate from the other duties.
- **Section 922(s)(6)(B):** The conditional destruction and information restrictions concern an officer to whom the purchaser statement is transmitted under (s)(1)(A)(i)(IV), unless the officer determines the transaction unlawful. An officer can receive a statement without voluntarily undertaking a background investigation. Consequently, general nonparticipation neither proves absence of this trigger nor by itself removes the duty. The destruction clock runs from the purchaser's statement, not from an assumed decision to participate. Conversely, a review cannot assume receipt or possession merely to create a live application against these plaintiffs.
- **Section 922(s)(6)(C):** Written reasons are required only when the officer makes an ineligibility determination and the individual requests reasons. The twenty-business-day clock runs from that request. Opposition to the program alone establishes neither predicate, and receipt of a statement does not automatically establish them. No unconditional duty to issue reasons may be attributed to these officers from this supplement.
- **Section 922(s)(7):** The specified protection against damages for failure to prevent an unlawful transfer or prevention of a lawful transfer is not an enacted compensation provision for administrative work. Its distinct subject does not resolve the alleged state-law expenditure exposure or establish a broader immunity from every liability.

Dealer duties, alternative transfer routes and an officer's discretionary notification remain separately governed by their text. The new assertions establish no standing to invalidate all such provisions and no universal conclusion about the operative remainder if a particular executive command is held invalid. A proposed judgment about a conditional duty must identify its legal scope, the established factual predicates and any actual remedial limit rather than silently turn nonparticipation into a transaction-specific finding.
