## Jurisdiction before remedy

Official 1995 Rule 13 gives ninety days from judgment or denial of timely rehearing, not mandate. Its text does not include the later Rules' express language about appropriately entertained untimely rehearing. Bowman v. Loperena, 311 U.S. 262, 266, distinguishes an unentertained untimely petition or a denied application for leave from a petition actually permitted and considered on the merits; only actual entertainment postpones finality. FPC v. Idaho Power Co., 344 U.S. 17, 21 n.1, applies that doctrine to review here. Its distinct principal holding also distinguishes a materially altered judgment from a formal repetition. The full official Bowman and Idaho Power opinions, including footnotes, were read.

The requested adversarial response, the description of the September paper as a rehearing petition, the recital of the competing Sheets positions, and the considered denial support a substantive-entertainment interpretation. The explicit denial of leave and absence of a grant or explanation support the competing view that the court only refused reopening. Neither interpretation is stipulated. If actual entertainment occurred, the December 27 filing is within ninety days of December 16 and review of the underlying judgment has a supported route. If it did not, the September 3 denial ordinarily yields a December 2 deadline for direct review; no valid extension or other timely original-judgment filing is established. Section 2106 cannot repair that jurisdictional failure. There is no invented extension.

The December refusal is also expressly challenged promptly. Its possible independent review cannot automatically restore expired review of the August judgment. A model relying only on the recall order must supply the legal authority, review standard, and remedy confined to that order. The present packet does not invent an independent reopening doctrine. A model may resolve the original-review channel through the actual-entertainment question without treating legal disagreement as a missing historical filing.

## Coverage record and relevant state law

The association obtained a $1.1 million jury verdict against the developer, Wellington Homes, and seeks indemnity from Continental. Continental furnished a defense under reservation of rights. The federal judgment rejected an occurrence under the policy's accident requirement, using the rule that natural and ordinary consequences of negligent work are not accidents. It relied materially on I.A. Construction, Ed. Winkler, and related construction-risk reasoning. Its opinion did not separately adjudicate every exclusion, causation question, or damage category.

The jury found breach of express and implied warranties on issues 1–3 and statutory description/quality misrepresentation on issue 5; it found for the developer on issues 4, 6, and 7. The saved instructions expressly say knowledge of falsity or intent to deceive is unnecessary for both issues 5 and 6. The reply's suggestion that both 6 and 7 required scienter is not adopted. Neither the adverse verdicts nor the favorable issue-5 verdict affirmatively determine whether the developer expected the physical damage. Dismissal of negligence causes of action under the economic-loss rule does not itself establish intentional conduct underlying the remaining causes.

The complete official Sheets opinion and dissent were reviewed. Sheets decides defense potentiality, not actual indemnity after the unsettled underlying case was compromised. Its accident analysis nevertheless states a substantive coverage rule: negligent acts causing damage unforeseen or unexpected by the insured may be accidents; intending the statement or expecting reliance does not alone establish expectation of the ensuing damage. It rejects the natural-and-probable-consequences formulation, disapproves Ed. Winkler to the extent inconsistent, and notes I.A. Construction's reliance on that formulation. It separately requires property damage and causal connection. Its defense-stage acceptance of allegations does not substitute for proof of those elements in an indemnity action. The dissent disputes whether volitional misrepresentation is accidental and whether the broader accident discussion was necessary; it does not control Maryland law.

The statutory appendix was read with its qualifications intact. Express warranties require a bargain-based writing, description, or model; modification requires the specified later signed detailed instrument. Implied warranties retain inspection and written-waiver conditions. Condominium warranties distinguish units and common elements, their commencement and durations, notice and suit conditions, proper plaintiffs, maintenance/abuse exclusions, and nonresidential exclusion. Selected consumer-protection definitions are distinct. None of those statutory duties independently supplies insurance coverage.

## Entering law and two supported paths

Lawrence's controlling six-Justice probability-and-equities rule governs: a sufficiently recent or intervening development must create a reasonable probability that an outcome-material premise would be rejected on reconsideration and that reconsideration may affect the outcome; finality, lower-court independence, cost, delay, and manipulation receive an independent assessment. The prior public Lawrence entry supplies Stevens's discretion explanation and Scalia's dissent joined by Thomas. Those positions remain noncontrolling. LAVAS, October 21, 1996, applies the rule to an actual new statute and expressly preserves timely review, the receiving court's judgment, and separate temporal questions. Its support by all eight Associates does not make every new-law allegation sufficient.

After establishing jurisdiction, one path is a limited GVR: Sheets directly disapproves the accident premise, its defense posture does not make that substantive premise irrelevant to indemnity, and the terse December denial does not establish that the lower court recognized and applied the altered state-law rule. The strongest contrary argument is that Sheets was available before judgment, was expressly briefed and considered later, and may not affect the actual indemnity grounds; the Court cannot demand an explanation simply because it dislikes an unexplained denial.

The contrary path denies further review: the court already had an adversarial presentation, the holding below may survive under indemnity and business-risk distinctions, and further remand may add expense without enough probability of a different material premise. Its strongest answer is that the only articulated federal accident rationale is the one Maryland's highest court rejected, and a requirement that petitioner establish coverage would collapse limited reconsideration into merits review. No path decides coverage or makes the jury find facts it did not find.

Its Kinman institutional attribution, Ayeni personal immunity, CompuServe personal jurisdiction, Vitronics claim construction, and Hincapie channel-specific immigration jurisdiction do not alter this Rule 13/Bowman or Lawrence question.
