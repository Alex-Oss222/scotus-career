## Patient rights and ordinary civil commitment

**Davis's qualified comparison.** At JA51–54, Davis initially answered broadly that patients in the predator program would have the same rights as other mental-health patients. Counsel objected specifically that evaluation, release and furlough provisions differ. The judge required clarification, and the testimony then identified the patient rights listed in §59-2929, including clothing, possessions, communications and money, with particular implementation qualifications. It is inaccurate to turn the initial general answer into a finding that all procedures or conditions were identical.

At JA72–75 the judge permitted cross-examination, if Davis knew the answers, on differences potentially bearing on the civil or criminal character of the program. Davis acknowledged that an ordinary hospital furlough was possible and was generally aware that an ordinary mental-health facility's director could release a patient without court intervention; he could not quote the relevant provisions. He distinguished the predator program and agreed with counsel's general reference to a jury trial. These are qualified administrative testimony and counsel's characterizations, not an authoritative construction requiring a jury in every release proceeding. The final packet's distinct annual-review, authorized-petition and unauthorized-petition provisions control the description of the Act. The actual statutory text, with its conditions, would be needed for a precise additional comparison with the ordinary Act.

**Logan's clinical comparison and the trial rulings.** At JA350–355, Dr. Logan explained that his teaching document primarily addressed release decisions for hospitalized patients with schizophrenia under ordinary mental-health care. The judge sustained objections to asking him for legal conclusions about admission under the ordinary Act, then allowed clinically framed questions. Logan described recent harmful behavior or inability to care for oneself and competence to decide on admission; he distinguished these admission questions from his document's focus on release after hospitalization. His account of recent behavior is clinical testimony, not a holding that a particular ordinary-commitment statute legally required the stated number of days or that Hendricks was eligible or ineligible under it.

Counsel referred to a motion in limine, but this excerpt does not supply that motion or its full order. ## Proposed placements and individualized risk evidence

**Outpatient care and a halfway house.** At JA183, Hendricks acknowledged that his conditional-release arrangements included outpatient treatment and living at a halfway house. The question's repeated September 11, 1984 date conflicts internally with its description of custody and the 1985 sentence. This supplement does not repair that transcription or questioning problem by inventing a date; the scheduled 1994 release chronology in the final packet remains unchanged. At JA189–190 he identified Dodge House in Wichita as his desired parole plan. These statements establish a described treatment condition and proposed placement, not the provider's acceptance, a funded and staffed treatment regimen, the placement's supervision terms, or a judicial finding that the arrangement would adequately manage his risk. The earlier reference to a halfway house “here in town” is not independently identified as Dodge House in that passage.

**Remote residence and the school concern.** At JA246–248, Dr. Befort reported that Hendricks wanted to live where there were few people, perhaps in a mountainous part of Virginia or Kentucky; Befort did not recall the location specifically. This was a reported aspiration, not a concrete residence, placement agreement or enforceable supervision plan. Befort also recalled discussing Dodge House, but had never visited it. He attributed the nearby-school concern to Hendricks and expressly did not know whether the description was accurate. Neither that account nor the separate school-proximity hypothetical put to Logan verifies Dodge House's actual location relative to a school.

Befort testified that Hendricks was likely to reoffend if permitted. His adjacent explanation relied on repeated past conduct, his view of pedophilia, limited appreciation and control, access to potential victims, and lack of effective prior treatment as he understood the history; he described risk if unsupervised. These are his clinical opinions and record-based impressions. They support concern about an unsupervised arrangement but do not establish that an identified supervised outpatient plan was evaluated and proved inadequate. Conversely, the discussion of supervision does not establish that any available supervision would suffice. Hendricks's inability to guarantee that he would never offend is testimony; it is not an adopted legal requirement of zero risk.

**Logan's qualifications matter.** At JA341–348, Logan treated motivation, family support, employment, treatment compliance, circumstances and access to potential victims as relevant clinical variables, while qualifying the significance of particular factors. His concern about a halfway house across from a school was not proof of that fact about Dodge House. His release checklist was not a pedophilia-specific statutory test, and medication was not part of every pedophilia treatment program. His reference to a Menninger incest-diversion program concerned the kinds of clinical decisions he made; it did not establish Hendricks's eligibility, acceptance or likely benefit there.

Logan distinguished a prudent clinical recommendation against release from a prediction that reoffending was more likely than not. His final answer at JA356–357 confirms that clinical risk avoidance need not rest on a probability exceeding fifty percent. His numerical assertions about untreated groups remain witness testimony, not judicial findings about this individual, independently verified research or a new legal definition of statutory likelihood. Neither the clinical checklist nor the percentages may silently replace the controlling legal inquiry.

## Supported alternatives and remaining factual limits

The State can rely on the concrete risk testimony and the absence of demonstrated safeguards for the described placements to contest their adequacy. Hendricks can rely on the described outpatient condition, halfway-house proposal, ordinary civil-care comparison and distinction between clinical caution and legally sufficient risk prediction to question whether secure commitment was adequately justified. Neither account may convert a proposal into a proved adequate route or a missing detailed assessment into a finding that every alternative failed. These sources permit the competing arguments; this validation selects neither conclusion.

Specific unresolved propositions, if a proposed legal rationale depends on them, require specific support:

- **The actual conditional-release plan:** the original parole or conditional-release documents, dates and conditions; the provider's acceptance; the halfway house's rules, capacity and staffing; the actual treatment and supervision plan; and verified location or victim-access information. Hendricks's and Befort's testimony alone does not establish these operational details.
- **Ordinary commitment as an available route:** the applicable contemporaneous statute with all eligibility, placement, furlough and release qualifications, together with a case-specific evaluation or finding applying it to Hendricks. Clinical discussion and counsel's legal questions establish neither eligibility nor ineligibility.
- **Adequacy of a particular less restrictive arrangement:** a supported individualized assessment of the proposed services, supervision, compliance and risk, including any agency or judicial evaluation of that arrangement. A general opinion about release or a program's existence does not supply that assessment.
- **Scope of excluded evidence:** the motion in limine and the actual order or complete pertinent ruling, if a contention depends on broader exclusion than the particular objections shown here.
- **Actual treatment and release administration:** the relevant contemporaneous clinical and administrative records, examination reports, petitions and orders, with their procedural status, if a contention depends on delivered services, an unmet release standard or a pattern of retaining qualifying patients. Later material cannot silently become original-record proof.

These are source limits, not findings that a litigant failed to present evidence and not a general requirement to retrieve every document before proceeding. A supported inference or narrower legal analysis may avoid an unresolved proposition, but the proposition may not be filled by assertion.
