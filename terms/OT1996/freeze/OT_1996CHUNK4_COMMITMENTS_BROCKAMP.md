## Statutory operations and precise decision scope

For a tax requiring a return, §6511(a) permits a claim within three years from when the return was filed or two years from payment, whichever expires later; if no return was filed, the payment-based two-year period applies. The distinct stamp-tax clause uses three years from payment. The operative return-based period is not three years from the return's due date. Whether a particular late return activates the three-year claim route can be assumed favorably to these taxpayers without defeating the amount limit.

Section 6511(b)(1) prohibits allowance after the subsection (a) period unless a claim was filed within that period. Separately, (b)(2)(A) caps a claim filed during the three-year route at taxes paid in the immediately preceding three years plus any extension of time for filing the return. Subparagraph (B) caps other claims at payments in the preceding two years. Subparagraph (C) separately governs allowance without a claim as if a claim were filed on the allowance date. Section 7422(a) requires a duly filed administrative claim before the refund suit. Each provision does different work.

**F — filing holding supported by all eight.** Assuming Irwin's ordinary presumption applies to these refund claims, the structure of this particular scheme rebuts the asserted authority to suspend §6511(a)'s filing periods for mental incapacity. The repeated claim/allowance restrictions, their coordination with an independently stated monetary ceiling, and the particular adjustments Congress supplied establish the controlling rule. Incapacity and absence of prejudice do not create an additional period. This is not a finding that either claimant necessarily loses on subsection (a) considered alone; a late return may satisfy a filing route while leaving zero recoverable payments.

**L — independent amount holding supported by all eight.** Mental incapacity does not extend the payment lookback in §6511(b)(2). Even assuming a timely claim under subsection (a), these tax payments fall outside the ordinary applicable lookback, including any supported filing-extension period. Tolling a filing deadline does not itself move the separately prescribed interval in which refundable payments must have occurred. This ground is independently sufficient against these ordinary tax-refund recoveries and must appear as a holding, not an unexplained consequence of F.

**I — Irwin treatment supported by all eight.** Irwin remains a rebuttable equitable-tolling presumption. The Court can assume the taxpayers' private-collector analogy brings them within it and decide rebuttal. It need not hold that all tax claims lack private analogues, that sovereign immunity alone excludes equity, or that mental illness can never justify tolling under any statute. Irwin's attorney-neglect holding does not establish that Scott's proven disability was ordinary neglect.

The specific adjustments in §6511(c), §6511(d), and §§1311–1314 provide corroborating structural evidence; their mere existence alone is not the entire rebuttal. No qualifying agreement, special-period tax item or mitigation circumstance is established here. Those provisions do not supply alternative relief on this record, and this decision does not announce a complete operative rule for an unused exception from a brief's shorthand. The statutory extension for filing a return is included in the (b)(2)(A) calculation and is not confused with an assessment-extension agreement. The taxpayers' proposal for a matching equitable extension of the Government's assessment period is not enacted law and cannot supply missing refund authority.

The agreed holding concerns the requested nonstatutory mental-incapacity tolling and associated extraordinary-inability theory. It need not decide fraud or affirmative governmental inducement on a different record, every form of tax equitable recoupment, universal jurisdictional classification, or every later/other version of the Code. No constitutional disability claim is independently adjudicated. Section 6514's characterization, supplied as the Government's argument rather than full verified text, is unnecessary to the selected holding.

## Enhanced competing paths and narrower alternatives

**Taxpayer path, taken at its strongest.** Once Congress permits refund suits, Irwin makes equity presumptively available even against the sovereign. Incapacity that actually prevents a claim is materially different from counsel's ordinary neglect. Scott has an adjudicated inability to act, followed by prompt filing after the incapacity ended, and an absence-of-prejudice finding. The historic collector action supplies a plausible private analogue. Congress did not expressly say “no disability tolling.” Specific tax adjustments can coexist with ordinary equitable principles; Stevens's Dalm dissent specifically warns against inferring exclusion solely from selected statutory mitigation provisions. A coordinated reading would suspend both filing and the incorporated lookback during proven incapacity, preserving a remedy rather than making equitable filing relief useless. Under this path Scott's judgment would be affirmed on the existing findings; Brockamp would retain a remand for proof of incapacity and its causal duration, diligence after capacity/representation, and otherwise applicable refund conditions. Neither mental illness by diagnosis alone nor absence of prejudice alone would suffice. McGill's entire $7,000 would not automatically be refunded.

**Government path selected, with its strongest objection answered.** The disputed question is not whether the taxpayers deserve relief or whether Congress used the word “equity.” Unlike an ordinary deadline appended to an authorized claim, §6511 separately specifies both when a claim may be made and which payments may be returned. The latter remains a concrete cap even on a concededly timely claim. Suspending that payment interval and constructing a corresponding Government assessment extension would change two statutory operations. The repeated allowance restriction and tailored statutory adjustments reinforce that conclusion. Irwin allows rebuttal from the enacted scheme without an express anti-equity sentence; the assumption that equity must preserve the practical usefulness of a tollable filing deadline cannot first establish that either provision is tollable. Administration of a national tax scheme corroborates this reading but does not independently establish it, and the Government's volume assertion is not a finding that every return entails an incapacity dispute.

**Narrower route actually tested.** All eight could dispose of the tax-payment claims solely under L while reserving F. That route has particular attraction for Stevens because of his Irwin agreement and Dalm dissent, and for Scalia because his Williams concurrence withheld an unnecessary alternative. The commitments nevertheless include F: the question granted contests nonstatutory suspension of this integrated mechanism, and the statutory interaction used to rebut tolling of the amount limit also explains why its associated claim period cannot receive the requested extra-statutory disability extension. The opinion must actually explain that interaction. If a proposed opinion merely classifies the money cap and then declares every filing rule jurisdictional, the all-eight F coalition is not supported; the narrow L judgment remains available. No Justice's acceptance of that narrower fallback is permission silently to report a broader join.

**Broader route rejected as a common opinion.** Dalm plus sovereign immunity cannot, without more, make Irwin irrelevant. Nor can the Court establish a universal rule against equitable tolling of all federal tax deadlines from these provisions. Kennedy and Thomas give greater independent weight to Congress's control of consent, but their commitments do not require the other six to join that broader account. Whether a party meets the enacted refund conditions is different from changing those conditions; Williams, Henderson and O'Gilvie preserve that distinction.

The district court's November 19, 1993 findings after the October trial establish, for the issue presented, inability to file from January 1985 to early November 1989; the November 29, 1989 return date controls over an introductory November 28 recitation. On the supplied record no independent statutory exception sustains the award. Its interest allowance falls with the underlying refund; the taxpayer's rejected request for interest from the original payments is not separately reopened or granted.

It does not hold that a suspense or excess-collections entry proves a deposit, that absence of an assessment proves one, or that the extension form necessarily defeats one.
