# 148. Pounders v. Watson

## A. Event, channel, participation and requested relief

**Docket and date:** No. 96-1383; October Term 1996; scheduled nonargued consideration June 27, 1997. The pending certiorari petition seeks review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the Ninth Circuit's December 13, 1996 judgment, 102 F.3d 433. The recovered filings do not establish a prior plenary grant, a Supreme Court merits argument, or an already entered Supreme Court judgment. A reasoned summary merits disposition requires the corresponding grant and lawful review action; the scheduled event is not itself a prior grant.

**Questions actually presented in the petition:** (1) Whether a state trial judge may summarily find an attorney in contempt during a multidefendant felony jury trial for twice willfully violating in-court orders not to ask prejudicial questions when the statute limits punishment to a $1,000 fine, five days' imprisonment, or both. (2) Whether the former 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) presumption for state factual findings is violated when a federal appellate court accepts the findings of willful disobedience but concludes that summary contempt was unnecessary to prevent further violations and disruption. The second question describes petitioner's characterization of the appellate decision; the opinion itself expressly did not decide whether Watson willfully disobeyed an order.

**Relief sought:** Pounders asks that certiorari be granted to review the adverse habeas judgment. Watson's opposition asks that certiorari be denied and defends the appellate judgment. A summary merits ruling, if chosen, must separately identify whether it rejects the appellate summary-process ground, resolves or preserves the unaddressed notice and willfulness claims, and what proceedings remain. No recovered filing supplies consent to a new contempt prosecution or an agreed remedy.

## B. Supported record and the judgments below

Watson represented William Mora with co-counsel in a six-defendant special-circumstance murder trial that began March 28, 1994. The case involved simultaneous juries. The judge warned counsel against discussing punishment on April 7, April 20 and April 21. The warnings were not all delivered in the same setting: Watson remained at counsel table during identified sidebars attended by co-counsel; other statements occurred in open court. Co-counsel stated that he had not relayed a sidebar admonition to her. Watson disputed actual knowledge and asserted that the context of her question differed from the earlier prohibited questioning. These assertions must remain distinct from the judge's findings that she knew the order and intentionally violated it.

On June 21, Watson asked Mora on redirect whether he had faced the death penalty until shortly before trial. The judge sustained an objection and told her to follow the prior admonitions. She next asked whether he was facing life without parole. At sidebar the judge asked why she should not be held in contempt. She explained that the testimony concerned Mora's state of mind in revealing that he had made the 911 call, rather than a request for jury sympathy about punishment. The judge rejected the explanation and orally found contempt in his immediate presence. He said incarceration during trial was impracticable and deferred punishment. After a further discussion with counsel, he instructed both juries to disregard the punishment questions and answers, identified their impropriety, and resumed the examination. The transcript excerpts do not themselves establish that the instruction eliminated prejudice.

The June 22 written order found beyond a reasonable doubt that Watson violated California Code of Civil Procedure §1209(a)(5), that the questions' sole purpose was improperly advising the jury of possible punishment, and that she was aware of the order. It imposed two days in jail. Execution was stayed until three days after submission of Mora's case to the jury and thereafter through a petition for extraordinary relief testing the order. On July 8, two days after submission, Watson through counsel received another opportunity to explain. The judge remained unpersuaded and characterized the punishment disclosure as permanent jury prejudice favoring her client. This is the trial judge's assessment; it is not an independently proved jury mental state, an appellate finding of actual effect on the verdict, or a substitute for the constitutional inquiry.

The California Court of Appeal denied habeas on July 14, 1994, and the California Supreme Court denied habeas and a stay on August 25. Their reproduced orders give no explanatory rationale. The federal District Court denied habeas on September 8, finding adequate warning from the open-court statements and rejecting the need for an evidentiary hearing because the necessary contempt elements were within the trial judge's knowledge. Its stay lasted until September 15. Watson's opposition states that she served the sentence September 16–17, 1994, and received notice of a State Bar investigation with possible professional consequences. The Ninth Circuit likewise held the appeal not moot because of possible bar discipline; no final disciplinary disposition is established.

The Ninth Circuit reversed and directed entry of judgment granting habeas. It expressly left Watson's notice and willfulness arguments undecided. Relying on Oliver, Wilson, Harris and circuit authorities, it concluded that two questions in a three-month trial did not pose a sufficiently serious threat to orderly proceedings to justify summary procedure; it contrasted pervasive repeated violations in other cases and found no indication of other disrespect or prospective repetition without immediate punishment. Whether that reasoning imposed a categorical pattern requirement or instead applied a contextual necessity inquiry is itself disputed. Petitioner's description of the reasoning is not the opinion's binding formulation. The record's continuation for approximately two weeks cannot by itself establish either that the summary adjudication was necessary or that it was unnecessary; the action already taken is part of that sequence.

## C. Governing text and entering law

**California provisions reproduced in the petition appendix, pages 47–49:** Section 1209(a)(5) identifies disobedience of a lawful judgment, order or process as contempt. Section 1211(a) permits summary punishment for contempt committed in the immediate view and presence of the court or judge at chambers, with an order reciting the observed facts, adjudging guilt and prescribing punishment. Conduct outside that immediate view and presence instead requires the specified affidavit or official factual statement. Section 1211(b) concerns the Judicial Council family-law contempt form and does not establish compliance here. Section 1218(a) permits, on a finding of guilt after answer and evidence, a fine no greater than $1,000, imprisonment no greater than five days, or both; its separate costs provision concerns a party or that party's agent violating an order and does not establish a fee award here. Section 1222 calls contempt judgments and orders final and conclusive; the recovered state habeas proceedings demonstrate that the present review did not proceed as an ordinary statutory appeal. State authorization remains subject to federal constitutional limits.

**Pre-AEDPA federal review:** The federal application necessarily preceded its September 8, 1994 denial. Lindh v. Murphy, June 23, 1997, now controls the noncapital application's chapter 153 temporal question: amended §2254(d) does not apply. Lindh preserves independently directed provisions, chapter 154's different express clause and Felker's separate successive-application setting. This case does not require reopening Lindh or treating Felker as a universal pending-case rule.

Former §2254(d), reproduced in the petition appendix, pages 45–47, presumes correct a qualifying state factual determination after a merits hearing, evidenced by a written finding or comparable adequate written indication. Its specified exceptions concern an unresolved factual dispute, inadequate factfinding procedure, inadequate development of material facts, absent jurisdiction, unconstitutional failure to appoint counsel for an indigent, lack of a full and fair hearing, other denial of due process, or lack of fair support in the record as a whole. Where the prescribed factual presumption applies and no exception is established or otherwise appears, the applicant bears the statutory convincing-evidence burden. The presumption concerns qualifying historical facts; it does not automatically turn the constitutional legality of summary procedure into a binding state fact. Sumner v. Mata supplies petitioner's factual-deference argument. The entering-law module **Independent Miranda custody review under the former habeas statute**, effective Thompson v. Keohane, November 29, 1995, preserves the distinction between historical facts and an independently reviewed constitutional mixed question in its own setting; it does not itself classify every contempt question.

**Summary contempt:** The Due Process Clause supplies the governing constitutional restriction. The date-eligible authorities identified in the neutral materials are Ex parte Terry; Cooke v. United States; In re Oliver; Sacher v. United States; In re McConnell; Harris v. United States; and United States v. Wilson. They distinguish ordinary notice and hearing protections from a narrow in-court summary power and address personal observation, obstruction, institutional control and timing. Sacher's allowance of deferred punishment in its setting must be addressed before treating delay as dispositive. Rule 42 is an analogy concerning federal procedure, not a statute governing this state contempt. The packet establishes neither a universal repetition requirement nor a new rule that every case requires a separate showing of imminent future disorder. Personal observation, seriousness of prejudice and a short sanction likewise do not automatically dispense with every constitutional safeguard. Any materially different reading requires the date-eligible source and its applicable scope.

**Collateral nonretroactivity and presentation:** Schiro v. Farley, January 19, 1994, makes Teague nonjurisdictional and permits, but does not require, declining a defense first raised late after earlier omissions. Caspari v. Bohlen, February 23, 1994, considers a consistently preserved subsidiary defense limiting the very constitutional rule and writ under review; preservation, fair inclusion and logical priority remain distinct. The operative registered module is **Late assertion of Teague nonretroactivity**, present operation effective Schiro, with Caspari's preserved-defense comparison. If properly considered, the inquiry asks whether the requested rule was dictated at finality and retains both contemporary Teague exceptions; disagreement among judges or new facts alone does not establish a new rule. The Caspari predicate of consistent preservation is not established by these sources. A filing omission neither automatically waives the defense nor automatically authorizes the Court to decide it.

## D. Competing positions and lawful alternatives

**Pounders:** The trial judge observed the questioning, had given repeated warnings during the trial and expressly warned Watson between the two questions. The deliberate disclosure threatened the jury's lawful decision process and the court's authority even if it did not stop testimony. Prior warnings to other counsel had proved inadequate. A petty sanction and an immediate contempt finding with delayed service preserved trial continuity. Petitioner invokes California authority including Gallagher and defends deference to state factual findings under former §2254(d).

**Watson:** The earlier warnings were delivered in settings where she was not present or did not understand their asserted scope; she sought relevant state-of-mind evidence and denied deliberate defiance. The two questions did not cause a breakdown requiring summary adjudication. Ordinary notice, counsel and an opportunity to establish intent were available. She defends the Ninth Circuit's contextual application of Oliver, Harris and Wilson and invokes Hallinan's caution against suppressing respectful advocacy. These are her claims, not resolved facts.

**Available analytical paths, without a recommendation:** The Court may deny the petition; grant for fuller briefing; or, if the appropriate summary-review standard is satisfied, grant and enter a reasoned disposition. On merits review it may sustain the appellate due-process ground if the received exception and this record support it; reject an excessive restriction on the summary power while preserving properly remaining notice or willfulness claims for remand; or resolve the relevant constitutional claims if their review scope and record permit. Any outright reinstatement of the contempt judgment must account for those unaddressed claims rather than silently treating them as already rejected by the Ninth Circuit. Any alternative habeas ground must be reviewable and supported. A remand for unresolved claims differs from a mandate directing a new contempt prosecution. A claim of judicial bias requires its own preserved basis; the judge's observation of contempt alone supplies none.
