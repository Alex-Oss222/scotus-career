## Highway easement: actual instrument and limits of its proof

**Authority, parties, date, and consideration.** The grant was executed May 8, 1970 by the United States through James R. Keaton, Superintendent of the Fort Berthold Agency, Bureau of Indian Affairs, Department of the Interior. It invokes Secretary's Order No. 2508 (14 F.R. 258), 10 BIAM 3 (34 F.R. 637), the Act of February 5, 1948 (62 Stat. 17; 25 U.S.C. §§ 323–328), and Part 161 of Title 25 C.F.R. The grantee is the North Dakota State Highway Department. The instrument acknowledges receipt of $5,546.50 and other good and valuable consideration. Its stated purpose is an easement for a right-of-way for the realignment and improvement of North Dakota State Highway No. 8 across described lands in Dunn County. Respondents' merits App. 1, 4–5.

**Land and geographic scope.** The instrument expressly applies to trust lands only and excludes non-trust property. It lists particular tracts and allotment identifiers, describes approximately 6.59 miles of route, and incorporates attached map/plats for variable widths and particulars. The map/plats are not reproduced with the five-page appendix instrument. Its header lists “TRIBAL: 0.00,” “INDIVIDUALLY OWNED: 87.48,” and “GOVERNMENT OWNED: 0.00.” The header should not be converted into a finding of the exact accident parcel's ownership, or into an assumption that all underlying land is collectively owned tribal land. The route description and trust-only term are express. Respondents' merits App. 1–3.

**Duration, existing rights, and termination.** The easement is subject to valid existing rights or adverse claims. It has no limitation as to tenure **so long as it is actually used for the stated purpose**. The United States grantor may terminate it in whole or in part on thirty days' written notice, if the grantee fails within that notice period to correct the basis for termination. Specified causes are: failure to comply with a grant term or applicable regulation; consecutive two-year nonuse for the granted purpose; abandonment; and failure, on completing construction, to file the affidavit of completion required by 25 C.F.R. § 161.16. These are express qualifications on tenure, not findings that any termination condition occurred. App. 3.

**Reserved crossings.** Indian landowners, their lessees, successors, and assigns retain the right to construct crossings wherever reasonably necessary to the undisturbed use and occupancy of the affected premises. Those crossings are to be constructed and maintained by the owners or lawful occupants and users at their own risk, with full responsibility for avoiding or repairing right-of-way damage caused by the crossings. The conditions bind and benefit the grantee's heirs, representatives, successors, and assigns. App. 3–4. The crossings reservation is distinct from the United States grantor's termination power; neither should be silently erased from a characterization of the grant.

**Public use, maintenance, and exclusion.** The briefs describe Highway 8 as a public highway maintained and controlled by North Dakota; respondents add that it provides access to Lake Sakakawea, controlled by the Army Corps of Engineers. The maintenance/public-road character is a factual recitation in the briefs and opinions, not additional quoted language in the instrument's purpose clause. Respondents' merits 1 n.1, 31–35; petitioners' merits 2–3, 19–21; JA 92–94. The instrument contains no express clause allocating exclusive regulatory or adjudicatory jurisdiction. Respondents' proposition that the right-of-way eliminated the Tribe's right to exclude motorists and should be treated like alienated fee land is a **legal argument about the grant's effect**, not the text of an express conveyance of every governmental power. Petitioners dispute that legal consequence and rely on the continued trust status and limited easement. Whether the Tribe has general authority to exclude public motorists is thus not answered merely by labeling the instrument an easement; the actual reservations and parties' opposing legal positions must be retained.

**Consent and compensation.** Petitioners and respondents discuss the statutory authorization in § 323, the consent requirements of § 324, and compensation under § 325. Respondents assert the required consent and compensation were obtained; petitioners do not claim that this easement was invalidly issued for failure to comply with those requirements. Petitioners' merits 19–21; respondents' merits 33–35. The instrument itself proves an acknowledged payment and the described grant. A separate signed consent document is not reproduced in this recovered core set. That documentary limit supplies no premise that consent was absent. Nor should the tribal-land and individually owned land consent provisions be flattened into one undifferentiated statutory condition if their exact operation becomes material. No new conclusion about statutory noncompliance follows from these filings.

## Collision, parties, contract, and disputed facts

The collision occurred November 9, 1990, on Highway 8 north of Twin Buttes within the Fort Berthold Reservation. Gisela Fredericks and Lyle Stockert were non-Indians and not members of the Three Affiliated Tribes. Stockert drove a gravel truck for A-1 Contractors, a non-Indian business based outside the reservation; the sources describe him as an employee and part owner. Fredericks was the widow of a tribal member and mother of five adult sons who are tribal members. Her injuries required twenty-four days' hospitalization. JA 20–21, 28, 42–43, 55, 92–94; petitioners' merits 2–3.

**Contract established; connection at the instant of collision unresolved.** A-1 had a $12,490 subcontract with LCM Corporation, a wholly tribal-owned corporation, for excavation, berming, and recompacting at a reservation community-center project. All work called for by that subcontract was on the reservation. The tribal complaint alleged work on November 9 and hauling within the reservation. JA 7–8; petitioners' merits 2–3; JA 92–93. This consensual relationship exists independently of the stronger disputed proposition that Stockert was performing it when the vehicles collided.

The tribal trial court expressly said no affidavits had been submitted: it had a bare record and relied on pleadings and briefs, treating facts as undisputed **for purposes of the dismissal motion**. JA 20–21. Its later description of a tort arising from A-1's reservation business, and the tribal appellate court's statement that A-1 was hauling gravel under the contract, must be read with that procedural record. JA 23–24, 28. The federal district court also recited that A-1 was performing the contract when the accident happened. JA 55. The en banc majority expressly said the record did not clearly show whether Stockert was then engaged in contract work; the record contained allegations rather than proof, and the district court's findings rested on pleadings, not evidence. JA 92–93 n.1. Judge Gibson's dissent inferred that no other plausible reason for the truck's presence appeared; this is a dissenting inference, not proof of the work connection. JA 122–123. Petitioners' reply 16 n.9 itself acknowledges that uncertainty about Stockert's work could matter both to merits questions and to a jurisdictional test keyed to tribal interests. The sources do not establish a specific load, trip assignment, return route, or completed delivery at the collision time.

**Contract terms require attribution.** The en banc majority mentioned a claimed Utah-law/forum provision but expressly declined to give the contract controlling weight because it was not part of the appellate record. JA 111 n.5. Respondents say a contract copy accompanied Fredericks' tribal-court opposition and rely on the provision as evidence against consent to this forum. Respondents' merits 40 n.27. Petitioners' reply 13 n.7 argues that even assuming the contract were properly considered, its clause concerns contract disputes, not Fredericks' independent tort claim. The district court separately recites the Tribe's reliance on contract obligations concerning tribal building, employment-rights, and other applicable tribal law. JA 61. These are attributed recitations and arguments, not a fully authenticated contract-based waiver of or submission to jurisdiction over every third-party tort.

**Residence: pleading admission and later dispute.** A-1 and Stockert's own federal complaint, paragraph 7, describes Fredericks as a non-Indian residing on the Fort Berthold Reservation; the tribal defendants' amended answer, paragraph 7, admits it. JA 42, 48. The tribal trial court treated reservation residence as undisputed for the motion; the tribal appellate court described forty years' residence. JA 20–21, 28. The district court later recognized that her residence had been disputed in the federal proceeding, but held it immaterial to the grounds on which it upheld jurisdiction. JA 55–58. It granted the tribal defendants' motion to strike exhibits and did not resolve the disputed residence by a new evidentiary finding. JA 64–66. Respondents' merits 1 n.2 calls residence unresolved; petitioners' reply 10 n.4 points back to respondents' own pleading. The en banc majority recites reservation residence while recounting the district court's treatment of the dispute. JA 93, 96. Do not transform the pleading admission into a never-contested historical fact, or transform the later dispute into a finding of nonresidence. Residence also does not make Fredericks a tribal member.

**Accident fault remains unadjudicated.** Respondents' account says Fredericks drove southbound in the northbound lane and Stockert braked and swerved; petitioners reply that this omits her position that she was turning left toward a driveway. Federal complaint, JA 43; respondents' merits 1; reply 1 n.1. The jurisdiction litigation did not try these competing negligence accounts.

## Procedural posture, issue scope, and remedies

Fredericks' tribal action was brought in May 1991. It included her injury claim, adult sons' consortium claims, claims against the insurer, and punitive relief. The tribal court denied the jurisdictional dismissal motion September 4, 1991. The tribal appellate court affirmed the jurisdiction ruling January 8, 1992, rejected the asserted timeliness/interlocutory impediment to that appeal, and returned remaining matters including consortium and insurer issues for further proceedings. JA 5–19, 20–37.

The parties stipulated February 3, 1992 to dismiss the insurer without prejudice; the order is dated February 4. JA 38–40. Petitioners' petition 5 n.4 and merits 4 n.1 say the punitive claim was dismissed with the insurer. The insurer-dismissal order itself should not be represented as expressly adjudicating every punitive theory against every remaining defendant.

A-1 and Stockert filed the federal action February 5, 1992, seeking a declaration that the tribal court lacked jurisdiction and injunctions against continuation of the tribal case and assertion of jurisdiction, together with costs and fees. JA 41–45. This is a federal jurisdiction challenge, not removal of the negligence case and not a federal damages adjudication. Federal-question jurisdiction under § 1331 and completion of the required tribal-court exhaustion were not disputed in the appellate posture. The district court entered summary judgment against the federal challengers September 16, 1992 and dismissed their action with prejudice. JA 54–67. The November 29, 1994 divided panel decision affirming that result was vacated on rehearing; it is not an extant second judgment sustaining jurisdiction. The en banc court reversed February 16, 1996, 76 F.3d 930, by an 8–4 division.

The appeal concerns **tribal subject-matter jurisdiction over Fredericks' claim**. It does not present a personal-jurisdiction question; the adult sons' consortium claims were not decided below and were not within the appeal. JA 96 n.3; petition 7 n.8; petitioners' merits 5 n.3. Their membership and asserted injuries may appear in arguments about tribal interests, but there is no adjudicated consortium jurisdiction holding to transfer to this appeal.

**Limited consent, not general immunity surrender.** The tribal defendants' amended answer allows their participation to defend the claim for declaratory relief regarding tribal civil jurisdiction, while expressly withholding consent to other causes, claims, issues, relief, or liability then or later. JA 49. Tribal Council Resolution 92-025-JJR, adopted March 20, 1992, confines its authorization to protecting, presenting, and asserting the Tribe's jurisdiction in this specified federal action; it excludes other claims and issues. JA 50–53. The district and en banc opinions describe a limited defense of federal injunctive claims. JA 55, 95. Preserve that difference in wording. Neither the primary documents nor the jurisdictional outcome supports a general waiver for all coercive relief or a new independent immunity holding.

Petitioners request reversal; respondents request affirmance and a remand for declaratory and injunctive relief. Petitioners' merits 30; reply 20; respondents' merits 45. A protective state action, No. 96-C-41, was filed; petitioners say it was needed to avoid the six-year limitations period. Respondents' merits 43–44 and reply 6 n.2. Thus neither absence of a state filing nor expiration of all state remedies is established. The jurisdiction dispute leaves negligence, damages, and unresolved tribal claims untried.

## Petitioners' principal arguments

1. **Territorial sovereignty and the limited conveyance.** Petitioners rely on retained tribal sovereignty over reservation territory and affairs absent congressional withdrawal. They distinguish alienated fee land from land still held in trust and distinguish an easement for highway purposes from a conveyance of every governmental power. Section 323 authorizes the grant but does not expressly withdraw jurisdiction. They invoke the consent provisions, retained trust ownership, and the instrument's limited purpose. Petitioners' merits 6–21; reply 7–10.
2. **Adjudication and regulation are different powers.** Petitioners read *Montana* in its fee-land/regulation setting, and rely on *Williams*, *Merrion*, *National Farmers*, and *Iowa Mutual* for retained authority, the role of tribal courts, and the absence of a general rule excluding civil suits involving non-Indians. Concurrent adjudication does not itself subject motorists to inconsistent substantive commands; a court can apply another sovereign's law. Their reply distinguishes the contacts needed to adjudicate a dispute from those needed to apply a particular sovereign's substantive law. Petitioners' merits 6–18, 24–30; reply 11–16. These are arguments for the scope of the cited decisions, to be considered against the entering law.
3. **Both Montana exceptions in the alternative.** A-1 voluntarily undertook substantial reservation work for a tribal corporation, and a foreseeable vehicle accident associated with that activity need not arise strictly within the four corners of the contract. The Tribe also has interests in road safety, resident families, and effects of serious injury on tribal members and services. Petitioners resist treating the missing evidence about Stockert's exact assignment as grounds for denying all adjudicatory power. Petitioners' merits 21–30; reply 13–16. Their reply acknowledges that an interest-based test could make that work uncertainty jurisdictionally material.
4. **Tribal law claims jurisdiction subject to federal limits.** The tribal code provisions quoted in the lower opinions reach reservation territory including fee land, easements, and rights-of-way, persons entering/residing/doing business, and civil claims arising within the boundaries. The territorial clause remains subject to restrictions imposed by the federal Constitution and laws. Quoted long-arm provisions include business activity, tortious conduct, and property relationships. JA 21–23, 33–35, 62–63. A tribal code assertion of power is not proof that federal law permits its full asserted reach. Petitioners add that the code permits state-law stipulations where tribal law is absent and that eligible jurors include residents as well as enrolled members. Merits 26 n.18; reply 17 n.10.
5. **Remedy and forum.** State-court availability does not itself extinguish concurrent tribal authority. Protective resort to the state court does not concede exclusive state jurisdiction. General claims about possible tribal-court bias are disputed and are not findings about this tribunal. Petitioners rely on the Indian Civil Rights Act, federal policy supporting tribal courts, and available review/comity arguments. Merits 24–30; reply 16–20.

## Respondents' principal arguments and the treaty response

1. **Nonmember authority and Montana.** Respondents argue that tribal power over nonmembers is implicitly limited and that *Montana* governs adjudicatory as well as regulatory authority. They read *National Farmers* and *Iowa Mutual* as exhaustion decisions, not independent grants of ultimate jurisdiction. They argue that retained tribal membership powers and nonmember territorial contacts are not interchangeable. Respondents' merits 3–14, 23–31.
2. **Public road and exclusion.** Respondents contend that a public state highway right-of-way eliminates the exclusion predicate supporting the landowner/regulatory cases and should be treated like alienated land for this issue. They invoke *Bourland* and other highway decisions, including the treatment of an earlier *Swift* decision after mootness, while petitioners dispute their readings and the relevance of fee-land analogies. Respondents' merits 23–35; petitioners' merits 19–21; reply 7–10. Any application of these authorities must use the supplied entering law rather than assume that a party's historical description supplies current controlling law.
3. **Neither exception.** Respondents say their commercial relation with LCM was not a consensual relation with Fredericks or her sons, the tort did not arise from that contract, the record does not prove contemporaneous contract work, and the asserted contract choice-of-law/forum provision cuts against tribal consent. They distinguish ordinary roadway negligence from a demonstrable serious threat to tribal political integrity, economic security, health, or welfare. They argue that a forum's general institutional interest, membership of an adult plaintiff's children, and state-forum availability do not establish the required tribal impact. Merits 35–44.
4. **Treaties and historical agreements.** Respondents invoke the 1825 Arikara, Mandan, and Minnetaree treaties: safe conduct for persons authorized to travel by the United States (Article V), and provisions against private revenge for injuries, directing complaints to the superintendent/agent and providing federal punishment or specified indemnification (Article VI). They also invoke the 1851 treaty's road and depredation provisions, and the 1866 Fort Berthold agreement concerning offenders, punishment, and compensation. Respondents disclose that the 1866 agreement was not ratified, although appropriations followed. They use *Mayfield* and other historical material to argue that federal protection/redress displaced the asserted tribal authority over non-Indians, and deny that modern court funding delegated the missing power. Merits 14–23, including n.12. These are treaty/statutory arguments, not an established grant in this record of exclusive state jurisdiction.
5. **Petitioners' procedural and substantive response to the treaty theory.** Petitioners say this theory was first raised in respondents' merits brief, was neither presented below nor exhausted in tribal court, and should not be reached under *National Farmers*, *Iowa Mutual*, and ordinary appellate issue limits. They also argue that provisions about criminal punishment, depredations, and federal protection do not withdraw concurrent civil adjudication of this private negligence action; they resist inferring loss of inherent authority merely from historical nonuse. Reply 5–7. This preservation objection is a party position requiring a disposition if relied on, not an already adjudicated bar.

## Specific residual limits of the recovered materials

- The grant instrument is available and its operative terms can be stated directly. Referenced map/plats and a separate signed consent instrument are not reproduced in this core set; their absence is not a fact of invalid issuance.
- The A-1/LCM relationship is established, but Stockert's exact work assignment and the truck's trip purpose at the instant of collision remain unproved in the described record. No witness trial or negligence determination resolves that uncertainty.
- The actual contract is described but was not accepted as part of the appellate record; quoted or characterized terms must remain attributed accordingly.
- The residence pleading admission, provisional tribal assumptions, and later federal dispute all coexist. The federal district court treated the controversy as immaterial and did not resolve it by taking evidence.
- The jurisdictional appeal does not decide personal jurisdiction, the adult sons' undecided consortium claims, ultimate tort liability, damages, a general sovereign-immunity waiver, or all potential state remedies.
