## 1. Foreman v. Dallas County — Nos. 96-987 and 96-1389

### Components and sufficient proposed grounds

**F1 — Two direct appeals.** Section 1253 permits qualifying interlocutory injunction appeals; nonfinality alone is not a defect. The subsequently entered final dismissal absorbs the same requested prospective-relief controversy for review. Dismiss No. 96-987 as superseded by the appeal from final judgment, under Shaffer v. Carter's merger principle, while exercising direct review in No. 96-1389. This does not characterize the initial order as a temporary restraining order or announce that §1253 requires finality. No separate surviving injunction issue is established.

**F2 — Covered kind of practice.** A changed formula selecting precinct officials who supervise voting on election day is a practice with a direct voting connection. Discretion under a precleared statute supplies no categorical exemption. Presley distinguishes ordinary road-government powers; Allen and Hampton County protect actual election administration; Monterey and Young now foreclose a blanket exemption for local administration or discretionary implementation. The Court can reject the erroneous exemption without declaring that the present method differs from the statutory baseline.

**F3 — Submission scope.** Preclearance of the 1985 recodification and its identified appointment timing/duration changes did not, on this supplied submission record, approve the unmentioned county partisan formulas. McCain, Clark, Monterey and Young require identification of the change actually submitted. The Attorney General need not reconstruct every possible discretionary arrangement from general enabling text. This neither rescinds the submitted code's approval nor holds that a sufficiently identified local implementation can never be precleared with a state enactment.

**F4 — Baseline and remedy.** Vacate the final dismissal in No. 96-1389 and remand for the actual November 1, 1972 appointment practice, comparison with the challenged procedures, and any genuinely established subsequent approval. A covered legally relevant change lacking preclearance cannot be administered prospectively; that consequence depends on findings not yet supplied. The Court therefore orders neither immediate universal nonuse nor continuation by permission of an established unprecleared practice. The local court determines coverage/submission and lawful relief; it cannot decide purpose/effect in place of D.D.C. or the Attorney General. No completed election, damages, appointment cancellation, new partisan formula or new hearing irrespective of need is ordered.

### Rival paths and limiting answers

The strongest affirmance path treats selecting appointed personnel as internal government organization under Presley, or treats the recodified grant of discretion as the submitted practice. It fails at the present legal-dismissal stage because these officials administer the act of voting and the challenged formulas were not identified. Presley protects a voting/governing boundary, not immunity for every appointed official. Young's submission rule gives the actual description controlling work.

The strongest immediate-injunction path argues that every post-1983 adjustment necessarily changed a postcoverage practice and should stop pending approval. The missing 1972 practice prevents the necessary comparison; neither the date of a later formula nor an inadequate submission supplies the absent benchmark. Correcting the legal exemptions and returning the baseline question preserves both §5's nonuse duty and the factual predicates for relief. Purpose and effect remain distinct from coverage.

## 2. Pounders v. Watson — No. 96-1383

### Petition and merits stages remain separate

The matter arrives as a pending certiorari petition under §1254(1), not an already granted merits case. The served two-day sentence and identified potential professional consequences preserve the controversy on the supplied record. No actual disciplinary outcome is assumed. A grant would cover the petition's summary-contempt due-process question and its related former-§2254(d) issue, with the latter corrected for the Ninth Circuit's express nondecision of willfulness.

**P1 — Petition management.** Stevens and Ginsburg would deny; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer would grant. Their grounds are separately recorded below. A grant is a stage change, not a merits vote. None proposes a new question, CVSG, reconstruction of an absent filing, or an unexplained GVR.

**P2 — Summary-process ground if review is granted.** Six Associates support a reasoned summary reversal of the ground on which the Ninth Circuit required a writ, followed by remand for the claims that court expressly left unresolved. A state trial judge's power to address observed, prejudicial disobedience during a continuing jury trial is not confined to physical stoppage, insulting behavior, a lengthy pattern or independently demonstrated future repetition. On the supplied record, the second punishment question followed the immediate admonition against the first. Protecting the jury's lawful consideration is part of protecting the trial's orderly administration. The appellate court gave insufficient legal weight to that observed interference and treated the absence of extended or prospective disruption as dispositive in this setting.

This is an assessment of the reasoning actually used; it does not falsely recite a categorical rule the Ninth Circuit expressly announced. The six do not adopt a universal permission to punish any in-court act summarily. The direct-contempt exception remains narrow: an observed violation materially threatening the ongoing judicial process, with its essential elements within the judge's knowledge, must justify use of the exceptional procedure. A short sanction alone, judicial irritation or a conclusory contempt label is insufficient. No new checklist, presumption of necessity or requirement of physical interruption is adopted.

**P3 — Notice, intent and factual review.** Reversal of that process ground does not resolve whether Watson knew a sufficiently definite order or willfully disobeyed it. The Ninth Circuit expressly left those claims undecided. The trial judge's findings exist, but the Court must not describe them as findings already accepted by the appellate court. Former §2254(d), applicable after Lindh, supplies its qualifying factual presumption and exceptions; it does not make the constitutional permissibility of summary adjudication a binding historical fact. Remand applies that distinction to the genuinely remaining claims, without presuming an exception, requiring an evidentiary hearing, relitigating every fact automatically or declaring the contempt judgment finally immune from habeas relief. A factual defense requiring evidence outside the judge's observation must receive its proper due-process assessment; merely labeling a disputed observed inference factual does not decide that assessment.

**P4 — Precise remedy.** Reverse the appellate summary-process holding and its dependent direction to grant the writ, and remand to the Ninth Circuit for the unaddressed notice/willfulness claims and lawful further disposition. Do not order Watson to serve a new term, repeat a completed sentence, stand trial for a new contempt, or undergo an automatic disciplinary disposition. The mandate does not direct a new contempt prosecution. No separately preserved judicial-bias claim is established.

**P5 — Teague and temporal law.** All eight apply Lindh's governing result to this pre-AEDPA noncapital application. None rests a present disposition on a newly supplied Teague defense. The three recovered petition-stage filings and reproduced orders contain none; preservation in unrecovered briefing is unknown, not disproved. Schiro permits discretionary refusal of a late nonjurisdictional defense and Caspari's consistently preserved-defense predicate is not established here. Declining to initiate that additional defense on this record is not a categorical waiver holding. No finality date or new-rule determination is invented; both contemporary exceptions remain intact.

### Alternative-path comparison

**Supported correction path:** Oliver and Cooke preserve a narrow direct-contempt exception. Wilson shows that intentional conduct can obstruct an ongoing trial without noise or violence; Sacher shows that protective delay in punishment does not itself extinguish summary power. The strongest objection is that neither case dispenses with a serious institutional need, and prejudicial questions could be addressed by instruction followed by ordinary proceedings. The six answer that the real-time duty includes safeguarding the jury's decision process, that a renewed question after the immediate admonition is materially different from McConnell's unfulfilled announcement, and that a later instruction and continuation do not prove the interruption unnecessary. The judge's prejudice assessment is relevant, not an independently established jury mental state. The preserved notice/intent claims prevent the correction from becoming automatic approval of the entire contempt judgment.

**Supported denial/affirmance path:** Stevens and Ginsburg regard the Ninth Circuit's disposition as a permissible contextual application of the narrow exception, with too little immediate necessity shown to justify summary reversal. Cooke, Oliver and Harris require more than personal observation and ordinary authority to punish; two questions followed by corrective instruction and continued examination can be addressed through a hearing. They acknowledge Wilson's nonphysical-obstruction principle and Sacher's timing rule, but distinguish an actual refusal that thwarted necessary trial proof and a sustained campaign of disobedience from this bounded questioning episode. They do not adopt a minimum-number rule, treat delay alone as controlling or find Watson blameless. The strongest objection is that their approach undervalues irreparable jury prejudice; they answer that a permissible punishment ground does not itself establish why ordinary safeguards were inadequate.

**Plenary-review alternative:** Full briefing remains lawful if review is taken and a majority cannot support summary merits resolution. Stevens and Ginsburg prefer it to summary reversal; the six regard the identified legal correction and express reservation of unaddressed claims as sufficiently bounded for summary action. Neither conditional preference supplies a present second grant vote or a predicted future merits judgment. Rule-of-Four and participating-majority requirements must be applied separately in assembly.
