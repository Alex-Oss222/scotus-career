## Questions, record and lower reasoning

The questions are whether this worker's employment connection permits a reasonable finding of Jones Act seaman status and whether the final LHWCA proceeding precludes his Jones Act claim. Harbor Tug seeks reversal; Papai seeks restoration of the jury route recognized below. The two questions are independent possible grounds. Status may be dispositive without deciding preclusion; no entering authority requires a universal status-first sequence.

Papai obtained short maritime assignments through the Inland Boatmen's Union hiring hall. Harbor Tug hired him for one day to finish painting the housing of its docked tug Pt. Barrow; he reported to a dockside port captain and was not expected to sail. He alleges a moving ladder caused his knee injury. He had worked for Harbor Tug twelve times in the preceding two and one-half months. The lower majority characterizes those prior jobs as deckhand engagements; the separate control-extracted recitation identifies twelve assignments and describes his general deckhand work as handling lines while vessels docked or undocked. These descriptions do not establish twelve sea voyages or the precise duties/duration of every assignment. His general hiring-hall work included maintenance, longshoring and deckhand tasks; most assignments lasted three days or less, with the longest approximately forty days. No common ownership or control of all hiring-hall vessels is established.

The District Court entered partial summary judgment on the Jones Act and unseaworthiness theories, relying on lack of a sufficiently permanent vessel connection; later proceedings left a judgment for Harbor Tug after trial of the remaining claims. Joanna Papai also asserted consortium. The present status inquiry does not itself decide negligence, causation, damages or the ultimate disposition of every related claim.

The lower majority (Takasugi, with Reinhardt) treated the entire employment circumstances as relevant. It reasoned that employers obtaining labor from a common hall could be treated as a common employer for status purposes, so genuine seagoing work would not lose protection merely because of daily assignments. Independently, the twelve Harbor Tug jobs might establish sufficient connection. It held genuine factual issues precluded summary judgment. Poole dissented: an identifiable fleet requires common ownership or control, and the hiring-hall approach would collapse the distinction between crew members and transitory maritime workers. He considered the record legally insufficient even when properly assessed.

The LHWCA proceeding was an adjudication, not merely voluntary payments. The lower opinion reports a June 2, 1992 hearing, an August 27, 1992 compensation award, no appeal, and finality. It took judicial notice of the order; Papai challenged notice, but did not dispute its existence or the supplied copy's authenticity. Poole quotes the ALJ's classification of the jobs as random, sporadic and transitory. That is an administrative determination, not a new preflight finding. The majority expressly rejected preclusion, extending Gizoni's credit/election-of-remedies reasoning: litigation should not immunize a resisting employer while voluntary payment does not. The packet's earlier claim that the issue was merely presented is therefore incomplete.

## Balanced analytical paths

1. **Reverse on status:** Define an identifiable fleet by a meaningful common operational relationship, reject unrelated hiring-hall assignments as sufficient by themselves, and determine whether the Harbor Tug-specific record supports only transitory dockside work. Strongest objection: the lower majority found evidence of twelve deckhand jobs and genuine employment-relationship disputes; summary judgment must not demand unsupported permanent assignment or resolve favorable inferences against Papai. Short tenure alone does not answer actual crew service or changed duties.
2. **Affirm or remand for status determination:** Treat repeated Harbor Tug engagements or the demonstrated working relationship as sufficient to create a triable substantial-connection issue, without finding ultimate status. A broader route would accept a functionally organized hiring-hall group. Strongest objection: a labor pool does not necessarily connect its members to an identifiable vessel group, and generic deckhand work may not show the necessary nature of this employment. Any remand must specify which supported factual dispute matters; no new evidence is presumed.
3. **Decide preclusion if necessary:** Harbor Tug may rely on the final litigated award and identity of the seaman-status issue. Papai may contest the statutory preclusive effect despite finality, the adequacy/identity of the determination under the governing status standard, and the election-of-remedies consequence. Gizoni's unadjudicated-payment holding alone resolves neither path. A final determination on preclusion that depends on the ALJ's complete issue definition, record or litigation opportunity requires the full administrative order and pertinent litigation material; the lower opinion establishes the reported award and its rationale, not every preclusion predicate. A status disposition may reserve this independent question expressly.

These are supported alternatives for independent analysis, not purported additional filed arguments or predicted votes.
