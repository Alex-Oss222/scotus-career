## Supported record and lower reasoning

The full Second Circuit merits and rehearing opinions supply the reported allegations and procedural facts. The complaints allege that Bosnian Croat and Muslim victims suffered killing, rape, forced prostitution and impregnation, torture and other atrocities during the Bosnian conflict; Karadzic personally planned or directed a campaign aimed at destroying the ethnic/religious groups and commanded the forces carrying it out. He headed the self-proclaimed Srpska government. The complaints alternatively allege official capacity or concerted action with Yugoslavia and Serbia. These are pleading-stage allegations accepted for the jurisdictional appeal, not findings that each plaintiff proved genocide intent, a conflict nexus, protected civilian status, defendant responsibility or state involvement.

Plaintiffs invoked the ATS, TVPA, §1331 and supplemental jurisdiction, seeking compensation, punitive relief and fees; one action also sought an injunction. The district court bypassed the originally briefed service and personal-jurisdiction issues and dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, treating state action as necessary for international-law violations and Srpska as failing the state definition. It did not consider the alternative concerted-action allegations. It also rejected the TVPA theory for absent state action and declined supplemental jurisdiction after dismissing federal claims.

The Second Circuit reversed and remanded. It distinguished prohibitions binding individuals—genocide and war crimes—from official torture or summary execution unconnected to those offenses. It allowed proof of governmental attributes despite nonrecognition, or of concerted action with an existing state. It treated contemporary established international norms as ascertainable from judicial decisions, state practice and public-law sources, rather than assuming all international norms have identical personal scope. It reserved whether Protocol II had acquired the necessary customary status and whether the international-conflict conventions applied on the alternative theories. Its suggestion that official authority's semblance may suffice is a lower-court view for consideration, not an adopted Supreme Court element.

On service, the Kadic papers were delivered through a security officer under an alternate-service order and Karadzic admitted receipt. In Doe the affidavits describe an attempted hand delivery in a hotel lobby and interception by guards, with a distance discrepancy; efficacy remains subject to proof. The reported locations were outside the U.N. headquarters district. The court rejected a general invitee immunity under the Headquarters Agreement or federal common law and reserved a possible narrow direct-transit immunity, which it found factually inapplicable. It did not create a general immunity exception for all ATS suits.

The State Department's March 24, 1993 letter stated that U.N. invitee status supplied no jurisdictional immunity. The United States' Statement of Interest, signed by the Solicitor General and State Department Legal Adviser, supported individual applicability of the genocide/war-crime norms and expressly disclaimed a political-question obstacle to these suits. These positions are established through the opinion's published quotation/recitation; the original letter and statement were not separately recovered. The opinion reports no present executive recognition or immunity determination in Karadzic's favor. A conjectural future one cannot be treated as an existing fact.

The appellate court applied Baker's issue-specific political-question approach. It found judicial standards available and no identified political-branch conflict requiring dismissal. Act of state had not been asserted below and was not before it. No more suitable forum was identified; its discussion of the former Yugoslav courts addressed that record, not a permanent worldwide forum finding. Section 1331 as an independent basis for every customary-law claim remained undecided. Rehearing rejected an asserted historical restriction of §1350 to a narrow prize-law class; it added no merits facts.

## Entering law, norms and action distinctions

[Reading slice](../entering-law/OT_1996CHUNK7_73_READING.md) contains mechanically copied register provisions, selected Holdings, and verbatim current neutral-projection sections. It is a reading copy, not independent authority.

Section 1350 grants jurisdiction over an alien's civil tort action for a violation of the law of nations or a United States treaty. **Abebe-Jira, January 14, 1997**, now controls the separation between statutory forum, actionable international norm, domestic action, personal liability and relief. All nine supported jurisdiction over substantial official-torture allegations and rejected a categorical foreign-setting political-question bar. Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, with Stone-Zsela, recognized a confined received domestic action for deliberate personal participation in or supervision of official torture. The norm requires severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment or intimidation. Its reception/common-law rationale is controlling at that scope; the decision expressly reserved private nonstate violence, a universal action for customary norms, and absent-superior liability. Compensation and independently justified aggravated-tort punishment were supported on proved personal torture, not merely title or pleading. Today's remand does not import those trial findings or awards.

**Atherton, January 14, 1997**, preserves source-specific limits on federal common law: general federal interests or desired uniformity do not alone create a federal duty. Abebe-Jira itself distinguishes O'Melveny on §1350's particular reception function; neither case automatically decides whether the additional norms here support the same kind of received action.

The strongest evidence for private genocide responsibility includes the Genocide Convention's definition and express coverage of private individuals. Genocide requires intent to destroy, in whole or part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such, through the defined destructive acts. The lower opinion quotes those acts and Article IV. The implementation statute criminalizes genocide under its own jurisdictional limits, while §1092 disclaims creation of an enforceable substantive or procedural right by that chapter. Whether a received ATS action already exists is distinct from using that criminal statute or treaty as an independent civil cause. The statutory no-new-right language is a real argument requiring treatment, not a finding that all prior remedies were repealed.

The strongest evidence for private war-crime responsibility includes the pre-divergence piracy/individual-duty cases, Yamashita's treatment of commanders' obligations and Common Article 3. That article binds parties to a noninternational armed conflict and protects persons taking no active part in hostilities against murder, mutilation, cruel treatment, torture, hostage-taking, degrading treatment and specified summary punishment. Its exact text and additional qualifications are in the lower source. The norm still requires the relevant conflict, victim and conduct predicates. Yamashita's military prosecution does not itself supply a federal civil action or a universal civil command-responsibility standard. The packet does not collapse deliberate direction, personal supervision, aiding conduct and mere command title.

The **TVPA** independently supplies its enacted action for defined torture and extrajudicial killing under actual or apparent authority or color of foreign law, with exhaustion of adequate available local remedies and a ten-year limitation. It does not erase its own official-action condition or conclusively settle an ATS claim based on a different norm. No finding is supplied that all its definitions and conditions have been met. A claim based on private torture unrelated to genocide or war crimes requires an independently supported rule; labels alone cannot substitute for that rule.

The copied Standards remain at their exact scopes: **Private enforcement of Adoption Assistance Act reasonable-efforts and reporting clauses**, Suter, March 25, 1992; **Culpable affirmative assistance to a securities fraud**, Central Bank, April 19, 1994; **Compensatory remedies for intentional Title IX discrimination**, Franklin, February 26, 1992; **Senate committee evidence-taking in an impeachment trial**, Nixon, January 13, 1993. Suter separates right and action; Central Bank supports a confined received-action analogy, not universal international accessory liability; Franklin presupposes an action and reserves punitive authority; Nixon supplies an issue-specific judicial-role analogy. Abebe-Jira directly updates their relevance here.

Baker's actual constitutional commitment, manageable standards and political-branch conflict inquiries govern justiciability. A tort dispute can implicate foreign affairs without itself deciding recognition, directing diplomacy or becoming categorically nonjusticiable. W. S. Kirkpatrick's act-of-state rule requires a necessary determination of an official sovereign act's validity; it is not a free-standing foreign embarrassment doctrine. Immunity, personal service and source of action remain separate even if justiciability is satisfied.

## Supported alternatives and relief

* Affirm the rejection of categorical nonstate/jurisdictional and political-question bars, with a norm-specific received-action analysis for genocide and war crimes, while preserving official-action requirements for ordinary torture and all proof, service and personal-responsibility requirements. Strongest objection: international prohibition of individual conduct does not by itself authorize domestic civil recovery; Abebe-Jira deliberately reserved these extensions.
* Preserve the forum and reject categorical political-question dismissal but decline or limit the additional received actions, remanding for the sufficiently alleged official-torture or enacted TVPA paths and any surviving supplemental claims. Strongest objection: the statute's reception rationale and well-defined individual wrongs may provide the source-specific justification; an official-action rule for torture does not justify requiring it for genocide or war crimes.
* Resolve a genuinely dispositive, preserved threshold obstacle if its facts and law are supported. The packet supplies no present immunity grant, no actual diplomatic conflict, no established alternate forum and no proved failure of service in both actions. Those cannot be invented to avoid the granted questions. A broad foreign-policy abstention holding must confront the actual executive position and Abebe-Jira's unanimous rejection of a categorical bar.

No path permits an immediate damages award, liability from command title alone, a universal action for all customary law, categorical invalidation of every immunity, or resolution of the unneeded treaty/§1331 questions. Remand may require proof and claim-specific decisions without treating the operator's unavailable filings as a litigant omission.
