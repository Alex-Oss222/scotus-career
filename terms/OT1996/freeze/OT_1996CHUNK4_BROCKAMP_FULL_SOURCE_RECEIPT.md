## Distinct records and requested relief

One Rule 12.4 petition, No. 95-1225, seeks review of two Ninth Circuit judgments entered October 5, 1995. Brockamp involves district-court summary judgment and an appellate reversal/remand for a triable incapacity issue. Scott involves findings after a bench trial and an appellate affirmance; the appellate incapacity review was for clear error. Treating both as merely allegations or both as established incapacity would conflate their postures. The United States requests reversal of both judgments. Respondents request affirmance in Scott and affirmance with district-court further proceedings in Brockamp. See US merits pp. 1–11, 41; respondents pp. 1–4, 25; petition appendices cited below.

**McGill/Brockamp.** McGill, age 93 in 1984, remitted $7,000 with an application to extend his 1983 return's filing time (Form 4868). The check itself did not identify its purpose or tax year. Sources describe April 15 or about April 16, 1984; no material issue turns on choosing between those dates. The remittance was held in suspense and later transferred to an excess-collections account. He never filed the 1983 return during his life. He died November 7, 1988; Brockamp became administrator January 4, 1989. Her refund letter was March 13, 1991; the estate's 1983 return was filed March 27, 1991 and showed $427 liability. The IRS denied refund August 8, 1991; the action was filed August 3, 1993. Her account of senile dementia and mistaken check amounts was declaration evidence, not an adjudicated finding that he was incompetent throughout the relevant time. Appellate remand leaves that factual issue unresolved. Pet. App. 1a–8a, 17a–30a; US merits pp. 4–9; respondents pp. 1–3. Do not convert the $7,000 remittance into a finding that no 1983 tax at all was due; the estate return's $427 must be retained.

**Live alternative deposit contention.** Brockamp also argued that the $7,000 was a returnable deposit rather than payment of tax, and thus outside these refund-payment limitation provisions. The district court rejected it, relying on the extension application and lack of evidence that the remittance was intended as a deposit, and discussing the absence of an assessment. The appellate majority did not reach that alternative because it remanded on tolling. The United States expressly acknowledges the unresolved appellate contention at merits pp. 5–6 n.2; respondents preserve its factual premise and district motion at pp. 1–2 n.2. Pet. App. 8a n.1 (dissent), 22a–24a. The neutral validator should preserve the issue in the remedial map, without treating an unadjudicated appellate alternative as either automatically successful or silently extinguished.

**Scott.** Estimated payments for 1984 totaled $30,096; his father made installments under a power of attorney, ending around December 1984/January 1985, and Scott made the final installment in January 1985. His late 1984 return, found filed November 29, 1989 in the trial findings, reported no tax liability and requested the overpayment. An introductory lower-court passage says November 28; the final factual findings supply November 29. The government's refund denial preceded this January 8, 1992 suit. The June 4, 1992 ruling denied the government's motion to dismiss on the proposed tolling theory. Following an October 5–7, 1993 trial, November 19, 1993 findings determined that alcoholism rendered Scott mentally incompetent and unable to file a return from January 1985 through early November 1989. Business transactions and the 1987–1988 incarceration did not defeat that finding; expert evidence and others' management of his affairs were considered. The court found no unfair governmental prejudice. Do not use the earlier dismissal opinion's preliminary factual assumptions to displace the later trial findings. Pet. App. 31a–76a; US merits pp. 10–11 and n.5; respondents pp. 3–4.

Scott's district judgment ordered refund of $30,096 with statutory interest under §6611 from the November 1989 return filing, rejecting his equitable request for earlier interest from the tax-payment date. The Ninth Circuit had a government appeal and taxpayer cross-appeal. The granted question presented by the United States is tolling the filing and amount limitations, not a free-standing invitation to rewrite the interest determination. The district court also described an extraordinary-circumstances/impossibility route to equitable relief, grounded in inability to file beyond the taxpayer's control; it did not create a separate nontax cause of action. Pet. App. 35a–59a. Preserve the legal availability issue separately from the actual incapacity finding.

## Statutory components and competing positions, as actually presented

The exact recovered text of §6511(a), (b)(1), (b)(2)(A)–(C), and §7422(a) is copied below from US merits pp. 2–4. Keep the following qualifications distinct: three years from return filing or two years from payment, whichever expires later; no-return two-year rule; separate stamp-tax rule; the bar on allowance absent a timely claim; three-year amount lookback plus an extension of time for filing the return; two-year amount lookback if the claim is not within the three-year period; and the separate no-claim allowance rule. A filing deadline and the amount recoverable are different statutory operations even where a party contends the same tolling should govern both.

**United States' argument.** The administrative claim requirement under §7422(a), the filing rules, repeated prohibition in §6511(b)(1), and separate amount limitations show a deliberate scheme of closure. Section 6514(a)(1)'s treatment of late refunds as erroneous and void reinforces that reading. Even if the §6511(a) filing period were tolled, the government says §6511(b)(2) independently leaves no recoverable amount because these payments fall outside either ordinary lookback. It calls this substantive repose rather than merely a filing deadline, analogizes Lampf's outside cutoff, and reads Dalm's rejection of untimely equitable recoupment as consistent with Irwin. Alternatively, it says Irwin's parity rationale does not extend to tax claims without private-defendant analogues. Its administrative argument invokes roughly 200 million returns annually (including several tax categories), stale evidence, and the uncertainty of retrospective incapacity adjudication. These are the government's legal/administrative submissions, not findings that every return generates a contested tolling case. US merits pp. 11–41.

**Respondents' argument.** Irwin supplies a general presumption once immunity is waived. Refund actions descend from private actions against collectors for money had and received, so the claimed absence of any private analogue is contested. Respondents argue that eliminating individual-collector suits in 1966 changed the defendant, not the action's equitable character. They read the filing and incorporated lookback periods together; if one is tolled, both should be tolled for the same time. Their legislative-history argument describes the lookback as preventing a small late payment from reopening a whole tax year, and as coordinating taxpayer refund rights with the government's assessment opportunity, rather than categorically excluding disability relief. They emphasize that failure to file can leave the government's assessment period open while disability prevents the taxpayer from seeking refund. Their proposed administrability safeguard is commensurate tolling of the assessment period whenever the taxpayer receives equitable tolling, reopening that tax year to governmental review. This is a requested judicial rule, not existing statutory text. Respondents pp. 5–25.

The parties' histories and characterizations conflict and must not be copied as settled law. In particular, respondents pp. 18–20 discuss earlier versions using return due dates and then characterize the 1958 amendment; the operative §6511(a) text copied here uses when the return **was filed**. Their footnotes rely on the Ninth Circuit's Miller late-return approach and distinguish Oropallo. Whether a late return activates a three-year claim period is a distinct statutory question; do not erase the lookback merely because a late return might satisfy a filing-period route. Respondents p. 13 invokes historical Lundy; the canonical simulated Lundy in the entering-law slice controls instead, with its own scope and express limits.

**Exceptions arguments.** The government cites §6511(d)'s specific extensions, including the seven-year bad-debt/worthless-security category and special carryback treatment, and §§1311–1314 mitigation of specified inconsistent tax treatment. The lower dissent also invokes the agreed assessment-extension mechanism in §6511(c). The government's point is that tailored exceptions rebut a general equitable power; the lower majority and respondents dispute that inference. No party establishes that either taxpayer meets one of these enumerated exceptions. No disposition here requires announcing a complete rule for every unused exception. If any specific exception becomes an operative ground, its complete operative text and qualifications must be obtained, rather than inferred from these briefs' examples. The reproduced text below is complete for the provisions it labels; it does not claim to reproduce all of §6511 or the mitigation subchapter.

**Precedent distinctions actually at issue.** Dalm addressed equitable recoupment asserted without independent timely refund jurisdiction; the Ninth Circuit and Scott district court distinguish tolling for inability to act and a claimant who lacked a prior effective opportunity. The government and dissent reject that distinction as an unauthorized equitable addition to the tax scheme. Lampf's outside cutoff and Oropallo's treatment of the tax lookback support the government's independent-repose theory; the contrary route contests the structural analogy and the premise that §6511(a) otherwise supplies no real limit. Irwin's rebuttable presumption is common ground as precedent but its coverage and rebuttal are contested. The predivision opinions supply existing law; later historical authorities must be screened against the simulation. Oropallo's per curiam panel included then-Judge Breyer, as the US merits brief expressly notes at p. 8; that is source-supported individual prior evidence, not a current-case vote prediction.

## Copied primary-source statutory passage

The following is mechanically copied from the July 1996 United States merits brief's statutory-provisions section; line wrapping, printed page numbers, and OCR are preserved. It is a date-eligible filing transcription, not a new official-code verification.

```text
STATUTORY PROVISIONS INVOLVED 


1. Section 6511 of the Internal Revenue Code, 26 
U.S.C. 6511, provides in relevant part: 


(a) Period of limitation on filing claim 


Claim for credit or refund of an overpayment of 
any tax imposed by this title in respect of which 
tax the taxpayer is required to file a return shall 
be filed by the taxpayer within 3 years from the 
time the return was filed or 2 years from the time 
the tax was paid, whichever of such periods 
expires the later, or if no return was filed by the 
taxpayer, within 2 years from the time the tax was 
paid. Claim for credit or refund of an overpayment 
of any tax imposed by this title which is required 
to be paid by means of a stamp shall be filed by the 
taxpayer within 3 years from the time the tax was 


paid. 


(b) Limitation on allowance of credits and 
refunds 


(1) Filing of claim within prescribed period 


No credit or refund shall be allowed or made 
after the expiration of the period of limitation 
prescribed in subsection (a) for the filing of a 
claim for credit or refund, unless a claim for 
credit or refund is filed by the taxpayer 
within such period. 


(2) Limit on amount of credit or refund 


(A) Limit where claim filed within 3- 
year period 


If the claim was filed by the taxpayer 
during the 3-year period prescribed in 
subsection (a), the amount of the credit or 
refund shall not exceed the portion of the 
tax paid within the period, immediately 
preceding the filing of the claim, equal to 
3 years plus the period of any extension 
of time for filing the return. If the tax 
was required to be paid by means of a 
stamp, the amount of the credit or refund 
shall not exceed the portion of the tax 
paid within the 3 years immediately 
preceding the filing of the claim. 


(B) Limit where claim not filed 
within 3-year period 


If the claim was not filed within such 
3-year period, the amount of the credit or 
refund shall not exceed the portion of the 
tax paid during the 2 years immediately 
preceding the filing of the claim. 


(C) Limit if no claim filed 


If no claim was filed, the credit or 
refund shall not exceed the amount which 
would be allowable under subparagraph 
(A) or (B), as the case may be, if claim 
was filed on the date the credit or refund 
is allowed. 


2. Section 7422(a) of the Internal Revenue Code, 26 
U.S.C. 7422(a), provides: 


(a) No suit prior to filing claim for refund 


No suit or proceeding shall be maintained in any 
court for the recovery of any internal revenue tax 
alleged to have been erroneously or illegally as- 
sessed or collected, or of any penalty claimed to 
have been collected without authority, or of any 
sum alleged to have been excessive or in any 
manner wrongfully collected, until a claim for 
refund or credit has been duly filed with the 
Secretary, according to the provisions of law in 
that regard, and the regulations of the Secretary 
established in pursuance thereof.
```
