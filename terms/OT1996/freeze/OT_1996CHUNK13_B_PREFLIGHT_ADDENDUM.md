## Printz and Mack — full lower-court scope and statute

The lower opinion, *Mack v. United States*, 66 F.3d 1025 (9th Cir. 1995), has been read through the majority, Fernandez's partial dissent and footnotes. Source: <https://openjurist.org/66/f3d/1025>. The enacted statute is separately verified against Public Law 103-159, 107 Stat. 1536, saved as `../sources/chunk13-b/brady-1993.pdf` and `.txt`. The official PDF's extracted text has OCR defects; no defective OCR spelling is a substitute for statutory text.

The two district courts invalidated §922(s)(2)'s mandatory inquiry but left the conditional explanation and record-destruction provisions standing; both severed rather than invalidated the entire Act. Both parties appealed. The Ninth Circuit reversed the anti-commandeering rulings and dismissed the sheriffs' severability appeals as moot. It also vacated competing rulings about the criminal penalty's application and remanded to dismiss the Fifth Amendment vagueness challenges as unripe, relying on the government's disavowal of prosecution and the absence of a realistic threat. The civil-proceeding vagueness theory had not been raised below. Mack's separate Thirteenth Amendment argument failed below because the duties attached to the office rather than compelled him personally to continue working. Fernandez joined those Fifth and Thirteenth Amendment dispositions but dissented on commandeering. Those distinct lower components are background, not silently reopened under the supplied grant limited to executive commands and their statutory consequences.

The Ninth Circuit majority distinguished commands to legislate or establish state policy from limited assistance in a federal program and read reasonable effort in light of available resources. Fernandez treated compulsory official research, deadlines, record disposal, restricted use and requested explanations as administration imposed without a state exit. The government argued alternatively for relief confined to these plaintiffs because neither action was a class action; the lower court did not decide that argument. Thus the remedy's scope is an independent issue, not automatic statewide invalidation.

The statutory duties must remain distinct. Section 922(s)(2) commands reasonable effort within five business days, including research in available state/local systems and a designated national system. The Act does not command an officer to send a prohibitory notice, arrest a purchaser or stop a transfer. Under §922(s)(6)(B), unless the officer determines the transaction unlawful, the officer must destroy the statement and derived/notice-created records within twenty business days after the purchaser's statement, disclose only to a person who needs the information to carry out the subsection, and use it only for that purpose. Under §922(s)(6)(C), if the officer finds the individual ineligible and that individual requests reasons, written reasons are due within twenty business days of the request. Those different triggers and clocks cannot be merged. The Act separately protects officials against the specified damages actions and permits designation of the CLEO's designee.

The dealer restrictions have their own alternatives: elapsed waiting period without adverse information or an earlier notice of no adverse information; an officer's recent written life-threat statement; a qualifying state permit issued within five years after the required eligibility check; qualifying state background-check law; the Secretary's approval under Internal Revenue Code §5812; or the Secretary's impracticability certification requiring the statutory low-officer-density, extreme-remoteness and absent-telecommunications conditions together. The existence of these provisions does not itself resolve the sheriffs' standing to invalidate them. The interim and permanent systems, and federal duties of private dealers, require separate severability and remedial analysis.

Lawful merits paths: extend New York's ordinary-commerce structural limitation to these mandatory executive tasks; distinguish limited assistance from transfer of federal regulatory responsibility; or distinguish particular commands, followed by an independently supported remedy. Neither low cost nor temporary duration alone resolves the structural question. Neither commerce authority over commercial firearm sales nor the Elections Clause holding in Voting Rights Coalition supplies an automatic answer about the chosen means. A saving construction must retain a textually permissible duty rather than replace 'shall' with a voluntary request. A broad individual Second Amendment ruling is outside these stated questions.

## Mussari and Schroeder — scope, elements and alternatives

The lower opinion, *United States v. Mussari*, 95 F.3d 787 (9th Cir. 1996), is verified at <https://openjurist.org/95/f3d/787>. It states the statutory text, both January 11, 1995 indictments, the distinct alleged beginning dates and the remand for trial. It recites the district court's objection that Congress cannot transform family nonpayment into interstate commerce merely through different residence, and the government's position that an already fixed obligation requires interstate satisfaction. The district court rejected reliance on AFDC fiscal effects and observed that the statute requires no flight or deliberate crossing. The circuit treated the fixed payment obligation as a thing in interstate commerce; this is the reasoning under review, not governing Supreme Court law. No flight element may be inserted to rescue the statute.

Official enacted source: Public Law 102-521, 106 Stat. 3403, saved as `../sources/chunk13-b/csra-1992.pdf` and `.txt`. Section 228(a) requires willful failure to pay a past-due support obligation with respect to a child residing in another State. Section 228(d)(1) requires an amount fixed by a court order or state-law administrative process for support of a child or the child and custodial parent, and then either unpaid longer than one year **or** greater than $5,000. The indictments allege both, but the statute does not require both. First offenses allow a fine, up to six months' imprisonment, or both; later offenses allow a fine, up to two years, or both. Section 228(c) separately requires restitution at sentencing equal to the then-existing past-due obligation. This pretrial constitutional review fixes no sentence, restitution amount or guilt.

Each prosecution must preserve the prohibition on punishment for pre-enactment conduct. Both charged periods begin before October 25, 1992; earlier arrears can identify an obligation but cannot themselves become completed federal offenses before enactment. The precise lawful restitution treatment of older arrears is not decided simply by that offense-date limitation. Willfulness, the amount/duration alternative and different residence remain proof questions. Neither inability to pay nor deliberate evasion is found here.

Available paths: sustain direct protection of the interstate payment obligation; reject that classification where a state domestic obligation and different residence do not establish a sufficient commercial object; or adopt a textually supported narrower application. *Lopez* bars the remote generic-effects chain and rewriting, while *Harris*, *Bishop/Stokes* and *Hale* preserve their distinct direct-instrumentality/integral-market grounds.
