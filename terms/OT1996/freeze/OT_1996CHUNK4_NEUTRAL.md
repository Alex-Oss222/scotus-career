## Supported record and distinctions

Facts and reasonable inferences favorable to the estate must be preserved. The district and appellate accounts do not justify simply treating all administrators as having identical personal conduct or knowledge.

Stephanie Uhlrig, a music/activity therapist, worked voluntarily at Topeka State Hospital. Kenneth Waddell had been found not guilty by reason of insanity after aggravated battery, first held at Larned and transferred to Topeka in April 1987. After a period in the forensic AWL unit, he spent more than a year in regular units. His unauthorized departure and a prosecutor's request for greater security led to his return to AWL; he later had a March 1991 altercation with staff. Budget reductions prompted closure of AWL in 1991. Dr. Mani Lee's memorandum cautioned that the unit served high-risk patients in a structured setting and needed careful phasing out. The appellate account attributes the closure decision to Harder and Vega and reports Vega's June 21 authorization to proceed expeditiously. Clinical personnel in the Patient Care Consultation Team, treatment team, and nursing staff made placement recommendations; none of the five defendants personally selected Waddell's destination. These distinctions matter to individual causation and culpability.

Waddell was placed in Rappaport South, raped and assaulted a patient, and was moved to Boisen South, where Uhlrig worked. Uhlrig had general warnings, safety training, access to Waddell's file, and a supervisor's specific discussion of his background and working or escorting him. The estate disputed that file access and entries established her understanding of the actual danger. Warnings are evidence bearing on the risk and officials' conduct; they are not an automatic constitutional waiver or categorical immunity for affirmative injury. On February 23, 1992, after a movie outing and return to the grounds, Waddell killed her. The injury did not occur off grounds.

The district court's individual account reports: Thompson became acting superintendent after the initial closure decision, had no authority independently to close/create a ward, relied on clinical staff, and did not diagnose Waddell; Harder's short tenure and budget role differed from later implementation; Whiteman was in the legislature at the initial decision; Vega became acting commissioner in June; Lee participated in budget discussions and warnings. ## Judgment below and its grounds

The district court granted summary judgment on absence of recklessness and, alternatively, qualified immunity. The Tenth Circuit affirmed on both constitutional sufficiency and immunity. Its five-part formulation required a limited, specifically definable group; substantial risk of serious, immediate and proximate harm caused by defendants; an obvious or known risk; reckless conscious disregard; and conduct viewed as a whole that shocks the conscience. That formulation is the lower court's rule being reviewed, not already this Court's binding test.

Crucially, the appellate court found a genuine issue concerning the defined group and defendants' awareness of risk. It expressly declined to affirm on a conclusive absence of awareness. It nevertheless found the record insufficient on substantial danger, recklessness and conscience-shocking conduct, emphasizing professional placement procedures, warnings, Waddell's prior time in regular units, and resource-allocation choices. It alternatively held no controlling precedent or clearly established weight of authority made the conduct unlawful. Its discussion of additional reply affidavits found them unnecessary to the result; it denied requested sanctions. The granted questions do not enlarge those procedural rulings into new claims.

## Standards and Tests selection: Voluntary public employment and substantive workplace safety duties

### Voluntary public employment and substantive workplace safety duties

**Governing question:** Whether a voluntary public-employment relationship creates a general substantive-due-process duty to provide workplace safety.

**Current rule:** Voluntary municipal employment does not itself create the custody or deprivation of liberty that supports affirmative constitutional duties of care, and substantive due process imposes no independent general obligation to provide such employees a workplace free from unreasonable risks. A state-law safety duty, even assuming it creates a protected liberty interest, does not convert its breach alone into a substantive constitutional wrong. The underlying federal right and constitutional arbitrariness remain distinct from state-law entitlement and municipal attribution.

**Authority by component:**

### Qualified-immunity legal appeals and factual sufficiency

**Governing question:** The boundary of immediate review under §1291 when qualified immunity is denied.

**Current rule:** Immediate review may address the separable legal question whether conduct on the relevant assumed facts violates clearly established law, including the legality of a categorical direct-evidence condition. ## Canonical Holdings selection: Collins v. City of Harker Heights, 503 U.S. 115 (1992)

#### Collins v. City of Harker Heights, 503 U.S. 115 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1279.
**Decided:** February 26, 1992.

##### Whether §1983 adds a freestanding governmental-power-abuse element for municipal employees

**Holding:** A municipal employee who establishes deprivation of a federal right under color of state law and municipal responsibility need not also prove an independent, freestanding abuse-of-governmental-power element. The existence of the constitutional wrong and its attribution to municipal policy or custom remain separate required inquiries.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part A of Stevens’s opinion, joined by the other eight Justices; unanimous statutory holding.

**Material application:** The Fifth Circuit’s additional statutory requirement cannot sustain dismissal merely because the city acted as an employer. Its rejection does not establish the underlying federal right or final municipal attribution; attribution is assumed only to resolve the constitutional question.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Employment alone does not establish municipal liability, and deficient training does not create a substantive constitutional right.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The extra statutory element is rejected, but dismissal of the federal claim is affirmed on the separate constitutional grounds below.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658, supplies municipal policy and causation requirements distinct from employment alone. *Canton v. Harris*, 489 U.S. 378, is clarified as an attribution doctrine, not a source of the underlying substantive right. *Oklahoma City v. Tuttle*, 471 U.S. 808, preserves the distinction between constitutional wrong and municipal responsibility.

##### Whether voluntary municipal employment or these pleaded safety omissions establish a substantive-due-process deprivation

**Holding:** The substantive Due Process Clause does not impose an independent general obligation on a municipality to provide its voluntary employees a workplace free from unreasonable risks of harm. Accepting public employment does not itself create the custody or deprivation of liberty that supports affirmative constitutional duties of care.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part B of Stevens’s opinion, joined by the other eight Justices; unanimous duty limitation.

**Material application:** The complaint alleges known hazards and deliberate failures to train, warn and equip sewer workers. Those allegations are accepted at dismissal; they do not make voluntary employment a custodial restraint.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding does not excuse violation of other applicable federal rights. Actual compulsion, deliberately inflicted bodily injury, a different properly pleaded affirmative-danger claim and procedural due process are not decided. State safety and tort remedies are not adjudicated, and knowing allegations are not reduced to negligence.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm dismissal of the federal substantive-due-process claim. No federal compensation or supervision order is entered; independently available state-law remedies retain their own merits and procedural requirements.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189, supplies the absence of a general protective duty while leaving distinct custody and affirmative-deprivation questions. *Youngberg v. Romeo*, 457 U.S. 307, is distinguished because voluntary employment is not involuntary custody. *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, preserves the distinction between constitutional due process and ordinary tort law without redescribing these allegations as negligence. *Bishop v. Wood*, 426 U.S. 341, rejects a general constitutional guarantee against employment mistakes.

**Independent alternative holding:** The pleaded municipal failures to train, warn and equip sewer workers against known hazards do not constitute arbitrary or conscience-shocking deprivation under substantive due process in this voluntary-employment setting. Even assuming the Texas safety statute creates the asserted duties and a protected liberty interest, breach alone does not establish that distinct substantive constitutional wrong.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** Part C of Stevens’s opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices control this alternative rejection. Stone’s agreement in the judgment does not join Part C.

**Limits, remedy, transition, or precedent treatment:** No deliberate bodily injury or direction knowingly sending Collins into a dangerous sewer is alleged. The public-program rationale recognizes competing social, political and economic choices as a legal presumption; it is not a finding that the city acted responsibly or that its budget justified these omissions. The alternative independently supports affirmance without deciding state remedies or a different affirmative-injury claim. *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, is distinguished from these protective omissions. *Bishop*, 426 U.S. 341, and *Daniels*, 474 U.S. 327, preserve the distinction between state employment or tort duties and substantive constitutional arbitrariness. *Harrah Independent School District v. Martin*, 440 U.S. 194, distinguishes an assumed state-created interest from arbitrary deprivation.

#### Pinder v. Johnson

**Docket or dockets:** Fourth Circuit No. 93-2125; no Supreme Court docket stated in the Record
**Decided:** May 21, 1996

##### Do protective assurances or custody of a private assailant establish a custodial duty to protect the threatened family?

**Holding:** Official knowledge of danger, promises of protection, and custody of the private assailant do not establish a custodial special relationship with a mother or her children. An alleged affirmative deprivation of otherwise available private protection remains analytically distinct from an omission of public protection; absence of custody of the threatened persons does not itself resolve that different claim.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor), Part II, joined by all eight other Justices; unanimous controlling rationale. Part I's separate appellate holding appears under Federal Courts, Qualified-immunity appeals and presentation.

**Controlling explanation:** DeShaney rejects a protection duty founded merely on official knowledge and assurances, but its discussion of increased vulnerability does not fully resolve this alleged affirmative course of conduct. The substantive-due-process inquiry concerns the official's own deprivation and the culpability attached to that deprivation, not simply the making of a statement. Daniels excludes mere negligence; the Court does not decide its reserved questions about recklessness or gross negligence. Pinder alleged that Johnson's assurance induced her to return to work rather than provide or arrange private protection. That favorable account must remain distinct from a general right to police protection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court establishes no general constitutional duty to detain an assailant, warn a victim, or fulfill every protective promise. It does not hold that custody is indispensable to every affirmative-danger claim, or decide the constitutional merits of the assumed affirmative-deprivation theory.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**
- DeShaney rejects knowledge-and-promise protection duties without deciding this entire affirmative-increased-vulnerability theory.
- Daniels excludes merely negligent deprivation while leaving its reserved culpability questions unresolved here.

##### Was Johnson's alleged conduct sufficiently established as unconstitutional by March 10, 1989 to defeat qualified immunity?

**Holding:** Qualified immunity turns on whether all relevant law existing when the officer acted made the particular conduct's unlawfulness apparent to a reasonable officer, without demanding a factual twin. On the favorable allegations here, law existing March 10, 1989 did not sufficiently establish that this noncoercive undertaking, release of the assailant, and uncorrected exposure constituted Johnson's own substantive-due-process deprivation; he therefore receives individual immunity on the reviewed claim.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor), Part II's notice standard joined by all nine Justices; Part III's application and affirmance joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, a five-Justice majority. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer dissent from the immunity disposition; their substantive theory is not controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** Anderson requires particularized apparent unlawfulness, not identical prior facts, and Elder requires consideration of all relevant law. DeShaney had rejected an omission-based duty while leaving the distinct affirmative theory incomplete. Bowers discussed active official wrongdoing but rejected release-based liability; Archie discussed obstruction of private aid in a materially different culpability setting and reached an adverse outcome; Jensen's discussion of a distinct danger did not preserve a knowledge-and-promise special relationship after DeShaney. Those sources did not sufficiently identify the assumed noncoercive interference here as the officer's own deprivation. A judge's disagreement, the number of judges on each side, or an officer's subjective belief in legality does not independently establish immunity. Later cases cannot retroactively supply the missing conduct-time notice.

**Material application:** The Court assumes that the assurance caused the mother to return to work and forgo alternative protection and that the remaining favorable allegations are true. It does not award immunity because actual reliance was absent, choose disputed facts against Pinder, or reject constitutional liability on the merits.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The holding is notice-specific and claim-specific. Collins, Hodge, Doe, Love and Sheets do not supply retroactive notice; Love establishes no mandatory merits-first sequence. Neither broader affirmative-danger liability nor the ultimate culpability and causation merits is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Fourth Circuit's reviewed individual-immunity judgment for Johnson is affirmed, 5-4. No new constitutional merits judgment or damages award is entered. Official-capacity and municipal claims, equal protection, section 1985, state wrongful-death and survival claims, and other excluded matters remain outside review.

## Distinct records, thresholds, and remedy

McGill/Brockamp is not an adjudicated incapacity case. At age 93 McGill remitted $7,000 with a 1983-return extension application in April 1984 and never filed that return during life. He died in November 1988; Brockamp became administrator in January 1989. Her March 1991 refund request preceded the estate's March 27 return showing $427 tax liability. The district court granted the Government summary judgment. The Ninth Circuit reversed and remanded because the dementia evidence raised a triable incompetence issue. ## Entering law and strongest competing paths

Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89 (December 3, 1990), establishes a rebuttable presumption of equitable tolling against the United States analogous to private litigation after waiver; Congress may provide otherwise. It allows relief sparingly and does not authorize a more favorable rule against the Government than against private defendants. Its examples include a timely but defective judicial filing and adversary misconduct; ordinary attorney neglect did not suffice. Those examples were not a holding resolving every incapacity claim. O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy joined the Court's entire opinion. Stevens expressly agreed both that equitable tolling was available and that the asserted neglect did not justify it; his dissent concerned who must receive the triggering notice. Souter did not participate. The White/Marshall concurrence rejecting tolling was not the Court's rule and must not be attributed to Scalia.

Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (March 20, 1990), held that equitable recoupment could not create independent refund jurisdiction after the required administrative claim period had elapsed. Kennedy wrote the majority joined by, among others, O'Connor and Scalia. Its integrated treatment of §§1346(a)(1), 7422(a) and 6511, rejection of a discovery-based payment date, and attention to express mitigation remain relevant. Its particular ground was recoupment without an independently timely proceeding, not a holding expressly adjudicating disability tolling under the later Irwin presumption. Stevens's dissent, joined by Brennan and Marshall, emphasized the Government's inconsistent taxation of one transaction and the taxpayer's practical lack of a forum; it is neither a general right to disregard every tax deadline nor controlling law.

Canonical Lundy requires separate attention to claim route and amount lookback, but decided a Tax Court deficiency-notice hypothetical, not disability tolling of these actual administrative claims. Williams recognizes the express refund waiver for its timely lien payer while retaining applicable administrative/timing conditions. Henderson requires attention to the function and text of a particular deadline and distinguishes procedure from consent; it supplies no general equitable extension of tax periods. O'Gilvie's current-term refund-recovery clock turns on receipt of the refund payment, and expressly creates no tolling rule. The exact rules, effective dates, coalitions and limits appear in the copied annex.

One supported path assumes Irwin's presumption applies and holds it rebutted here: repeated claim/allowance restrictions, independently expressed amount windows, and tailored exceptions can establish a deliberately closed tax-refund mechanism. The Government emphasizes administrability and stale incapacity inquiries and urges that even tolling the filing period leaves the separate lookback unsatisfied. The strongest answer is that Irwin adopted a general presumption despite waiver language, specialized exceptions do not necessarily exclude ordinary equity, and inability to act differs from excusable neglect or Dalm's chosen sequential litigation. An actual justification must show how this statute defeats the presumption, not simply invoke sovereign immunity as an automatic answer.

A competing supported path applies Irwin and finds the presumption unrebutted for legally sufficient incapacity. The taxpayers rely on refund actions' historic private-collector analogues, the lack of an express general disability prohibition, and a coordinated reading in which tolling the incorporated period affects both filing and lookback. Their proposal to toll the Government's assessment period by the same amount is an offered judicial safeguard, not enacted text. The strongest objection is that a court cannot preserve a filing remedy by rewriting an independent ceiling on recoverable payments, and that an equitable enlargement plus a newly extended assessment period may alter rather than interpret the statutory scheme. Any adoption must specify the legal standard, its application to Scott's findings, and what remains to be proved in Brockamp; sympathy or lack of prejudice alone does not supply the rule.

A narrower route may decide only the independent amount limit, leaving whether a filing period could be tolled unresolved; conversely, a decision against all nonstatutory mental-incapacity tolling must explain its reach to both components rather than treating them as identical. The private-analogue objection can be decided or assumed without announcing a universal rule for all claims against the Government. No path authorizes new findings about either taxpayer, a generalized equitable refund outside the statute, or a categorical classification of every tax deadline.

If tolling is unavailable, Scott's refund judgment cannot stand on its tolling ground; Brockamp's further proceedings must preserve any properly remaining deposit issue. If tolling is available, Scott's adjudicated incapacity and Brockamp's unresolved factual issue require different treatment. A legal ruling for the taxpayers does not automatically establish the entire $7,000 as an overpayment or eliminate other preserved statutory questions.

#### I. The Government's appeal in the father's action was timely

### State-court review

Section 1257(a), as enacted by Pub. L. 100-352 §3, 102 Stat. 662 (1988), authorizes certiorari review of final judgments or decrees of the highest state court in which a decision could be had when the specified federal issue or federal right is presented. Section 7 made the amendment effective ninety days after June 27, 1988, with exceptions for then-pending Supreme Court cases and earlier judgments. Those transition exceptions do not govern this 1996 state judgment. The modern certiorari channel must not be confused with the former mandatory-appeal subsection used in older opinions. [Official enactment](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-102/pdf/STATUTE-102-Pg662.pdf).

*Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469, 477–487 (1975), recognizes four settings in which a finally decided federal issue can support review before completion of the state proceedings: (1) the federal determination is conclusive or further proceedings are preordained, leaving the case practically concluded; (2) the federal issue necessarily survives whatever happens next; (3) later federal review will be unavailable whatever the ultimate outcome; or (4) the applicant might prevail later on nonfederal grounds, federal reversal would end litigation on the relevant cause of action, and postponement might seriously erode federal policy. Final decision of a legal issue alone does not supply these further conditions. [Opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/420/469), [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/420/us/469).

*Flynt v. Ohio*, 451 U.S. 619, 620–623 (1981), applies the fourth category's separate policy requirement and warns against piecemeal federal review. Even litigation-ending reversal of a constitutional issue does not automatically make an interlocutory judgment reviewable. *Goodyear Atomic Corp. v. Miller*, 486 U.S. 174, 178–180 (1988), applies category four to federal nuclear-production policy and an agency remand; the exception is not limited to speech cases. Neither decision makes the importance of a constitutional right, standing alone, a substitute for the statutory conditions. [Flynt](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/451/619), [Goodyear Atomic](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/486/174), [Goodyear cross-check](https://openjurist.org/486/us/174).

*Fort Wayne Books, Inc. v. Indiana*, 489 U.S. 46, 54–57 (1989), applies category four to a facial First Amendment challenge that would terminate the relevant RICO charges. Justice O'Connor's separate opinion, 489 U.S. at 68–70, instead insists that the anticipated trial and possible later federal issues defeat jurisdiction in that companion matter; she distinguishes the other case's already-imposed seizure affecting protected expression. This is a source of competing legal approaches, not a decision about development charges. [Opinion and writings](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/489/46), [cross-check](https://openjurist.org/489/us/46).

*Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1037–1044 (1983), permits federal review when the decision appears primarily federal or interwoven with federal law and the independence and adequacy of a state ground are unclear. A clear, bona fide, independently sufficient state ground prevents review that could not alter the judgment. The inquiry concerns the actual basis of the judgment, not an available state argument that no controlling state coalition adopted. Justice O'Connor wrote the Court's opinion; Justice Stevens's dissent favored a presumption against jurisdiction where an adequate state ground's independence remained uncertain. ## Date-eligible legal limits

The former §2254(b)–(c), quoted in the original answer, bars granting habeas relief absent exhaustion, an absence of available state corrective process, or circumstances making that process ineffective. It distinguishes an unavailable remedy from an available procedure for raising the question. A merits ruling in another prisoner's case does not establish that Lynce personally completed state review.

*Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 510, 520–522 (1982), requires dismissal of a petition containing both exhausted and unexhausted claims and permits the prisoner to proceed with the exhausted claims after removing the others. Its mixed-petition rule concerns claims actually asserted. It neither forces a prisoner to litigate every conceivable reincarceration theory nor supplies exhaustion of the sole remaining claim. The Court's holding does not automatically adjudicate the future availability of claims omitted here. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/455/509).

*Granberry v. Greer*, 481 U.S. 129, 131–136 (1987), establishes that exhaustion is not a jurisdictional limit on appellate consideration. Its specific discretionary holding addresses a State that failed to raise nonexhaustion in district court; it rejects both automatic dismissal and automatic waiver in that posture. Relevant comity considerations include unresolved state-law or factual questions and unnecessary additional proceedings. Lynce's express district objection distinguishes that posture. Granberry cannot be cited as holding that an expressly maintained, applicable defense may always be disregarded whenever a court wishes to reach the merits. Its nonjurisdictional rule does establish that source validation need not treat exhaustion as an unexplained absence of adjudicative power. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/481/129); [parallel primary text](https://openjurist.org/481/us/129).

*Duckworth v. Serrano*, 454 U.S. 1, 3–4 (1981), rejects a new exception based merely on an apparently clear constitutional violation and judicial economy. It distinguishes the lack of an opportunity for state redress or a clearly deficient corrective process. Its footnote also distinguishes repetitive applications after completed exhaustion from first presentation of an unexhausted claim. Canonical Morales's force on the merits therefore cannot itself dispense with §2254. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/454/1).

*Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 130 (1982), rejects predicted futility as cause for a state procedural default: even a court that previously rejected a constitutional argument may reconsider it. That is a procedural-default holding, not a determination that Lynce has defaulted under an identified Florida rule. It nevertheless defeats a casual general proposition that adverse precedent alone always excuses presentation. No procedural default or cause-and-prejudice issue is created here without an applicable state procedural bar. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/456/107).

Petitioner's cited *Schall v. Martin*, 467 U.S. 253, 261 (1984), reports the district court's conclusion that an identical challenge had already been rejected by the highest state court, making exhaustion futile. The cited passage is a procedural recitation, not an express Supreme Court holding establishing a universal adverse-precedent exception. It gives the petitioner's argument a specific date-eligible analogue, but cannot erase Duckworth or establish that every new prisoner's claim is identical. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/467/253).

## 2. Operative provisions, qualifications, and the appellate modification

The mechanically copied full decree appears in section 6. The legally material distinctions are:

- Paragraph 1(a) already separately prohibits trespass, sitting in, blocking, impeding, or obstructing ingress or egress, including parking lots, parking-lot entrances, driveways, and driveway entrances. Its breadth matters to the claim that the added geographic restrictions are unnecessary.
- Paragraph 1(b) prohibits **demonstrating**, not mere presence, within fifteen feet of the specified edges/front of doorways, doorway entrances, parking-lot entrances, driveways, and driveway entrances. It also prohibits demonstrating within fifteen feet of any person **or vehicle** seeking access to or leaving a covered facility. The fixed and mobile restrictions are distinct applications requiring distinct examination.
- Covered facilities are any facilities where abortions are performed in the Western District of New York, not just the named plaintiffs' facilities. The mobile zone is not limited to patients or women; it also protects other persons or vehicles seeking access or leaving.
- The exception allows nonthreatening conversational sidewalk counseling by no more than **two people with each person or group** they seek to counsel. It is not a cap of two counselors in an entire fixed zone. Paragraph 1(b) incorporates this exception into the fixed and floating restrictions. It allows an initial approach without affirmative advance consent.
- Paragraph 1(c) separately prohibits physically abusing, grabbing, touching, pushing, shoving, or crowding persons entering, leaving, working at, or using services at covered facilities. The counseling exception does not authorize these acts.
- The original decree says that an unwilling recipient, or one wishing to leave or walk away, may do so; counselors must cease "such counseling" and then comply with paragraph 1(b)'s personal-distance provision. The appellate court directed a textual modification on remand to make explicit the district court's construction: the person or group must **indicate, verbally or nonverbally, that they do not wish to be counseled**. Mere uncommunicated unwillingness is not the trigger as construed.
- The appellate construction of cessation is retreat from the personal fifteen-foot zone. It does **not** require ending all communication with that listener: advocacy from fifteen feet remains allowed. Approaches to other persons, distribution of leaflets to passersby, and other demonstration within the decree's bounds remain possible.
- Footnote 5 records the appellees' oral-argument concession that the decree as written does not prevent entering a rebuffing person's zone to counsel another person who has not refused. The court did not decide whether such a prohibition could validly be imposed and reported no occurrence of this scenario. This is an important limit, not a general permission to resume counseling the unwilling person.
- The same footnote leaves defendants free to seek modification if escorts drown out their message. It does not find that this allegation has been disproved or grant relief on it.
- Footnote 4 leaves the geographic reach of a floating zone away from a clinic unresolved, anticipating case-by-case district-court adjudication. Do not invent a maximum travel distance or represent a precise endpoint as established.

## 3. Findings, evidentiary limits, and remedial facts

The Oakes opinion reports the district court's findings that the demonstrations were usually peaceful but often became emotionally charged. It recounts yelling, intimidating and obstructive crowding, grabbing, pushing and shoving patients, escorts, and staff; congestion at vehicle entrances; and counselors who, frustrated by refusal, persisted, raised their voices, crowded patients, and continued after indications that counseling was unwanted. These are published recitations of findings, not independent review of the underlying testimony by this validator. This opinion does not verify the packet's additional assertions of spitting or harassment of police; those require another authorized source if material.

The district court found stress, physical injury in some instances, interference with the clinical environment, delays caused by frightened or confused patients leaving, and ineffective local enforcement because continuous protests exceeded available resources and arrests did not deter repeated conduct. The medical testimony recited includes elevated blood pressure, hyperventilation, sedation and additional counseling, inability to remain still during procedures, increased risks from later-term delay, and risks from delayed removal of pre-abortion laminaria or missed follow-up care. Women traveling substantial distances could have difficulty rescheduling. These support arguments about safety and access, but do not erase the distinct tailoring inquiry.

The defendants stipulated that a physical blockade could be enjoined. A threatened September 28, 1990 blockade did not occur after issuance of the TRO; defendants held a peaceful protest instead. The judges disputed what the record established about the need for expanded relief. The majority accepted general TRO compliance with notable exceptions and held that a preliminary injunction may take account of pre-TRO misconduct; failure of a prior order was not treated as an indispensable prerequisite. The district court's quoted findings also criticize patient escorts for raising their voices to drown out counselors and blocking counselors, aggravating confrontations; that conduct bears on the parties' competing neutrality and remedy arguments.

## 4. Lower rationales and material disagreements

**Oakes opinion, joined by Newman, Kearse, Miner, Walker, Leval, Calabresi, Cabranes, and Parker.** The injunction was treated as directed at harmful conduct rather than viewpoint, and assessed under the historical Madsen requirement of no more speech burden than necessary for significant interests. The identified interests were medical safety, public safety, and lawful access. The majority viewed the fifteen-foot limits as smaller and more permeable than Madsen's thirty-six-foot entry zone because two counselors could enter. It nevertheless acknowledged that these zones also move with persons, unlike that earlier fixed zone. It regarded alternative demonstration nearby, available personal counseling, the clinical consequences of confrontations, and the district court's record familiarity as supporting fit. It did not deem post-TRO failure necessary to justify an injunction following a fuller evidentiary hearing.

For cessation, Oakes distinguished a bar on all uninvited approaches from a rule allowing an approach until an affirmative verbal/nonverbal refusal. It upheld retreat as protection against unwanted physical proximity and persistent pursuit of medically vulnerable listeners, rather than a ban on the message. It used captive-audience reasoning for persons who could avoid the confrontation only by forgoing needed care, and allowed continued advocacy from fifteen feet. The construction and open questions in section 2 are part of this rationale, not optional details.

**Winter concurrence in the result, joined by Newman, Kearse, Mahoney, McLaughlin, Jacobs, Walker, Leval, Calabresi, and Cabranes.** Winter proposed a broader, generally applicable principle: coercive or obstructionist conduct is unprotected in any forum, and persuasion rights do not authorize forcing an audience to receive advocacy. He expressly preserved breathing space for insulting, strong, but nonthreatening expression. Targeting particular people at places they cannot easily avoid increases the opportunity for coercion. On the documented obstruction and intimidation, physical separation was a permissible and comparatively precise response, even without the more extensive record in Madsen. His rationale did not depend on captive-audience or nonpublic-forum doctrine, and he rejected the view that general compliance with a TRO erased the significance of earlier misconduct.

**Jacobs concurrence in the result, joined by Mahoney.** Jacobs joined Winter fully and cautioned that an abortion-patient-health-only rationale was too narrow and potentially message-specific. Because the decree protects any person or vehicle entering or leaving, including a male staff member walking alone, its support must rest on general public safety, traffic, property, and freedom from coercion as well as care of patients. This is a material distinction between rationales supporting the same lower judgment.

**Meskill dissent, joined by Altimari.** The dissent accepted that some injunction was proper and did not oppose restraints on obstruction or physical harassment. It challenged these additional provisions as cumulative restraints on protected signs, leaflets, peaceful picketing, and speech, lacking a demonstrated need beyond existing paragraphs 1(a), 1(c), and 1(d). It emphasized substantial TRO compliance, limited subsequent obstruction, differing physical sites, and the duty of independent First Amendment review rather than deference to a court applying the older time/place/manner test. It argued that isolated coercive acts do not justify banning peaceful expression collectively, relying on Claiborne Hardware and Milk Wagon Drivers to distinguish protected advocacy from pervasive intimidation.

On cessation, Meskill considered the initial-approach distinction insufficient: a listener still gained control over otherwise protected public-forum expression. He disputed the extension of captive-audience protection from patients receiving care inside a clinic to persons walking toward it, and maintained that emotional offense is not itself grounds for suppression. He distinguished prohibited threats, fighting words, or violent coercion from protected expression; cited the independent prohibitions on physical interference; and questioned viewpoint neutrality where escorts could engage in loud close conduct. He reported that the ACLU amicus generally supported the clinics but regarded cessation as too restrictive in this respect. This is the opinion's description of that amicus position; the brief itself was not read.

**Altimari separate dissent.** Altimari joined Meskill and particularly stressed the floating zone's invisible, moving boundary, uncertain travel distance, and notice/chilling problems. He accepted that location and medical interests may inform the public-forum analysis but denied that they change its stringent standard. He considered the existing anti-crowding and access provisions material alternatives, distinguished Madsen's constrained physical access and more extensive misconduct, and objected to an unwilling listener's power to move protected expression fifteen feet away. He retained his earlier view that the injunction itself was content neutral while criticizing the Oakes rationale as content based.
