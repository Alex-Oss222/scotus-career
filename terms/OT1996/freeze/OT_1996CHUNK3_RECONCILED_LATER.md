## 34. Ingalls Shipbuilding v. Director, OWCP — February 18, 1997

### I1 — predeath entitlement and §33(g)

All eight would affirm rejection of forfeiture based on Mrs. Yates's predeath releases. Cowart's governing register is **Vested LHWCA entitlement and third-party settlement approval**, June 22, 1992: an existing substantive right, not payment or an award, triggers the condition. A qualifying widow's §9 right depended on the worker's covered death and her qualifying survivorship. Those substantive conditions had not occurred when the predeath releases were signed. Settling a possible wrongful-death claim does not itself create then-current LHWCA death-benefit entitlement. Employer protection cannot delete that statutory threshold.

The strongest contrary path treats future entitlement as enough because release of a claim may later reduce an employer's protection. It confuses a potential future claim with an existing right and extends Cowart beyond its vesting premise. The fact that Mr. Yates already had his own disability claim does not transfer that entitlement to his spouse. The later death and causation stipulation do not retroactively establish her status when she signed the earlier releases.

Temporal limits matter: the predeath settlements ended in January 1984; the subsequently codified amendments are not automatically imposed retroactively. Both relevant formulations use the entitlement threshold; no preserved temporal challenge requires a decision that every later procedural detail applied to every earlier release. Cowart's current requirements remain distinct: for a covered below-entitlement settlement, prescribed prior written employer-and-carrier approval and thirty-day filing; for judgments and sufficiently large settlements, notification rather than that prior approval. This case adds no holding about approval of every recovery, waiver, gross/net calculations or a benefit amount. The postdeath approved settlements, §33(f) allocation and contract-credit issues are outside the two granted questions.

### I2 — respondent status under the then-effective Rule 15(a)

All eight would affirm the Director's respondent participation in this employer-initiated Fifth Circuit review. Rule 15(a) requires the agency to be named respondent and includes an agency, board, commission or officer. The Department administers the Act; the Benefits Review Board performs the adjudicative function. The Secretary's designated Director can represent the agency in the live review of that administrative disposition. The then-effective §802.410(b) designates the Director as the Secretary's proper party in §21(c) review, and §921a provides Secretary-appointed representation in the stated proceedings while expressly excepting proceedings in this Court. None of those provisions grants an independent appeal merely from disagreement with an award.

The competing Board-only interpretation gives weight to the Board's issuance of the order. It does not establish that Rule 15 excludes the administrative Department's designated litigating officer or requires the adjudicative Board personally to advocate. The regulation implements the representation role; it cannot manufacture statutory aggrievement. The employer and widow furnish the continuing adversarial controversy, so no Article III injury rule for an independent Director appeal is being evaded.

The governing register is **Administrative interest and LHWCA appellate aggrievement**, Newport News, March 21, 1995. Its express respondent-status reservation is respected. No opinion decides whether the Director may initiate review on a general interest, sustain the litigation after every private party withdraws, obtain relief beyond the live appeal, or receive merits deference. Statutory meaning resolves the compensation question without a new deference holding.

## 35. Robinson v. Shell Oil Co. — February 18, 1997

### Coverage, alternatives and remedy

R1 is the §704(a) former-employee question; R2 is the relationship to Walters; R3 is the Rule 12(b)(6) judgment. All eight would hold that the provision's employees include former employees for the alleged former-employer retaliation at issue, reverse the categorical dismissal and remand for the unresolved elements and defenses. No Justice finds that Shell actually supplied false information, acted with retaliatory motive, caused injury, or owes a specified remedy. The separate discriminatory-discharge merits are not reopened by this holding.

The definition of employee as an individual employed by an employer does not specify an exclusively current temporal relationship for every use. Section 704(a)'s protection for making a charge and participating in proceedings operates alongside discharge remedies and enforcement after separation. Section 706(g)'s reinstatement of employees confirms that the noun can refer to a person whose employment has ended. Including applicants identifies persons who need never have had an employment relationship; it does not compel excluding those who previously did. This is a reading of enacted context, not an override of clear text on policy grounds.

The strongest current-only argument invokes the ordinary present relationship, the express applicant category and a consistent statutory definition. The response preserves the definition while distinguishing its temporal application: some provisions need a present relationship, while this one protects resort to the enforcement process, including after discharge. A definition can identify the kind of relationship without prescribing a single time of reference. The narrower formulation covering the pleaded former-employer reference retaliation is sufficient; the Court need not create a universal action for any conduct toward any past acquaintance or decide a separate categorical employment-nexus element.

**Walters is completed law**, January 14, 1997, not a pending packet. Its unanimous first holding counts genuine employment relationships for each relevant working day under §701(b); separated workers do not count on later days. That daily employer-size inquiry remains intact. Section 704(a) contains no corresponding daily-counting requirement. Neither Darden's ERISA worker-status inquiry nor Town & Country's NLRA compatible-service holding supplies a universal temporal definition. The governing Darden register is **ERISA employee status under general common-law agency**, March 24, 1992; it is an analogy about relationship and statutory context, not Title VII's retaliation rule. Walters expressly reserved retaliation liability and remedy.

No later general materially-adverse-act formulation is adopted. The original packet's phrase identifies an unresolved question, not a presently controlling test. The remand leaves the conduct, prohibited reason, causal connection and legally available relief for ordinary adjudication. Coverage alone orders no damages, hiring, reinstatement to Shell, trial or fixed filing schedule. EEOC's supported amicus position is consistent with the construction; no new deference rule is needed.
