## Raines v. Byrd — No. 96-1671

### Source addition, review and competing grounds

All eight accept the direct appeal from the single district judge and the supplied dates. Article III must precede the disputed constitutional merits. The Houses did not authorize these plaintiffs to represent them; their amici defend validity without taking a standing position. Hatfield's retirement does not cure or defeat the five continuing Members' claims.

**No-standing path.** These Members' votes were counted and lost; none was excluded, deprived of a decisive vote, or subjected to personal financial or informational injury. The claimed diminution of future legislative influence is institutional. Coleman concerns votes whose nullification changed a result; Powell concerns personal exclusion and its incidents. Lujan and Bender require the plaintiff's own concrete stake. Akins establishes that sharing does not make an actual informational deprivation abstract, but supplies no similar deprivation here. Congress can remove prudential barriers within its enactment's scope, not Article III.

The strongest objection is that the Act changes the legal effect of the Members' present votes by making the resulting package subject to executive cancellation. The six no-standing Justices answer that equal voting weight and actual participation remain, while Congress holds the disputed institutional authority. A legislature's enactments often change the consequences of future legislative work. The availability of repeal or protection of later legislation reinforces the institutional character without becoming an exhaustion requirement or an independent automatic bar. They do not hold every official injury abstract, every institutional suit unavailable or every Article I dispute a political question.

**Standing path.** Stevens and Breyer regard the immediate alteration of a Member's own voting function as concrete official injury, with statutory authorization confirming protection of that interest. Judicial invalidation would remove the alleged alteration without awaiting a specific cancellation. Coleman protects effective votes; Akins rejects numerical exclusivity; Lujan requires concreteness, not money or loss of office. The complete district source supports current budgetary work and impending appropriations votes, making this injury theory ripe without invented subsequent events.

Their strongest difficulty is Stevens's own Bender opinion, which bars an individual member from appropriating his body's appeal. They distinguish an expressly authorized suit concerning the Member's present voting power from an unauthorized appeal imposing no individual obligation. Statutory permission alone does not create standing; the asserted functional injury supplies it. A chance to persuade a majority to repeal the law does not erase that injury.

### Merits alternatives for the standing Justices

The complete source supports two distinct legal paths. The repeal account treats cancellation after signature as a new removal of enacted legal force, subject to Chadha's prescribed legislative procedure irrespective of intelligible standards. The execution account treats the Act and later law together as a conditional enactment, with Field v. Clark, Hampton and ordinary delegation principles supporting bounded executive implementation. It must answer permanent cancellation and the distinct direct-spending and tax effects; permissive appropriations alone cannot resolve all categories.

Stevens adopts the repeal account and would affirm the declaration on Article I grounds. His pre-divergence Bowsher concurrence, Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority Court opinion and Chadha join distinguish the constitutionally allocated function from an efficient rearrangement. A signed bill becomes law regardless of private presidential reservations; later cancellation changes its legal effect. Discretionary rescission, extinguishment of the specified new spending authority or obligation, and removal of a qualifying tax provision's force differ in detail but share that challenged mechanism. Field's contingent implementation does not establish a general power to select which enacted provisions survive. His ground is structural, not insufficiency of the deficit standard or subjective lack of presidential approval.

Breyer reserves an Act-merits vote in his dissent from the jurisdictional dismissal. This is an independent scope decision: his disagreement is that the Court should adjudicate the merits, and deciding every category's validity is unnecessary to explain it. It is not a research-gap shortcut. The conditional-execution account is substantial under Field, Hampton and Mistretta and consistent with the functional separation analysis of his current Clinton concurrence; permanent removal of enacted obligations and tax provisions remains a serious contrary consideration. His frozen position is to reject jurisdictional dismissal and reach the merits, neither sustain the Act nor join Stevens's invalidation. No later position is imported.

## Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union — No. 96-511

### Components, review and complete operative statutory boundaries

All eight Associates accept the supplied direct-appeal route under Act §561 and the live pre-enforcement speech controversy. The appeal concerns the preliminary order as clarified, not a new permanent injunction or damages claim. No disputed filing date, standing defect or expiration is inferred.

The separate judgment components are: (R1) §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency transmission offense and §223(a)(2)'s associated knowing, intentional facility permission; (R2) §223(d)(1)(A)'s sending to a specified minor or minors and §223(d)(2)'s corresponding facility permission; (R3) §223(d)(1)(B)'s display available to minors and the corresponding §223(d)(2) liability; and (R4) severability and the retained scope of relief. R1 requires knowing making, creation or solicitation and initiation by a telecommunications device, with knowledge that the recipient is under eighteen. R2 and R3 involve knowing use of an interactive computer service and contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs patently offensive under contemporary community standards. R1's device definition excludes an interactive computer service; the provisions cannot be collapsed into one generic Internet offense. The text applies regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The facility offenses require both knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for prohibited activity. The challenged crimes permit a fine, imprisonment up to two years, or both.

The defense is not strict liability for mere carriage. Section (e)(1) protects access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the person's control, including incidental transmission, downloading, intermediate storage and access software, without content creation. Sections (e)(2) and (3) withhold that defense respectively from a conspirator actively involved with creation or knowing distribution, a knowing advertiser of the prohibited communications, and an access provider owning or controlling the offending network. Under (e)(4), employer liability requires within-scope conduct plus knowledgeable authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5), applying to (a)(1)(B), (d), and the corresponding (a)(2) activity, separately protects good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate access-restriction measures under the circumstances, including feasible available technology, OR restricted access using a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult PIN. A speaker need not satisfy both alternatives.

Under (e)(6) the FCC may describe suitable measures for (d), but may not enforce their use, approve or sanction them, or endorse specific products; described measures are evidence of good faith, not an exclusive license. Interactive computer services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers. Section (f)(1) protects otherwise lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions; (f)(2) preserves the stated complementary intrastate and uncovered-conduct powers while barring inconsistent liability within its named commercial-entity, library and higher-education scope. Section (g) preserves other federal law. None of those savings terms narrows the challenged content categories to obscenity or cures their impact on speakers creating protected content.

### Issue-specific alternatives

**Path A: affirm the challenged injunction in full.** The criminal scheme selects protected content, suppresses lawful adult communication and demands impracticable age separation from many small or noncommercial speakers. The context-sensitive terms and knowledge requirements do not supply the missing fit. Sable distinguishes a compelling child-protection purpose from adequate means; ACT/Pacifica preserves adult speech and independent necessity review even in broadcasting; Denver's controlling compulsory-scheme holding addresses actual deterrence and access burdens. Internet communication is not justified by broadcast scarcity or unavoidable household intrusion. Turner and the March 1997 application concern content-neutral carriage, not permission to defer away a content-selected criminal burden.

The strongest objection is that targeted sending to known minors is textually distinct from public display and can operate without excluding any adult. The full-injunction Justices conclude that the actual offenses and defenses remain substantially overbroad as enacted: the transmission provisions reach communications with adult as well as minor recipients; the statutory categories do not adopt Ginsberg's variable-obscenity limits, including its serious-value protection; uncertainty backed by criminal penalties deters protected sexual, health, artistic and political discussion. The Court can preserve obscenity by excising the distinct indecency category, but cannot legislate a new minors-only, harmful-to-minors offense or an effective age-verification system. This is a judgment about substantial protected reach, not a claim that every conceivable application is unconstitutional.

**Path B: retain a separable direct-to-minor core.** O'Connor and Thomas distinguish a communication confined to one or more particular recipients known to be minors from mixed-age conversation and unrestricted display. Government may protect minors through the former restriction without reducing any adult's access. Ginsberg supplies support for age-differentiated restrictions; the intact good-faith and verified-access defenses are supplementary safeguards, not a substitute for evaluating the audience. The strongest objection is that the indecency definitions are broader than harmful-to-minors obscenity and that the Court would rewrite the Act. They answer only at the severability/remedial level: leave a valid direct-recipient application enforceable while barring the adult-burdening reach, and do not add new elements or change the statutory definition. They do not hold all protected speech to minors regulable, settle every minor's access claim, or reject a specific vagueness challenge to a later prosecution. Their disagreement is the demonstrated facial remedy's reach, not permission for general Internet censorship.

Neither path treats parental controls as already universal, free, perfectly effective or technologically implemented. The record supports the practical shortcomings of the enacted defenses for many speakers, not the impossibility of every future feasible age screen. No Justice here uses a public-forum designation as the necessary premise.

## Vacco v. Quill — No. 95-1858

### Enhanced comparison and common narrow proposition

All eight Associates vote to reverse the Second Circuit's equal-protection judgment and remand consistently with rejection of the asserted unequal-treatment theory. The physician controversy is live on the packet; the initial patients' deaths are not converted into total mootness. No Justice obtains a vote from same-day Glucksberg.

**Path A, accepted at least at the bounded proposition below:** New York may distinguish a patient's rejection of unwanted bodily intervention from a physician's intentional assistance in bringing about death. All competent persons have the same legally stated treatment-refusal option, and none has an entitlement under this classification to intentional suicide assistance. The patient comparison remains necessary: the fact that one dying patient is dependent on life support and another is not produces a different practical opportunity to hasten death. That difference is rationally connected to protecting bodily consent while restricting lethal assistance, reducing risks of mistake or pressure, preserving life and maintaining professional responsibilities. Equal protection does not compel elimination of one recognized liberty merely to equalize every practical consequence, or extension of its scope to every act producing death.

**Path B, rejected on this record but retained as an argument:** Competent terminally ill patients may seek the same end and physicians may anticipate death from either withdrawal or medication; therefore differential availability of assistance can look arbitrary. Under actual Heller and Romer, calling the categories conduct-based and reciting a legitimate purpose are insufficient without explaining their connection to the actual disadvantage. The answer is not a universally true physiological distinction. Consent to continued intervention is independently material, and law can distinguish an intended lethal act from cessation of unwanted intervention or good-faith pain relief despite foreseeable death. The risks attaching to a requested new lethal intervention are a rational reason to retain that distinction. The equality theory presented does not establish that patients in a concrete exceptional application have no legally material difference, nor prove that permitted palliation is covertly forbidden. Narrower regulation is possible; ordinary rational-basis review does not require its adoption.

The sufficient proposition is ordinary rationality of this treatment-refusal/intentional-assistance distinction in the presented claim. It does not depend on declaring all patients alike or unlike for every purpose, discovering animus, making intent conclusive about every medical act, finding every withdrawal unintended, denying the constitutional significance of bodily integrity, or declaring no future end-of-life application can fail. No suspect classification or independently established fundamental-right entitlement triggers heightened equality review here. Cruzan's assumed competent-refusal premise does not itself create a right to lethal assistance. Casey protects its defined reproductive liberty and does not constitutionalize every physician-assisted choice. Beach's permissible generalization operates with Heller's actual-disadvantage requirement and Romer's ordinary rationality constraint.

### Remedy, separate writings and reserved law

The mandate reverses the Second Circuit's holding that the presented distinction denies equal protection. It requires no finding that every withdrawal patient lacks an intention to die and no finding that every assisted-death patient is coerced, depressed or incompetent. It does not reinstate a dead patient's claim, dictate a new trial, declare every medical application constitutional, or issue a new injunction. Properly remaining matters have their ordinary posture, with no new claim invented.

Breyer's concurrence treats avoidance of severe suffering as a serious liberty concern but finds that issue unpresented by a law permitting the described care. Those are voluntary explanatory writings; their broader reservations are not holdings of the Court and do not create a remand obligation.

## Washington v. Glucksberg — No. 96-110

### Enhanced comparison: asserted liberty, sufficient ground and alternatives

All eight Associates vote to reverse the Ninth Circuit's recognition of the asserted right and resulting invalidation. This does not mean that all eight adopt a single general substantive-due-process methodology. The claim concerns willing physicians prescribing lethal medicine for competent terminally ill adults who seek to end their lives. It neither demands compelled service or public financing nor is limited to withdrawing unwanted bodily intervention. Describing it accurately preserves both its terminal-illness setting and the additional affirmative lethal assistance sought.

**Path A: no protected fundamental entitlement to the asserted assistance.** The preexisting bodily-integrity sources and actual Casey do not establish that entitlement. The longstanding legal distinction between refusal and assisting suicide, and public authority to prevent error, coercion and abusive intervention, weigh against recognition. At the presented scope the prohibition rationally serves preservation of life, protection of vulnerable people and maintenance of medical responsibilities. Terminal illness and an asserted present wish do not by themselves eliminate diagnostic error, pressure, impaired judgment or the difficulty of policing a legally authorized lethal practice. These are reasons for preventive regulation, not findings that any named claimant lacks capacity. The constitutional conclusion does not require proof that every risk materializes in every case.

The strongest counterargument is that tradition formulated around suicide may fail to address a competent dying person's liberty to govern an unavoidable death with a willing physician, and that preserving life abstractly can disregard the person. The response is specific: refusal protects against unwanted bodily intervention; the present claim adds permission for another's intended lethal act. Casey's supported reproductive choice remains controlling in its domain but does not supply an unrestricted autonomy rule. The State's protection of patients and legitimate medical boundaries remains concrete despite the limited class proposed. No compelled treatment or denial of lawful palliation is being approved.

**Path B: reasoned evaluation of a substantial liberty interest without making historical prohibition dispositive.** Souter, Stevens and Breyer insist that the constitutional interest must be evaluated in its real medical and personal setting; Ginsburg shares the refusal to foreclose all narrower cases. The strongest argument for invalidation is a demonstrated voluntary, informed and persistent decision in an extreme terminal condition where the asserted protective reasons do not justify the prohibition. That possible individual application differs from the general terminally-ill class entitlement adjudicated here; rejecting the latter rests on the explained protective grounds, without inferring that unreproduced patient evidence is absent from the Court's file. Actual Casey protects medical judgment within an established choice, not every proposed new procedure. On this presentation, serious protective risks and the uncertain effects of the requested rule justify rejecting the lower court's classwide constitutional entitlement. These Justices therefore reverse without endorsing a categorical rule that history forecloses every liberty claim or every application.

This alternative is a substantive conclusion at the scope presented, not a failure to decide the case, an invented requirement to exhaust legislation, a judicial moratorium or an automatic further factual hearing. No source permits assuming that palliation invariably eliminates suffering, that every request reflects depression, or that a foreign jurisdiction's experience conclusively proves the effects of a proposed American rule. The decision does not establish a universal six-step medical-liberty framework. It does not overrule Casey, Cruzan, Heller, Romer or any prior holding.
