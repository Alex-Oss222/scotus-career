## Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Association - February 18, 1997

### Validated facts, scope and posture

The Ninth Circuit's published decision is 83 F.3d 1168 (1996), affirming the District of Oregon's order to disclose BLM newsletter recipients' names and addresses. Interior had already released organizational names and addresses; the live dispute concerns private individuals. The district court stayed its disclosure order pending the Ninth Circuit appeal. That historical stay does not establish a later Supreme Court stay or its duration.

The lower majority recognized that the requester's particular purpose does not itself enter the FOIA public-interest balance. It nevertheless considered public access to the recipients, to whom ONDA wished to send competing information, a substantial public interest. It also concluded that existing receipt of related mail reduced the privacy invasion. Judge Fernandez disagreed: receipt of an official newsletter revealed personal interest, and disclosure would make the list available to everyone; ONDA's intended use could not confer a requester-specific entitlement.

Separate the claim that the list itself demonstrates how BLM chooses its audience from the claim that ONDA can improve public debate by contacting that audience. The latter depends on the requester's intended future use; the former requires an actual incremental disclosure about agency conduct. Neither the requested information's subject nor an asserted beneficial use supplies that showing automatically. Joining one mailing list is not established consent to public dissemination. The government retains the burden of establishing the exemption, and the governing threshold is Exemption 6's clearly-unwarranted invasion, not Exemption 7(C)'s different language.

## California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Construction - February 18, 1997

### Validated facts, scope and posture

The Ninth Circuit's published decision is 57 F.3d 712 (1995). The District Court granted summary judgment to the state defendants on the ERISA and NLRA theories. The Ninth Circuit reversed on ERISA preemption and rejected the Fitzgerald Act saving theory; it did not reach the independent NLRA ground or the contingent section 1983 damages claim. It rejected the particular request for section 1988 fees based on an asserted ERISA right that Dillingham had not sought to enforce under section 1983. These distinctions preclude a preflight assumption that removing the ERISA ground necessarily requires final judgment against every remaining claim.

Sound Systems first worked under a collective agreement associated with an approved program. Following the union's withdrawal and a new agreement, it used the new Electronic and Communications JATC before state approval. Later approval was not retroactive. The apprenticeship wage payments did not meet prevailing journeyman rates; officials issued a withholding notice to Dillingham, which was responsible for its subcontractor under state law. The parties agreed that the material summary-judgment facts were not disputed. The state's contractual-estoppel argument had not been presented to the district court and the Ninth Circuit declined to consider it for the first time on appeal. Do not infer a new waiver or decide an ungranted ground.

The reviewed rule ties the lower public-works apprentice wage to an approved apprenticeship program; otherwise the prevailing wage is due. The statutory text and the lower court's interpretation must remain distinct: do not turn this challenged wage consequence into a general judicial mandate for every ERISA training plan to obtain approval, nor invalidate every feature of California's apprenticeship system.

### Law, supported alternatives and source limits

The operative register is ERISA plan commands and indirect economic effects: Travelers, April 26, 1995, supplies the indirect-effects boundary; Board of Trade, December 14, 1992, retains the mandatory covered-plan-content benchmark. Travelers's insurer and HMO holdings were unanimous. They preserve possible effects sufficiently acute to operate as substantive coverage commands or effectively restrict insurer choice. They create neither a numerical safe harbor nor immunity for generally applicable laws. Its reserved direct-self-funded claim is not a holding sustaining direct plan regulation.

For section 514(a), separately test whether the wage law legally refers to ERISA plans, and whether its actual connection dictates plan benefits, structure or administration. The existence of funded ERISA programs does not establish that all apprenticeship programs are covered. The lower court's finding that this program is a covered plan may be accepted solely for deciding preemption without issuing a broader coverage holding. The strongest contrary path is that the economic consequences effectively require conformity with state plan standards. Its success requires the legally operative compulsion; incentive alone does not distinguish Travelers. If section 514(a) does not reach this application, section 514(d) and the Fitzgerald Act saving argument need not be decided.
