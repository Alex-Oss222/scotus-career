## Judgment and reachability

All eight Associate Justices participate and commit to reverse the Eleventh Circuit and remand for appropriate relief against enforcement of the challenged candidate-testing requirement. Chandler's stated and uncontested intention to run again sustains the prospective controversy; the completed 1994 election does not eliminate that live claim. The Court reaches the granted Fourth Amendment question, not independent First Amendment or state-law qualifications questions. No damages, test result, drug use or individualized candidate disqualification is adjudicated.

## Propositions available for full common joinder

**C1 — Search and voluntary candidacy.** Government-required collection and chemical testing of urine constitute a Fourth Amendment search. Seeking public office does not waive that protection. The candidacy setting may inform the reasonableness inquiry, but consent to public scrutiny of qualifications is not automatic consent to a compelled bodily search.

**C2 — The need must justify this suspicionless program.** A special need beyond ordinary law enforcement can justify dispensing with individualized suspicion when the actual governmental need and operation of the program, considered with the privacy interests and intrusion, make the search reasonable. An interest in preventing drug use or promoting public confidence does not by itself authorize testing any category of citizens. The rule does not require a proven catastrophe, quantified efficacy, exhaustion of every less intrusive alternative or safety-sensitive duties in every conceivable valid program.

**C3 — Georgia's candidate program fails that inquiry.** The State has not shown a need sufficient to justify its advance-scheduled testing of candidates for this broad set of offices. The supplied record shows neither a concrete drug problem among these officeholders nor a common duty furnishing the protective risk that supports suspicionless testing in the cited precedents. General demands for sound judgment, integrity and public confidence remain important; they do not explain why compelled testing of every covered candidate is needed or why this single predictable prequalification test meaningfully addresses the asserted concern. Advance notice and the thirty-day window limit what the certificate establishes about future service. The Court does not find that every scheduled test is useless or that deterrence is never a cognizable interest.

**C4 — Reduced intrusion cannot supply the missing need.** Use of a personal physician and disclosure initially to the candidate reduce the actual intrusion. They do not eliminate bodily and medical privacy or independently justify the search. The Court evaluates the program on those supported safeguards and does not invent public dissemination of positive results, intrusive observation, police use or unrelated medical testing. Public visibility of candidates differs from the custodial responsibility, athletic privacy context and demonstrated protective concerns underlying Vernonia. This holding invalidates the challenged requirement; it neither overrules the earlier special-needs cases nor forecloses a materially different, supported program.

**C5 — Relief.** The final judgment sustaining this testing condition is reversed, with remand for appropriate declaratory or injunctive relief on the live Fourth Amendment challenge. The Court prescribes no substitute testing regime, constitutional candidate qualification or new state election rule. No election already held is set aside.

## Enhanced adversarial comparison

**Affirm by contextual balancing.** Georgia's strongest case combines the responsibility and public visibility of officeholders, deterrence, confidence in government, advance notice, physician-controlled collection and limited disclosure. Vernonia rejects a demand for a confirmed catastrophe or universal least-restrictive-means proof, so lack of documented drug use alone cannot defeat the program. The controlling objection is the combination: broad offices without a shared protective risk, no concrete problem or operational need supplying an alternative ground, and a predictable one-time test weakly connected to the quality of future service. All eight reject affirmance on that combination, not on a single missing factual checkbox.

**Reverse under an absolute suspicion rule or categorical ban on scheduled testing.** Such a rule would resolve this case but conflict with the actual majority rule in Vernonia and extend beyond even the published dissent's recognition of special-needs exceptions. All eight decline it. A scheduled test is not inherently unconstitutional, and the State need not wait for a catastrophe before addressing a supported risk.

**Reverse on the narrower program-specific insufficiency.** C1–C5 preserve the lawful special-needs exceptions while requiring enough reason for this actual search. Its strongest counterargument is that judicial assessment undervalues deterrence and the relatively small intrusion. The answer is bounded: a reduced intrusion still requires a constitutionally adequate governmental justification, and the actual program's fit matters even without mandatory numerical proof. The five Associates who sustained Vernonia can join this ground without renouncing that decision; Stevens, O'Connor and Souter can join it without resolving their broader disagreement there.

## Precedent treatment and source boundary

Skinner supplies urine-search status and attention to substantiated protective need and privacy safeguards. Von Raab supplies contextual special-needs analysis beyond an exclusively catastrophic-risk category. Vernonia is applied for search/no-waiver and distinguished on its actual protective, custodial and athletic facts; its five-Justice rule, eight-Justice facial-prescription ruling and nine-Justice search rule remain distinct. T.L.O.'s school-responsibility rationale does not transfer wholesale to political candidates.
