## 115. Rash: valuation and preserved uncertainty

The proposed plan retains the truck for the debtor's freight-hauling business while Associates retains its lien and receives deferred payments. The claim balance is $41,171.01. The lower courts fixed $31,875 as the secured amount. The lower opinion reports that the debtors' expert derived that figure as seventy-five percent of a $42,500 retail estimate and explained dealer reconditioning and commission costs; Associates advanced $41,000. These are estimates and evidence, not a binding Supreme Court dollar finding.

Section 506(a)'s first sentence identifies the allowed secured portion by the value of the creditor's interest in the estate's interest. Its second sentence directs attention to valuation purpose and proposed disposition or use. Section 1325(a)(5) distinguishes acceptance, retention with lien/present-value protection, and surrender. The question is which market corresponds to the chosen retention/use path. No party has chosen surrender here.

Path A uses the cost of acquiring a comparable truck for the same retained use, adjusted to the actual collateral and relevant market. It gives independent effect to the second sentence and does not subtract a creditor's hypothetical foreclosure expense when no foreclosure is proposed. Replacement value is not automatically a dealer's unadjusted retail sticker price, the value of a brand-new truck, the debtor's entire business earnings, or a new superpriority. Age, condition, relevant market, and items included in a quoted price but not present in the retained collateral remain for evidentiary determination.

Its strongest counterargument is that the first sentence values a security interest, not the debtor's ownership advantage, and that paying replacement cost could shift reorganization value from unsecured creditors. The answer available to Path A is that the statute itself requires valuation in the proposed-use setting, and collateral value is distinct from compensation for delayed foreclosure. Timbers' denial of an undersecured creditor's lost-use interest does not prescribe a liquidation market for confirmation.

Path B affirms the supported foreclosure/wholesale starting point, while allowing case-specific adjustment for use affecting the security. Its strongest support is the creditor-interest wording, state-created lien rights, and the Code's allocation of reorganization benefits. Its strongest obstacle is that it makes ordinary retention and surrender use the same valuation despite Congress's express instruction to consider proposed use. Whether that reading gives the second sentence adequate independent work is the statutory disagreement.

Dewsnup's §506(d) Chapter 7 construction and Nobelman's principal-residence rights rule do not decide this truck valuation. BFP requires an independent federal inquiry in the actual forced-sale setting; its realistic-forced-sale comparator is specific to §548 and an actual foreclosure transfer. It does not prescribe that market for a retained asset. Noland bars replacing enacted distributions through general equitable preference; neither Path A nor B may simply select the most desirable creditor allocation.

The lawful remedy for Path A is reversal and remand for the proper valuation and consequential plan review, without fixing $41,000, ordering liquidation, deciding a rate of interest, or guaranteeing confirmation. Path B affirms the existing valuation judgment. No material historical-source gap prevents either statutory path.

## 116. Hughes Aircraft: temporal operation

The relevant alleged false claims concern 1982–1984 cost allocations; the 1986 amendments took effect October 27, and Schumer filed January 20, 1989. The former §3730(b)(4) required dismissal when the action was based on evidence or information already held by the Government when brought. Government possession before this action is a supplied and lower-opinion-recited predicate; no new finding about the truth of the alleged fraud follows.

The amendment narrowed the private-action bar to defined public disclosures, subject to the Attorney General and original-source qualifications. The definition of original source requires direct and independent knowledge and voluntary provision of the information to the Government before filing. Those exceptions are described to identify the change, not resolved as applied here.

Landgraf's actual temporal-direction/new-consequence sequence controls, with Rivers preserving the distinction between legislative expansion and judicial interpretation. Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas previously adopted their separate relevant-activity/textual-command method. Breyer's Reynoldsville opinion concerns judicial retroactivity and independent remedial rules; it does not convert a new statute into a judicial declaration of what old law always meant.

Path A applies the former bar. No sufficient congressional temporal direction has been identified. Removing the complete defense to this privately initiated action creates new exposure concerning completed claims, despite the Government's preexisting ability to enforce its own claim. A change determining who may bring a previously unavailable action differs from merely moving an existing cause to another forum. The 1989 filing date does not by itself make that exposure prospective.

The strongest contrary path treats the new rule as governing judicial authority exercised after enactment, with no new prohibition because fraud was already unlawful and the United States could already sue. It correctly cautions against assuming all jurisdictional amendments are substantive. Its difficulty is the new relator-initiated action that the former rule foreclosed; a jurisdictional label cannot decide actual temporal operation. The distinction must be stated narrowly, preserving genuinely forum-regulating provisions.

The lawful Path A remedy is reversal and remand for dismissal under the former bar. The Court need not decide the amended public-disclosure/original-source issue, whether loss to the public fisc is necessary, the alleged fraud, damages, or excluded constitutional objections. No finding that Hughes acted lawfully follows. General increased damages under other provisions are not needed to decide the temporal reach of this private-action expansion.

## 117. Mazurek: complete framework and provisional posture

The Ninth Circuit's brief opinion vacated the denial of preliminary relief and remanded; it did not itself adjudicate final invalidity. It identified the District Court's overly restricted purpose inquiry and failure to weigh patient hardships and the State's countervailing hardships. Its purpose discussion uses historical Casey language, which must be conformed to the actual governing Casey/Fargo holdings. The packet also reports later interim protection against enforcement as to Cahill. This summary appellate event does not itself create a new application or authorize a guessed expiration of that protection.

Casey's complete six-step inquiry remains necessary: identify the duty and protected baseline; examine function and objective purpose; identify attributable interference and a stable affected population; apply the correct substantive branch and allocation; preserve clinical and communication safeguards; match relief to the violation. A baseline violation, objectively established obstructive purpose, or substantial obstacle independently invalidates the duty. Remaining severe interference requires compelling permissible justification and narrow tailoring. Remaining meaningful interference requires substantiated permissible justification and fit, including an answer to a concretely identified practicable, substantially less burdensome alternative. Below meaningful interference the heightened access inquiry ends, but prohibited-purpose and independent constitutional claims remain. No numerical facial threshold or mandatory new study is added.

Fargo makes absence of a substantial obstacle insufficient to end review; it requires a supported assessment and prompt ordinary interim consideration. Lambert removes only a specific bypass-wording objection and leaves the complete framework applicable to preserved independent burdens. The historical assertion that Casey categorically approved a physician-only performance rule cannot replace the actual holdings. Casey's physician-delivery holding concerns consent information, not automatic authorization for every nonphysician to perform an abortion.

The relevant neutral facts are Cahill's longstanding supervised practice with state-board approval, her position as the only affected nonphysician provider, the enactment's removal of that eligibility, and the District Court's absence-of-substantial-obstacle finding. Her removal alone does not establish lost capacity, delay, cost, clinical risk, obstructive purpose, or a particular justification tier. Nor does the absence of closure of an entire facility conclusively exclude a burden on patients. Relevant evidence concerns actual supervision, clinical competence, usable physician alternatives, capacity, travel, cost, and the law's incremental operation.

Path A vacates the appellate judgment and returns the provisional assessment for the complete controlling standard, rather than directing automatic reinstatement of the District Court's denial. It neither endorses the Ninth Circuit's existing purpose conclusion as final nor decides likely success under an omitted branch. The District Court can address the record, make appropriate findings, and apply the ordinary interim factors promptly. No final constitutional holding, new automatic injunction, categorical protection for physician-only rules, or statewide relief without a matched showing follows.

The strongest objection is that this record lacks proof of lost access and an appellate remand should not be used to rescue an inadequate showing. The response available to Path A is limited: neither court employed the entire binding standard; lack of an obstacle does not dispose of distinct meaningful/severe interference, and actual clinical/supervision evidence may matter to fit. Challengers retain their duty, population, causation, and triggering-interference burdens. A remand does not establish those facts or permit litigation of unpreserved new claims.

Path B reverses the remand and reinstates denial of preliminary relief because the supplied proof does not establish a legally adequate likelihood/fair-chance showing and the health-qualification choice is reasonable. Kennedy's published reasonable-regulation position and Scalia's and Thomas's published constitutional objections make this an individually supportable dissenting path; those views cannot be represented as the Court's current framework. A narrower version respecting the framework would require a supported determination that all preserved branches fail on the actual record, which the supplied lower decisions do not themselves provide.

The summary disposition must expressly preserve ordinary legal authority over existing interim orders. It should neither assume their duration nor declare that an earlier order automatically survives forever. Further interim relief requires ordinary equitable predicates and a scope matched to the supported violation.

## 118. Crawford-El: proof, discovery, and the unchanged merits

The D.C. Circuit abandoned its direct-only rule, adopted a clear-and-convincing motive quantum, and produced differing views about discovery. The new quantum and a categorical demand for proof before needed discovery must be evaluated separately; a proposal by fewer judges is not automatically part of the lower court's common holding.

Kimberlin's unanimous decision already requires legally competent direct or circumstantial proof and ordinary procedural burdens, preserving specificity, tailored discovery, and the objective clearly-established-law defense. It expressly did not adopt the alternative clear-and-convincing burden. Leatherman's municipal pleading holding remains an analogy with an express reservation about individual-officer immunity pleading. Settle preserves Mt. Healthy's actual causation and same-decision allocation: an official's merely possible innocent explanation is not automatically conclusive.

Path A rejects the special proof quantum. Neither §1983, Harlow, the actual claim, nor the Rules supplies it here. Motive in the constitutional wrong remains distinct from the objective immunity defense. New York Times' protection for speech from defamation liability does not itself establish the same heightened quantum for damages against an official accused of retaliation. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby makes summary-judgment assessment responsive to an independently applicable substantive burden; it does not authorize courts to invent that burden.

Path B would treat the costs and deterrent effects of motive litigation as grounds for a higher quantum or a categorical pre-discovery evidence requirement. Its strongest argument is Harlow's protection from litigation costs, not merely liability. Its obstacle is Kimberlin's specific separation of motive elements from immunity and its retention of ordinary procedural burdens. The case-management alternative is concrete: decide dispositive legal immunity first; require identification of challenged conduct and nonconclusory factual support; use rule-authorized clarification, staged discovery, protective orders, and Rule 56's lawful procedures. Unsupported allegations do not justify unlimited discovery, but a court may not demand proof obtainable only through properly targeted necessary discovery as a categorical price of obtaining it.

The remand concerns the live retaliation claim and the legal proof/discovery directions. It orders neither trial nor a particular deposition, and decides no protected press-conference entitlement, motive, personal participation, causation, compensable injury, damages, or ultimate immunity. Separate previously resolved access-to-courts and due-process claims are not revived. There is no supported independent ground on this handoff for finally rejecting the retaliation claim.
