* **Reasoning Below:** The Special Master recommended use of the Convention’s normal baseline rather than Alaska’s ten-mile rule.

* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** Abnormal temporary submergence of a stable island presents a different question.

## 126. United States v. Alaska, No. 84, Original

The United States filed the original action in 1979 concerning Beaufort Sea mineral leasing; Alaska counterclaimed to quiet title to coastal submerged lands within the Petroleum Reserve and Arctic Wildlife Range. ### Fringing-island claim: competing historical materials

Alaska claims a general publicly announced federal practice, from the early twentieth century until 1971, treating waters behind near-fringing islands as inland when entrances do not exceed ten geographical miles. It does not claim historic title to Stefansson Sound through specific continuous federal assertions of sovereignty and foreign acquiescence. Its theory is that the general practice defined the coastline at statehood; it also asserts preclusion based on the Alabama/Mississippi boundary litigation. The Master catalogued diplomatic and domestic-boundary materials from 1903–1971 and found the asserted general practice intricate and insufficiently definite to require Alaska's proposed classification (Report 55). Alaska objects that he gave undue weight to minor variations and contradictions.

The competing materials include the United States' 1930 Hague codification proposals. The assimilation proposal would add small enclaves of high sea to territorial water where a line of no more than four nautical miles closed the enclave. Its separate straits proposal distinguished a strait connecting two high seas, whose entrances were less than six nautical miles, from a channel to an inland sea, to which ten-mile bay-closing principles would apply. These are the propositions in the proposed instruments; whether they establish or conflict with Alaska's asserted general practice is the disputed inference. ## Standards and Tests selection: Modification of institutional consent decrees

### Modification of institutional consent decrees

**Governing question:** When Rule 60(b)(5) permits modification of a continuing institutional consent decree and how the change must be framed.

**Current rule:** The party seeking modification must prove a significant change in fact or law making continued enforcement inequitable. An unforeseeable catastrophe is unnecessary, but actual anticipation and acceptance of the relevant risk impose a heavy burden to justify relief. Dissatisfaction, a new administration, or the availability of a cheaper constitutional alternative alone does not suffice. Once the threshold is met, the modification must be suitably tailored to the proven change and preserve lawful protection of the decree's remaining purposes; the court may not simply rewrite the decree to the constitutional minimum. Practical administration and cost may inform the selection of lawful relief but cannot substitute for the threshold showing or authorize unconstitutional conditions. A clarification that the Constitution permits an alternative practice does not by itself eliminate a voluntarily accepted stricter obligation.

**Authority by component:**

- Significant change, inequity, movant's burden, and significance of accepted risk: Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail — Threshold for modifying an institutional consent decree, January 15, 1992.
- Responsive modification, preservation of remaining purposes, and constitutional and financial limits: Rufo — Fit and limits of the proposed modification, January 15, 1992. Both components are controlled by Stone's opinion joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; O'Connor's remand vote does not join that rationale.

**Present operation effective:** Rufo, January 15, 1992; additional covered-prison-relief conditions from PLRA, April 26, 1996, as applied in Lewis v. Casey, June 24, 1996.


**Sequence or decisionmaker:** The decree remains binding until lawfully modified; a request for modification does not itself suspend its obligations.

**Holdings navigation:** Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail; Rapone v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, 502 U.S. 367 (1992) — Prospective equitable relief; Lewis v. Casey, 518 U.S. 343 (1996).

**Exceptions and limits:** For covered prison-conditions prospective relief, the original §3626 conditions and separate termination/stay mechanisms apply alongside this general rule; see Prison-conditions prospective relief and Prison-conditions termination motions and automatic stays. PLRA §§802–804 — Source, authority and operative text (§802, April 26, 1996) and Lewis v. Casey — Holdings IV–VI (June 24, 1996) preserve that additional statutory operation. A pending Rule 60 request alone is not substituted for the statute's actual motion and timing requirements. Modification under Rule 60(b)(5) concerns the continuing prospective operation of an existing institutional decree following a demonstrated significant change in fact or law. It does not establish a legislative power to compel reinstatement of a completed private damages or limitations judgment, nor does it decide whether a separate valid statutory remedy may provide collateral relief from such a judgment.


## Standards and Tests selection: Civil forfeiture and criminal punishment for Double Jeopardy

### Civil forfeiture and criminal punishment for Double Jeopardy

**Governing question:** Classification of forfeiture under §§881(a)(6), 881(a)(7) and 981(a)(1)(A) for successive-punishment purposes.

**Current rule:** First determine whether Congress intended civil proceedings. Civil intent can be overcome only by the clearest proof that the scheme is so punitive in purpose or effect as to be criminal. Neither a civil label nor proceeding against property supplies an irrebuttable exemption. Deterrence, burden on an owner, connection to crime and owner defenses alone do not establish the demanding contrary showing. Forfeiture’s nonpunitive functions include removing facilitating property, discouraging unlawful use and recovering actual illicit proceeds; it is not universally limited to reimbursing Government costs under Halper’s method for fixed personal civil penalties. Excessive Fines coverage does not itself establish Double Jeopardy criminal punishment; Austin’s distinct protection remains.

**Authority by component:**

- Civil-intent/clearest-proof test and separate clause classification: I. These civil forfeiture proceedings do not constitute a second criminal punishment; II. Excessive Fines coverage does not itself establish Double Jeopardy punishment, 1996-06-24; O’Connor with Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer (five).

**Present operation effective:** Ursery and United States Currency, 1996-06-24.

**Exceptions and limits:** Halper’s own setting and Kurth Ranch’s extraordinary-tax rule survive. Statutory nexus, provenance, ownership, owner defenses and excessiveness remain independent. Whether punitive civil proceedings place a person in jeopardy, offense identity under Blockburger/retained Grady and coordinated-proceeding treatment remain unadjudicated.

**Holdings navigation:** United States v. Ursery; United States v. $405,089.23 in United States Currency, 518 U.S. 267 (1996).


## Standards and Tests selection: Continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity-acquittal predicate ends

### Continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity-acquittal predicate ends

**Governing question:** Whether a State may continue psychiatric confinement under insanity-acquittee authority after conceding that the acquittee is no longer mentally ill.

**Current rule:** The State may not continue that confinement solely because the acquittee cannot prove harmlessness. The acquittal supplies neither a suspended criminal sentence nor indefinite psychiatric authority after the illness predicate disappears. Continued psychiatric custody requires constitutionally adequate proceedings establishing its own proper grounds; ordinary civil commitment requires current mental illness and dangerousness proved by clear and convincing evidence, and additional procedure cannot supply a missing substantive ground. A legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure an indefinite psychiatric regime requiring a no-longer-mentally-ill acquittee to prove nondangerousness. Any independently lawful restraint must rest on its own authority and procedures; the holding does not prescribe a universal preventive-detention burden or scrutiny tier.

**Authority by component:**
- Ended predicate and absence of punitive authority: Foucha v. Louisiana, “1. The insanity judgment no longer authorizes this psychiatric confinement”, May 18, 1992; White Part II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter (six).
- Appropriate new grounds and procedures; ordinary civil-commitment burden: Foucha, “2. Psychiatric custody requires appropriate grounds and procedures,” May 18, 1992; White Part II-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter (five). Addington's existing burden is retained within that channel.
- Limits of the asserted preventive alternative: Foucha, “3. Public safety does not sustain this unbounded dangerousness regime,” May 18, 1992; White Part II-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter (six).

**Present operation effective:** Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992.

**Open question:** Materially narrower preventive regimes, maximum duration of an otherwise lawful commitment and the scope of state insanity defenses remain undecided. The equal-protection plurality supplies no controlling rule.

**Holdings navigation:** Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71 (1992) — Psychiatric confinement.

### Private religious viewpoints in public forums and printing benefits

**Governing question:** Viewpoint-neutral access for private religious expression and the distinct establishment inquiry in the forums and benefits actually opened.

**Current rule:** When a public school admits private family-life and child-rearing discussion to an after-hours forum, it cannot exclude an otherwise eligible presentation solely for its religious viewpoint, even assuming a nonpublic forum. Neutral restrictions outside the forum's actual subjects remain possible. Equal access to voluntary, publicly accessible after-hours films without school sponsorship has a secular forum purpose, does not primarily advance religion as governmental activity and causes no excessive entanglement. Chabad separately holds that an interior capitol location does not establish religion when actual context makes the expression clearly private. In a generally available, privately edited student-publication program, a religious editorial viewpoint cannot exclude an otherwise eligible publication from printing support. Its secular expression purpose, broad neutral availability, independent attribution and controlled vendor payments together permit that benefit; neutrality alone does not authorize devotional financing or governmental worship.

## Standards and Tests selection: Program-wide athletic opportunity and independent sex-classification review

### Program-wide athletic opportunity and independent sex-classification review

**Governing question:** Title IX accommodation and constitutional review during public-university athletic restructuring.

**Current rule:** Title IX does not guarantee a particular varsity team or sport-for-sport counterparts. Lawful separate teams remain subject to equal opportunity. Effective accommodation may be shown by any one of substantial proportionality, a history and continuing responsive expansion for the underrepresented sex, or full and effective accommodation of that sex’s interests and abilities; other regulatory duties remain independently binding. A public university’s sex-conscious restructuring separately must serve an actual important objective and be substantially related to it. Genuine fiscal or programmatic change may preserve opportunities against demonstrated inequality, but ratios, budget pressure or attempted federal compliance alone are insufficient. Stereotypes, pretext and measures materially broader than demonstrated need are forbidden.

## Standards and Tests selection: Boundary-preserving conditions for artificial coastal extension

### Boundary-preserving conditions for artificial coastal extension

**Governing question:** The Corps' authority under Rivers and Harbors Act §10, 33 U.S.C. §403, to protect federal submerged-land rights when permitting an artificial coastal extension.

**Current rule:** Section 10 permits consideration of an artificial extension's prospective effect on federal submerged-land rights and permits conditioning approval on preservation of the existing federal-state boundary for additional entitlement attributable to the project. The Submerged Lands Act does not compel unconditional approval or invalidate such a condition when existing state property and claims remain intact. The condition must address the same boundary harm supporting withholding of approval; this holding supplies no power to demand unrelated existing territory or approve different conditions.

The source adopts this construction through the Nome condition's stated limits; it does not grant unconditional approval to all future disclaimers.

**Present operation effective:** Alaska, April 21, 1992.
