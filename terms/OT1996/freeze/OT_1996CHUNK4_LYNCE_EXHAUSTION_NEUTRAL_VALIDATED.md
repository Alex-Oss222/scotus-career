## What the new sources establish

The district answer expressly asserted nonexhaustion and expressly refused waiver. The answer nevertheless said the State would agree that exhaustion was futile if the facts and issues concerned only the retroactive cancellation addressed in Griffin and Langley. It identified Lynce's earlier release as potentially supporting **additional** reincarceration claims requiring factual determinations and state-law interpretation. In its transition to the merits, that same answer described the retroactive-cancellation Ex Post Facto theory as petitioner's sole claim. Both parts must be retained: the source does not establish a blanket district waiver, and it does not describe an independently pursued due-process or state-law reincarceration count.

Petitioner's merits footnote characterizes the State's position as conceding futility for the central cancellation question and invokes the Florida Supreme Court's adverse decisions. Respondent's later merits footnote affirmatively states that petitioner elected to proceed solely on his Ex Post Facto claim. Its accompanying discussion distinguishes the other constitutional or state-law claims that might have followed reincarceration and argues that petitioner is now foreclosed from pursuing them. The election is affirmative source evidence about the claim actually pursued. The asserted preclusion of every possible other claim remains the State's legal argument, not an additional holding supplied by this packet.

Thus this is **not a demonstrated mixed petition merely because other legal theories were conceivable**. No supplementary due-process or state-law theory may be manufactured to create a mixed-petition problem. Equally, dropping or declining those theories does not by itself prove that the remaining federal claim was actually presented through Florida's available procedures. The sources acknowledge that Lynce did not exhaust and advance a reason for excusing it. They establish neither a later state-court exhaustion event nor an express current appellate waiver.

The new extraction provides genuine litigation-conduct evidence. It is unnecessary to infer what the State did from the absence of an appellate file. The legally significant question is how the State's affirmative, qualified concession and its later account of the sole claim fit together, not whether silence can be converted into waiver.

## Date-eligible legal limits

The former §2254(b)–(c), quoted in the original answer, bars granting habeas relief absent exhaustion, an absence of available state corrective process, or circumstances making that process ineffective. It distinguishes an unavailable remedy from an available procedure for raising the question. A merits ruling in another prisoner's case does not establish that Lynce personally completed state review.

*Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 510, 520–522 (1982), requires dismissal of a petition containing both exhausted and unexhausted claims and permits the prisoner to proceed with the exhausted claims after removing the others. Its mixed-petition rule concerns claims actually asserted. It neither forces a prisoner to litigate every conceivable reincarceration theory nor supplies exhaustion of the sole remaining claim. The Court's holding does not automatically adjudicate the future availability of claims omitted here. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/455/509).

*Granberry v. Greer*, 481 U.S. 129, 131–136 (1987), establishes that exhaustion is not a jurisdictional limit on appellate consideration. Its specific discretionary holding addresses a State that failed to raise nonexhaustion in district court; it rejects both automatic dismissal and automatic waiver in that posture. Relevant comity considerations include unresolved state-law or factual questions and unnecessary additional proceedings. Lynce's express district objection distinguishes that posture. Granberry cannot be cited as holding that an expressly maintained, applicable defense may always be disregarded whenever a court wishes to reach the merits. Its nonjurisdictional rule does establish that source validation need not treat exhaustion as an unexplained absence of adjudicative power. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/481/129); [parallel primary text](https://openjurist.org/481/us/129).

*Duckworth v. Serrano*, 454 U.S. 1, 3–4 (1981), rejects a new exception based merely on an apparently clear constitutional violation and judicial economy. It distinguishes the lack of an opportunity for state redress or a clearly deficient corrective process. Its footnote also distinguishes repetitive applications after completed exhaustion from first presentation of an unexhausted claim. Canonical Morales's force on the merits therefore cannot itself dispense with §2254. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/454/1).

*Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 130 (1982), rejects predicted futility as cause for a state procedural default: even a court that previously rejected a constitutional argument may reconsider it. That is a procedural-default holding, not a determination that Lynce has defaulted under an identified Florida rule. It nevertheless defeats a casual general proposition that adverse precedent alone always excuses presentation. No procedural default or cause-and-prejudice issue is created here without an applicable state procedural bar. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/456/107).

Petitioner's cited *Schall v. Martin*, 467 U.S. 253, 261 (1984), reports the district court's conclusion that an identical challenge had already been rejected by the highest state court, making exhaustion futile. The cited passage is a procedural recitation, not an express Supreme Court holding establishing a universal adverse-precedent exception. It gives the petitioner's argument a specific date-eligible analogue, but cannot erase Duckworth or establish that every new prisoner's claim is identical. [Primary text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/467/253).

The 1996 Act must not be silently replaced by current law or a later decision on temporal application. Section 104 retains the unavailable/ineffective-process exceptions, permits denial on the merits notwithstanding nonexhaustion, and adds that a State is not deemed to waive or be estopped absent counsel's express waiver. This proceeding was already in the federal courts in 1995. This supplement makes no general ruling on applying the amendment to pending cases. The narrow analysis below does not need an implied-waiver holding under either version. If a proposed rationale relies on a difference between the versions, temporal applicability becomes a distinct legal question requiring date-eligible support. [Public Law 104-132, §104, 110 Stat. 1218–1219](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ132/pdf/PLAW-104publ132.pdf).

## Narrow threshold analysis available on this record

**A claim-specific route to merits consideration is supported for independent legal evaluation.** A court can examine whether the State's affirmative futility concession applies to the sole cancellation claim once the possible additional reincarceration theories are excluded. That approach uses the content and limits of the State's actual submissions. It need not assert that the State remained silent, that it expressly waived every exhaustion objection, that all adverse precedent makes exhaustion futile, or that a meritorious claim is exempt from exhaustion.

For that route, the necessary explanation is concrete: the federal question pursued is retroactive cancellation of release credit; the State affirmatively agreed that further state proceedings on that common issue would be futile; its identified reason for nevertheless demanding exhaustion concerned additional fact- and state-law-dependent reincarceration theories; and its merits submission expressly confirms that those theories are not pursued. The court must determine that the claim it decides stays within that concession and that accepting the concession is consistent with the statutory exception and comity. The State's legal concession is relevant but does not bind the Court to an erroneous construction of §2254.

This reasoning has a genuine opposing reading: the district answer's initial qualification referred to prisoners still in custody, expressly distinguished Lynce, listed available procedures, and ended with a refusal to waive. A Justice could understand that language as preserving an exhaustion objection even after the other theories are excluded, or could conclude that agreement about predicted failure on the merits does not establish an ineffective state process. The later statement of claim election confirms scope; standing alone it does not expressly withdraw every exhaustion defense. The record supports assessing these competing legal readings. It does not permit reporting one as an already adjudicated waiver.

**A narrower further-proceedings route remains available if the claim-specific reading is rejected or cannot be resolved.** Exhaustion need not be made into a missing-facts fiction: the court may retain or restore the case for determination of the actual statutory predicate before any grant of the writ. If a reviewing court addresses a separable error in the lower court's categorical merits approach, it must explain why limited appellate consideration is proper under the nonjurisdictional doctrine and preserve the exhaustion issue for resolution before habeas relief. A remand label alone is not a license to evade a mandatory predicate or to issue a constitutional ruling otherwise unnecessary to the disposition. Alternatively, a threshold-only remand can leave the merits open. Neither route assumes an existing waiver or itself orders release.

**A merits denial and a grant of relief have different statutory consequences.** If a court chooses a lawful merits-denial path, denial notwithstanding nonexhaustion has explicit support in the 1996 text and a pre-amendment analogue in Granberry's discussion of plainly nonmeritorious petitions. That does not authorize granting relief on the same assumed exhaustion premise. This packet selects neither path and supplies no view about the strength of Lynce's Ex Post Facto merits.

## Scope of any reached merits and precise remaining gaps

Canonical Cavanaugh and Morales concern meaningful release consideration and punishment effects, with their own remedy limits. Cavanaugh's opportunity-only civil-rights route does not convert a challenge seeking restored credits and shorter confinement into a claim categorically free of habeas exhaustion. Morales's rule also does not establish the continuity of every Florida credit statute. Their relevance to this claim remains as stated in the corrected neutral packet: the administrative purpose of credits is distinct from their effect on confinement; offense-date entitlement, later statutory changes, and the protected amount require their own analysis.

The present sources are sufficient to model the narrow scope/concession issue without inventing filings, a procedural default, or a new broad exhaustion rule. No additional original document is indispensable merely to identify that supported route. There is, however, **no verified categorical waiver finding**. If a proposed disposition specifically requires an express appellate abandonment or express waiver, the indispensable missing item is the affirmative dated passage establishing it; the existing extracts do not do so. If relief instead depends on independently establishing absent or ineffective state corrective process beyond the concession, the needed proposition is why the identified Florida procedures cannot provide meaningful review of this exact sole claim, rather than merely why petitioner expects to lose. That requires bounded primary support, not an inference from the file inventory.

Remaining credit calculations, statutory-continuity disputes, and the separate unchallenged administrative-credit cancellation retain the corrected packet's scope and may be left to proper further proceedings. Do not enlarge the case to other reincarceration claims in order to create an exhaustion obstacle, and do not erase a supported obstacle merely to reach the merits. The final neutral/model context must state which legal reading and scope it adopts before applying the merits authorities.
