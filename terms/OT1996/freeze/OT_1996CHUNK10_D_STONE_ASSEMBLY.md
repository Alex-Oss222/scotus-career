## B. ANSWERS AND REASONING

**Question 4 — jurisdiction first.** Stone would require Article III before deciding the disputed statutory merits. His jurisdictional commitment concerns the Court's actual authority, not an intuition that the statutory question is easier. A substantial cause-of-action dispute is not automatically jurisdictional merely because the enforcing subsection also mentions courts' jurisdiction. He would distinguish a permissible threshold jurisdiction ruling from issuing a binding merits answer while assuming constitutional power.

**Question 2 — informational injury.** Stone would recognize deprivation of an individually held statutory informational right as the holder's injury where the law supplies a remedy; he would not demand an additional separate loss merely to relabel that invasion as concrete. Impaired actual use in protecting health, planning activity, or participating in a legally relevant process independently demonstrates the practical interest here. An interest in seeing regulations obeyed is insufficient. He would require CBE to identify the affected interests through litigation-stage-appropriate allegations or evidence, distinguishing a personal informational entitlement from abstract compliance. Lujan's actual majority limits govern the Court; any interpretation requiring extra injury beyond invasion of such an individually held right would conflict with Stone's expressly stated jurisdiction position, which he would identify as a proposed departure. This injury position does not supply redressability for a completed violation.

**Question 3 — redressability.** Treasury penalties do not redress a completed personal information loss merely by punishing a violator. Deterrence can matter to a shown continuing or imminent injury, but the all-reports-filed record supplies no automatic recurrence finding. Investigation and litigation costs incurred to establish a violation do not themselves manufacture jurisdiction over penalties. The verified complaint contains no continuing-violation or imminent-recurrence allegation. Future inspection and report-copy orders therefore do not repair the lost timeliness; a declaration does not resolve a live dispute over the acknowledged past default; the Treasury penalties do not compensate CBE; and the invoked cost-shifting provision does not create a separate compensatory claim for information-gathering losses. Stone would dismiss this complaint for lack of redressability, not remand to search for an injury that was not alleged.

**Question 1 — complete proposed statutory answer, conditional on jurisdiction.** If a live controversy is established, Stone would read EPCRA's reference to statutory reporting requirements to include timely submission, not submission at any convenient date. Its own wording differs from Gwaltney, and the 60-day notice provision supplies a chance to cure and for agency enforcement without expressly extinguishing every accrued violation. He would therefore reject a categorical statutory bar to past reporting defaults while enforcing notice, governmental-enforcement bars and legally authorized remedies. This is the user's approved conditional statutory position, not a merits holding Stone would issue in a jurisdictionally deficient case.

## C. PRECISE PROPOSED DISPOSITION

Vacate the Seventh Circuit's merits judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss this complaint for lack of Article III redressability. Issue no binding EPCRA merits determination, penalty, or injunction. The approved conditional answer to Question 1 remains a stated legal position for a properly justiciable case, not an advisory holding in this one. This result follows the verified complaint and Stone's own injury-and-remedy commitments; it does not import the later historical judgment as governing precedent.
