No universal choice among injury, injury-discovery and injury-and-pattern-discovery accrual is necessary to these conclusions. The initial purchase loss is not revived under any label. Nor does this handoff find every later cow illness, feed loss or repair expense independently actionable. A new period attaches only to the properly established injury and applicable accrual facts; it is not a four-year lookback into an otherwise revived old claim.

### Supported competing paths

**Revival versus separate accrual.** The broad last-act theory ties limitations to completion or continuation of an alleged pattern. Its strongest argument is that RICO condemns a pattern rather than isolated acts and may require later acts before a pattern is knowable. Agency Holding's Clayton Act analogy and §1964(c)'s injury requirement nevertheless supply the decisive objection: once a particular claim is actionable and discoverable under an otherwise applicable rule, unrelated later acts cannot indefinitely renew it. Whether pattern discovery is part of a universal trigger remains open. The separate-accrual route addresses a different event, an independently caused injury, and so does not depend on accepting revival.

**Affirmance versus bounded remand.** Respondents' strongest record-based position is that poor feed, animal-health problems and falling milk output were recurring consequences of the failed 1974 silo, observable long before the limitations boundary. Later advertising, repairs and continued use do not alone contradict that account. Petitioners' strongest preserved alternative is that later representations induced later use or spending and caused new, avoidable losses. The lower court's same-source/type/pattern classification cannot settle that causal issue merely by naming those common features. On the supplied summary-judgment posture, the Associates choose correction of that legal classification with ordinary review of the actual causal showing, rather than a Supreme Court finding for either side on unrecovered tables. The remand may lawfully end in renewed judgment if the evidence shows only continuing effects or fails the pertinent causal and temporal requirements.

**Concealment and speculative damages.** Bailey and Holmberg require reasonable diligence; repeated assurances may bear on what reasonable inquiry would disclose but are not a substitute for diligence. An untimely purchase claim does not become timely because the farmer later calculates its aggregate size. Conversely, Zenith's later-provable-damages alternative cannot be rejected merely by restating the prohibition on a new predicate reviving old loss. Its applicability requires its actual predicates, not a general assertion that all future loss was speculative.

The six-Justice nominal position is a legal permission on proved predicates, not an obligatory award for every permanent injury. Existing findings about crane training, sustained work, physical demands, work availability, wages and job-loss risk must be considered in their actual administrative setting. If findings under the correct standard suffice, there is no automatic duty to hear new evidence. Any later substantial award remains subject to §22's enacted grounds and timing. True §8(i) settlements remain distinct; this award was not one. No estoppel holding, present benefits amount or new statutory deadline is adopted.

### Competing statutory paths

**Integrated future-capacity construction.** Section 8(h)'s direction to consider natural future effects makes prospective injury-related economic vulnerability part of the capacity assessment. Actual wages establish capacity only when they fairly and reasonably represent it. A supported significant potential of later loss can therefore justify a nominal award even though the existing economic effect cannot yet be measured as an ordinary periodic award. Section 22 explains the importance of maintaining a lawful modification route; it does not itself create the risk predicate. The strongest objection is the payment formula: a nominal dollar must not become a judicial exception to the requirement of economic disability. The six answer that the significant future vulnerability is the legally relevant capacity impairment expressly contemplated by §8(h), rather than a payment for bodily impairment or administrative convenience.

**Present-loss/formula construction.** Scalia and Thomas read §§2(10) and 8(c)(21) as requiring an earning incapacity that supports compensation under the enacted formula; a possibility of future incapacity is not present incapacity. Section 8(h) helps estimate actual capacity and future effects within that statutory determination, but does not authorize an arbitrary nominal payment after capacity has been found undiminished. Their strongest difficulty is the combination of the future-effects text and §22's limit, which may close the route before the loss occurs. They regard that limit as a legislative choice rather than authority to create compensation whose only operative work is preserving jurisdiction. Their position does not treat high wages as conclusive when they misrepresent capacity or eliminate the employer's burden under Rambo I.

**Proof quantum.** Both statutory positions accept Greenwich Collieries as current law. For the six who adopt the new significant-risk category, persuasion concerns whether that category has been established, not whether its possible future event is itself more probable than not. A well-proved significant risk can be below one-half; an unsupported assertion of possibility can fail despite permanent impairment. This distinction does not establish an actuarial percentage or an added multi-factor test.

## 125. O'Dell v. Netherland, No. 96-6867

The preserved Teague issue is properly before the Court. O'Dell's finality date is October 3, 1988. Current Graham, Gilmore, Caspari, Simmons, Allridge, Gray and Lambrix govern how that finality inquiry is performed, but later decisions are not placed into the body of precedent available in 1988. For the five, the remand is not an invitation to disregard the record's actual distinctions. O'Dell's statement that sixteen additional years on an earlier parole violation effectively meant life at his age, and that he would never get out, is not an accurate explanation of this conviction's legally mandatory parole exclusion. It is nevertheless part of what the jury heard and must be considered when assessing the complete presentation and effect. Refusal of a requested instruction alone is not structural error. The verdict found both dangerousness and vileness; neither vileness nor evidence of danger in prison proves harmlessness by itself. No exception to Simmons for prison-danger arguments is created.

The five do not select a universal collateral harmless-error rule. Brecht applies Chapman to its preserved Doyle claim; O'Neal extends it to its identified personal-intent claim; Gray found the actual injury harmful under both competing approaches. None mechanically decides this additional category. The lower courts must resolve any necessary category-specific standard under those actual precedents, or a supported outcome under both, before relief; they may not import a historical general Brecht standard. This handoff neither finds harm under both standards nor directs use of a prisoner prejudice burden as settled local law. Gray's genuinely applicable and unadjudicated statutory conditions remain separate. No hypothetical condition is invented and no genuinely applicable one is deemed satisfied.

### Adversarial comparison

**Old-rule route.** Gardner and Skipper protected the ability to answer material reasons advanced for death. When danger is the asserted reason and ordinary parole is legally impossible under the only noncapital sentence, withholding the accurate explanation defeats that protected function. The strongest contrary argument is that the proposed response describes the law of punishment rather than a fact about the defendant, and that Ramos preserved state latitude over sentencing information. The five answer that the difference does not change the function: this is an accurate, legally fixed response to the State's own future-danger presentation, not a demand for speculative release forecasting or a universal instruction. Ramos permitted truthful commutation information; it did not authorize depriving the defendant of this responsive opportunity. Gray and Lambrix reinforce the required functional comparison without independently supplying 1988 law.

**New-rule route.** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the force of the rebuttal analogy but see a material legal extension. Gardner concerned undisclosed adverse information, and Skipper excluded factual evidence bearing on future conduct. Neither fixed the boundary between a State's sentencing-law instructions and a defendant's evidentiary response. Ramos's recognition of latitude makes that distinct obligation not compelled at finality. Their conclusion rests on the substantive legal/factual distinction, not the mere later date of Simmons or disagreement among courts. Current Simmons remains binding prospectively within its actual scope. Gray's last-minute evidentiary preparation injury and Lambrix's consequential jury-guidance holding do not eliminate all distinctions among later procedural applications.

**Exceptions if new.** The rule regulates selection of an otherwise permitted punishment rather than removing conduct, persons or punishment from state power. Accurate parole information may materially improve sentencing deliberation, but that importance alone does not make this defined disclosure/rebuttal protection a watershed procedure essential to the fundamental accuracy and fairness of the criminal process under Teague, Sawyer, Gilmore and Caspari. The three reject both exceptions on this claim; they do not abolish the watershed category. The five need no alternative exception holding because their old-rule conclusion is independently sufficient.

### Competing paths and exact qualifications

**Fringing-island practice.** Alaska's best argument is the accumulated public federal treatment of enclosed waters, including the Chapman Line, Webb letter, Gulf litigation submissions and 1961 Solicitor General letter, reinforced by the general-practice language in the 1985 opinion. That material cannot be dismissed by demanding a Stefansson-specific historic-title claim Alaska does not make. The contrary path nevertheless prevails because the 1930 assimilation proposal used four-mile closing lines, the distinct high-sea straits proposal used six-mile entrances and the inland-sea proposal used ten-mile bay principles; the later materials retained distinctions and did not uniformly reduce to the asserted rule. The 1949 submissions and Alaska's description of assimilation do not cure those differences. The Chapman Line lacks a contemporaneous exclusively ten-mile explanation, and the Webb letter distinguishes island belts and forms of strait. The Gulf concessions concerned specified waters. The 1985 Court found actual historic sovereignty and acquiescence concerning Mississippi Sound, expressly leaving other theories undecided. Its general-policy language was part of that evidentiary discussion, not a necessary determination of Alaska's asserted universal rule. This preserves Mississippi Sound's title and the force of the later decrees. No general power to contract existing state territory is recognized.

**Dinkum.** Alaska's best textual argument is that Article 10(1) adds no explicit permanency adjective and normal coastal boundaries can change with natural conditions. Older surveys, charts and observed emergence supply factual support. The competing reading gives effect to the definition's high-tide condition as an ordinary characteristic, while separating tide level from seasonal movement of the formation itself. The Master found frequent below-datum conditions; the joint monitoring survey found no surveyed elevation above the datum, with two uncertain readings possibly affected by disturbance; later seasonal observations do not establish an ordinarily above-datum island. Those findings, rather than an invented absolute-stability requirement, support rejection. Natural accretion and an otherwise enduring island's exceptional submergence remain outside this holding.

**Petroleum Reserve.** Utah Division requires clear inclusion and clear intent to defeat future state title as distinct matters. Alaska correctly objects that a generic federal purpose, a drawn boundary or retained legislative jurisdiction alone does not satisfy both. Here the unusual, specific ocean-side boundary expressly encloses the identified lagoons and tidelands, and the petroleum reservation requires control of the petroleum-bearing estate. Congress then singled out the naval petroleum reserve in preserving its exclusive federal legislative setting over federally owned military lands at admission. Read with the identified reservation, this is specific confirmation of continued federal retention of that estate, rather than the generic land-disposal language found insufficient in Utah Division. Section 11(b) is not redescribed as an express submerged-title clause. Its particular operation and reference, together with the established inclusion and reservation purpose, supply the inference of title retention. The result therefore does not depend on holding that the Pickett Act independently authorizes every executive withdrawal of submerged lands. The order's unrecovered opening sentence is not supplied or used to enlarge the excerpted boundary.

**Arctic Range.** The six's strongest path reads §6(e)'s alternative phrase 'otherwise set apart' to reach a legally operative prestatehood segregation for the identified refuge purpose, even though the final reservation order came in 1960. The proposed boundary's change to extreme low water and the polar-bear, fox, seal and whale habitat justification support submerged inclusion and an enduring wildlife purpose. The regulation's specified disposal list, its limitation to the effects the proposed reservation would have, and its preservation of administrative jurisdiction remain operative: temporary segregation is not a completed final refuge designation or a transfer of administrative control. Those limits nevertheless leave a present legal segregation for the identified purpose. Section 6(e)'s specific wildlife exception supplies the continuation at statehood that generic administrative segregation alone would lack.

Scalia and Thomas's strongest contrary route treats the difference between lands segregated while a refuge is contemplated and lands set apart as a refuge as decisive. The application reserved a later determination whether all or only some described land was necessary. The regulation protected that prospective choice against listed public-land dispositions without converting the requested reserve into an existing refuge, and preserved administrative jurisdiction. Under Utah Division, a preliminary withdrawal from public-land disposal does not itself defeat equal-footing title; 'otherwise set apart' covers effective forms of refuge establishment beyond a formal withdrawal, not every pending application. Their reading gives the phrase work without treating final designation as surplusage. The six answer that it would underread an already effective, purpose-specific protective segregation, but that difference remains unresolved between these coalitions. The 1960 order does not retrospectively create the 1959 predicate for either side.

Alaska's absence of a separate exception to the Master's geographic discussion is not treated as a waiver of every inclusion/intent argument offered to defend its favorable Range recommendation. The six accept the supported geographic findings on their substance. Scalia and Thomas find timing legally dispositive and need not reverse those findings to favor Alaska. No 1980 enlargement enters the prestatehood inquiry.
