## Strongest substantive counterargument

Kennedy's full Allegheny writing, joined by Scalia, permits noncoercive acknowledgment and rejects making mere government ownership or religious content dispositive. Its city-hall-cross example concerns an extreme, permanent use placing governmental weight behind religious proselytizing; its Friedman citation is not a holding invalidating every seal. Those limitations give the City's composite historical-emblem argument real force. Actual Pinette and Chabad reservations about government adoption likewise identify when their private-access rationale no longer answers the case; they do not independently prove that every adopted symbol violates the Clause.

The reconciled R3 rests on the additional institutional function: the City makes this freestanding Christian symbol one of the selected identifiers of present government throughout its continuing ordinary operations. That sustained official sectarian identification is the bounded extreme that the three accept, even assuming historical purpose and no church participation. It is not a seasonal acknowledgment, display of a particular historical artifact, or an observer's mistaken attribution of someone else's speech. A specific proselytizing inscription and graphic predominance are possible evidence, not universal prerequisites to that official-identification conclusion. The fresh facts sharpen the source limits without supplying a new forbidden motive or deciding comparative graphic prominence.
