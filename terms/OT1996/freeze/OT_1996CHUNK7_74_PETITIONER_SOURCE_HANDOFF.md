# Brown — petitioner merits and reply source handoff

This handoff attributes party submissions and their cited record locations. It supplies no judicial resolution, Justice position or preferred outcome. The complete petitioner merits brief and reply were read; the other filings and underlying evidence require the separate assigned source reader's coverage.

## Procedure and alternative training theory

Petitioner merits pages 8–10 expressly identify distinct jury interrogatories finding deliberate indifference in the County's hiring policy and in its training policy, each in the case of Burns; they cite the petition appendix at 40a–41a. The brief reports County motions at the close of the plaintiff's case and at the close of all evidence, denied, and charge objections at JA 128a–132a. It reports the first appellate opinion's rejection of training liability and the substituted opinion's removal of that analysis, leaving affirmance solely on inadequate screening. Pages 24–26 urge that the withdrawn analysis was correct and deny that deleting it establishes an independent ground for affirmance. These are the County's procedural description and argument; neither the withdrawn opinion nor counsel's defense of it is an operative appellate decision on training.

The brief also lists the jury's findings against Burns concerning arrest, force, imprisonment and qualified immunity. It reports judgments for Moore and Morrison and the removal of earnings amounts. Its damage totals and combined description of punitive relief should be checked against the actual judgment; this reader does not resolve which defendant owes each component from that compressed account. Review of the County's municipal hiring theory must not silently retry the underlying officer findings or reopen unrelated judgments.

## Criminal history and screening

Petitioner merits pages 3–4 describe Burns's own account of the September 1989 campus encounter, with citations JA 89a–90a: a fraternity-pledge member hit his car, he confronted the group, and a pushing encounter followed. Counsel describes the assault/battery, public-drunkenness and false-identification pleas as arising from the encounter and expressly says it is unclear whether the traffic offenses were related or earlier. Pages 21–22 distinguish being charged with resisting arrest from a conviction and cite JA 43a, 81a and 100a. This is not an independently established finding that every criminal entry arose from one event, nor does one assault conviction establish what the printout disclosed to a reasonable reviewer. The underlying testimony and printout govern those distinctions.

Pages 4–5 say Moore obtained the driving record and NCIC report, knew of traffic tickets and an actual-physical-control/intoxication arrest, but did not know of the assault/battery or public-drunkenness pleas; JA 114a–115a. Counsel distinguishes that claimed actual knowledge from the information available in the reports. The relation is that Burns was the son of Moore's nephew. The brief describes CLEET enrollment beginning May 6, 1991, ride-along instruction, the absence of firearm and driving authority, and a restriction on forcible arrests without a supervising deputy, citing JA 74a, 95a and 117a–118a and trial transcript 579–580. Those asserted precautions are evidence for the County's position, not proof that coercive duties or possible risk were absent.

## Statutory compliance and final policy

Petitioner merits pages 2–3 and footnote 7 on page 20 reproduce Oklahoma certification requirements, including the felony **or moral-turpitude** bar, psychological suitability procedures, educational requirements and application to reserve officers. Counsel's shorter rhetoric elsewhere refers only to felonies or no misdemeanor bar; that shorthand must not replace the full text. Its compliance argument relies partly on Schweizer's concessions at JA 47a–50a. Compliance and its federal legal effect remain different questions.

Pages 18–20 argue that the stipulation concerning Moore as policymaker for the Sheriff's Department does not establish final County employment-policy authority and that state standards supply the relevant hiring policy. Reply pages 2–3 dispute the respondent's claim that this issue was first raised here: they quote the earlier appellate brief page 22, cite charge objections at JA 124a–125a and 128a–132a and petition pages 6–7, 9–11 and 17. Objection to treating a single hire as a policy, objection to the identity of the final policymaker, and the scope of the stipulation are distinct. A fresh analysis must inspect the actual objection and prior ruling before deciding preservation. A requested good-faith instruction is merely a party request and supplies no municipal immunity.

## Legal submissions and their limits

The principal County position rejects liability for an isolated facially lawful hire without either a pattern of constitutional injuries or an exceptionally certain connection to the later constitutional injury. It argues from Monell's rejection of employer liability, Tuttle's policy-and-causation limits, the distinctions in Pembaur, and Canton's fault and close-causation requirements. It invokes federalism as support for a bounded construction of section 1983 and respect for state personnel standards, rather than establishing that state compliance itself resolves the federal claim.

The County presents both a broad single-hire objection and a narrower conditional argument. Reply pages 8–10 expressly contemplate that, if single-hire liability is legally available, a known psychiatric condition manifested by serious violent attacks certain to recur could supply the required connection; the hypothetical is not evidence about Burns. The reply objects to ordinary foreseeability, a potential disposition toward aggression and the proposition that the injury would not have occurred without the hire. It disputes the respondent's use of ordinary tort concepts and characterizes the desired constitutional-risk threshold more stringently than mere recklessness. These competing characterizations must be tested against the respondent's own actual submission and governing law.

Reply footnote 7, pages 10–11, says the expert described a potential for physical aggression at trial 316/JA 43a; the County argues that potential is insufficient and notes that physical action can be lawful police work. Reply footnotes 2–4, pages 4–5, invoke the absence of earlier complaints, earlier uses of the arm-bar technique and objections to a single-incident jury formulation. Absence of a prior complaint does not independently establish the safety of a practice; these are the County's evidentiary and legal inferences. References to later historical Supreme Court authorities in the filings require the actual corresponding simulated law, not automatic adoption of the quoted historical formulations.
