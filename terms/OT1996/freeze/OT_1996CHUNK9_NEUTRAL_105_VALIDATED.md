* **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 781 (1997); No. 96-542

* **Question Presented:** For purposes of § 1983 municipal liability, is an Alabama sheriff a final policymaker for the county or for the State when conducting law-enforcement investigations and related prosecutorial functions?
* **Question Granted:** Whether Monroe County could be held liable under Monell for the sheriff’s alleged law-enforcement misconduct on the theory that the sheriff was a county policymaker in that field.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Eleventh Circuit affirmed dismissal of the claims against Monroe County based on Sheriff Tate's alleged law-enforcement misconduct, holding that he was not the County's final policymaker for that function. He sued Monroe County and officials, alleging that Sheriff Tate and investigators manufactured testimony and suppressed evidence. The District Court dismissed the County claims and the official-capacity claims against Tate and investigator Ikner because their alleged acts did not represent County law-enforcement policy. McMillian appealed the ruling as to Tate; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed. The individual-capacity claims proceeded separately. No jury verdict against Monroe County is part of this reviewed judgment. Alabama's constitutional, statutory and judicial allocation of law-enforcement authority is central to attribution.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Identity of the policymaking sovereign

- **Reasoning Below:** The Eleventh Circuit held that Sheriff Tate was not a final policymaker for Monroe County in law enforcement because the County possessed no authority to make that policy.
- **Petitioner or Appellant:** McMillian argues that local election, territorial service, County funding and the sheriff's local institutional role establish County policymaking for the challenged law-enforcement activity.
- **Respondent or Appellee:** Monroe County argues that Alabama assigns the relevant authority to a state executive officer whom the County cannot direct or control; local election and funding do not make his law-enforcement acts County policy.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may resolve only the law-enforcement function and leave unrelated sheriff functions and any other county policy theory open.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** State labels are relevant but cannot conceal actual county control if the legal structure places the function in county hands.

## Posture and scope

The July 9, 1996 Eleventh Circuit opinion states that it permitted appeal under 28 U.S.C. 1292(b) from the district court's interlocutory orders. This is a distinct statutory route, not the pendent county appeal rejected in simulated Swint. Swint vacated the earlier circuit disposition for lack of jurisdiction and did not decide its attribution merits. The lower court here reexamined those merits; it did not treat the vacated circuit opinion as binding.

The County dismissal concerns the theory that Sheriff Tate's criminal-investigation and law-enforcement acts are attributable to Monroe County. The reviewed question is not all lower rulings about individual defendants, coercion, hearsay, or summary-judgment evidence. The lower opinion's separate hearsay analysis and other individual rulings remain part of its procedural background, not automatically additional granted issues. McMillian's conviction was reversed for nondisclosure of exculpatory and impeachment material; the State dismissed the charges and began a new investigation. The allegations against the defendants are not trial findings in this civil action, and there is no County liability verdict to affirm or reinstate.

The lower court expressly held it unnecessary to decide whether the sheriff is a final STATE policymaker in order to decide that he is not a final COUNTY policymaker. The binary wording in the original question must not force an unnecessary affirmative State-attribution holding. Likewise, rejection of County attribution does not itself adjudicate State liability, Eleventh Amendment immunity, or the personal liability of any officer.

## Competing attribution analyses

Monell's rejection of respondeat superior and its recognition of governmental policy are distinct from the antecedent federal-right inquiry. Pembaur permits liability for a single qualifying final policy decision; it does not designate every sheriff nationwide as a county policymaker. Praprotnik and Jett direct attention to positive state law and custom or usage carrying legal force, to the particular function, and to final authority rather than mere discretion. The federal consequence is a federal question informed by state allocation of authority, not conclusively answered by state tort-immunity labels.

For the County's position, the lower opinion quotes Alabama Constitution article V, section 112 placing a sheriff for each county in the state executive department and discusses Parker v. Amerson and Hereford on state employment and state immunity. It reasons that Alabama counties possess only delegated powers and possess no criminal-law-enforcement power; geography and required funding alone do not supply that power. The statutory record assigns criminal detection, arrest, securing evidence and reporting it to the district attorney to the sheriff; state officials and the district attorney may order special investigations. This supports examining whose authority the sheriff exercises, not merely whether another official can review a particular act.

For McMillian's position, the same record establishes county election, a courthouse office, county salaries, equipment and operating support, territorial responsibility and broad final operational authority. He argues that the independent elected local office is itself part of county government; absence of commission control over a final policymaker need not disprove County policy. The analysis must confront that institutional argument rather than define the County as only its commission. His comparison to Ohio in Pembaur and to the circuit's Alabama jail-personnel precedent tests whether state labels and assignment of powers adequately distinguish otherwise similar local arrangements.

Neither local funding/election nor state designation is assumed sufficient by itself. The lower opinion itself disclaims a label-only rule. State law may allocate different functions of one officer differently; a decision about the challenged law-enforcement function does not establish a universal status for jail administration, personnel decisions, fiscal administration or every sheriff in every State. The lower majority distinguishes Parker v. Williams's county-jail partnership rationale; Judge Propst separately disputes that rationale. His concurrence is not a controlling change to jail law and the present appeal need not decide the jail-administration question.

The statutory excerpts preserve material fiscal qualifications: special-investigation expenses require the described sworn statement and approval; the county audits necessity except that governor/attorney-general approval controls the authorized amount. Required provision of reasonable office resources differs from discretionary professional-association dues. Fee-payment provisions retain the prisoner-feeding exception and the county-resolution qualification. These are specific allocations of financial duties, not a general grant of operational supervision. The appendix supplies selected provisions, not every word of Alabama law; omitted subsections may not be filled by invention.

If County attribution is rejected, the dismissal may be sustained on that theory without deciding that a compensable federal violation never occurred. If County attribution is recognized, the appropriate further proceedings must still address the remaining federal deprivation, causal connection, defenses and relief; reversal of attribution dismissal is not a damages award. A separately supported County policy/custom theory, if any, cannot be disposed of merely by resolving this sheriff-attribution theory, but no new theory is invented from absent documents.
