# General Motors v. Tracy — supported objective record extraction

For fresh neutral validation under Engine §14. This handoff supplies objective record, statutory-category and lower-judgment recitations only. It contains no Supreme Court outcome, rationale, vote, authorship or private position. Retrieved October 12, 2026.

Source: Court-authored recitations, 519 U.S. 278, 281–86, 293–96 and nn. 1, 4, 11, 12, 13, 16; [official Reports](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/519bv.pdf#page=448). Cornell copy: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1232.ZO.html . These source locations were examined by control; neutral receives this extraction alone.

- **Period and posture:** The purchases cover October 1, 1986 through June 30, 1990 (n.1). General Motors bought virtually all gas for its Ohio plants from out-of-state marketers, taking delivery of much of it through local utilities (Part I and n.4, citing App. 156–57). The Tax Commissioner applied the use tax, the Board of Tax Appeals sustained that action, and General Motors appealed. This source establishes liability/assessment litigation. It does **not** identify the assessment amount, prove payment, or establish that the initiating proceeding was a refund application. Do not convert liability for a tax into a proved prior payment.
- **Tax structure during the relevant period:** Ohio imposed a 5% sales tax and parallel 5% use tax; authorized local increments could raise the combined rate to 7%. The statutory exemption was for natural-gas sales by a statutory natural gas company. The recited definition covered a person engaged in supplying natural gas for lighting, power or heating to consumers within Ohio. It was undisputed that Ohio local distribution companies (LDCs) qualified. The Ohio court's same-day Chrysler decision interpreted the category to exclude independent marketers without physical assets to distribute gas. Both in-state and out-of-state nonqualifying marketers were taxed. These are statutory classifications as interpreted below, not a finding that the words expressly distinguish sellers by residence.
- **Other taxes recited in n.16:** Public utilities paid personal-property tax using 88% of true value; marketers used 25%. Utilities paid assessments for Public Utilities Commission and Ohio Consumers' Counsel expenses and a 4.75% gross-receipts tax on gas sales. Marketers instead were subject to a franchise tax that did not apply to utilities. The source does not establish equivalence of those burdens to this sales/use tax or an amount attributable to the particular transactions.
- **LDC regulation recited:** Supply/demand forecasts; accounting, reporting and disclosure rules; permission for securities and certain contracts; just-and-reasonable rates including a single average gas cost and limited return; nondiscriminatory service obligations; constrained termination procedures; and firm backup gas and protective arrangements for human-needs customers. LDCs could not select only the profitable customers in their service territory. These regulatory descriptions are not transaction-specific cost findings.
- **Industrial-market limits:** The source describes a potential competitive market including large purchasers such as General Motors and says the record supplies virtually no detail about that market. It does not establish that Ohio LDC sales competing for General Motors were unregulated special-contract sales. At oral argument petitioner asserted that some utilities' negotiated, unregulated sales during the relevant tax period remained tax exempt; n.11 states that the record contained no support for that assertion. Treat it as an unsupported contention, not a record fact. The 1996 enactment effective September 17, 1996 allowing specified deregulation postdates these purchases.
- **Judgment below and claims:** The Ohio Supreme Court rejected General Motors's state-law contention that independent marketers qualified as natural gas companies by relying on Chrysler. It initially reasoned that nonqualifying sellers' sales were equally taxed regardless of location, then stated that General Motors lacked standing to assert the alleged burden on out-of-state sellers. It treated equal protection as submerged in the Commerce Clause argument. The granted issues included standing and the Commerce and Equal Protection Clause challenges. The recitation identifies General Motors's Commerce Clause case as facial discrimination; it does not establish a separately preserved Pike balancing claim. The parties did not brief whether the then-amended Natural Gas Act retained an express authorization of the challenged state treatment (n.13).

**Gap classification:** Supported published recitations adequately establish the listed facts. Amount, payment and refund-proceeding status remain unverified. Their absence does not establish litigant default and does not prevent considering whether a supported assessment-review disposition is available. If exact payment or a particular refund entitlement becomes outcome material, that proposition requires the underlying Ohio decision, assessment record or petition/appendix support; no reconstruction assumption adopts it here.
