## Identity and lower-court posture

Auer v. Robbins, No. 95-897, concerns St. Louis police sergeants and a lieutenant seeking FLSA overtime from the police commissioners. The approved event schedule is February 19, 1997; argument date December 10, 1996. The Eighth Circuit judgment under review treated all petitioners as exempt executive or administrative employees. The officers seek reversal and further proceedings on overtime; the commissioners defend exemption. Do not assume any overtime amount, final damages award, or completed reimbursement from that posture.

The statute supplies the Secretary's authority to define and delimit the bona fide executive, administrative, and professional exemption, 29 U.S.C. §213(a)(1). The general overtime provision and the special law-enforcement work period are distinct: the petitioners' merits questions identify §207(k)'s 171 hours in a 28-day period. Salary amount, salary basis, and duties are distinct regulatory conditions. Respondents' merits brief, printed pp.15–16, reports a stipulation that each petitioner earned at least $250 weekly, allowing the executive and administrative short tests. It states that all petitioners were ultimately found to satisfy the respective duties tests and that these duties holdings are not in dispute. This is consistent with the United States' description below.

## Scope correction supported by the actual filings

The petition's three questions concern (1) whether the salary-basis test applies to public employees; (2) actual short disciplinary suspensions versus being subject to them under a personnel rule and the Department's interpretation; and (3) whether correction can preserve group exemption through revocation of only part of an offending rule, without revocation of the broader rule or actual restoration of lost compensation. ## Salary-basis facts and competing positions

The departmental manual allowed disciplinary sanctions including unpaid suspensions shorter than a full workweek. The United States describes a manual covering exempt and nonexempt personnel; it disputes treating an undifferentiated discretionary range of penalties as an automatic loss of salaried status. Sergeant Guzy received a negotiated two-day unpaid suspension for a residency violation rather than the proposed termination. The appellate court treated that circumstance as unique. The residency rule was later changed to make termination the sole penalty. Neither that amendment nor the fact of one identified deduction establishes that every other challenged disciplinary authorization was removed.

The officers argue that employees practically subject to impermissible deductions lose the exemption even without an actual deduction from each employee's pay. They rely on the manual and evidence of employee understanding and disciplinary practice. The United States argues for an actual-practice or significant-likelihood interpretation: an actual practice of improper deductions, or a communicated policy creating a significant likelihood of them, can suffice; merely theoretical eligibility does not. An effective communication can matter before a deduction occurs. Its printed p.22 n.6 expressly leaves the exact frequency and extent of notice necessary unresolved on the circumstances it addresses. Do not supply a numerical threshold or turn this interpretation into an actual-deductions-only test.

The United States objects that additional alleged examples and witness-understanding evidence were not presented to the court of appeals or in the petition. The officers' reply disputes waiver and distinguishes a preserved issue from additional record evidence supporting it. These are competing preservation positions, not an adjudicated forfeiture or a basis to invent default. ## Separate exceptions and correction conditions

The operative regulation's complete paragraph (a), including (1)–(6), is copied below. The full-week rule permits no salary for a week in which no work is done. Paragraph (a)(5) concerns penalties imposed in good faith for safety rules of major significance, with its express limitation to prevention of serious danger to the plant or other employees. Neither provision is a blanket allowance for every short police disciplinary suspension. Paragraph (a)(2)'s personal-absence exception, (a)(3)'s qualifying sickness/disability arrangements, and (a)(4)'s jury/witness/military treatment have their own conditions.

The final 1992 §541.5d, also copied below, treats partial-day personal/illness/injury absences under specified public-accountability pay systems. Its accrued-leave conditions are express alternatives. Its budget-furlough paragraph separately withdraws protection for the workweek in which the furlough occurs and pay is reduced. These provisions must not be collapsed into a general public-sector disciplinary exception.

Under §541.118(a)(6), inadvertence and reasons other than lack of work are alternatives; reimbursement and a promise of future compliance are cumulative conditions. The officers urge that the employer has not shown an available correction because the deduction was intentional, the broader authorizations remain, and compensation was not promptly restored. The United States reads the text to permit correction of an isolated disciplinary deduction even if intentional, because its reason is other than lack of work, while distinguishing regular or recurring deductions reflecting a settled policy. Its brief also relies on a December 24, 1991 Department opinion letter for that distinction.

Crucially, the United States' printed p.29 says that the conditions have not all yet been fulfilled. Its proposed course is reimbursement of Guzy and a future-compliance promise on remand, without dismissal of the action until those conditions are satisfied; it describes this as apparently the course the court of appeals contemplated. That is the amicus's proposed remedial implementation, not an adjudicated finding that payment or compliance has occurred. Eligibility to use correction, satisfaction of its conditions, and the remedy if conditions remain unsatisfied are separate questions.

## Rule validity, agency interpretation, and factual application

The United States' full amicus brief recounts the 1954 notice-and-comment history: a proposal allowing deductions for other major disciplinary reasons was omitted from the final salary rule while the major-safety provision remained. It also recounts the 1991 interim and 1992 final public-accountability revisions, which changed partial-day-absence treatment while leaving the disciplinary limitation. It defends the Secretary's authority and reasons for retaining that distinction.

Respondents' merits brief, printed pp.10–15, contends that rigid application to paramilitary public-safety departments is irrational and beyond the intended salary test. It invokes Congress's different treatment of public law-enforcement hours and compensation, the need for effective short suspensions, and the major-safety exception. It disputes whether the 1992 rulemaking actually opened or adequately considered the disciplinary provision, despite Department recognition that public employment warranted some change. The officers' reply, printed pp.8–11, answers that the statutory delegation is express, policy disagreement does not establish invalidity, Congress tailored other public-sector provisions without changing this exemption, and the Department considered but rejected the disciplinary change.

The officers distinguish deference to a reasonable interpretation of a regulation from deference to the Solicitor General's factual application in a department the agency had not investigated or subjected to an enforcement proceeding. They invoke the limits on unsupported litigation positions. They support the Secretary on validity but oppose the employer-favorable application and correction position. The source does not establish that the officers reject all agency interpretive authority merely because they dispute this application.

## Immunity facts and opposing procedural claims

Respondents' merits brief, printed pp.7–10, asserts that the Board is an arm of Missouri: state legislation creates and governs it, the governor appoints members, and state statutes regulate operations and compensation, including restrictions on extra or compensatory time for sergeants and higher ranks. These are its asserted structural grounds; state creation and appointment are not themselves a supplied immunity finding.

The officers' reply, printed pp.13–19, counters that the city treasury bears salary and claim payments under Missouri §§84.160 and 84.210 and that the Board is solely municipally financed, quoting the trial decision. It identifies an annual certified city budget, local appropriation and restrictions on inter-item transfers absent the municipal Board of Estimate and Apportionment, and §84.190's power to compel city revenue. It identifies four gubernatorial appointees and the mayor as the fifth member under §84.020, Board selection of its president and treasurer, lack of asserted state operational veto/approval, and city approval for certain additional officers. These are attributed statutory and record representations, not an independent finding about every possible source of a judgment's payment.

The reply also claims waiver. It quotes an initial motion as seeking dismissal of individual-capacity claims while expressly conceding jurisdiction over the official-capacity FLSA claim; it says the answers admitted jurisdiction. It invokes an August 1994 consent judgment and the conditional reimbursement course as additional conduct. This extraction has verified those assertions in the reply, not independently retrieved the original initial written motion or adjudicated their legal effect.

The actual September 21, 1993 trial motion at Joint Appendix pp.94–95 has been read. Counsel said he moved to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction to preserve the contention that the San Antonio decision extending the FLSA to governmental agencies was wrong and unconstitutional, while acknowledging the contrary existing Supreme Court rule. The trial court permitted the argument and denied it at that time to preserve the record. This passage preserves a constitutional-coverage contention; it does not expressly assert an arm-of-state/Eleventh Amendment theory. Respondents' characterization of that passage cannot replace its actual terms. Neither immunity, waiver, nor jurisdiction is decided in this extraction.

### Petition appendix, pp.81a-82a -- section 541.118(a), all six subparagraphs

```text
§ 541.118 Salary basis. 


(a) An employee will be considered to be “on a 
salary basis” within the meaning of the regulations if 
under his employment agreement he regularly receives 
each pay period on a weekly, or less frequent basis, a 
predetermined amount constituting all or part of his com- 
pensation, which amount is not subject to reduction be- 
cause of variations in the quality or quantity of the work 


performed. Subject to the exceptions provided below, the 
employee must receive his full salary for any week in 
which he performs any work without regard to the number 
of days or hours worked. This policy is also subject to 
the general rule that an employee need not be paid for 
any workweek in which he performs no work. 


(1) An employee will not be considered to be “on a 
salary basis” if deductions from his predetermined com- 
pensation are made for absences occasioned by the em- 
ployer or by the operating requirements of the business. 
Accordingly, if the employee is ready, willing, and able 
to work, deductions may not be made for time when 
work is not available. 


(2) Deductions may be made, however, when the em- 
ployee absents himself from work for a day or more for 
personal reasons, other than sickness or accident. Thus, 
if an employee is absent for a day or longer to handle 
personal affairs, his salaried status will not be affected if 
deductions are made from his salary for such absences. 


(3) Deductions may also be made for absences of a 
day or more occasioned by sickness or disability (includ- 
ing industrial accidents) if the deduction is made in ac- 
cordance with a bona fide plan, policy or practice of 
providing compensation for loss of salary occasioned by 
both sickness and disability. Thus, if the employer’s par- 
ticular plan, policy or practice provides compensation for 
such absences, deductions for absences of a day or longer 
because of sickness or disability may be made before an 
employee has qualified under such plan, policy or practice, 
and after he has exhausted his leave allowance thereunder. 
It is not required that the employee be paid any portion 
of his salary for such days or days for which he receives 
compensation for leave under such plan, policy or prac- 
tice. Similarly, if the employer operates under a State 
sickness and disability insurance law, or a private sickness 
and disability insurance plan, deductions may be made 
for absences of a working day or longer if benefits are 


82a 


provided in accordance with the particular law or plan. 
In the case of an industrial accident, the “salary basis” 
requirement will be met if the employee is compensated 
for loss of salary in accordance with the applicable com- 
pensation law or the plan adopted by the empioyer, pro- 
vided the employer also has some plan, policy or practice 
of providing compensation for sickness and disability 
other than that relating to industrial accidents. 

(4) Deductions may not be made for absences of an 
employee caused by jury duty, attendance as a witness, 
or temporary military leave. The employer may, however, 
offset any amounts received by an employee as jury or 
witness fees or military pay for a particular week against 
the salary due for that particular week without loss of the 
exemption. 

(5) Penalties imposed in good faith for infractions of 
safety rules of major significance will not affect the em- 
ployee’s salaried status. Safety rules of major significance 
include only those relating to the prevention of serious 
danger to the plant, or other employees, such as rules 
prohibiting smoking in explosive plants, oil refineries, and 
coal mines. 7 

(6) The effect of making a deduction which is not 
permitted under these interpretations will depend upon the 
facts in the particular case. Where deductions are gen- 
erally made when there is no work available, it indicates 
that there was no intention to pay the employee on a 
salary basis. In such a case the exemption would not be 
applicable to him during the entire period when such 
deductions were being made. On the other hand, where 
a deduction not permitted by these interpretations is in- 
advertent, or is made for reasons other than lack of work, 
the exemption will not be considered to have been lost if 
the employer reimburses the employee for such deductions 
and promises to comply in the future.
```
