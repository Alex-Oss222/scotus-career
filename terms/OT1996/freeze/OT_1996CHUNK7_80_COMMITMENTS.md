## Components and competing paths

**Fleet identification.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer would reject the proposition that employers' use of the same hiring hall, without common ownership or operational control of their vessels, turns all those vessels into the identifiable fleet Chandris requires. A common source of labor is not common control of the vessels. That is an express clarification of the identifiable-fleet requirement, not a holding imported from the present historical case. Stevens can join that limited rejection, while reserving whether another supported operational arrangement or the actual ship's-company relationship could justify a different result. No Justice concludes that a worker dispatched by a hall can never be a seaman: actual qualifying assignment to one vessel or a properly identified fleet remains possible.

The strongest alternative treats the common bargaining and dispatch arrangement as a functional employment group, preventing the method of daily hiring from stripping true seagoing crew work of protection. It correctly identifies why short tenure cannot be a categorical exclusion. It fails as a sufficient fleet definition because the record supplies no common operational relationship among all vessels and would make the group whatever vessels the worker happened to visit. Daily hiring and a genuine vessel connection can coexist without aggregating unrelated owners' vessels.

**Harbor Tug-specific status.** The painting contributed to the vessel's function, but contribution is not enough. The question is whether the one-day dockside assignment, with twelve prior Harbor Tug deckhand engagements over two and one-half months and the described line-handling work, permits a reasonable conclusion that Papai belonged to the tug's or a qualifying fleet's shipboard workforce. Twelve engagements do not establish twelve voyages, a duration percentage or permanent crew membership. Conversely, missing detailed descriptions do not establish that every engagement was shoreside painting, and an ALJ's conclusion is not a substitute for proper summary-judgment assessment.

**Reversal path:** Chandris and Wilander distinguish ship-based service from incidental work aboard. The actual contract was a separately obtained one-day painting job under a dockside port captain with no expected sailing; prior brief engagements and generic line handling do not supply a substantial sea-based relationship or make this independent maintenance assignment an established continuation of a crew post. No duration calculation is needed when the nature of the connection is insufficient. The strongest objection is that the lower majority identifies recurring deckhand work for the same owner, so a conclusion of pure transience may weigh favorable evidence or reinstate the district court's impermissible permanence requirement. The path rejects permanence and rests on the independently engaged dockside work and absence of a supported continuing ship's-company role, while accepting the twelve jobs as described.

**Remand path:** Reject hiring-hall aggregation but preserve the appellate judgment reopening the Harbor Tug-specific inquiry. Repeated deckhand employment for the same tug operator can permit a finding of crew service; the one-day painting label does not conclusively establish that the essential employment relationship had changed. Chandris's actual-assignment rule and Gizoni's resistance to occupational labels require lawful assessment of that supported inference. The strongest objection is that a claimant needs evidence of substantial connection, not the mere possibility of finding more facts, and line handling while docked may be transitory. This path therefore orders no exploration merely because sources are unavailable, finds no ultimate status, and does not count twelve jobs as twelve voyages. It identifies the existing repeated-deckhand/relationship inference as the dispute requiring ordinary lower-court resolution under the corrected fleet rule.

## Administrative award component

All eight refuse to adopt the Ninth Circuit's broad proposition that the credit provision and Gizoni necessarily eliminate the effect of every final litigated LHWCA award. Gizoni expressly reserved final determinations; a double-recovery credit is different from an adjudication of an identical issue. Conversely, no Associate finds automatic estoppel merely from the existence and finality of this award or the quoted ALJ conclusion. A decision must establish the actual issue necessarily determined, legal standard, litigation opportunity, finality and any statutory limit on preclusion.

O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer **reserve preclusion as unnecessary** because their status disposition is sufficient. Their reservation neither approves the circuit's reasoning nor removes the existing award. Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg **leave actual preclusion for ordinary remand consideration** under those precise predicates if Harbor Tug continues to rely on it; they would not affirm the categorical no-preclusion rule. Their status vote can support affirming reopening with modification of that preclusion direction, or a formal vacatur and remand preserving the same underlying result if the Court's decree requires it. They support no final resolution of estoppel without the actual order and relevant litigation material. This is a need tied to a proposed dispositive ground, not a litigant default or an in-world production order.
