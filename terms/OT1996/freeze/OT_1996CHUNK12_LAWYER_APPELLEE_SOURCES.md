## Original federal claim

All three appellees briefs identify the April 14, 1994 complaint as an equal-protection challenge to the racial territorial design of District 21 under Plan 330. State pp. 1, 4 & n.1 (JA 11–15) describes three requested remedies: declaration of invalidity, prohibition of elections under Plan 330, and redrawing according to traditional districting principles to eliminate the alleged racial gerrymander. Private pp. 1–2 identifies complaint paragraph 13 (JA 13). United States pp. 2–3 gives the most detailed description: irregular boundaries intended to secure a minority population percentage; combination of minority groups with divergent interests in different communities; and alleged racial segregation for voting purposes. The requested replacement conformed to traditional districting principles (JA 14).

None of these three briefs asserts that the original complaint contains an independent vote-dilution, exclusion, unequal-participation, malapportionment, or other federal injury beyond that district-design claim. None identifies an intervenor counterclaim asserting such an independent violation by Plan 330. This conclusion is bounded to what these complete briefs present; the complaint and intervention pleadings remain the direct sources for their own contents.

Potentially confusing voting-rights passages do not establish a second pleaded predicate:

- State pp. 10–12, Private pp. 5–7, and United States pp. 5–7 describe evidence defending Plan 386: population equality, meaningful participation, fair electoral opportunity, and avoidance of disruptive out-of-cycle elections. Private pp. 22–23 additionally argues that Plan 386 meets standards for a court-ordered remedy because it uses single-member districts, has a low population deviation, and does not dilute minority voting strength. These are descriptions or defenses of the proposed remedy, not claims that Plan 330 denied population equality or diluted votes.
- State p. 12 and United States p. 7 describe testimony on racially polarized voting and black voters' opportunity to elect preferred candidates (JA 129–133). United States p. 7 says Lawyer's proposed approximately 23% black-VAP district would eliminate a significant opportunity for black voters to elect preferred candidates. That is evidence concerning alternatives to Plan 330, not a pleaded finding of vote dilution under Plan 330.
- State pp. 2–4 and United States pp. 1–2 recount the 1992 Section 5 objection to the earlier legislatively enacted plan and the resulting adoption of Plan 330. Private p. 3 and United States p. 4 report preclearance of Plan 386. Neither episode is presented as an independent unresolved federal claim against Plan 330 in this suit.

## How Plan 386 acquired force

The briefs expressly distinguish officials' settlement proposal from enactment in a formal legislative session. State pp. 5–8, 19, 33–35; Private pp. 2–3, 17–22; United States pp. 3–4, 11, 16–18 describe officials and the two chambers participating as litigants, the legislature declining to convene or otherwise enact a replacement, and Plan 386 being proposed to and approved by the district court. They do not identify a legislative enactment of Plan 386.

The reported settlement provided that the court-approved plan would govern until Florida adopted a replacement conforming to federal and state law (JA 19; State pp. 8, 39; United States pp. 4, 17). Thus the briefs attribute Plan 386's operative election requirement to the court's March 19, 1996 order approving the settlement (United States p. 8), not to an independently effective legislative enactment.

Private p. 22 expressly separates the question whether an officials' proposal adopted outside formal session should be classified as legislative or court-ordered for remedial standards. It argues that issue need not be decided because preclearance was obtained and the plan meets either set of relevant standards. The district court's characterization of the action as primarily legislative (JA 206; United States p. 9) therefore must not be silently converted into a finding of formal enactment.

## What was conceded, and what was not adjudicated

The settlement's operative concession, as recited at State p. 8 and United States p. 4, was that plaintiffs' claim had a reasonable factual and legal basis (JA 17). The briefs commonly characterize the record as establishing a prima facie case. United States pp. 8–9 reproduces the lower court's explanation that parties either asserted unconstitutionality unequivocally or conceded prima facie unconstitutionality for settlement purposes (JA 201 n.3). Those distinct formulations should not be collapsed into a unanimous unconditional admission that Plan 330 violated federal law.

All three briefs acknowledge that the district court majority did not finally adjudicate Plan 330's unconstitutionality. They distinguish Chief Judge Tjoflat's concurrence, which would have made that determination from the existing record and considered it necessary (State pp. 14–16; Private pp. 9–11; United States pp. 8–10; JA 199–209). United States pp. 10, 14–16 rhetorically describes liability as resolved by consent, but this is the United States' legal characterization of the procedure. It does not add an unconditional liability admission to the written settlement.

Lawyer did not consent to Plan 386. The briefs say he continued to seek a declaration invalidating Plan 330 and challenged Plan 386. State pp. 8–9, 13; United States pp. 15–16 distinguish that objection from the agreement of all parties that Plan 330 should be displaced. Authority of officials to represent their entities in litigation and consent to settlement was separately addressed (JA 137–138, 162, 197, 206; State pp. 6 n.4, 8 n.6, 39). Litigation authority is not itself enactment.

The briefs do not reproduce the entire settlement instrument. Exact application of any additional nonadmission, withdrawal, or nonapproval provision must use JA 17–21 directly; this memo supplies no missing term.

## Claimed federal authority despite absence of adjudicated liability

The appellees advance a bounded consent-decree theory, not a theory that any desired change in state law can be judicially ordered on consent alone:

- State pp. 36–39 invokes equity and settlement principles, including Armour, Rufo, and Local Number 93. It argues that the asserted federal claim must have a reasonable basis in fact and law, that serious risk of liability must be demonstrated rather than merely facially alleged, that officials must have settlement authority, and that the court must guard against usurpation of state authority. It relies also on the ability of Florida to replace the decree's plan. Its asserted factual/legal foundation is the racial-design challenge under Miller; it supplies no distinct federal injury as an alternative.
- United States pp. 12–15 contrasts contested liability with settlement. Under its account of Local Number 93, consent permits disposition without full merits adjudication, but the decree must lie within the pleadings and advance the objectives of the law on which the complaint rests. It emphasizes judicial scrutiny against collusive state-law displacement, a factual basis for serious illegality charges, authorized state representatives, and absence of contrary congressional or state-law direction. Its concrete factual foundation on p. 14 is Plan 330's contorted fingers and the Florida Supreme Court's expressed preference for racial fairness over community of interest. Those are territorial-design facts.
- United States pp. 15–16 also acknowledges that settlement cannot prevent a nonconsenting party from vindicating substantive rights; it argues Lawyer suffered no such impairment because Plan 330 was removed and he could challenge the replacement. State pp. 35–36 and Private pp. 15–16, 23 separately argue lack of appellate injury and harmlessness. These are advocacy arguments about review and relief, not independent substantive federal claims.
- Private pp. 24–25 relies on authorized and unified state officials, a prima facie constitutional violation supported by the record, and a lawful remedy. It distinguishes cases with absent illegality, an attorney general acting against his clients, or local officials overriding statewide statutes. It supplies no independent vote-dilution or participation claim to replace the original racial-design theory.

Given the supplied current-law premise that territorial racial design alone is not cognizable, these briefs do not themselves identify another pleaded federal predicate. Their historical references to Miller and its successors explain the parties' actual litigation theory; they must not be silently imported as governing current law. Whether the available pleadings and record support federal judicial action under current law is for the appropriately scoped modeling context, not this source review.

## Primary-precedent verification added at operator request

The official [Library of Congress scan of Firefighters v. Cleveland, 478 U.S. 501 (1986)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep478/usrep478501/usrep478501.pdf) and its complete extracted text are saved beside the briefs as `firefighters-v-cleveland-478-us-501-official-loc.pdf` and `.txt`. Directly read printed pp. 524–530; current simulated-law applicability remains for the entering-law reviewer.

At pp. 525–526, the majority requires a dispute within subject-matter jurisdiction, relief within the pleadings' general scope, and advancement of the underlying law's objectives. Consent adds legal force and can support relief broader than a post-trial award, but cannot authorize action conflicting with the underlying statute or immunize constitutional violations.

At pp. 528–530, withholding consent alone does not veto other parties' settlement. An intervenor must be heard and may present evidence. A decree cannot extinguish properly raised valid claims or impose obligations on a nonconsenter. The union there incurred no duties; its possible substantive claims were left for the district court. The issue decided concerned Title VII § 706(g), not every possible substantive objection. O'Connor's concurrence begins at p. 530 and emphasizes that narrow scope.
