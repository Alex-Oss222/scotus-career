# 132. Metro-North Commuter Railroad Co. v. Buckley

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Metro-North Commuter Railroad Co. v. Buckley
* **Citation or Docket:** 521 U.S. 424 (1997); No. 96-320
* **October Term:** 1996
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued FELA merits case reviewing emotional-distress and medical-monitoring claims by an asymptomatic railroad worker negligently exposed to asbestos.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 23, 1997 (historical)
* **Question Presented:** May a railroad worker negligently exposed to asbestos but presently without disease recover under FELA for fear of future illness and for the incremental cost of medically reasonable monitoring?
* **Question Granted:** Whether asbestos contact constitutes a recovery-permitting physical impact for negligent emotional distress under Gottshall and whether FELA permits recovery of future medical-monitoring costs before symptoms appear.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Second Circuit reversed dismissal, holding that Buckley’s extensive asbestos contact satisfied the physical-impact route for emotional distress and that a jury could award medical-monitoring costs. Metro-North Commuter Railroad Co. v. Buckley, 79 F.3d 1337 (2d Cir. 1996).
* **Relief Requested:** Metro-North seeks reversal and dismissal of both claims. Buckley seeks affirmance and remand for jury consideration.
* **Material Record:** Buckley worked as a pipefitter in Grand Central Terminal and was negligently exposed to asbestos-containing insulation for several years. Metro-North conceded negligence. Medical examinations showed no asbestos-related disease, but experts testified to an increased risk and the need for periodic monitoring. Buckley testified to fear and anger but introduced no professional mental-health evidence. The District Court found insufficient proof of a real emotional injury and did not separately analyze monitoring.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The emotional-distress and monitoring theories are distinct injuries and remedies. The Court must apply FELA’s text and incorporated common-law principles without treating the railroad as an insurer, while recognizing the Act’s remedial operation. The record’s sufficiency and the legal availability of each claim must remain separate.
* **Relevant Dates:** The Second Circuit judgment issued in 1996; argument occurred February 18, 1997; the Supreme Court event date is June 23, 1997.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None identified as a separate matter requiring adjudication.

### Issue 1: Negligent infliction of emotional distress

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** The actual simulated Gottshall zone-of-danger holding governs; its unresolved future-disease scope must be addressed rather than borrowing Stone's earlier dissent or historical gloss.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Gottshall requires physical impact or immediate risk of physical harm, with fear for oneself on the threatened-impact branch; negligence, cognizable injury and causation remain necessary. Seriousness and foreseeability do not replace that gate, and the decision reserves the full future-disease category.
* **Other Governing Law:** 45 U.S.C. § 51; Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Gottshall; Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Buell; common-law emotional-distress principles.
* **Material Other Authority:** Exposure evidence, medical risk testimony, Buckley’s own testimony, mental-health evidence, and District Court findings.
* **Open Point:** Whether a later manifested disease or objectively severe distress would support a different claim.

### Issue 2: Medical-monitoring costs

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** No supplied simulated holding squarely resolves an asymptomatic FELA monitoring claim.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Separate present economic costs of medically necessary surveillance from damages for fear or future disease. Any recovery must be tied to negligent exposure, a materially increased risk, reasonable medical necessity, and incremental costs rather than speculative lump-sum damages.
* **Other Governing Law:** 45 U.S.C. § 51; Urie v. Thompson; Ayers and other date-eligible common-law monitoring authorities; FELA causation and damages principles.
* **Material Other Authority:** Expert testimony on risk, recommended tests, frequency, comparative baseline care, costs, and any monitoring already provided.
* **Open Point:** The exact elements, form of relief, periodic fund or reimbursement mechanism, offsets, and proof on remand.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Emotional distress

* **Reasoning Below:** The Second Circuit treated tangible asbestos contact as physical impact and found the fear objectively reasonable.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Gottshall does not make exposure to a carcinogen a present traumatic impact, and Buckley proved neither disease nor severe emotional injury.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** Repeated physical contact with asbestos is literal impact and creates a reasonable fear of deadly disease within FELA’s remedial purpose.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may resolve the claim on insufficient evidence of serious emotional injury without defining every form of toxic physical impact.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** Dismissal of current fear damages does not bar recovery for a later manifested disease or qualifying distress.
### Issue 2: Medical monitoring

* **Reasoning Below:** The Second Circuit recognized a jury question on monitoring costs caused by the negligent exposure.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Asymptomatic risk is not an injury under FELA, and lump-sum monitoring awards invite speculative and duplicative liability.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** Reasonably necessary tests are present economic costs caused by admitted negligence and can be measured without compensating hypothetical disease.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may recognize a narrow claim while requiring a tailored remedy, proof of incremental necessity, and credit for employer-provided monitoring.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** A monitoring claim cannot be based on exposure alone, generalized anxiety, or tests no reasonable physician would prescribe.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The claims should be classified separately. The emotional-distress judgment may be reversed on the record-specific absence of objective severe injury without resolving every toxic-contact question. The medical-monitoring claim may proceed if FELA’s “injury” encompasses present, medically necessary incremental costs caused by admitted negligence and materially increased risk. A remand can require focused proof and a remedy no broader than actual monitoring need. A categorical rejection would treat a current cost as though it were only fear of future harm.

## F. VERIFIED SIMULATED-LAW APPLICATION

### Reconciled entering-law application — Gottshall controls

Simulated Gottshall (June 24, 1994) is a five-Justice Thomas holding: FELA negligent emotional injury requires employer-caused physical impact or immediate risk of physical harm; threatened-impact distress must result from fear for the employee himself. Negligence, cognizable injury and FELA causation remain independent. Seriousness, genuineness and foreseeability alone do not replace the zone-of-danger gate. The Court left physical-manifestation requirements and the full fear-of-future-disease category unresolved. Gottshall received a first-instance application remand; Carlisle's ordinary prolonged work stress required judgment for Conrail. Buckley's repeated asbestos contact presents the reserved question whether this toxic contact meets the physical-impact branch, not a license to substitute objectively serious fear for that branch. His monitoring theory separately asks whether present incremental surveillance costs are a cognizable FELA injury; Gottshall did not decide that question. Neither existing liability nor the form or amount of recovery may be presumed from conceded negligence.

Relief must correspond to the monitoring actually necessary and available.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The claimed injury is the present need to purchase additional medical surveillance, not cancer that may never occur. FELA’s text reaches injury resulting from employer negligence, and common-law reluctance toward fear damages does not erase a measurable cost undertaken to detect a risk the railroad wrongfully created. ### Existing equitable authority and the intervening statutory route

Simulated **Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail (January 15, 1992)**, in Stone's five-Justice opinion, permits a suitably tailored modification on a significant change in facts or law making continued enforcement inequitable. A catastrophe is not required; ordinary dissatisfaction, a changed administration or a cheaper constitutional alternative is not enough by itself, and accepted anticipated risks may require a stronger showing. Those principles explain why a consent decree remains judicially enforceable but prospectively adjustable. They do not replace Congress's separate enacted termination route with an extra Rule 60 prerequisite.

The original 1996 PLRA §802 applies §3626 to existing prison-conditions relief. Under §3626(b)(2), a defendant or intervener may obtain termination where relief was approved without findings that it was narrowly drawn, no broader than necessary to correct the federal violation and the least intrusive means. **Subsection (b)(3)** preserves relief upon written record-based findings that it remains necessary to correct a **current or ongoing** federal violation and satisfies the narrowness, necessity and least-intrusive requirements. The original text says **or**, not the later amended **and**. Relief is not dissolved merely by enactment; the particular motion and its predicates matter. Subsection (e)'s automatic-stay periods are distinct from a merits termination judgment and are not a question in this appeal.

Simulated **Lewis v. Casey (June 24, 1996)** applies these statutory requirements to existing relief. The eight Associates' Part IV reasoning, while Stone joined only the statutory disposition, recognizes that existing substantive findings may satisfy the statutory conditions without a ritual repetition of words and that relief must address the demonstrated violation. The original current-or-ongoing exception must be considered. Lewis did **not** decide §802's constitutionality. Its governing access-to-courts and remedial holdings protect practical access and tie groupwide relief to established common barriers; the historical case's different opinion and extra fractured thresholds do not govern. Applied here, a decree's age alone does not immunize it, its lack of formulaic wording alone does not dispose of the findings question, and lawful termination cannot erase an independently established federal violation requiring suitably bounded relief.

### Final judgments, changed law and asserted decree rights

**Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., 511 U.S. 658 (May 23, 1994)**. Fifth Circuit's judgment sustaining the statutory reinstatement procedure affirmed, 5–3; O'Connor does not participate. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Blackmun, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. Scalia dissents, joined in full by Kennedy and Thomas.

**Holding:** Article III permits §27A(b)'s expressly retrospective requirement to reinstate a private §10(b) action commenced on or before June 19, 1991, dismissed as time barred after June 19, 1991, and timely under the jurisdiction's law, including retroactivity principles, as that law existed on June 19, 1991, upon a motion filed within sixty days after the provision's December 19, 1991 enactment. The federal court must independently determine whether those cumulative conditions are satisfied and, if they are, must reinstate the action without a discretionary balancing test, retaining independent responsibility for the claims, defenses and remedies; this bounded entitlement to adjudication neither directs a merits judgment nor establishes an unrestricted power to reopen completed federal judgments. Pending litigation, prospective relief and independently authorized judicial reopening remain distinct.

**Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc. (April 18, 1995)**. Sixth Circuit reversed, 5–4; remanded for reinstatement under §27A(b) and further proceedings on the securities claims. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas.

**Holding:** Under Morgan Stanley, Article III permits Congress to require reinstatement under §27A(b) of a qualifying private §10(b) action even though its limitations dismissal became final without appeal before the provision's December 19, 1991 enactment: the action must have commenced on or before June 19, 1991, been dismissed as time barred after that date, and been timely under the jurisdiction's law, including retroactivity principles, as it existed June 19, 1991, with a reinstatement motion filed within sixty days after enactment. The court independently determines those cumulative conditions and, when they are satisfied, must reinstate without discretionary balancing, retaining independent responsibility for all claims, defenses, and remedies; this expressly retrospective entitlement does not authorize legislative findings, dictated liability, or unrestricted reopening of completed merits judgments.

Simulated **Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society (March 25, 1992)** permits a genuine change in the applicable law while preserving the judicial task of applying it. Section 3626 changes the authority to maintain prospective relief and leaves courts to determine its factual and statutory predicates; whether it impermissibly commands an outcome under unchanged law must be tested against that operation. Simulated Landgraf likewise distinguishes new regulation of future relief from new liability for completed conduct; §802 supplies an express existing-relief direction.

A judicial decree is enforceable under the court's lawful authority, but treating every additional negotiated term as itself the underlying federal right would make the enacted termination limit self-defeating. The question concerns continued federal prospective enforcement, not whether an accrued damages award, a separately enforceable private contract or an independent state-law claim is extinguished. Due process requires identifying the particular protected interest and the actual process; a mutable prospective decree does not by its label promise immunity from future lawful legislation.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL POSITIONS

The prisoners argue that a final federal decree creates federal rights, that Congress cannot undo their negotiated bargain or direct Article III courts to terminate it, and that deprivation of its protections denies due process. Their broader challenge below also alleged unequal access to courts for prisoners. The State and United States argue that Congress changed the permissible scope of prospective federal relief, left judicial determination of the statutory conditions and preserved relief necessary to remedy actual federal violations. The material disagreement is whether the decree's additional terms qualify as independent federal rights and whether lawful termination can be decided without overlooking §3626(b)(3)'s actual findings.

Michigan Document Services, operated by James Smith, sold university coursepacks containing professors' selections from six copyrighted scholarly works without publisher permission. The excerpts included substantial passages; established permission licensing was available. The copied text supplied assigned reading rather than commentary on the original works. Smith maintained that the copying was fair use.

The district court rejected fair use, awarded $5,000 per work, and entered an injunction. The operative appeal is the en banc judgment following a superseded panel decision. Willfulness, damages, and the wording of prospective relief are distinct from whether the copying infringed. Smith's belief and the reasonableness of that belief require attention; intentional copying alone does not identify the damages category.

### Questions Presented

1. Whether a commercial copy shop's unauthorized reproduction and sale of excerpts from copyrighted scholarly works in coursepacks assembled for university instruction constitutes fair use under 17 U.S.C. §107.
2. Whether the commercial character of the entity reproducing and distributing copyrighted material outweighs the educational purpose for which the material is ultimately used when evaluating the purpose and character of the use under §107(1).
3. Whether the potential loss of licensing fees in an existing permissions market constitutes cognizable market harm under §107(4), or whether treating unpaid licensing fees as market harm improperly presupposes that the copyright owner has a right to compensation for the disputed use.
4. Whether an infringement may be deemed willful for purposes of enhanced statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. §504(c)(2) when the defendant intentionally copied the works but held a good-faith belief that the copying constituted fair use.

**Integrated statutory inquiry.** Simulated *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music* requires the four §107 factors to be considered together and rejects categorical shortcuts based on profit or a use's label. Its particular rejection of a presumption of market harm involved transformative parody; it does not abolish consideration of substitution where commercial copies reproduce the originals' instructional function. Nor does Campbell make transformation indispensable to fair use. Educational purpose therefore matters, but it does not automatically immunize the commercial supplier.

For purpose and character, the record permits distinguishing the instructor's educational selection and student's study from the copy shop's sale of unaltered excerpts. Both are relevant to the actual use. For nature, published scholarly expression may be closer to factual instruction than unpublished creative work, but factual content remains protected in its expression. For amount, numerical percentages alone are inadequate: assess the excerpt's size and qualitative significance against the asserted educational purpose and actual market substitution.

For market effect, Campbell distinguishes cognizable substitution in legitimate markets from suppression of criticism or a licensing demand defined solely by the challenged use's illegality. Here an established coursepack-permission market may support actual substitution evidence. That is an argument requiring proof, not a rule that any unpaid hypothetical fee defeats fair use. The alternative purchase must be assessed realistically; the relevant possible market may be licensed excerpts rather than purchase of every entire book.

**Remedies remain separate.** Section 504(c)(2) requires willfulness for enhanced statutory damages, while the statute separately addresses innocent infringement. Intentional copying, a mistaken fair-use view, and knowing or reckless infringement are different propositions. Campbell did not decide willfulness for coursepacks. An injunction must describe prohibited conduct with sufficient specificity and must not extinguish lawful future fair use by implication. The relevant standards do not permit simply adopting the en banc conclusion as already governing simulation law.

**Record and proceedings:** Alfredo Kolster, a Venezuelan citizen, entered in 1980 on a student visa, completed high school and college, and reentered on September 11, 1988 as an immediate family member of an international-organization employee. Congress may enforce the Fourteenth Amendment through preventive and remedial legislation reaching some conduct that would not itself violate the Constitution. Stone would require a supportable connection between the constitutional violations or serious threats Congress may address and the reach of the statutory remedy. The governing constitutional right remains the starting point; enforcement power cannot be converted into a power to replace that right's judicially enforceable content.

Congruence and proportionality require a substantive relationship between the constitutional injury Congress seeks to prevent or remedy and the means it adopts. That relationship must be assessed in light of the constitutional right, the nature of the threatened violation, and the practical operation of the legislation. Courts examine the actual statutory design and constitutional justification, not merely the quantity of legislative evidence.

Congress need not establish a prescribed number of constitutional violations, produce formal legislative findings, or demonstrate unconstitutional conduct in every jurisdiction subject to the legislation. Preventive legislation need not establish a violation by every state. Nor are geographic limitations, expiration provisions, or individualized remedies indispensable conditions of valid enforcement legislation, and no universal sunset, geographic restriction, or individualized proof requirement follows.

Congress retains discretion to enact appropriately tailored preventive measures, including measures extending beyond conduct independently forbidden by the Constitution. The constitutional boundary is crossed when legislation ceases to enforce an established constitutional guarantee and instead substitutes a new substantive constitutional standard.

RFRA imposes its heightened statutory command across all state and local laws, governmental functions and subjects. Its universal coverage is not bounded by an identified class of constitutional violations, a particular administrative setting, a supported remedial problem or a comparable limiting principle. Stone does not demand a commissioned study, formal findings or certainty as universal prerequisites. His objection concerns the substantive breadth of this rule compared with the constitutional wrong Congress may prevent. The enactment replaces Smith's governing constitutional boundary generally rather than supplying a fitted remedy for conduct violating that boundary.

Stone therefore proposes a congruence-and-proportionality limit on this use of §5 and concludes that RFRA's universal state and local application exceeds it. This is his answer to the power question now presented. It does not decide the permit dispute's free-exercise merits, reconsider Smith, or deny that Congress can protect religious exercise through a properly supported enforcement measure or another available constitutional source.

### Issue 1: Primary § 10(b) liability for deceptive misappropriation

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the merits. The theory concerns O'Hagan's own alleged deception, not imputed primary liability or the private aiding-and-abetting cause of action. The source relationship, transaction connection, scienter, and use of the information are supported sufficiently to resolve the legal rule, while ordinary record challenges remain for remand.
- **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether § 10(b) reaches a trader who secretly monetizes information entrusted by a source participating in an actual or contemplated securities transaction.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies § 10(b)'s antifraud language while supplying a source-bound limitation. Misappropriation is primary fraud when the source entrusted material nonpublic information in connection with an actual or contemplated securities transaction, the trader owed the source a defined duty of fidelity or confidentiality, the trader deceptively withheld the planned use, and the trader used the information to trade securities materially affected by the source's transaction. Disclosure to the source before trading removes the deception required by this theory. A breach involving information unrelated to the source's securities activity is not transformed into § 10(b) fraud merely because the defendant later trades.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** A trader commits primary fraud under § 10(b) when, with scienter, the trader secretly uses material nonpublic information entrusted by a source participating in an actual or contemplated securities transaction, in breach of a defined duty to that source, to trade securities materially affected by the source's transaction; the trading must consummate or monetize the deceptive breach.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** O'Hagan's firm represented the contemplated bidder, and the confidential information concerned the very tender offer that changed the value of the target securities he acquired. His trading was not an unrelated use followed by a coincidental securities purchase. It was the means by which the concealed disloyalty produced gain. Requiring a defined source duty, source participation in the securities transaction, scienter, and transaction-linked trading gives independent work to both fraud and in connection with and avoids a general federal law of confidential information.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding creates no parity-of-information duty, reaches no innocent receipt, and reserves relationships involving family, friendship, government service, ordinary employment, or commerce when the source is not participating in the securities transaction. It does not narrow the distinct simulated rule for secondary assistance.

### Issue 2: SEC authority for Rule 14e-3(a)

- **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the validity of Rule 14e-3(a). The question is one of statutory authority, not whether every application of the rule is constitutional or whether the SEC possesses a general power to equalize market information.
- **Stone's Legal Position:** Stone applies the express preventive-rule authority in § 14(e). Rule 14e-3(a) addresses the distinctive secrecy, timing, and proof risks of tender offers by regulating trading on material nonpublic tender-offer information obtained from specified transaction participants when the trader knows or has reason to know the source. The bounded rule is reasonably designed to prevent tender-offer fraud and remains within the statutory domain.
- **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Section 14(e) authorizes Rule 14e-3(a)'s bounded prohibition on trading while possessing material nonpublic tender-offer information obtained from the offeror, issuer, or persons acting for them under the knowledge conditions stated in the rule.
- **Stone's Reasoning and Record Application:** Congress did more than forbid completed common-law fraud. It expressly authorized preventive rules in the tender-offer field. Tender offers operate under compressed timing and unusual secrecy, and trading before announcement can make source deception difficult to prove after the fact. The rule addresses those risks within a defined transaction, source, information, and knowledge structure. It does not claim authority over all trading or all confidential information.
- **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding does not authorize SEC regulation outside the tender-offer and antifraud domain, eliminate the rule's source and knowledge limits, or permit an agency to create power from statutory silence.

# 142. Akins v. FEC

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

**Merits docket status: SIM COURT GRANTED — TO BE HEARD ON THE MERITS.** Review is granted on the questions stated in this packet. This case is on the OT1996 merits docket under the established simulation calendar.

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Akins v. FEC
- **Citation or Docket:** 101 F.3d 731 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc), decided December 6; amended January 3, 1997; court-of-appeals No. 94-5088
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Procedural Category:** Simulation-assigned appellate merits
- **Lower-Court Event Date:** 1997-01-03; this is a source date, not an assigned simulation event.

Six politically active voters challenged the FEC's dismissal of their administrative complaint alleging that AIPAC had to register and disclose as a political committee. The Commission found likely campaign contributions above the relevant $1,000 threshold but treated an organization's major campaign purpose as an additional prerequisite. AIPAC's broader activity was lobbying; the Commission therefore declined committee classification. Separately, it found probable cause concerning the corporate contribution/expenditure prohibition but declined action because it regarded the membership-communications question as close. That prior consideration must be distinguished from the coverage question Stone would return to the Commission for committee-status purposes. The district court upheld the major-purpose interpretation.

The operative en banc appeal addressed voter informational standing, the express FECA review channel, and whether the major-purpose limitation applied to these allegedly candidate-connected payments. The requested relief is correction of the dismissal and further lawful Commission action, not a direct judicial finding of all violations or automatic penalties.

### Questions Presented

1. Whether voters denied information that federal election law requires a political committee to disclose suffer an injury in fact sufficient to establish Article III standing when the informational deprivation is shared by numerous other voters.
2. Whether a statutory authorization to challenge the Federal Election Commission's dismissal of an administrative complaint permits judicial review of the Commission's determination notwithstanding ordinary principles limiting review of executive enforcement discretion.
3. Whether the Federal Election Campaign Act's definition of a political committee encompasses an organization making covered contributions or coordinated expenditures above the statutory threshold, even when the organization's major purpose is not the nomination or election of candidates.
4. Whether the Commission may rely upon its interpretation of the statute's political-committee requirements to withhold enforcement and thereby deny the public the disclosures that would otherwise be required.

**Standing must precede statutory merits.** Simulated *Lujan* requires a concrete, personal injury and traceability and redressability, with proof appropriate to the stage. It does not equate widespread injury with abstract grievance or erase Congress's ability to protect concrete interests. A voter seeking particular legally required campaign information for participation in elections asserts a different injury from a citizen demanding general compliance. The individual connection must be identified; filing an administrative complaint alone is not an Article III injury.

Pre-divergence *Public Citizen v. Department of Justice* supplies the relevant informational-access analogy. Simulated *United Food & Commercial Workers v. Brown Group* confirms, in its WARN setting, that Congress can alter prudential representational rules without eliminating the constitutional requirement of concrete injury. It does not make every statutory complainant constitutionally injured. These voters' own informational claims must therefore be assessed independently of any abstract ability to complain.

**Statutory review and remedy.** FECA §437g(a)(8) expressly allows an aggrieved complainant to challenge dismissal, permits a contrary-to-law declaration and an order to conform within thirty days, and supplies a subsequent private action if the Commission fails to conform. That express channel differs from unreviewable enforcement discretion in the absence of a statutory review provision. Agency discretion on lawful grounds does not automatically eliminate judicial power to correct the legal premise actually used. The likelihood of obtaining useful information through the statutory process remains relevant; an order cannot rest on speculation disconnected from these transactions.

**Substantive distinction and limits.** Pre-divergence Buckley and MCFL must be read in their actual independent-expression contexts. Simulated *Colorado Republican* protects the genuinely independent party advertisement and rejects party status alone as proof of coordination. Its six-Justice holding does not adopt a universal express-advocacy formula, immunize actual contributions, or resolve every coordinated-expenditure limitation. Applying its boundary here requires identifying genuine candidate-connected transactions, not inferring coordination from lobbying or political sympathy. The statute's separate exclusion for qualifying membership communications must also be applied before treating communications as covered expenditures; the agency's membership classification cannot be skipped. Later proposed membership regulations are not operative OT1996 law. The question whether the major-purpose limitation must extend to covered transactions is open; a broad reading of judicial language is not itself delegated agency policymaking.

Simulated *FEC v. NRA Political Victory Fund* concerns authority to petition for certiorari and timely ratification. It does not settle standing, the political-committee definition, or all Commission enforcement authority.

## C. LITIGATION POSITIONS, LAWFUL PATHS, AND UNCERTAINTY

The voters contend that withholding required information affects their own electoral choices and that FECA expressly permits correction of an erroneous dismissal. They argue that a large lobbying organization cannot avoid disclosure of qualifying candidate-related financing merely because that financing is a small part of its budget. The Commission disputes standing and redressability, invokes enforcement discretion, and reads Buckley/MCFL as establishing an organization-wide major-purpose limit.

The Court may affirm standing and require reconsideration under a corrected construction, sustain the Commission's interpretation, or resolve a genuine jurisdictional deficiency before merits. A remand may require findings on the actual character, amount, and statutory treatment of payments. It cannot automatically classify all lobbying expenditures as campaign expenditures or order all associational information disclosed without a statutory basis. Any future enforcement choice must follow the lawful statute; the Court does not select penalties or dictate a discretionary settlement here.

## B. ANSWERS AND APPLICATION

1. **Yes, the voters have Article III standing, and sharing the injury with many others does not defeat it.** FECA gives voters an individual right to the information it requires political committees to disclose, and these voters use that information in deciding how to vote. Being denied it is a concrete injury even though many voters suffer the same denial. Disagreement with AIPAC's policy is insufficient by itself, and the holding does not give any citizen standing to compel enforcement of any statute. Stone applies Lujan's requirements and does not reject them: the injury is the denial of a specific statutory disclosure, it falls on these voters, and the record identifies practical electoral use.

2. **The express FECA channel permits judicial review of this dismissal.** Stone distinguishes an agency's choice among lawful enforcement priorities from its claim that the statute excludes the organization altogether. Correcting a legal premise can restore the process directed toward disclosure. The retained possibility of a later lawful discretionary decision does not necessarily defeat redressability where the disputed premise now blocks that process and the statute provides a further enforcement route.

3. **The membership-communications exclusion is decided first, and the major-purpose question is reserved.** FECA excludes qualifying communications by a membership organization to its members from the definition of expenditure. The Commission considered membership uncertainty when it declined action under the separate corporate contribution/expenditure prohibition; it did not finally resolve that uncertainty as a ground determining the committee-status coverage question presented here. That statutory question may resolve part or all of the dispute, so the Court does not reach whether a major campaign purpose limits committee status. It leaves open whether the limiting construction in Buckley and MCFL applies to actual qualifying contributions or coordinated transactions above the statutory threshold, and it leaves Colorado Republican's requirement of a real candidate connection untouched.

4. **The Commission may not sustain the dismissal without first deciding the membership-communications question.** It may interpret genuine statutory ambiguities within lawful authority and exercise remaining discretion on adequately stated permissible grounds. It receives no special power to conclusively determine the scope of constitutional precedent. Stone returns the unresolved coverage and transaction questions to the agency rather than replacing them with a judicial finding that any asserted dollar is covered.

## C. PRECISE PROPOSED DISPOSITION

Affirm the en banc court's holding that the voters have standing and may obtain review of the Commission's dismissal. Vacate the remainder of its judgment, which rested on rejecting a major-purpose limitation, and remand through the statutory review channel so that the Commission first decides whether AIPAC's challenged communications are membership communications and then reconsiders any remaining questions. The major-purpose question is reserved. Preserve source-specific coverage findings, constitutional protection of genuine independent expression, and lawful enforcement discretion. No committee designation, compelled disclosure outside the statute's scope, or penalty is imposed.
