## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Caption:** CompuServe, Inc. v. Patterson

**Lower-court decision:** 89 F.3d 1257 (6th Cir. 1996); No. 95-3452; decided 1996-07-22. The index separately supplies the scheduled OT1996 merits-hearing date.

**Procedural category:** Simulated-grant appellate merits. **Area of law:** Civil procedure; electronic commerce; specific personal jurisdiction.

**Questions for the authorized simulation review:**

1. Whether a person purposefully avails himself of the benefits of conducting business in a forum state, consistently with the Due Process Clause, by entering an ongoing commercial relationship with a computer network service located in that state and distributing software through its electronic systems.
2. Whether personal jurisdiction over an out-of-state defendant may rest upon electronic commercial transactions and related communications when the defendant has no physical presence in the forum.
3. What relationship must exist between the defendant's electronic commercial contacts and the plaintiff's claims to support specific personal jurisdiction.

The questions stated in this packet define the established simulation grant; they are not quotations attributed to a historical Supreme Court order. Any vehicle clarification is identified in the question-change record.

**Record and proceedings:** CompuServe, headquartered in Columbus, Ohio, operated a computer network through which subscribers could obtain and license shareware. Richard Patterson, a Texas lawyer doing business as FlashPoint Development, subscribed and became a software provider. FlashPoint was his business name, not a distinct established corporate defendant. He accepted a Shareware Registration Agreement electronically by typing agreement to its terms. Incorporated terms stated Ohio formation and Ohio governing law; the record does not establish a forum-selection or consent-to-jurisdiction clause.

Between 1991 and 1994 Patterson sent thirty-two master software files to CompuServe's Ohio system, advertised through it and received fees collected by CompuServe in Ohio after a fifteen-percent deduction. CompuServe alleged exclusive marketing through its system. Patterson reported less than $650 in sales to twelve Ohio residents and said he had never visited Ohio. He later asserted common-law rights in the names WinNAV, Windows Navigator and FlashPoint Windows Navigator, accused CompuServe of infringing those names through competing software, sent electronic and ordinary correspondence and demanded at least $100,000 to resolve the dispute.

CompuServe brought a diversity-based declaratory action in the Southern District of Ohio concerning common-law trademarks and unfair or deceptive trade practices. Patterson sought dismissal on several grounds. The District Court, without an evidentiary hearing, relied in part on his affidavit and dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction; it also denied reconsideration. The Sixth Circuit reversed and remanded, finding a prima facie jurisdictional showing. It did not decide Patterson's other dismissal grounds or intellectual-property merits. Patterson filed no appellate brief and did not appear at circuit argument; those historical absences do not establish nonparticipation in this simulated Supreme Court proceeding.

**Relief and scope:** Patterson seeks reinstatement of the personal-jurisdiction dismissal. CompuServe seeks affirmance of the appellate judgment allowing the action to continue. The issue is specific jurisdiction over this commercial dispute on a papers-only motion, not general jurisdiction, venue, liability on the marks, or jurisdiction over every ordinary subscriber.

### The governing test and the actual forum contacts

The federal diversity court uses the applicable Ohio long-arm statute, including Ohio Revised Code §2307.382(A)(1)'s transacting-business provision, and the Fourteenth Amendment's due-process limit through the applicable service/jurisdiction rules. International Shoe, Hanson v. Denckla, World-Wide Volkswagen and Burger King supply the surviving predivergence framework: defendant-created purposeful affiliation, a sufficient relationship between the forum contacts and this controversy, and fair play and substantial justice. A contract or choice-of-law clause alone is not dispositive; prior dealings, contemplated obligations, actual course of performance and the nature of the disputed relationship matter. Physical entry is not an indispensable prerequisite. A plaintiff's residence and a defendant's mere foreseeability that information might reach a state are insufficient by themselves.

Simulated **Quill Corp. v. North Dakota (May 26, 1992)** supplies a relevant, bounded confirmation. Eight Justices held that purposeful sustained remote solicitation could satisfy due process for a use-tax collection obligation without physical presence. Stone did not reach that due-process issue. Its retained Commerce Clause collection rule, supported at the narrower precedent ground, and its five-Justice finding about insignificant licensed software do not create a general software-nexus rule or a defense to ordinary civil personal jurisdiction. Applied here, Quill prevents treating physical absence as mechanically dispositive, while Burger King and the specific-jurisdiction authorities still require a claim-linked examination of Patterson's own activities. No tax threshold or presumed minimum volume of software sales is imported.

The Asahi stream-of-commerce opinions were fractured. No majority adopted a universal rule that mere distribution everywhere establishes purposeful availment everywhere. Patterson's knowing, continuing relationship with this identified Ohio intermediary presents more specific alleged conduct than passive availability or an unidentified downstream sale. The state connection must still be attributable to him, and the common-law trademark controversy must arise out of or relate to the relevant commercial contacts. The reasonableness inquiry considers his actual Texas-to-Ohio litigation burden, Ohio's legitimate interest, CompuServe's interest in effective relief and the interstate interests; commercial scale alone gives CompuServe no jurisdictional privilege.

The District Court decided without an evidentiary hearing. On the governing prima facie motion standard, supported jurisdictional allegations are considered favorably to the plaintiff, rather than resolving conflicting affidavits against it as if a trial had occurred. That provisional showing is not a final determination of disputed jurisdictional facts or a judgment on liability. The Declaratory Judgment Act supplies a remedy, not independent subject-matter jurisdiction; the pleaded diversity basis and unresolved defenses retain their distinct operation.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL POSITIONS

CompuServe emphasizes Patterson's deliberate provider agreement, repeated uploads to its Ohio system, marketing, Ohio collection of fees and the dispute arising from that relationship. Patterson emphasizes physical absence, small direct Ohio sales, standardized terms prepared by CompuServe and the fact that trademark claims might exist even had he marketed elsewhere. The District Court regarded the network's Ohio location as incidental; the Sixth Circuit treated the actual ongoing provider relationship as central. The Court must decide from those contacts and this claim, without assuming that electronic communication either dissolves territorial limits or is legally less real than other deliberate interstate dealings.

The Court can affirm the Sixth Circuit's reversal and permit further proceedings subject to proof and unresolved defenses; reverse and restore dismissal if the alleged contacts or connection to the claim are insufficient; or order a limited factual inquiry if the motion posture legally requires it. # 97. Vitronics Corp. v. Conceptronic, Inc.

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Whether the meaning of disputed patent-claim language must be determined primarily from intrinsic evidence, including the claims, specification, and prosecution history, before reliance upon extrinsic evidence.

2. Whether a federal court may rely upon expert testimony or other extrinsic evidence to adopt a claim interpretation inconsistent with the meaning established by the patent's intrinsic record.

3. Under what circumstances may technical dictionaries, scientific publications, and expert testimony properly assist judicial construction of patent claims.

## B. RECORD, POSTURE AND RELIEF

The dispute concerns “solder reflow temperature” in a patent for soldering printed-circuit assemblies. After hearing the trial evidence, the district judge adopted a liquidus-temperature construction; Vitronics then conceded that construction required judgment as a matter of law because it had offered no infringement proof under that reading. The jury trial ended in judgment for Conceptronic without a jury infringement verdict. The Federal Circuit read the specification as requiring peak reflow temperature and reversed and remanded for further proceedings. Prosecution history was not in evidence. The selected questions concern construction and evidentiary use; the packet does not add an equivalents claim or direct a finding of infringement.

## C. ENTERING LAW APPLIED TO THE RECORD

Simulated Markman PartI unanimously assigns legally operative claim meaning to the judge while preserving disputed infringement facts for their proper factfinder. PartII has eight votes and includes genuine technical-usage and expert-credibility disputes embedded in construction within the judge's work. Stone did not join PartII; his separate view cannot narrow that majority. PartIII rejects any general rule that any intrinsic evidence always defeats any expert account. It also leaves appellate review of subsidiary findings open.

The claim language, specification and any relevant prosecution history anchor public notice and construction. Technical evidence can explain actual usage and comprehension but cannot simply replace an evident definition with a different limitation. Here the specification's described temperatures and integrated embodiment bear directly on the disputed term. The absence of prosecution evidence is not a basis to invent a disclaimer. Dictionaries may assist understanding consistently with the patent. Later Phillips and Teva rules are unavailable, and Markman does not require a separate hearing in every case.

## D. PARTY POSITIONS, AVAILABLE DISPOSITIONS AND LIMITS

The patentee relies on the specification's integrated description and contends that the liquidus reading excludes its disclosed embodiment. The accused infringer relies on technical accounts supporting the narrower construction. The Court must evaluate whether those accounts illuminate genuine usage or contradict the instrument's resolved meaning. It may affirm a corrected construction and remand infringement, or return a genuine unresolved construction issue to the assigned judge. No construction ruling alone establishes that every claim element is present in the accused process.

### Provision-specific temporal and review rules

In simulated **Landgraf v. USI Film Products (April 26, 1994)**, five Justices, including Stone, adopted the sequential inquiry: follow Congress's prescribed temporal reach, subject to constitutional limits; otherwise identify whether the particular provision imposes new legal consequences on a completed event. Pending litigation alone does not establish retroactivity, and a jurisdictional label alone does not defeat it. The distinct three-Justice relevant-activity approach is not the Court's general rationale. Accordingly, the court must distinguish withdrawing future ordinary appellate review under §440(a), eliminating substantive eligibility for discretionary relief under §440(d), and changing the consequences of an earlier plea or concession. The date on which each occurred matters. A disappointed hope of favorable discretion is not automatically an impaired entitlement, but the discretionary character of the ultimate benefit does not by itself resolve every completed-transaction question.

In simulated **Felker v. Turpin (June 28, 1996)**, the Court unanimously enforced §2244(b)(3)(E)'s bar on certiorari review of a successive-petition authorization decision while preserving the distinct original authority expressly left in §2241. Its temporal result concerns authorization and review proceedings begun after April 24, with six Justices including Stone joining the provision-specific Landgraf explanation. It does not decide pending immigration appeals or all AEDPA provisions. Its constitutional holding depended on actual independent consideration through the retained route and an adequate traditional ground for denying that original writ. Merely naming a possible remedy is insufficient to reproduce that reasoning. Section 1651 assists existing jurisdiction; it does not create a substitute forum.

The older immigration statute expressly authorized direct appellate review under 8 U.S.C. §1105a(a) and contained a specific custody-habeas provision in paragraph (10). AEDPA §440(a), enacted April 24, 1996, replaced paragraph (10) with a prohibition on review of final deportation orders based on specified convictions. Section 440(f)'s express effective-date provision concerns §440(e), not a universal saving clause for these petitions. The general federal habeas statute, §2241, and the traditional distinction between restricted appellate review and habeas remain separately relevant. Ex parte McCardle, Ex parte Yerger and Heikkila v. Barber supply predivergence historical context; none authorizes a court to ignore the precise later enactment. Whether §440(a) leaves §2241 review of a deportation order's legal basis, and what review the Constitution independently requires, are open questions here, not holdings already supplied by Felker.

Simulated **Reno v. Flores (March 23, 1993)** upheld specified juvenile-custody arrangements and request-based review on a facial record while preserving statutory dispatch limits, actual habeas protection, effective notice, requested review and individual constitutional challenges. The six-Justice Court rationale does not include Stone's narrower separate analysis. Applied here, Flores confirms that lawful immigration administration and genuine procedural limits can coexist with enforceable individual safeguards; it does not establish that a hypothetical detention-only proceeding can decide every objection to a final removal order. The Japanese Immigrant Case supplies the antecedent due-process protection in deportation proceedings.

No later historical Supreme Court construction of AEDPA or IIRIRA governs this simulation automatically. These are old deportation proceedings with original agency final orders entered before September 30, 1996. A later enactment must be applied through its own effective-date and transition provisions; the mere date of this Court's simulated consideration does not convert the underlying proceeding into a newly commenced removal case.

### Application to this petition

Landgraf requires assessing the changed channel on the assumed February 16 filing date. Section 440(a) addresses future appellate adjudication of the covered order; the record identifies neither a defense surrendered in reliance on direct review nor a new §440(d) denial of eligibility. Nevertheless, the removal of all judicial consideration of a preserved legal defect would raise a different issue from moving or narrowing ordinary appellate review. Felker permits the distinction only after the relevant alternative is identified and shown to perform the necessary work. Here the Government's detention-only concession does not itself establish review of the legal authority for deportation. Flores likewise preserved actual statutory and individual safeguards rather than upholding every administrative action merely because some review might exist.

### Issue 1: User-added equipment is other property

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the classification because it is the legal basis of the judgment.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Under the maritime economic-loss rule of East River, is equipment added to a product by the initial user after purchase “other property” when a defect in the original product later destroys both the product and the added equipment after resale?
* **Stone's Legal Position:** Apply East River to the product the manufacturer supplied and avoid converting resale bundling into retroactive expansion of that product.
* **Stone's Proposed Holding:** Equipment added by the initial user after the manufacturer sold the vessel is “other property” for the maritime economic-loss rule when a defect in the original vessel later destroys that equipment, even if the equipment is transferred with the vessel to a subsequent purchaser.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The rule rests on the original commercial bargain: warranty law governs the manufacturer’s product, while tort law protects outside property. The manufacturer did not supply or price the added equipment and the initial purchaser could have recovered for its loss. Resale does not transform the nature of the property or reduce the later owner’s protection.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not classify equipment supplied by the manufacturer, replacement components integrated into the original product, or items whose provenance is disputed.
