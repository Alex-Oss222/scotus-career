# 143. Raines v. Byrd

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Raines v. Byrd
* **Citation or Docket:** 521 U.S. 811 (1997); No. 96-1671
* **October Term:** 1996
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued expedited direct appeal from a three-judge district court declaring the Line Item Veto Act unconstitutional at the suit of six Members of Congress.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 26, 1997 (historical)
* **Question Presented:** Do individual Members of Congress who voted against the Line Item Veto Act have Article III standing to challenge it based on alleged dilution of their legislative power before the President has used the cancellation authority against a measure producing a personal, concrete injury?
* **Question Granted:** Whether the plaintiff legislators had standing and, only if so, whether the Act violated Article I’s lawmaking requirements or nondelegation principles.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The three-judge District Court denied dismissal, found standing and ripeness, and entered summary judgment declaring the Act unconstitutional under the Presentment Clause and nondelegation doctrine. Byrd v. Raines, 956 F. Supp. 25 (D.D.C. 1997).
* **Relief Requested:** Executive officials seek vacatur and dismissal for lack of standing or reversal on the merits. The Members seek affirmance of the constitutional judgment.
* **Material Record:** Four Senators and two Representatives voted against the Line Item Veto Act, which nevertheless passed both Houses and was signed. The Act authorized the President, after signing specified legislation, to cancel certain spending and tax items subject to statutory procedures. The Members sued the day after the Act took effect, before any cancellation harmed them personally. They alleged that the Act altered the meaning and practical effect of their future votes and shifted institutional power to the President. Neither House authorized them to represent Congress, and both Houses opposed the suit’s premise or participated separately.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Article III standing is jurisdictional and cannot be supplied by Congress’s statutory authorization to sue. The Court must distinguish personal injury, vote nullification, and abstract institutional dilution. If standing fails, the constitutional merits and ripeness need not be reached.
* **Relevant Dates:** The Act was signed April 4, 1996 and took effect January 1, 1997; suit was filed January 2, 1997; the District Court ruled April 10; argument occurred May 27; the Supreme Court event date is June 26, 1997.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None identified as a separate matter requiring adjudication.

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** No supplied simulated holding squarely resolves this federal legislator-standing claim. Existing Article III rules require concrete, particularized injury, causation, and redressability and place thresholds before merits.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Concrete injury

* **Reasoning Below:** The District Court held that altered voting power and institutional subservience were sufficient injuries and that the claim was ripe immediately.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** The Members’ votes were counted and lost. They allege only abstract, widely shared institutional dilution, lack authorization from their Houses, and retain legislative remedies.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** The Act changes the legal effect of every appropriation vote and removes Members from the constitutionally prescribed repeal process, causing present official-capacity injury.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** Even if Congress eliminated prudential standing limits, the Court must dismiss unless the injury meets Article III’s personal and particularized requirements.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** Dismissal leaves the Act open to challenge by a person concretely injured by an actual cancellation.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The threshold path is dispositive. If the Members’ votes on the Act were fully counted and their asserted harm is a generalized institutional loss shared by all Members, Coleman’s vote-nullification exception does not apply. The District Court judgment must be vacated and the complaint dismissed. The Court should state no position on Presentment, delegation, or the Act’s validity in a suit by a proper plaintiff.

# 144. Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union

- **Entry Stage and Event:** Direct appeal from a three-judge district court; argued March 19, 1997; plenary merits decision pending.

- **Question Presented:** Whether the Communications Decency Act's criminal restrictions on transmitting or displaying indecent or patently offensive material in a manner available to minors violate the First Amendment.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the challenged indecency and patently-offensive provisions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad, and not narrowly fitted to protecting minors.

# 145. Vacco v. Quill

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Vacco v. Quill
- **Citation or Docket:** 521 U.S. 793; No. 95-1858; useful lower-court citation: 80 F.3d 716 (2d Cir. 1996) (en banc).
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Certiorari granted; argued January 8, 1997; plenary merits decision pending.
- **Simulated Event Date:** June 26, 1997.
- **Question Presented:** Whether New York's prohibition of assisted suicide violates equal protection because the State permits a competent person to refuse life-sustaining medical treatment.
- **Question Granted:** Whether the State may distinguish between refusing unwanted treatment and intentionally assisting suicide without violating the Equal Protection Clause.
- **Legal Field:** Equal protection, end-of-life medical decisions, treatment refusal, assisted suicide, and rational-basis review.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The en banc Second Circuit held New York's distinction unconstitutional as applied to competent, terminally ill persons in the circumstances presented.
- **Requested Supreme Court Judgment and Relief:** New York asks the Court to reverse. The physicians and patients ask the Court to affirm the equal-protection judgment.
- **Material Facts and Record:** New York criminalized intentionally aiding another person's suicide while recognizing a competent patient's authority to refuse or discontinue unwanted medical treatment and permitting good-faith palliative care even when death might be foreseen. The challenged distinction turned on consent to bodily intervention, the actor's purpose, and the legal cause assigned to death.
- **Preservation, Threshold, and Vehicle Matters:** The law applies without an express status classification. The Court must analyze the conduct distinction rather than equate acts solely because both may precede death. Treatment refusal, advance directives, pain relief, and good-faith palliation remain independently protected or regulated under their own sources. No evidence establishes that New York enacted or administered the prohibition from animus toward terminally ill or disabled persons.

### Issue 1: Equal-protection distinction between treatment refusal and assisted suicide

- **Controlling Simulated Law:** Actual simulated Romer invalidates Amendment 2's particular blanket disability under ordinary rational-basis review; it creates no new status tier or independent hostility-proof prerequisite. ## F. VERIFIED SIMULATED-LAW APPLICATION

The actual simulated Romer applies ordinary rational-basis review to Amendment 2's unexplained blanket disability, without a new tier or separate mandatory hostility finding; its equality constraint is not restricted to laws using a status label. Heller and Beach permit supported generalization and conceivable legitimate purposes while retaining the requirement of a relationship to the actual disadvantage and the Moreno/Cleburne limit. Compare the challenged acts and persons here: capacity to refuse unwanted bodily intervention, intentional assistance in causing death, and the resulting practical distinction. Simulated Casey protects the specific reproductive choice and related clinical interests under its own six-step standard; it supplies no general holding equating all end-of-life choices. Cruzan, a pre-divergence case, recognizes the traditional interest in refusing unwanted treatment but assumed for its disposition a competent person's constitutional right to refuse artificial nutrition/hydration and upheld Missouri's clear-and-convincing safeguard for an incompetent patient's prior wishes. It does not itself settle every competent refusal or assisted-death question.
