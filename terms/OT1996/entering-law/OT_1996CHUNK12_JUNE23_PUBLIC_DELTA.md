# Kolster v. INS; First Circuit No. 96-1194; 101 F.3d 785; no separate Supreme Court docket assigned.

Authority: Kolster_v_INS_merits_1997-06-23.md.

## Event

Kolster v. INS; First Circuit No. 96-1194, 101 F.3d 785. On review of the First Circuit's December 4, 1996 dismissal of a petition challenging a deportation order. Heard on the merits and decided June 23, 1997. The questions are whether AEDPA §440(a) governs the pending petition, whether withdrawal of ordinary appellate review is lawful, and what judicial consideration remains for substantial legal and constitutional objections. Kolster disputes the legal eligibility rule that prevented consideration of §212(c) relief; he does not merely seek a more favorable discretionary balance. The domicile merits are outside this review.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate in the merits hearing and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the dismissal of the ordinary petition for review.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The direct petition ends. The Court does not decide the domicile calculation, grant a waiver or adjudicate, convert or transfer a collateral petition.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete transition construction; eight direct joins |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Completed-event analysis and retained §2241 authority; six direct joins |
| Opinion of the Court, Part IV | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous confined mandate | Entire part |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Affirms on distinct temporal grounds; reserves collateral construction | Joins Court Parts I and IV, not II–III |

Stone-Zsela agrees with Part I's disposition without joining its reasoning.

## Holdings

### I. The saved proceeding remains subject to AEDPA's independent restriction

**Holding and operative rule:** Corrected IIRIRA §§306(c)(1) and 309(c)(1) preserve the former review scheme for this proceeding begun before April 1, 1997, subject to their particular exceptions; §242(g) reaches old proceedings but does not itself withdraw review of this legal objection to a completed adjudication. Section 306(c)(2) protects an actual judicial judgment or order entered under former INA §106, applying that section as amended by AEDPA §440, against an operation of §306(c)(1) that would invalidate it or require reconsideration; that protection neither supplies jurisdiction, reopens merits review nor bars ordinary appellate review of the judgment's correctness.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with this transition disposition without joining its reasoning.

**Controlling explanation:** Yang supplies the April 1 commencement rule; Reyes-Hernandez, Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro distinguish the executive decisions or actions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases and execute orders from review of a completed legal adjudication. Section 309(c)(2) permits election of new procedures only before the evidentiary hearing has commenced and with notice at least thirty days before it. Paragraph (3) permits termination and reinitiation only before a final administrative decision. No qualifying election is established. Paragraph (4) requires a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996; the January 30, 1996 order does not meet that condition. Public Law 104-302 made §306(c)(1)'s general operation subject to §309 while retaining §242(g)'s cross-temporal reach; it left §306(c)(2) unchanged. The First Circuit's December 4, 1996 dismissal applied former §106 as amended by §440(a), so paragraph (2) protects it against invalidation or required reconsideration attributable to paragraph (1), not against this ordinary correctness review. These savings do not erase AEDPA's separate restriction merely because the old procedural scheme otherwise survives.

### II. Section 440(a) governs this ordinary petition

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA §440(a) withdraws ordinary appellate review of Kolster's final deportation order based on the admitted drug and aggravated-felony grounds, independently supported by the conviction documents, notwithstanding his February 28, 1996 petition. On the established facts, application of the channel restriction imposes no supported new legal disability on his plea, concession or another protected completed event; this conclusion establishes neither a universal actual-reliance requirement nor a rule that pleas or eligibility for discretionary relief can never support nonretroactivity.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the dismissal on a different temporal ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf requires statutory temporal direction first and, absent sufficient direction, an inquiry into new consequences of completed acts. Rivers preserves that provision-specific method. Section 440(f)'s express timing clause concerns subsection (e), not a saving clause for subsection (a). Reyes-Hernandez protected an actual surrendered colorable defense; Kolster identifies none, and conviction documents independently supported deportability. Even under his preferred domicile calculation, his September 1992 plea preceded seven-year eligibility by three years. The Court does not infer his subjective motives from those facts. They establish no lost legal option of the kind asserted. Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro establish that filing and an expectation of review alone do not preserve this channel; their holdings do not make surrender of a defense the only possible protected event. Hughes Aircraft concerns creation of a previously unavailable private enforcement action, a new consequence not established here. A jurisdictional label alone supplies no answer, and §440(d)'s distinct substantive eligibility amendment remains unadjudicated.

### III. Independent habeas jurisdiction survives for substantial legal and constitutional objections

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 440(a)'s replacement of a particular INA review channel does not clearly repeal the independent jurisdiction enacted in 28 U.S.C. §2241. When custody, proper respondent, forum, exhaustion and other applicable predicates are satisfied, that jurisdiction permits consideration of a substantial legal or constitutional claim that executive custody or deportation lacks lawful authority, including a claim that an unlawful eligibility rule prevented consideration of discretionary relief, and permits effective relief if the claim warrants it.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** Felker distinguishes repeal of a specified appellate mechanism from the separate statutory habeas authority that Congress leaves in place. McCardle and Yerger likewise require attention to the channel actually withdrawn; Heikkila distinguishes restricted ordinary immigration review from habeas. The replacement of former §106(a)(10) does not identify or clearly repeal §2241's separate grant. Unlike Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro, this case presents a developed legal objection: the agency refused to consider a waiver because of its construction of eligibility. Judicial reweighing of discretionary equities is different. A detention-only remedy required to assume the challenged legal rule valid would not answer this objection. Congress may channel presentation and impose valid procedural conditions, but the retained court must be able to determine the substantial authority claim actually presented and afford effective relief when warranted. This construction permits withdrawal of ordinary review without approving insulation of that claim from meaningful judicial consideration. The objection that no collateral petition is before the Court limits the disposition: the Court decides statutory capability, not whether Kolster satisfies its predicates or deserves relief. No freestanding jurisdiction arises from §1651, and no constitutional violation follows automatically from every statutory error. The Court reserves a demonstrated failure of this retained route and does not define every constitutional minimum or reopen factual and discretionary questions generally.

### IV. The direct petition ends without a decision on eligibility

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Circuit's dismissal of the direct petition is affirmed. The Court neither decides the lawful-domicile calculation nor orders a waiver, agency reconsideration, conversion or transfer of the petition, collateral relief, a stay or execution of deportation.

**Authority:** Part IV of Souter's opinion is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The absence of direct-review jurisdiction prevents adjudication of the eligibility merits through this petition. Recognition of a conditional collateral source does not establish custody, a proper respondent or forum, exhaustion, or an actual collateral filing. Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro preserve this channel-specific remedy. The sentencing judge's recommendation does not bind the immigration decisionmaker. Any properly brought collateral challenge must establish its own predicates before its legal claim and requested relief can be decided.

## Precedent Treatment

- Yang supplies the commencement rule and separates legal eligibility from discretion; no agency-merits decision follows here.
- Reyes-Hernandez supplies the limited §242(g) construction and is distinguished on the actual lost-defense premise; other completed-event predicates remain possible.
- Hincapie-Nieto applies to filing and expectations alone; its reservation concerning an unpresented legal defect is now addressed at the conditional statutory scope stated here.
- Salazar-Haro supplies the full transition protection and is distinguished because this record presents a developed legal eligibility objection.
- Landgraf supplies the controlling temporal sequence; Rivers preserves its provision-specific operation. Neither is replaced by the concurrence's relevant-activity approach.
- Hughes Aircraft is distinguished because new private enforcement exposure differs from the channel change established here.
- Felker's separation of withdrawn and retained statutory channels is extended to the independently enacted immigration habeas authority at issue; its meaningful-review principle does not make a nominal substitute sufficient.
- McCardle and Yerger support channel-specific repeal analysis; Heikkila supplies the distinction between ordinary immigration review and habeas.
- Flores retains its custody-specific safeguards and does not itself decide review of a deportation order's legal basis.

## Law After Decision

Section 440(a) withdraws this ordinary petition, while §2241 remains an independently enacted, conditional route for substantial legal and constitutional challenges to executive custody or deportation authority. The legal eligibility question may be considered through that route only if its actual jurisdictional and procedural predicates are met. Neither the substantive seven-year rule nor §440(d)'s separate temporal operation is decided. Section 306(c)(2) protects qualifying judicial judgments and orders under former §106 as amended by AEDPA §440 only against invalidation or required reconsideration caused by §306(c)(1); it creates no jurisdiction and bars no ordinary correctness review. No precedent is overruled.

## Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurs in part and in the judgment. The relevant statutory activity is the court's present exercise of ordinary review, so the new restriction governs that activity. The concurrence joins the transition construction and confined mandate but does not adopt the Court's general completed-event analysis or its construction of an unfiled collateral route. The three Justices recognize the difference between a legal eligibility objection and discretionary reweighing; they reserve the source, scope and predicates of collateral review and do not pronounce an unidentified remedy adequate or approve elimination of every meaningful forum.

## Procedure After Action

The First Circuit's dismissal stands. A properly brought collateral challenge must establish its own custody, respondent, forum, exhaustion and other applicable requirements. The mandate orders no agency reconsideration, waiver, transfer, stay, release or execution of deportation.

## Source Notes

The First Circuit's opinion, 101 F.3d 785, supplies the record and lower judgment. AEDPA §440, IIRIRA §§306 and 309 as corrected by Public Law 104-302, and 28 U.S.C. §2241 supply the statutory provisions. No custody finding, filed habeas petition or adjudication of the domicile merits is supplied by this record.

# Lindh v. Murphy; No. 96-6298.

Authority: Lindh_v_Murphy_merits_1997-06-23.md.

## Event

Lindh v. Murphy, No. 96-6298. Argued April 14, 1997; decided June 23, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, which applied amended chapter 153 to Lindh's pending habeas application and denied relief. The question is whether amended §2254(d) and the related chapter 153 provisions govern a noncapital application filed before AEDPA's April 24, 1996 enactment. Lindh filed July 9, 1992; his appeal was pending when the Act became law. The underlying Confrontation Clause claim is not decided.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate in the merits hearing and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Seventh Circuit's judgment and remands for consideration under pre-AEDPA habeas law.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Seventh Circuit must adjudicate Lindh's appeal under the habeas law governing the application before AEDPA. No writ or other merits relief is awarded.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion |
| Dissent | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm the temporal determination | Entire dissent; no independent confrontation adjudication |

## Holdings

### The chapter 153 amendments at issue do not govern this pre-enactment noncapital application

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA's chapter 153 amendments, including amended §2254(d), do not govern Lindh's noncapital federal habeas application filed before April 24, 1996. The application-date construction preserves separately directed provisions, chapter 154's express pending-case rule and the successive-petition provisions governed by Felker; it does not exempt every pending proceeding from every AEDPA provision.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer. Six Justices directly adopt the statutory rule, its limits and reversal with remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf makes Congress's temporal choice the first inquiry. Ordinary statutory construction may establish that choice without a prescribed formula. Congress enacted the neighboring ordinary-habeas and capital-habeas chapters together and expressly directed chapter 154 to pending cases in §107(c), while omitting that direction for chapter 153. That deliberate contrast supplies the application-date inference here. The contrary reading stresses the capital chapter's distinct State-qualification system. Those differences do not deprive Congress's adjacent and express treatment of pending applications of significance. Applying the same pending-case reach to chapter 153 would erase that contrast. The changed standards governing an already filed request for relief reinforce the need to honor the enacted distinction; the Court need not classify every amendment as substantive or procedural to decide the case. Felker applied a particular authorization-review restriction to proceedings begun after enactment and expressly reserved other Title I temporal questions. It neither fixes chapter 153's present reach nor displaces the structural inquiry. Lindh filed on July 9, 1992, almost four years before enactment. His appeal must therefore be considered under the habeas law governing that application before AEDPA. The Court expresses no view on the psychiatrist cross-examination claim, bias, harmlessness, exhaustion, chapter 154 State qualification or ultimate entitlement to a writ. No constitutional right to permanent retention of every filing-date judicial rule is announced.

## Precedent Treatment

- Landgraf supplies the prior inquiry into statutory temporal direction; ordinary construction, including the enacted chapter contrast, resolves that inquiry here. Its default completed-event framework remains intact.
- Rivers preserves the provision-specific method; the decision establishes no timing rule for every AEDPA amendment.
- Felker is distinguished because it applied its own authorization-review provisions to proceedings begun after enactment and reserved other Title I questions. Its successive-petition and retained-original-authority holdings remain in force.
- Hughes Aircraft preserves examination of the actual statutory change and does not replace the structural inquiry or the distinct temporal methods previously expressed.

## Law After Decision

Pre-enactment noncapital applications such as Lindh's remain governed by pre-AEDPA chapter 153 law. Applications filed after enactment, chapter 154 cases within its express clause, successive-petition provisions governed by Felker and provisions carrying their own temporal commands retain their distinct treatment. The Court overrules no precedent and decides no underlying habeas merits.

## Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. Chapter 154 addresses a distinct capital scheme dependent on State qualification, so its express pending-case clause does not reliably prescribe the contrary operation for chapter 153. Amended §2254(d) regulates the court's present authority to grant relief; the dissent would apply it to this still-pending adjudication. Hughes Aircraft's expansion of private enforcement exposure differs from this restriction on current judicial relief. The dissent would affirm at the temporal scope reviewed, without deciding the confrontation claim anew or making every AEDPA provision immediately applicable.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Seventh Circuit for adjudication under pre-AEDPA standards. The Court expresses no view on the Confrontation Clause claim or ultimate entitlement to habeas relief.

## Source Notes

The application date and procedural history are stated in Lindh v. Murphy, 96 F.3d 856. AEDPA §§101–107, particularly §107(c), supply the enacted temporal comparison. This decision does not require findings about State qualification for chapter 154 or the merits of the cross-examination claim.

# Metro-North Commuter Railroad Co. v. Buckley; No. 96-320.

Authority: Metro_North_Commuter_Railroad_Co_v_Buckley_merits_1997-06-23.md.

## Event

Metro-North Commuter Railroad Co. v. Buckley, No. 96-320. Argued February 18, 1997; decided June 23, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit. At the close of Buckley's evidence the District Court entered judgment under Rule 50; the Second Circuit vacated and remanded on April 1, 1996, permitting emotional-distress and medical-monitoring claims. Buckley had extensive negligent asbestos exposure but no manifested asbestos disease. The questions are whether that contact satisfies Gottshall's physical-impact category and whether FELA permits the monitoring-cost recovery recognized below. Negligence was conceded; compensable injury, causation and damages were not.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate in the merits hearing and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the appellate determinations on emotional distress and the independent monitoring recovery and remands for proceedings consistent with its holdings.

## Judgment & Remedy

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Present emotional-distress claim | Reversed, 9–0 in judgment | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | None | Present distress theory cannot proceed; later distinct injury is not decided |
| Independent monitoring recovery recognized below | Reversed, 6–3 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg | No entitlement to the unqualified damages approved below; different properly supported arrangements remain open |

The case is remanded consistently with these holdings. No damages award, monitoring fund, schedule or automatic trial is ordered.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Reverses both components | Impact boundary and bounded monitoring rejection in full; six votes |
| Concurrence in the judgment in part and dissent in part, Part I | Ginsburg | Stevens | Supports distress reversal on different grounds | Impact accepted; severe-injury proof insufficient |
| Same separate writing, Part II | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens | Opposes monitoring reversal | Medically necessary incremental-cost claim and bounded further proceedings |
| Concurrence in the distress judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports distress reversal; also joins Ginsburg's monitoring dissent | Assumes impact without deciding it and finds distress proof insufficient |

## Holdings

### I. Symptomless asbestos exposure does not satisfy the physical-danger boundary for this distress claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Physical contact with asbestos that has caused no manifested disease and presents only a risk of later disease does not qualify as the physical impact required for this FELA negligent-emotional-injury claim. An increased risk of disease in the future likewise does not alone establish Gottshall's alternative requirement of immediate risk of physical harm; the present emotional-distress claim cannot proceed.

**Authority:** Part I of Thomas's Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: six direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg support reversal of the distress determination on evidence-sufficiency grounds, without joining this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Gottshall requires the employee to cross an independent physical-danger boundary and to establish negligence, cognizable injury and causation. Its impact category is a common-law boundary, not a rule making every particle contact a compensable impact. The extensive contact and medically supported future risk here do not supply an immediately harmful impact or the alternative immediate danger. The contrary reading correctly observes that contact occurred, but would turn the reserved future-disease category into an unrestricted route around the boundary merely by describing toxic exposure as literal touching. Urie preserves compensation for occupational disease; it does not equate the prospect of disease with present disease. Buell leaves the precise emotional-injury question to development under FELA, while Monessen and Ellis require attention to common-law limits and reject insurer liability. The Court does not import the threatened-impact branch's fear-for-self qualification into the actual-impact branch. Nor does it require psychiatric treatment or expert mental-health testimony in every case, decide every physical-manifestation question, or foreclose a later distinct manifested disease or otherwise qualifying injury. Conceded negligence and relaxed FELA causation do not eliminate the separate injury boundary. This legal ground resolves the present claim without deciding whether Buckley's distress evidence independently satisfies a universal severity test.

### II. FELA does not authorize the independent, unqualified monitoring damages recognized below

**Holding and operative rule:** Negligent asbestos exposure, increased future disease risk and the monitoring recommendations in this asymptomatic record do not establish an entitlement under FELA to the independent, unqualified lump-sum medical-monitoring recovery recognized by the Second Circuit. Such damages cannot be attached to the unsuccessful emotional-distress claim; the Court leaves more carefully limited programs, reimbursement of actual necessary costs, later illness and materially different records undecided.

**Authority:** Part II of Thomas's Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: six direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg dissent from reversal of the monitoring determination.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 51 requires injury resulting from railroad negligence. Gottshall keeps injury, negligence and causation separate; useful precautions against a future risk do not automatically establish the statutory damages category asserted here. Common-law guidance under Monessen does not freeze negligence law at its enactment, but the sources do not establish the unrestricted independent recovery approved below. Buckley argues that additional surveillance is a present expense rather than compensation for a disease that may never develop. That distinction has force as a description of the proposed spending, but it does not itself resolve the statutory injury question or the problems of speculative and duplicative lump-sum recovery. Nor can monitoring damages be recovered as an element of a distress claim that fails. Urie's coverage of actual occupational disease and Rogers's causal-contribution rule supply no missing injury predicate. The Court does not find that Buckley already receives equivalent benefits or that no properly supported alternative arrangement could ever be considered. It decides the damages entitlement sustained by the appellate judgment, not every possible monitoring remedy, and orders no particular fund, program, test schedule or automatic trial on a different claim.

## Precedent Treatment

- Gottshall is applied to its reserved toxic-contact category: literal symptomless exposure with only later disease risk is not the qualifying impact. Its independent negligence, injury and causation requirements, and threatened-impact fear-for-self limit, remain distinct.
- Buell's reserved emotional-injury inquiry is developed at this bounded scope; its treatment of independently cognizable FELA claims remains intact.
- Urie retains occupational-disease coverage; prospective disease risk is distinguished from manifested occupational disease.
- Monessen supplies common-law guidance without making every tort rule immutable.
- Ellis preserves negligence-based liability rather than workplace insurance.
- Rogers preserves FELA's causal-contribution rule without replacing the separate compensable-injury requirement.

## Law After Decision

FELA does not permit this symptomless-exposure distress claim or the independent, unqualified lump-sum monitoring recovery approved below. The Court's six-Justice rationale controls; the three evidence-sufficiency positions do not supply an additional majority holding. Later distinct illness, otherwise qualifying distress and properly supported different monitoring arrangements remain open. No rule requires professional psychiatric treatment in every case or declares every medical-monitoring remedy unavailable.

## Separate Writings

Ginsburg, joined by Stevens on distress, accepts Buckley's extensive bodily contact as physical impact. She nevertheless would reverse because the evidence, viewed favorably under Rule 50, does not establish sufficiently severe emotional injury. Anger, fear, medical risk and corroborating worker complaints are real evidence; they do not on this showing establish that injury. The lack of treatment or professional mental-health evidence bears on this record without becoming a universal prerequisite.

On monitoring, Ginsburg is joined by Stevens and Stone-Zsela. Medically necessary additional surveillance can be a present economic loss distinct from fear or future disease. They would preserve further proceedings requiring negligent exposure, materially increased serious-disease risk, competent medical proof that reasonable care calls for monitoring different from ordinary care, causation and actual incremental costs. Relief must correspond to monitoring actually necessary and available, avoiding speculative or duplicative recovery and considering comparable services if proved. They fix no amount, schedule, fund or offset and would not deny a supported tailored remedy merely because the complaint requested money.

Stone-Zsela separately concurs in the distress judgment. He assumes the disputed impact question favorably without deciding it, but finds the evidence insufficient to establish severe emotional injury even with its corroboration and lawful favorable inferences. Seriousness cannot replace Gottshall's independent physical-danger gate. He would dismiss the present distress theory without prejudice to a later distinct injury and joins Ginsburg's monitoring dissent.

## Procedure After Action

The Second Circuit must implement the reversal of the present distress theory and the monitoring-damages entitlement it recognized. Remand does not itself require adjudication of an unpleaded or unsupported alternative arrangement, guarantee a trial or prevent consideration of a different legally supported claim through its proper procedure.

## Source Notes

The record and Rule 50 posture are described in the Second Circuit's opinion, 79 F.3d 1337. FELA, 45 U.S.C. §51, supplies the injury and negligence requirements. The record does not establish a particular monitoring schedule, cost, offset, existing benefits package or damages award.

# Plyler v. Moore; Fourth Circuit No. 96-6884; 100 F.3d 365; no separate Supreme Court docket assigned.

Authority: Plyler_v_Moore_merits_1997-06-23.md.

## Event

Plyler v. Moore; Fourth Circuit No. 96-6884, 100 F.3d 365. Heard on the merits and decided June 23, 1997. On review of the Fourth Circuit's November 14, 1996 judgment sustaining termination of a federal prison-conditions consent decree entered in March 1986. The State moved under original PLRA §3626(b)(2). The questions concern Article III, the continuing rather than completed operation of the decree, whether its additional agreed protections are independent federal rights that prevent termination, and due process. The appeal presents those legal objections without a supported preserved claim that qualifying findings or a current or ongoing violation were disregarded.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate in the merits hearing and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgment sustaining termination of prospective federal enforcement against the legal challenges presented.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The original §3626(b)(3) safeguard remains part of the governing rule. The Court orders no new evidentiary hearing and makes no finding that every prison condition presently complies with federal law.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous affirmance | All four holdings and every statutory and remedial qualification |

## Holdings

**Authority for all holdings:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt the following rules and qualifications.

### I. The original PLRA supplies an independent, conditional termination rule

**Holding and operative rule:** For covered federal prison-conditions prospective relief, including relief entered before April 26, 1996, original §3626(b)(2) entitles a defendant or intervener to immediate termination when the relief was approved without findings that it was narrowly drawn, extended no further than necessary to correct the federal-right violation and was the least intrusive necessary means. Under original §3626(b)(3), relief must nevertheless continue upon written, record-based findings that it remains necessary to correct a current **or** ongoing federal violation, is no broader than necessary, and is narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means; existing substantive findings may satisfy the statute without ritual wording.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 802 expressly reaches existing prospective relief. Lewis requires examination of the actual findings and the original preservation clause; enactment alone dissolves nothing. This motion uses (b)(2), not (b)(1)'s waiting-period route or (e)'s automatic-stay mechanism. The State must establish the statutory basis for termination, and the court must independently apply (b)(3). Rufo recognizes continuing authority to modify a decree upon a significant change in fact or law, but its equitable threshold is not an additional prerequisite to this independent statutory route. Nor does relief's exceeding a constitutional minimum alone establish that it is unnecessary: a remedy may require measures beyond the minimum conduct rule to correct a proved violation. The decree's age or lack of a repeated statutory phrase likewise supplies no automatic answer. The actual appeal presents legal objections, without an established preserved failure to consider qualifying findings or a supported current or ongoing violation requiring a new hearing. The Court affirms at that scope, making no statewide compliance finding and adjudicating no accrued monetary liability.

### II. Conditional termination changes governing law without displacing Article III adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3626(b)(2), applied with the full original (b)(3) safeguard, does not violate Article III by changing the legal authority for continued prospective enforcement of this decree. Courts retain responsibility to decide the federal violation, the actual findings and the necessity and scope of continued relief; Congress does not command false facts or a particular result under unchanged law.

**Controlling explanation:** Robertson permits a genuine change in governing law while preserving judicial application. The statute changes which prospective remedies federal courts may continue to enforce and makes the outcome depend on conditions courts must determine. The command to terminate when those conditions are met is a consequence of the changed rule, not a legislative finding that South Carolina's prisons comply. The decree expressly retained supervision and modification, and the State previously invoked that process. Rufo and the prospective-relief setting of Wheeling Bridge explain why future operation remains subject to lawful changes. The prisoners' final-judgment objection does not transform this continuing decree into an accrued damages judgment. Morgan Stanley and Plaut sustain a distinct, bounded statutory reinstatement procedure for completed private limitations dismissals; that procedure is neither the source of authority here nor a general license to reopen adjudicated merits. A statute eliminating independent determination of the actual predicates would present a different question. The Court decides no cancellation of accrued damages and no general power to contradict completed judicial findings.

### III. Negotiated decree protections are not automatically independent federal rights

**Holding and operative rule:** Incorporating agreed protections into a federal consent decree does not make every protection exceeding underlying federal law the independent federal right whose violation prevents termination under §3626(b)(2). Continuing prospective enforcement remains subject to the statute, while relief necessary to correct an actual federal violation must be preserved under the full original (b)(3) conditions.

**Controlling explanation:** Rufo recognizes that lawful consent obligations may exceed constitutional minima and remain enforceable under the court's authority; it does not give each obligation immunity from a later valid statute governing that authority. Treating the decree itself as the federal-right violation sufficient to preserve every term would nullify the termination rule for consent decrees. The distinction concerns the source of the protected right, not whether every remedial detail must itself duplicate a constitutional command. Lewis permits measures needed to correct the demonstrated wrong, within the statute's findings and tailoring requirements. Joseph A. enforces an agreed performance-based termination condition where no prison-conditions predicate for the PLRA existed; its distinction between performance and modification does not displace this enacted route. The Court does not adjudicate a separate contract, state-law remedy or independently accrued right, and does not authorize termination of relief that satisfies (b)(3).

### IV. The presented due-process claim does not bar termination

**Holding and operative rule:** Beneficiaries of this mutable prospective decree have no vested entitlement to perpetual federal enforcement of its unchanged terms despite valid later law. On the presented legal challenge, the judicial motion process, opportunity to oppose termination and appellate review satisfy due process; no additional evidentiary remand follows from an unestablished possibility that a statutory preservation condition was overlooked.

**Controlling explanation:** The asserted protected interest must be identified at its actual scope. The parties obtained an enforceable decree, but its continuing operation remained subject to supervision, lawful modification and the subsequently enacted termination rule. That does not promise unchanging prospective relief. The prisoners could contest the statutory predicates and constitutional authority through the judicial proceedings now reviewed. The Court presumes no unrecorded hearing and decides no actual denial of an opportunity to present a preserved factual claim. Lewis's protection of meaningful access and of relief justified by the original current-or-ongoing safeguard remains operative. A demonstrated failure to apply that safeguard would be a different case; mere invocation of state sovereignty would not excuse it. Separately accrued or contractual rights, general equal-protection questions, other PLRA provisions and the legality of any particular present prison condition remain outside this decision.

## Precedent Treatment

- Lewis supplies the complete original PLRA conditions and the rule that substantive findings need no ritual wording. Its reserved Article III question is resolved here only for this conditional prospective-termination operation; its meaningful-access protections remain.
- Rufo remains controlling for equitable modification based on significant changed circumstances and suitable tailoring. The PLRA provides an independent route, without an extra Rufo threshold.
- Robertson supplies the distinction between changed law and compelled adjudication under unchanged law; actual statutory predicates remain for courts.
- Wheeling Bridge supports the distinction between continuing prospective relief and completed adjudication.
- Morgan Stanley retains its bounded reinstatement rule for specified completed private limitations dismissals; that separate procedure does not supply this prospective-termination authority.
- Plaut retains its application of Morgan Stanley, without authorizing unrestricted reopening or establishing immunity of this prospective decree.
- Joseph A. is distinguished because it concerned proof of an agreed performance condition without an established PLRA prison-conditions predicate.
- Landgraf's distinction between prospective operation and new consequences of completed acts remains; §802 expressly reaches existing prospective relief.

## Law After Decision

Original §3626(b)(2), with original (b)(3), validly governs this ongoing prison-conditions decree. A court must determine the actual findings and preserve necessary, narrowly drawn, no-broader-than-necessary and least-intrusive relief for a current or ongoing federal violation; neither a consent decree's age nor missing ritual language suffices. The decree does not create a perpetual right to unchanged federal enforcement. This decision does not reach automatic stays, preliminary-order expiration, prisoner-release orders, accrued damages, separate state or contractual rights, or the constitutionality of all PLRA provisions. No precedent is overruled.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The Fourth Circuit's judgment sustaining termination remains in force at its prospective federal-enforcement scope. No new compliance finding or evidentiary remand is ordered. Independently established federal rights and any separately available state or contractual remedies are not extinguished by this judgment.

# Princeton University Press v. Michigan Document Services; Sixth Circuit No. 94-1778; 99 F.3d 1381 (en banc); no separate Supreme Court docket assigned.

Authority: Princeton_University_Press_v_Michigan_Document_Services_merits_1997-06-23.md.

## Event

Princeton University Press v. Michigan Document Services; Sixth Circuit No. 94-1778, 99 F.3d 1381 (en banc). Heard on the merits and decided June 23, 1997. On review of the en banc November 8, 1996 judgment affirming infringement, rejecting willfulness, vacating damages and remanding damages and injunctive implementation. Michigan Document Services, operated by James Smith, sold the coursepacks. The questions concern fair use of six scholarly excerpts sold in coursepacks, commercial and educational purpose, harm to the actual excerpt-permissions market, and willfulness when copying rests on a good-faith fair-use belief. The original $5,000-per-work damages award was vacated below.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate in the merits hearing and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the rejection of fair use and enhanced willfulness and preserves the remand for damages and a separately entered, specific injunction.

| Prospective injunction | Remand direction affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | None | Separate, sufficiently specific injunction must preserve lawful fair use |

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Holdings

**Authority for all holdings:** Scalia's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt each rule, application and remedial qualification below.

### I. These six coursepack reproductions do not establish fair use

**Holding and operative rule:** A commercial supplier's reproduction and sale of these substantial, unaltered excerpts for assigned reading is not fair use under §107 when all four factors are considered together: genuine educational purpose and published scholarly content do not outweigh the actual quantity and role of the copying and its substitution in the demonstrated excerpt-permissions market. Neither commerciality, lack of transformation, educational purpose, a fixed copying percentage nor a demand for license fees alone decides fair use.

**Controlling explanation:** Campbell requires one integrated inquiry and preserves fair use as an affirmative defense. On purpose and character, the professors' pedagogical selection and students' study matter, but the shop sells the same expression to perform the originals' assigned-reading function. The supplier's reproduction is not identical to a student's personal study. Transformation is not indispensable; its absence here identifies the unchanged substitutive function rather than an automatic disqualification. On nature, published factual scholarship favors educational use relative to unpublished creative expression, but facts and their protected expression remain different. On amount, the six passages range from 17 to 95 pages and 5% to 30% of the works. Their quantity and qualitative role must be evaluated against the asserted teaching purpose; no percentage alone establishes liability. Taken as assigned-reading substitutes, these substantial passages weigh against the defense. On market effect, the record identifies an existing, ordinarily exploitable coursepack-permissions market for the same excerpts. Comparable unlicensed substitution would impair that market; the Court does not assume that students would otherwise buy every entire book. The shop's strongest objection is circularity: any proprietor could demand a fee and call its absence market harm. Campbell forecloses that reasoning for protected criticism and otherwise fair use. Here the independent substitute function and demonstrated licensing practice establish the connection, rather than the bare demand. Sony's home-use protection and Harper & Row's attention to legitimate markets remain contextual rules. Nonstatutory classroom-copying guidelines supply no maximum allowance or independent infringement rule. Considering all four factors on this record, the educational advantages do not establish the defense for any of the six challenged reproductions. No general rule decides individual student, nonprofit, reserve-room or digital copying.

### II. Intentional copying does not establish willful infringement by itself

**Holding and operative rule:** Enhanced statutory damages under §504(c)(2) require knowing or reckless infringement, not simply intentional copying that ultimately fails a fair-use defense. On this record, the publishers' noncontest of Smith's actual good faith and the reasonably arguable fair-use position defeat the enhanced willfulness determination; ordinary statutory damages remain for determination without that erroneous premise.

**Controlling explanation:** Liability and culpability answer different questions. Smith deliberately copied and sold the excerpts, but intentional acts do not themselves establish knowledge or reckless disregard of infringement where a genuine, legally tenable defense is asserted. The publishers contested the reasonableness of his view rather than his actual good faith. The substantial dispute over the legal treatment of educational copying makes enhancement unwarranted here even though the Court rejects the defense. Campbell's integrated method does not collapse a losing defense into willfulness. Conversely, merely announcing sincerity cannot insulate deliberate disregard or objectively reckless infringement. Rejection of enhancement also does not establish the separate statutory conditions for innocent-infringer treatment. The en banc court vacated the original $5,000-per-work award; the Court preserves its remand rather than affirming a damages amount no longer in force. The lower court must determine ordinary statutory damages and any properly supported distinct innocent-infringer question without assuming either enhanced or reduced damages from the fair-use result alone.

### III. The injunction must identify infringement without extinguishing lawful fair use

**Holding and operative rule:** On remand a separately entered, sufficiently specific injunction must describe the prohibited infringing conduct and leave lawful future fair use available. The decision neither immunizes later coursepacks nor categorically forbids coverage of later copyrighted works; it supplies no replacement injunction language.

**Controlling explanation:** The present infringement judgment supports prospective relief against infringement, but the scope of relief must remain distinguishable from the scope of lawful copying. A command read to prohibit every future use of protected material would exceed the liability rule, because §107 remains applicable to future uses on their actual facts. The en banc court's separate-entry and specificity directions must therefore be implemented along with the damages remand. The question whether a sufficiently specific injunction may cover later works is distinct from whether it can bar fair use; the Court adopts no categorical restriction on the former. The Court decides no future transaction or materially different copying arrangement.

## Precedent Treatment

- Campbell supplies integrated consideration of all four §107 factors, the affirmative-defense burden, the absence of an indispensable transformation condition, and the distinction between actual substitution and a veto over criticism. The same method applies to this different assigned-reading use.
- Sony retains its home-time-shifting result and contextual inquiry; neither its commerciality language nor students' end use automatically decides the copy shop's reproduction.
- Harper & Row preserves attention to protected expression, qualitative substantiality and legitimate markets, without creating a categorical commercial-use rule.
- ProCD preserves the difference between an actual personal promise and copyright rights against the public. No promise by this shop is established, and that decision's fair-use reservation is not treated as permission or prohibition here.

## Law After Decision

These six commercial assigned-reading reproductions infringe after the full four-factor analysis. Established substitute licensing may demonstrate cognizable harm, but a demanded fee cannot establish its own legal entitlement. Education remains relevant; profit, percentages and lack of transformation remain nondispositive. Willfulness requires its separate knowing-or-reckless showing, and ordinary statutory damages and a fair-use-respecting injunction remain for implementation. No precedent is overruled and no universal coursepack rule is announced.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The lower courts must determine statutory damages without the rejected enhancement premise and enter a separate, sufficiently specific injunction preserving lawful fair use. The Court fixes no amount or replacement language and adjudicates no future copying transaction.

## Source Notes

The en banc opinion, 99 F.3d 1381, supplies the excerpt ranges, existing permissions market, uncontested actual good faith and operative damages/injunction mandate. Sections 107 and 504(c)(2) supply the distinct fair-use and enhanced-damages inquiries. The review concerns the stated fair-use and willfulness questions and the operative damages and injunction mandate.

# Richardson v. McKnight; No. 96-318.

Authority: Richardson_v_McKnight_merits_1997-06-23.md.

## Event

Richardson v. McKnight, No. 96-318. Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 23, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which affirmed denial of qualified immunity on June 7, 1996. The question is whether guards employed by a private, for-profit prison contractor may invoke the qualified immunity available to comparable public officers. McKnight alleges injury from excessively tight restraints. The appeal presents legal eligibility; state action is assumed for this appeal and the alleged constitutional violation remains undecided.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate in the merits hearing and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the denial of qualified immunity and remands for further proceedings consistent with the decision.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

Denial of immunity permits ordinary further proceedings. It establishes no liability, damages or automatic trial entitlement.

## Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Affirms and remands | Entire eligibility rule, limits and remedy |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas | Would reverse categorical ineligibility and remand | Individual custodial-function eligibility; ultimate immunity reserved |

## Holdings

**Authority for both holdings:** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six Justices directly adopt the eligibility rule, its limits and the bounded affirmance/remand.

### I. These commercial prison employees are not eligible for Harlow immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Guards employed full time by a private, for-profit firm that independently undertakes substantial continuing prison management under limited direct governmental supervision and potential market competition are not entitled to Harlow qualified immunity merely because they exercise delegated governmental custody. Neither the historical showing nor the functional justification establishes that immunity in this institutional setting; brief private assistance, adjunct service, close official supervision and integration into government remain open.

**Controlling explanation:** Wyatt separates attribution of conduct to the State from eligibility for an immunity. Performing a public task therefore begins rather than ends the inquiry. No firmly established general historical entitlement to the modern immunity from suit is demonstrated for these employees; the Court need not declare every historical private jailer liable or equate every prior defense with Harlow immunity. The functional setting also differs from public service. Potential competition, contractual accountability, insurance and private employment arrangements supply mechanisms for managing performance, staffing and liability that bear on the asserted need for immunity. Those considerations do not prove perfect competition, actual coverage of these guards or complete freedom from litigation costs. The guards' strongest argument is that an individual making a coercive custodial decision may hesitate under threat of damages regardless of the employer's identity. The Court recognizes the shared task but concludes that its independent commercial organization materially changes the claimed necessity for the additional immunity from suit. Wyatt's debt-collection setting is not identical; its demand for a separate historical and functional justification applies, while the actual operational distinctions supply the present answer. Fletcher likewise requires justification for the particular function rather than immunity by title. Harlow remains applicable to eligible public officials; the Court neither abolishes that defense nor adopts a replacement notice or conduct test. Eligibility must exist before the content of qualified immunity can be applied. This holding concerns these employees and this setting, not every private person whose act may be attributed to government.

### II. Denial of immunity does not establish a constitutional violation or personal liability

**Holding and operative rule:** The Sixth Circuit's denial of qualified immunity is affirmed, and the case is remanded for ordinary further proceedings on any properly unresolved state-action issue, the constitutional merits, causation and damages. The availability, elements and burdens of a distinct private good-faith defense remain undecided; no corporate-immunity or corporate-liability determination follows.

**Controlling explanation:** The appeal presents a separable legal immunity question on the assumed facts. Behrens and Johnson preserve that legal inquiry without allowing appellate selection of disputed facts. McKnight's allegation that the restraints were excessively tight is not a finding that the Constitution was violated. State action is assumed only for this appeal, not conclusively established for every further purpose. Wyatt distinguishes immunity from suit from a potential defense on the merits and leaves the latter to its own source and requirements. Fletcher and Crawford-El preserve the need to establish the actual constitutional elements and independent defenses after a ruling removes one immunity obstacle. The Court therefore neither orders an automatic trial nor awards damages, decides insurance or indemnification, or assigns a new burden for an unadjudicated good-faith defense. The claims may continue under the ordinary rules consistent with the absence of Harlow immunity for these guards.

## Precedent Treatment

- Wyatt supplies the separation of state attribution, Harlow immunity and a possible independent good-faith defense; its historical/functional inquiry is applied to this different commercial custodial setting.
- Harlow retains its protection for eligible officials; it is not extended to these employees and no replacement immunity test is adopted.
- Fletcher supplies function-specific justification and separation of immunity from ultimate liability; its qualified-immunity reservation for an eligible prosecutor does not establish universal private-contractor eligibility.
- Behrens preserves legal collateral-order review on proper factual assumptions, without deciding factual truth or merits sufficiency.
- Johnson v. Jones preserves the boundary against factual-sufficiency appeals; this decision resolves a legal eligibility issue.
- Elder preserves independent consideration of relevant law at the proper conduct-date scope, without supplying eligibility or retrospective notice.
- Crawford-El preserves ordinary proof of actual constitutional elements and independent defenses; immunity concerns create no additional evidentiary burden here.

## Law After Decision

These full-time commercial prison employees lack Harlow immunity in the independent operating setting presented. Their public task alone does not establish immunity, and loss of that defense alone establishes no constitutional wrong. Different private-assistance or close-supervision arrangements, state-action predicates, a distinct private good-faith defense and corporate questions remain unresolved. No precedent is overruled, and the qualified-immunity framework for eligible officials remains unchanged.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents. The guards perform delegated coercive custody, and an individual making that public decision may hesitate under threat of personal liability regardless of the employer's corporate form. Wyatt's self-interested private use of seizure machinery is different. Competition may discipline the employer without protecting the guard's discretionary judgment; profit and assumed insurance coverage therefore do not justify categorical ineligibility. The dissent would recognize eligibility and remand for the ordinary particularized immunity inquiry. It would not award immunity, approve excessive force, extend protection to every private state actor or decide the corporation's position.

## Procedure After Action

The action returns for ordinary consideration of any properly unresolved state-action question, the constitutional merits, causation, damages and independently available defenses. The Court reserves whether a private good-faith defense exists and, if it does, its elements and burdens. No damages award, mandatory trial or corporate disposition is ordered.
