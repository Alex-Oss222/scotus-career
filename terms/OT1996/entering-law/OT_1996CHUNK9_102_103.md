## Standards and Tests selection: ERISA plan commands and indirect economic effects

### ERISA plan commands and indirect economic effects

**Governing question:** When state benefit mandates or indirect price effects relate to covered plans under §514(a).

**Current rule:** ERISA §514(a) preempts a replacement-coverage obligation insofar as it uses a covered plan’s benefit content as the mandatory legal measure, even if replacement is separately administered through exempt workers’ compensation arrangements. Requirements measured solely by exempt governmental or church plans remain outside that holding. By contrast, the reviewed commercial-insurer hospital differentials and conditional HMO assessment are not preempted merely because price or participation effects influence plan choices: the measures neither use ERISA status or plan benefits as their legal benchmark nor command benefits, eligibility, uniform administration or an alternative enforcement remedy. Ordinary indirect economic influence is not itself such a command. The replacement-coverage obligation’s 52-week cap supplies no federal safe harbor.

**Authority by component:**

- Covered-plan content as a mandatory legal measure: District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade — Covered-plan content as a mandatory legal measure, 1992-12-14. Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; eight Justices adopt the same operative rule.

- The reviewed 13% hospital differential is not preempted: The reviewed 13% hospital differential is not preempted, 1995-04-26. Justice Souter’s opinion of the Court, joined on this proposition by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes. The direct self-funded application is outside this merits holding.
- The additional commercial-insurer 11% charge is not preempted: The additional commercial-insurer 11% charge is not preempted, 1995-04-26. Souter’s Court opinion and the same nine named Justices directly adopt this distinct application. It is not a holding that the additional 11% applies to every payor or that the reproduced period fixes liability under a later extension.
- The conditional HMO assessment is not preempted: The conditional HMO assessment is not preempted, 1995-04-26. Souter’s Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine direct votes for this separately analyzed assessment. The decision supplies no insurance-saving holding for the distinct 9% measure.

**Present operation effective:** Travelers, April 26, 1995, clarifies the previously reserved indirect-economic-effects boundary; Board of Trade, December 14, 1992, continues to govern covered-plan benchmarks.

**Holdings navigation:** District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, 506 U.S. 125 (1992); The reviewed 13% hospital differential is not preempted; The additional commercial-insurer 11% charge is not preempted; The conditional HMO assessment is not preempted.

**Exceptions and limits:** No rate or combined-charge safe harbor is created. The possibility of effects sufficiently acute to operate as a substantive coverage command or effectively restrict insurer choice remains open. This is a rule about the ERISA connection, not a new hospital-payment code or permission to disregard a measure’s enacted conditions.

**Open question:** Direct self-funded application, insurance saving and deemer questions remain unresolved. The insurer/HMO rulings do not decide them. Laws specifically referring to covered plans, mandating benefits, interfering with uniform administration or supplying alternative enforcement remain outside the nonpreemption holding.

## Script-copied holding from state/holdings/labor-and-employment.md

#### District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, 506 U.S. 125 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-1326.

**Decided:** December 14, 1992.

##### Covered-plan content as a mandatory legal measure

**Holding:** ERISA §514(a) preempts this continuation obligation insofar as it incorporates an ERISA-covered health plan’s benefit content as the legal measure of mandatory replacement coverage, even when an employer may administer the replacement benefits separately through an exempt workers’ compensation arrangement. No §514(b) savings claim is decided, and this ruling does not preempt requirements measured solely by an ERISA-exempt governmental or church plan.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; eight Justices adopt the same operative rule.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85 — applied; its separate-unit permission concerns independently fixed exempt benefits and does not eliminate operative reference to a different covered plan.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825 — applied; specific legal reference is distinguished from ordinary general garnishment.
- Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133 — applied; dependence on a covered plan can trigger preemption despite the nominal subject of the state rule.
- Alessi v. Raybestos-Manhattan, Inc., 451 U.S. 504 — applied; regulation through workers’ compensation does not avoid ERISA’s covered-plan restriction.
- Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 — distinguished; a one-time payment without ongoing administration differs from this health plan.
- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724 — preserved; its insurance-saving holding supplies no additional §4(b) escape from a covered-plan reference.
- Morales v. Trans World Airlines, June 1, 1992 — consistent similar-language analogy only; its aviation holding does not replace ERISA’s own coverage and saving provisions.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The obligation's 52-week cap is not a federal safe harbor. A separately maintained workers' compensation, unemployment-compensation or disability-insurance arrangement may remain exempt under §4(b)(3), but that does not exempt the different covered plan used as the mandatory benchmark. Exempt-only governmental or church-plan measures, §514(b) savings claims and ordinary indirect economic effects are outside the holding.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm preemption in the covered-plan-dependent application; the continuation mandate cannot be enforced to that extent.

#### New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co. / Pataki v. Travelers Insurance Co. / Hospital Association of New York State v. Travelers Insurance Co.

**Docket or dockets:** Nos. 93-1408; 93-1414; 93-1415.
**Decided:** April 26, 1995.

For the other questions in this decision, see Federal Courts — Component-specific vacatur and independent relief (The reserved direct-plan claim remains live after limited vacatur).

##### The reviewed 13% hospital differential is not preempted

**Holding:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the reviewed 13% hospital differential merely because its price effect may influence a covered plan’s choice of insurer or coverage arrangement. In these commercial-insurance applications the measure neither uses ERISA status or covered-plan benefits as its legal benchmark nor commands plan benefits, eligibility, uniform administration or an alternative enforcement remedy; the Court reserves economic effects sufficiently acute to operate as a substantive coverage command or effectively restrict insurer choice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85: applies the connection-or-reference inquiry and its remote-effects boundary without making every cost consequence preemptive.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825: applies the distinction between singling out ERISA plans and ordinary legal effects, including costs, without making general applicability an automatic defense.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguishes its mandatory covered-plan benefit benchmark, which these reviewed payment categories do not contain; that holding remains intact.
- FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52: distinguishes its alteration of plan subrogation and benefit calculation; its separate saving/deemer architecture remains undisturbed.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 2807-c(1) excludes Medicare payments and permits alternative reimbursement methodologies authorized under subdivision four, paragraphs (e)–(i). Paragraph (a) covers state governmental agencies, article 43 insurance corporations’ expense-incurred policies, the described payments by those corporations for foreign-corporation subscribers under subdivision 12(d), and article 44 HMO subscribers. The diagnosis-related-group case payment includes reimbursable operating costs, capital expenses, bad-debt and charity-care allowances, the primary-care allowance, the allowance for financially distressed hospitals, the rate-year trend factor and the subdivision four (a)–(d) case-payment adjustments. Paragraph (a-1) gives local-government payments for local correctional inmates the state-agency rates.

Paragraph (b) covers expense-incurred payments under workers’ compensation, volunteer firefighters’, volunteer ambulance workers’ and no-fault laws; self-insured funds paying hospitals directly, except enrollees covered by a rate-method agreement under paragraph (a) of subdivision two; licensed commercial accident-and-health insurers whose policies cover inpatient services on that basis; and out-of-plan benefits authorized under §4406, except those offered by a not-for-profit corporation or an organization qualifying under Internal Revenue Code §501(c). The payment equals the article 43 case-payment basis, adjusted for uncovered services, increased by 13%, and adjusted under subdivision 11(i) where applicable. A hospital may not refuse a qualifying direct-payment arrangement without due cause. Paragraphs (a) and (b) payors may not choose the lower of charges and the case payment or apportion the case payment by excluding a preexisting or acquired condition that must be treated along with the reason for admission. These are statutory payment rules; they are not findings that an ERISA plan changed its benefits.

Paragraph (c) uses hospital charges for patients without an authorized case-payment arrangement under paragraphs (a) or (b), or coverage under subdivision two (a). The subdivision four (d) short-stay definition applies, with that paragraph’s developed payment increased by 13%. For a charge-paying patient the ceiling is 120% of the paragraph (b) diagnosis-related-group case payment, without the subdivision 11(i) adjustment. A hospital must apply its charge schedule uniformly. This is not another application of the full 24% differential.

##### The additional commercial-insurer 11% charge is not preempted

**Holding:** The reviewed additional 11% commercial-insurer charge likewise is not preempted under §514(a): its revenue destination and additional price differential do not establish a required plan benefit, insurer choice or administrative arrangement on this record. This application adopts neither a numerical safe harbor for combined charges nor immunity for financial pressure that actually operates as a substantive coverage command or effectively restricts insurer choice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Shaw: applies its relation boundary to the additional charge’s actual operation, rather than treating legislative purpose or percentage alone as conclusive.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between ordinary added economic costs and the relation ERISA preempts; it supplies no quantitative exemption.
- Board of Trade: preserves its covered-plan-content benchmark and distinguishes this additional payor charge, whose amount does not use plan benefits as the legal measure.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The reproduced subdivision 11(i) applies the additional 11%, totaling 24% with the existing 13%, to the identified commercial-insurer expense-incurred discharges from April 1, 1992 through March 31, 1993. The appendix contains an ellipsis after that provision’s total-24% statement. Subdivision 14-e creates the statewide supplementary pool: hospitals submit the additional component, and the commissioner deposits it and investment income into the general fund. The complete extension text and resulting current rate liability are not decided here.

##### The conditional HMO assessment is not preempted

**Holding:** The reviewed HMO assessment of up to 9%, subject to its actual Medicaid participation, enrollment, exemption, reduction and payment conditions, is not preempted by §514(a) on this record. It affects HMO prices and participation choices without a demonstrated command to an ERISA plan’s benefit structure, eligibility, insurer choice or uniform administration; its conditions receive no categorical immunity from preemption if a different application establishes such a connection or effective compulsion.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Shaw: applies connection-or-reference analysis separately to the HMO measure and preserves the possibility that more acute indirect effects could suffice.
- Mackey: applies the distinction between actual administrative commands and economic consequences without dismissing duties merely because imposed through generally applicable law.
- FMC: distinguishes compulsory changes to plan reimbursement rights from the HMO participation and payment conditions shown here, retaining the insured/self-funded distinction where saving and deemer issues must be reached.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No direct self-funded merits ruling, new insurance-saving test, numerical safe harbor, uniform-cost guarantee, FEHBA ruling, stop-loss-letter ruling, Tax Injunction Act holding, or amended-law rate command is adopted. These are record-specific applications of the relation boundary, not categorical immunity for health regulation, price regulation, generally applicable statutes or indirect burdens.

Subdivision 2-a(a) addresses article 44 and article 43 HMO inpatient discharges on or after July 1, 1992. Medicaid-eligible subscribers and participants in the 1988 chapter 703 regional pilot projects are excluded. The base is the subdivision one case payment, subdivision four per-diem rate, or negotiated and approved subdivision two (b) rate, whichever applies. The additional factor is 9%, subject to elimination or reduction. Notice was due June 1 for the 1992 rate period and December 1 for each following annual January rate period. An appeal by May 1 can obtain a July 1 adjustment.

Elimination under subdivision 2-a(b)(i) requires status as a Social Services Law §364-j managed-care provider in each social-services district in the service area and enrollment of at least 90% of the sum of the target numbers of Medicaid recipients not exempt from managed care. The reproduced determination dates are May 1 for the 1992 rate period, November 1 preceding the 1993 rate year, and May 1 when an appeal is taken for the six-month period beginning July 1. Reaching 25%, 50% or 75% of total target enrollment gives the corresponding 25%, 50% or 75% reduction, rather than elimination.

An HMO may apply to the social-services commissioner for a district participation exemption by May 1 for the 1992 period or October 1 preceding the 1993 rate year, based on a good-faith effort to obtain a managed-care contract or other commissioner-established criteria. The following nonexclusive good-faith routes apply only to the reproduced 1992 and 1993 rate years. First, the HMO submitted an implementation plan and would have begun enrollment but for the district’s failure or refusal to contract. Second, it submitted letters of intent to all districts, contracted with half by July 1, 1992 and two-thirds by January 1, 1993, and could have contracted with the rest but for Department-agreed implementation delay. Third, the same letters and agreed-delay conditions apply where contracts cover districts containing half and then two-thirds of the service area’s nonexempt Medicaid population on those dates. Fourth, it meets the total enrollment target for its otherwise nonexempt districts despite incomplete contracts. For the fourth route, 1992–1993 fee-for-service enrollment is calculated from annual Medicaid visits to a licensed diagnostic and treatment center operated by the HMO or a distinct licensed nonprofit providing or arranging a majority of its services, divided by commercial subscribers’ annual average visits to those facilities. The commissioner also considers implementation of a plan to capitate a substantial percentage of that enrollment under an approved viable schedule. This fourth route permits only a 75% reduction, not elimination.

An exempt district is removed from the service area for the calculation. An HMO with noncontiguous regions may apply across its whole service area or separately on an aggregate basis for each region. The district target equals the ratio of its commercial subscribers to all HMO commercial subscribers in that district, excluding Medicaid, Medicare and regional-pilot subscribers from both sides, multiplied by the district enrollment objective. That objective is the greatest applicable measure: the approved nonexempt Medicaid enrollment goal as of July 1 of the preceding year; 5% of the district’s nonexempt Medicaid population before completion of the first full year of its approved managed-care plan; or actual nonexempt enrollment on July 1 of the preceding year, except May 1, 1992 for the first period. The actual-enrollment measure excludes providers that are neither HMOs nor entities authorized under §4403-a. The most recent subscriber statistics govern the ratio. Medicaid enrollees under an affiliation contract with a prepaid health-services plan may count when the commissioner approves the contract in consultation with the social-services commissioner.

Payment and administration: HMOs remit the adjusted assessment directly to a statewide pool for the preceding month’s discharges; pool funds and investment income go to the general fund. Estimated payment is due within fifteen days after month-end unless the actual payment has been made within that period. Collection stops when the commissioner, consulting the budget director, determines that $31 million has been or will be collected for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1993. Excess collections for that year are refunded in proportion to each HMO’s share of total payments. Arrearages bear subdivision twenty interest and penalties on the basis used for the bad-debt and charity-care pools. The commissioner may contract with the subdivision sixteen (c) pool administrator or, if unavailable, another designated administrator. If a contract is made, annual receipt-and-distribution audits are required; approved reasonable costs may be paid from the pool, with annual personnel services limited to $200,000. The $31 million cap is not declared permanent.

Subdivision 2-a(d) extends the adjusted factor to point-of-service inpatient business when an article 43 corporation offering those benefits was HMO-certified on April 1, 1992 and later transfers to its point-of-service product reduce HMO enrollment by more than 20%. The pre-April-1 subscriber ratio then controls the target. Satisfactory evidence that the transfers occurred for reasons beyond the corporation’s control prevents that provision’s application. The same pool and payment procedure apply. Subdivision 2-a(e) requires the information needed to administer the scheme, including county enrollment data by May 1 each year, identifying Medicaid, Medicare and regional-pilot subscribers separately and reflecting enrollment no earlier than the previous year.

ERISA §514(a), subject to subsection (b), supersedes state laws insofar as they relate to a §4(a) plan not exempt under §4(b); its effective date is January 1, 1975, and §514(b)(1) excludes earlier causes of action, acts and omissions. Section 514(b)(2)(A), subject to (B), preserves state insurance, banking and securities regulation. Subparagraph (B) prevents a covered nonexempt plan or its trust from being deemed an insurer, bank, trust company or investment company, or engaged in insurance or banking, for the stated state-regulatory purposes; its express exception is a plan established primarily to provide death benefits. A separate exempt workers’ compensation, unemployment or disability plan does not exempt a different covered health plan. Relation, saving and deeming are distinct questions. The direct self-funded application remains for remand; the separate insurance-saving theory for the up-to-9% assessment is not established as preserved. No new ruling on the Tax Injunction Act, laches, FEHBA, or the stop-loss letter is made.

**Operative remedy or transition:** In all three dockets, reverse the decided commercial-insurance and HMO ERISA rulings and dissolve the associated ERISA relief; vacate reviewed direct-self-funded adjudication and ERISA surcharge protection insofar as they reach the reserved application, and remand that live claim. This removes no independently surviving FEHBA or Actuarial Letter protection and decides no direct-plan merits or right to interim relief. No new payment, escrow, reimbursement or accrued-liability command is entered.

## Script-copied current-term public law, positions, or procedure

### 1997-02-18 - California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Construction, N.A., Inc.; No. 95-789; OT1996, chunk 3, matter 32.

Authority: [Record](../records/California_Division_of_Labor_Standards_Enforcement_v_Dillingham_merits_1997-02-18.md).

#### Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, Parts I-III, is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Each proposition below has nine direct rationale joins; no narrower-opinion calculation is needed.

##### I. The wage criterion does not refer to ERISA plans

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the challenged public-works wage application as a reference to ERISA plans: the lower apprentice rate turns on state approval of an apprenticeship program, not on its ERISA status or the benefit content of a covered plan. The Court accepts this program's covered status for purposes of deciding preemption without holding that every apprenticeship or training arrangement is an ERISA plan.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers distinguishes state laws whose legal operation identifies covered plans or uses their benefits as a benchmark from laws operating independently of that status. Board of Trade supplies the contrasting case: its replacement-coverage obligation used a covered plan's benefit content as the mandatory measure. California's criterion concerns approval of apprenticeship programs as such, and programs subject to that criterion need not all be ERISA plans. Its operation therefore does not depend on the existence of a covered plan.

The contrary argument begins with this program's covered status and the fact that approval conditions affect its operation. But a law does not acquire an ERISA-specific reference merely because a covered plan encounters it. That would replace the statutory inquiry with the presence of an ERISA litigant. The particular program may be assumed covered without making that assumption the legal measure of the State's wage rule. The Court does not decide every requirement in California's apprenticeship system or the classification of other training arrangements.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers is applied for independently operating state cost rules. Board of Trade is distinguished because its mandatory covered-plan benchmark is absent; its holding remains intact.

##### II. The wage choice does not establish a preempted plan command

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, §514(a) does not preempt California's requirement that contractors pay prevailing journeyman wages when they do not use apprentices from an approved program. The approval-linked wage incentive neither dictates covered-plan benefits, funding, structure, administration or remedies nor is shown to have economic effects so acute that the retained wage choice becomes an effective plan command; a direct command or demonstrated coercive application remains outside this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers requires examination of a law's actual operation. Its hospital-payment holdings preserve ordinary economic influence while reserving effects that operate as substantive coverage commands or effectively restrict choice. A contractor here may retain an unapproved program and pay the prevailing journeyman wage. The State's incentive favors approved training, but no supported finding shows that the higher wage eliminates that alternative or requires a change in a covered plan's internal terms. Later approval of the program was not retroactive and does not erase the dispute over the earlier wages.

Dillingham's strongest contention is that the wage differential pressures programs to conform to substantive state training standards. Influence is real, but it does not by itself establish the compulsion Travelers requires. Treating every substantial cost consequence as preemption would discard that decision's distinction. Traditional state responsibility for wages and apprenticeship informs the statutory setting; it does not immunize every public-works measure or establish a percentage safe harbor. The Court applies the concrete command-and-effects inquiry, not a formula under which any verbal relationship to a covered plan suffices. It neither creates a competing ERISA enforcement remedy nor sustains regulation of every plan merely because the State acts through a contractor.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers's indirect-effects rule and acute-effects reservation are applied without change. Board of Trade remains controlling when plan benefits supply the mandatory legal benchmark, which this wage application does not use.

##### III. Reversal of the ERISA ground leaves independent issues for remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's ERISA-preemption judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with the absence of §514(a) preemption of this wage application. The mandate removes that federal obstacle to enforcement without directing final judgment on the independently unresolved NLRA theory, contingent §1983 damages, any properly remaining fee issue, nonpreemption defenses or the underlying wage calculation.

**Controlling explanation:** The District Court granted judgment to the state defendants on the ERISA and NLRA theories. The Ninth Circuit reversed on ERISA, rejected the Fitzgerald Act saving theory, and did not decide the independent NLRA issue or its contingent damages consequence. Removing the ERISA ground therefore does not supply a decision on those unreviewed questions. The separate rejection of the particular §1988 fee request based on an asserted ERISA right not sought to be enforced under §1983 is not automatically reversed, and the State's contractual-estoppel contention, declined below because it was first raised on appeal, is not revived.

The contrary remedial path would reinstate final judgment on every claim merely because the sole appellate preemption ground fails. That would decide more than the granted ERISA question and eliminate issues the Court of Appeals has not reached. Conversely, remand does not authorize a new challenge to the settled absence of §514(a) preemption on these facts. Because that provision does not reach this application, the Court need not decide §514(d) or whether the Fitzgerald Act would save an otherwise preempted rule. Nor does the State obtain blanket immunity merely from public ownership of the project.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers governs the ERISA ground at its actual scope. Neither Boston Harbor nor Chamber of Commerce v. Reich supplies an automatic public-contract immunity or decides the independent NLRA and damages issues left for remand.

#### Precedent Treatment

- New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co., April 26, 1995: applied to the concrete difference between ordinary economic influence and a direct or effective plan command. Its acute-effects reservation and unresolved direct-self-funded questions remain intact; no numerical threshold or general-applicability immunity is added.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguished because California's wage criterion does not use covered-plan benefit content as a mandatory measure. The covered-plan benchmark holding remains binding.
- Building & Construction Trades Council v. Associated Builders & Contractors (Boston Harbor), March 8, 1993: its protection of the lawful proprietary labor agreement supplies no blanket ERISA immunity for every public-works condition. The Court does not resolve the independent NLRA claim by applying a procurement label.
- Chamber of Commerce v. Reich, January 14, 1997: its particular limit on federal procurement authority does not decide this ERISA application or the separate NLRA claim and associated remedies. Its refusal to make a procurement label dispositive remains undisturbed.

#### Law After Decision

California may apply the reviewed wage consequence without the ERISA bar imposed below, subject to independently unresolved issues and ordinary enforcement requirements. State approval of apprenticeship programs as such is not a reference to ERISA status, and this record's wage incentive is not an actual or effective plan command. Direct plan regulation, an ERISA-specific legal benchmark, competing enforcement remedies and demonstrated acute compulsion remain outside the holding. No categorical immunity for wage laws, apprenticeship laws, public purchasing or indirect economic effects is adopted.

### 1997-05-12 — Inter-Modal Rail Employees Association v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co.; No. 96-491; OT1996, chunk 7, matter 81.

Authority: [Record](../records/Inter_Modal_Rail_Employees_v_Atchison_merits_1997-05-12.md).

#### Holdings

##### I. Section 510 includes prospective welfare-plan rights

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §510 protects against a covered discharge or other prohibited action undertaken for the purpose of interfering with attainment of a right a participant may become entitled to under an employee welfare benefit plan, even though the benefit is not subject to ERISA's statutory vesting requirements. Lawful authority to amend or terminate a welfare plan does not itself authorize interference through conduct forbidden by §510.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Ginsburg's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices. All nine directly adopt this statutory coverage rule and its limits, notwithstanding their different formal dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that welfare benefits are within the attainment clause's protection. ERISA defines employee benefit plans to include welfare and pension plans. Section 510 addresses future attainment, while Congress elsewhere used narrower pension and nonforfeitable-benefit terms. Section 1051(1) exempts welfare plans from the participation and vesting part; it does not create an additional exemption from §510. The separate exercise-of-right and prospective-attainment clauses must retain their distinct work.

Respondents' strongest objection is that protection of amendable benefits would vest them indirectly and obstruct ordinary cost-saving restructuring. But a lawful revision of plan terms differs from a covered employment action undertaken for the prohibited purpose. Curtiss-Wright preserves amendment authority while requiring its lawful exercise. Lockheed separates sponsor design from implementation and independent statutory commands. Those decisions do not authorize evasion of plan procedures through a prohibited discharge. Enforcing §510's own conduct and purpose conditions answers the objection without erasing an entire defined category of plans.

The employees allege that the transfer of cargo-handling work and their discharge were designed to avoid contributions and minimize benefit payments. Those allegations must not be treated as findings. The absence of statutory vesting cannot alone sustain dismissal of their welfare theory. This holding does not promise continued employment, freeze plan terms, establish intent from cost reduction or settle every meaning of attainment. In particular, the separate argument that employees already eligible for benefits have already attained the relevant right remains open. Actual eligibility, entitlement, action, purpose, causation and authorized relief require their own consideration.

##### II. The categorical dismissal is vacated for the remaining statutory inquiries

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's welfare-interference dismissal is vacated because its categorical nonvesting ground is erroneous, and the case is remanded for consideration of the properly remaining grounds. Plaintiffs must establish the applicable participant or beneficiary status, relevant plan right, prohibited action, interference purpose, causation and remedy authorized through §510's reference to §502; this decision establishes no violation or automatic recovery.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Ginsburg's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight direct joins support this vacatur and its scope. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and would reverse the welfare dismissal with the reinstatement direction stated in his separate opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The court of appeals dismissed the welfare theory because the benefits did not vest. Rejection of that ground does not decide every alternative argument the lower courts have not addressed. Whether a participant already eligible for the particular welfare benefit can invoke interference with attainment requires first consideration below. So do properly preserved questions concerning specific intent, eligibility, causation, mixed motives or business justification, proper defendants and relief. The Court adopts no new business-necessity test or presumption that a benefits-reducing transfer is unlawful.

Varity's function-specific fiduciary holdings and qualified individual-equity rule do not resolve this independent §510 theory. Mertens preserves the limits of the remedy actually authorized by §502; statutory coverage does not itself authorize compensatory damages. No employer or administrator status automatically establishes liability, and the circuit's nonemployer-conspiracy footnote is not adopted as a new holding of this Court.

The pension claim's reinstatement, Railroad Retirement Act exclusion, association-standing ruling, conditional request for individual class representatives, FELA and Rule 23 rulings, and fee-sanction remand are not independently reopened. The limitations contention declined below because it was first raised at appellate argument is not revived. These boundaries follow the granted welfare question; they do not affirm or reverse unrelated lower rulings by implication.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen:** Preserves lawful amendment authority and its actual procedural exercise; that power supplies no immunity for independently prohibited discharge.
- **Lockheed Corp. v. Spink:** Applies the distinction between sponsor design and independent statutory obligations without classifying every business decision as fiduciary administration.
- **Varity Corp. v. Howe:** Its fiduciary and qualified individual-equity holdings remain separate; neither those holdings nor the disagreement about them eliminates §510's express route.
- **Mertens v. Hewitt Associates:** Preserves the equitable-category limit where §502(a)(3) relief is sought; coverage creates no compensatory-damages authorization.
- **Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon:** Its pension-interference setting remains intact; it did not already decide the disputed welfare-plan coverage question.

#### Law After Decision

Section 510 now expressly reaches prospective welfare-plan attainment without a vesting prerequisite. The distinction between lawful changes to plan terms and prohibited conduct directed at participants governs; nonvesting benefits remain amendable. Purpose, actual protected rights, proper defendants, causation and authorized remedies retain independent force. The meaning of attainment for already eligible participants remains unresolved, and no general unfair-dismissal or permanent-benefits rule is created.

### 1997-05-12 — Inter-Modal Rail Employees Association v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co.; No. 96-491; OT1996, chunk 7, matter 81.

Authority: [Record](../records/Inter_Modal_Rail_Employees_v_Atchison_merits_1997-05-12.md).

Stone-Zsela's May 12, 1997 concurrence in Part I and dissent from the formal disposition proposes reversal with a direction to reinstate the welfare claims for the remaining statutory inquiries. The coverage rule he joins controls; his distinct reversal/reinstatement mandate does not. His complete material position and limits are reproduced in the Public Projection's Separate Writings block. No additional Associate writing is published.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the formal disposition.** He agrees that the enacted definition and future-attainment language include welfare plans, and that lawful amendment power does not authorize evasion through a prohibited discharge. Congress knew how to refer to pensions and nonforfeitable benefits and did not impose a vesting element in §510. He would reverse the welfare-claim dismissal and direct reinstatement of those claims for consideration of specific intent, eligibility, causation, any preserved mixed-motive or business-justification issue, proper defendants and remedy. He makes no finding of a violation. Whether these employees had already attained the particular benefits remains open, and neither nonvesting benefits nor a defendant's nonemployer or nonadministrator role is converted into automatic liability. His disposition does not restore every dismissed claim, guarantee recovery or create permanent benefits.
