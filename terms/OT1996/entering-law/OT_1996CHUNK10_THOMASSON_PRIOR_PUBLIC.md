### Dawson v. Delaware, No. 90-6704

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 12, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Delaware Supreme Court, 581 A.2d 1078](https://static.case.law/a2d/581/html/1078-01.html).

Question presented: Whether Delaware violated the First and Fourteenth Amendments by introducing the stipulated Aryan Brotherhood membership evidence at capital sentencing; no challenge to the convictions is decided.

Dawson and the murder victim were white. The parties stipulated that the Aryan Brotherhood began as a racist California prison gang and that a separate gang with that name existed in Delaware. The stipulation and tattoo evidence did not establish the Delaware organization’s violent conduct or aims, an organizational connection with the crime, or racial motivation for this murder. Dawson preserved his constitutional objection to the agreed evidentiary presentation. His evidence favoring a lesser sentence, or mitigation, included family kindness and participation in drug and alcohol programs; the State established three statutory aggravating circumstances supporting a harsher sentence. Delaware upheld the death sentence, treating the membership evidence as relevant to character and mitigation. Dawson seeks relief from that sentence; Delaware defends relevance and alternatively raises harmless error. The stipulation is not evidence of every fact about other prison gangs. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 159](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-159.pdf).

#### Judgment

The judgment sustaining the death sentence is vacated and the case remanded, 8–1. The convictions remain intact.

| Judgment sustaining the death sentence | Vacated and remanded | 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Thomas | Delaware must conduct further proceedings consistent with the constitutional relevance ruling; convictions remain intact. |
| Direction on harmless error | Availability and application left open | 7–2 | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Stone-Zsela, Thomas | Stone-Zsela would require Chapman review and a new sentencing proceeding unless the State proves harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. Thomas would affirm the sentence without reaching a remedial inquiry. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Protected association must bear on a legitimate sentencing issue

**Controlling proposition:** The State may not use protected association to aggravate a capital sentence when the evidence establishes only abstract beliefs and supplies no supported connection to the crime, future danger, another legitimate sentencing issue, or the mitigation actually offered. The stipulated association evidence here lacks that connection and its admission violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

**Authority:** Part A of White’s opinion; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter join White at this level of generality.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unpopular association does not become sentencing evidence merely because its beliefs are objectionable. NAACP and Bates protect association; Abel shows that those protections do not exclude evidence that actually establishes a witness’s bias. Barclay likewise involved demonstrated racial motivation for the crime. This stipulation supplies neither connection. Even assuming that the Delaware group shared racist beliefs, the State established no link between those beliefs and this murder, and no violent organizational objective attributable to Dawson. The evidence also does not contradict his actual mitigation concerning family kindness and participation in rehabilitation programs. Payne permits relevant rebuttal, not punishment for a belief labeled bad character. Zant’s distinction between legitimate aggravation and protected beliefs therefore controls the application. The Court announces no universal bar against evidence of association and no rule that a white victim always excludes racial motive. A materially different evidentiary record may establish relevance that this stipulation does not.

**Precedent treatment:**

- NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449 — applied: freedom of association protects unpopular affiliations.
- Bates v. Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516 — applied: protected association cannot itself justify adverse governmental treatment.
- United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 — distinguished on the facts: association established witness bias there; relevant association remains admissible.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939 — distinguished: demonstrated racial motivation connected the association to the crime.
- Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862 — applied: protected beliefs cannot themselves supply an aggravating circumstance.
- Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S. 808 — applied with its relevance limit: rebuttal of mitigation does not make unrelated belief probative.
- Schware v. Board of Bar Examiners, 353 U.S. 232 — applied by analogy: an affiliation without a rational connection to the relevant character inquiry is insufficient.

##### Scope of the remand

**Controlling proposition:** The state judgment sustaining this sentence cannot stand on the constitutional reasoning it adopted. On remand the state court may consider Delaware’s harmless-error contention; this Court decides neither whether harmless-error analysis is available for this violation nor whether the error was harmless.

**Authority:** Part B of White’s opinion, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remand’s reserved scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction of the constitutional error requires vacating the judgment that sustained the sentence, while leaving the convictions intact. The relevance ruling answers the question reviewed here. It does not establish how the challenged evidence affected this jury’s sentencing decision, and it does not decide every condition under which constitutional evidentiary error can be harmless. Delaware advanced harmlessness as an alternative ground, but the state court did not decide that contention under the constitutional rule now stated. Chapman supplies a demanding burden when its form of harmless-error review applies; citing that framework does not decide its availability for this particular infringement. The proper course is to permit consideration of the issue on remand without prejudging either branch. Neither the existence of other aggravators nor the Court’s finding of irrelevance supplies an automatic harmlessness finding. Conversely, the Court does not now command a new sentencing hearing regardless of further lawful review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 — preserved: its constitutional harmless-error burden is not relaxed; applicability and satisfaction in this case remain undecided.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective March 9, the constitutional relevance rule is applied to this stipulation; no categorical association-evidence ban is added. The remand’s seven-Justice reservation leaves both availability and application of harmless-error treatment open. Stone-Zsela’s mandatory Chapman/new-sentencing sequence is not the Court’s instruction. No earlier rule is displaced.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in the relevance ruling and vacatur but would require Delaware to prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt on the trial record, with new sentencing otherwise. Blackmun joins the Court and emphasizes that the permissibility of harmless-error treatment itself remains unresolved; this safeguard may protect against chilling association as well as an unreliable sentence. Thomas dissents: voluntary prison-gang membership can support a character and future-danger inference and rebut favorable character evidence. He would treat ordinary evidentiary fairness, rather than a categorical First Amendment insulation, as the inquiry. His inference is not a Court finding about this Delaware group.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of the sentence ends; the case returns to Delaware for further proceedings. No resentencing date, harmlessness finding, stay, or retained Supreme Court supervision is created. The convictions remain intact.

**End of entry: Dawson v. Delaware, Merits decision, March 9, 1992.**

### Heller v. Doe, No. 92-351

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-22; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Sixth Circuit, Doe v. Cowherd, 965 F.2d 109 (1992).

**Question:** Whether Kentucky’s different burdens of proof and family-participation rules for involuntary commitment of persons with intellectual disability and persons with mental illness violate equal protection, and whether family party status violates procedural due process.

**Material record:** Kentucky required proof of dangerousness, likely benefit from treatment, and a least restrictive placement predicate in the relevant commitment proceedings. For the category described in the then-operative statute as “mental retardation,” proof was clear and convincing and guardians or immediate family could participate as parties. For mental illness, proof was beyond a reasonable doubt and that party status was not supplied. The class challenged these differences. The medical and developmental distinctions offered as rationales concern typical onset, diagnostic history, prediction, and the information held by families; they are not individual findings that any class member is dangerous or incompetent.

**Relief sought:** Kentucky seeks reversal of the challenged federal rulings. The committed class seeks affirmance. Other district-court grounds outside the questions under review remain separate.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. Unequal proof burdens invalidated, 5–4; categorical invalidation of family-party participation reversed, 6–3.

| Family/guardian party status: equal protection and categorical due-process invalidation | Reverse categorical invalidation (6–3) | Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Party participation may continue with counsel, effective contest and independent adjudication. Blackmun, Stevens and Souter would invalidate on equal protection and do not reach the independent due-process question. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The lower overall proof protection lacks a rational relation to the disadvantage imposed

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky’s clear-and-convincing burden for involuntary commitment of adults with intellectual disability, compared with proof beyond a reasonable doubt for mental illness, violates equal protection on the preserved rational-basis challenge: the offered diagnostic, prediction and treatment differences do not rationally justify assigning this class the greater risk of mistaken confinement. Addington’s federal minimum remains unchanged; the remedy must address lawful equalization and severability rather than impose a national reasonable-doubt standard or order every class member released.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory distinction must relate to the particular procedural disadvantage. Cleburne permits relevant distinctions between conditions but does not allow a label to substitute for that relationship. A longer history may make diagnosis or some predictions easier. Commitment, however, also requires dangerousness, likely treatment benefit and a least restrictive placement; the interests at stake remain physical liberty and protection from erroneous confinement. Addington explains the allocation of that error risk and supplies a constitutional minimum, not immunity from an equality challenge when a State provides greater protection to another class. The asserted difference in the intrusiveness of treatment likewise does not adequately explain the lower overall safeguard against confinement. Beach does not demand formal findings or perfect empirical fit, and the Court imposes neither requirement. It rejects the proffered relationship, not legislative generalization as such. The appropriate limited remedy must respect severability and the State’s lawful choices, while every continuing confinement still needs an independent lawful basis.

**Precedent treatment:**

- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: rational relationship to the actual disadvantage required; no new scrutiny tier adopted.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S.418: the clear-and-convincing minimum survives; unequal safeguards remain separately reviewable.
- FCC v. Beach Communications, June 1, 1993: no formal-findings or empirical-certainty requirement imposed; its specific economic justification does not decide this classification.
- Foucha, May 18, 1992: current lawful confinement predicates preserved; noncontrolling equal-protection reasoning is not promoted to law.

##### Family-party participation has a distinct rational basis

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky may distinguish the two commitment proceedings by allowing guardians and immediate family members to participate as parties for adults with a lifelong developmental disability. Continuing family knowledge rationally supports that participation; the availability of testimony or a less extensive form of participation does not by itself defeat ordinary rational-basis review.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that gathering useful information is different from assigning a person less protection against erroneous confinement. A developmental condition and sustained family involvement plausibly give relatives and guardians knowledge of abilities, daily functioning and available care that assists the proceeding. Parham recognizes the value of family information without giving a relative authority to decide whether an adult should be confined. Cleburne requires a rational relationship, and that informational relationship supports the participation distinction here. Kentucky need not show that every family has useful knowledge or that no less extensive procedure could work. Nor does the existence of mental illness within some families eliminate the permissible general distinction. The Court does not presume that family members always share the adult’s interests. Their preferences or convenience establish none of the statutory grounds for confinement, and the independent adjudicator must evaluate their evidence under the lawful burden. This holding sustains party participation as a category, not every application of it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cleburne, 473 U.S.432: relevant differences may support a particular procedural distinction.
- Parham v. J.R., 442 U.S.584: family information may be useful; adult confinement is not delegated to relatives.
- Schweiker v. Wilson, 450 U.S.221: ordinary review does not require the least restrictive legislative alternative.

##### Party participation does not itself deprive the adult of fair adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** Allowing relatives or guardians to participate as parties does not categorically violate procedural due process where the adult has counsel and an effective opportunity to contest adverse evidence before an independent decisionmaker. Relatives may neither supply a substitute for the statutory commitment grounds nor control the decision or suppress the adult’s position.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged party status must be examined within the actual procedural safeguards. Mathews requires consideration of the private interest, the risk of error and value of safeguards, and the government’s interests. The liberty interest is substantial. Kentucky nevertheless provides counsel, professional examination, an opportunity to testify and present evidence, cross-examination and independent adjudication. Relevant family information can improve accuracy when tested through those procedures. The possibility of adverse family interests requires such testing; it does not establish that adding a party inherently makes an accurate decision impossible. Parham does not permit the State to call an adult’s involuntary placement voluntary merely because a relative requested it. The adjudicator must find the statutory grounds under the lawful burden, regardless of the family’s preference. Proceedings that defeat effective contest or independent decisionmaking remain challengeable. The Court reverses the categorical due-process invalidation without determining that every class member’s hearing was fair.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S.319: private interest, error risk and safeguards, and government interests applied separately from equality review.
- Parham, 442 U.S.584: informational participation distinguished from final decisional control.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, the proof distinction is invalid under rational-basis review, with severability and lawful equalization left for remand. The family distinction and party status survive the categorical challenges subject to independent adjudication and effective contest. Addington, the substantive confinement predicates and the unresolved heightened-scrutiny question remain distinct. Kentucky’s repealed voluntary-admission rule and other unreviewed grounds are not decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, would sustain the proof distinction. They regard easier diagnosis, a longer behavioral history supporting prediction, and generally less intrusive treatment as independently sufficient rational grounds. Addington’s minimum is met, and ordinary review permits imperfect generalizations. Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would also invalidate family-party status: useful information can be obtained through witnesses, while party status adds an adversary able to call and cross-examine witnesses and appeal a refusal to confine. They find no rational connection between that additional burden and the different condition and therefore do not reach due process. Blackmun separately preserves his view that disability classifications and substantial restraints on liberty warrant heightened review, while accepting that the proof distinction fails the ordinary standard used here.

These source texts support the docket, dates, review posture, record facts and cited authorities. Reporter citations identify the linked source texts; the Court’s disposition and opinion joins are those stated above. Allegations and unresolved facts retain their stated status.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of this merits event is complete. Determine severability and lawful state choices to equalize safeguards; no national reasonable-doubt rule or blanket release. Party participation may continue with counsel, effective contest and independent adjudication. Blackmun, Stevens and Souter would invalidate on equal protection and do not reach the independent due-process question. Ordinary mandate procedure applies; no special issuance date, later filing or retained Supreme Court proceeding is established.

### FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., No. 92-603

Merits decision, 1993-06-01

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-29; decided 1993-06-01. Review of the District of Columbia Circuit, 965 F.2d 1103 (1992).

Question: Whether the Cable Act’s exemption for facilities serving multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, without using a public right-of-way, survives the reviewed equal-protection challenge.

Satellite master antenna television operators challenged the distinction between systems serving commonly controlled buildings and systems serving separately owned buildings. After an earlier remand for an explanation, the court of appeals found the distinction unsupported and required extension of the exemption. The 1992 Cable Act renumbered the relevant definition from §522(6) to §522(7); that citation change did not enact the court of appeals’ constitutional conclusion. The reviewed exemption applies only to facilities serving subscribers in one or more multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, unless the facilities use a public right-of-way. Separate First Amendment scrutiny was not adjudicated below on the new statutory footing.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0 in the judgment.

|---|---|---|
| Continuing controversy despite the 1992 amendment | Reject mootness: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent | Congress did not resolve the live constitutional controversy merely by reenacting the definition without changing the ownership distinction. |
| Equal protection of the ownership-based exemption | Reverse: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent | The ownership distinction survives ordinary rational-basis review; further proceedings may address the separate questions not decided here. |

| Opinion of the Court, Part II-B | Thomas | Joined by White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (7 total). Independent first-mover/competitive-entry justification. Stone-Zsela does not join this alternative ground. | Reverse |
| Opinion of the Court, threshold portion | Thomas | Joined by White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (7 total). The 1992 amendment does not moot this controversy. Stone-Zsela agrees only with the threshold disposition. | Reject mootness |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | No other Justice joins (1 total). Private ownership use versus entry into a regulated market; does not join speculative size or monopoly rationales. | Reverse |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Reenactment does not moot the live constitutional controversy

**Controlling proposition:** Congress’s reenactment of the cable definition without changing the challenged distinction does not adopt a lower court’s constitutional invalidation or eliminate the live controversy defended by the FCC. The Court therefore reaches the reviewed equal-protection question.

**Authority:** Thomas’s threshold opinion; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the parties continue to dispute the validity of an operative statutory distinction. Congress’s failure to alter that distinction while revising other portions of the cable legislation does not turn the court of appeals’ judgment into an act of Congress. A legislative adoption inference sometimes used to understand statutory language does not establish that Congress enacted a judicial conclusion about constitutional validity. The FCC continues to defend the distinction, and reversal would alter the parties’ rights under the exemption. The amendment therefore leaves a concrete controversy rather than an advisory question. This conclusion decides only the proposed ground for mootness. It does not treat congressional silence as approval of every agency interpretation, decide the new legislation’s constitutionality, or resolve the separate speech objections. Those objections must be addressed through the questions and record actually presented for their consideration.

**Precedent treatment:**

- The lower court’s constitutional judgment is reviewed, not treated as legislation adopted by congressional silence; no earlier controlling decision is overruled.

##### Common-owner bargaining and regulatory costs sustain the economic line

**Controlling proposition:** For an economic classification involving neither a suspect class nor a fundamental right under the question reviewed, a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis suffices; the challenger must negate such a basis, and formal legislative findings are unnecessary. Congress could rationally distinguish these commonly owned, controlled or managed dwellings from separately owned properties because bargaining arrangements and the relationship between regulatory cost and consumer protection differ; Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid irrational or impermissibly motivated classifications.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Parts I and II-A; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress could make this ownership distinction without proving that it was the only satisfactory line. Fritz permits economic classifications supported by a conceivable legitimate relationship, and the Nordlinger decision confirms that imperfect fit does not itself defeat rationality. A common owner can bargain for service on behalf of the property and can choose among suppliers before committing the premises. Congress could also conclude that regulating small systems imposes costs disproportionate to the consumer protection obtained. Separate ownership changes those bargaining conditions and permits expansion without the same common control. The absence of a fixed subscriber cap and the existence of atypically large exempt facilities do not negate the general relationship. Neither legislative silence about an actual motive nor the absence of empirical findings shifts the challenger’s burden. Moreno and Cleburne still exclude a forbidden purpose or irrational prejudice; no such defect or settled contrary fact defeats this relationship here. Separate speech scrutiny remains undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Railroad Retirement Board v. Fritz, 449 U.S.166: conceivable legitimate grounds and imperfect economic fit applied.
- Sullivan v. Stroop, 496 U.S.478: ordinary review of social and economic classifications applied.
- Nordlinger v. Hahn, June 18, 1992: the rational-basis holding applied without converting its particular tax grounds into universal immunity from review.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S.528: illegitimate-purpose limit preserved.
- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: irrational-prejudice limit preserved.

##### Competitive entry independently supports the ownership distinction

**Controlling proposition:** Congress could independently distinguish separately owned buildings because an incumbent provider can add nearby buildings at a lower marginal cost than a new entrant can incur to install its own reception facilities, creating a plausible first-mover advantage. This conceivable competition concern is sufficient under ordinary rational-basis review without proof that every regulated system possesses monopoly power.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Part II-B; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the ownership line has a sufficient competition rationale independent of the regulatory-cost explanation. A provider already operating a reception facility may connect additional separately owned buildings more cheaply than a new competitor can construct the facilities necessary to serve them. Congress could regard that advantage as a reason to retain franchising oversight. In a commonly owned development, by contrast, a single owner can negotiate for the whole property before selecting a provider. Fritz permits Congress to address such differences through an administrable category even when particular systems do not display the predicted advantage. The argument that all systems avoid public rights-of-way identifies a common characteristic but does not eliminate this different competitive characteristic. The holding does not find actual monopoly power, decide an antitrust claim or require a regulator to prove market dominance in every application. Nor does it decide whether a different standard applies to a separately presented burden on protected speech.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fritz, 449 U.S.166: a second conceivable legitimate economic basis independently sustains the line.
- Nordlinger, June 18, 1992: multiple sufficient rational grounds and tolerance for imperfect fit applied within ordinary review.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: ordinary rational-basis review and Nordlinger governed economic classifications, while the cable ownership line was contested. After this decision: the line is sustained by eight Justices on bargaining/regulatory cost and seven on the independently sufficient competition rationale. Seven supply the threshold mootness rationale. No First Amendment holding or heightened-scrutiny resolution enters; no precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, concurring in the judgment, accepts a concrete distinction between an owner’s private use of property and reaching beyond that property to participate in a regulated market. He doubts that hypothesized system size or competitive advantage, detached from the actual statutory distinction, provides a satisfactory account. Fritz’s presumption of legitimate, impartial legislative action does not in his view make any imagined relationship sufficient regardless of context. His narrower explanation supports reversal but is noncontrolling.

#### Source Notes

[Official report and underlying record description, 508 U.S.307](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508307/usrep508307.pdf); [Judgment below, 965 F.2d 1103](https://static.case.law/f2d/965/html/1103-01.html) supplies the actual invalidation and prior-remand posture. The official report at 309–315 identifies the Cable Act renumbering, continuing controversy and reserved First Amendment question.

Record and statutory descriptions are supported by these sources. The public opinion summaries are paraphrases, not verbatim quotations. The 1992 renumbering did not change the challenged ownership distinction. Unrelated provisions of that legislation and separate speech claims are outside this holding. No special mandate date or subsequent lower-court outcome is reported.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment invalidating the ownership distinction is reversed and the case remanded. No exemption is judicially extended to all private-property systems. The Court leaves separate speech claims, any properly presented heightened-scrutiny issue and challenges to particular applications for appropriate proceedings; it does not decide those claims or authorize public-right-of-way use under the exemption.

Supreme Court review is complete. The District of Columbia Circuit receives the case for proceedings consistent with the sustained economic distinction and the reserved questions. No special mandate date is supplied.

### Wisconsin v. Mitchell, No. 92-515

Merits decision, 1993-06-11

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-21; decided 1993-06-11. Review of the Wisconsin Supreme Court, 169 Wis.2d153, 485 N.W.2d807 (1992), which held the penalty enhancement unconstitutional.

Question: Whether the First Amendment forbids enhancement of the penalty for an independently criminal battery because the defendant intentionally selected the victim on account of race, and whether evidentiary use of speech makes the provision substantially overbroad.

A jury convicted Mitchell of aggravated battery after he directed a group attack on a white youth. The jury also found intentional racial selection under the applicable Wisconsin statute. The ordinary two-year maximum became seven years, and Mitchell received four years. Section939.645 applies when a person commits a covered crime and intentionally selects the victim, or property because of the owner’s or occupant’s status, on account of race, religion, color, disability, sexual orientation, national origin or ancestry. It requires a special verdict on those predicates and does not apply where proof of such status is already required for conviction of the underlying crime. The felony maximum may increase by up to five years and the fine by up to $5,000; the separate misdemeanor tiers are not the sentence at issue. Amendments enacted in 1992 are outside this case. The state court characterized the practical effect as punishment of thought; it did not supply a narrowing definition that eliminates the intentional-selection predicate.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0.

| First Amendment validity of the intentional-selection enhancement | Reverse: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent | The State may enhance punishment for this independently criminal battery on the proved selection predicate; abstract belief remains protected. |
| Facial overbreadth based on possible future evidentiary use | Reject the challenge: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent | Eight Justices adopt the speculative-chill rationale; Stone-Zsela agrees only with the disposition of this component. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Federal constitutional effect is not settled by a state court’s characterization

**Controlling proposition:** The Court accepts the state court’s authoritative construction of statutory terms, but independently determines the federal constitutional effect of the law so construed. Wisconsin’s characterization of the intentional-selection enhancement as punishment of thought therefore does not resolve the First Amendment question or remove the statutory requirement of an independently committed crime.

**Authority:** White’s Parts I and II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respect for state-law construction does not surrender the federal constitutional inquiry. Ferber and Terminiello require acceptance of authoritative state definitions that determine what a law prohibits. The Wisconsin court here did not redefine a statutory term to eliminate the underlying crime or intentional victim selection. It instead characterized the practical effect of considering discriminatory motive as punishment of thought. Whether that effect violates the First Amendment is the question this Court must decide. The State’s reply that the statute concerns conduct is not, by itself, a complete answer either: the enhanced penalty does depend on a particular motive. The Court must therefore examine whether that motive is constitutionally relevant to the criminal act rather than presume validity from a label. This method preserves binding state-law meanings while allowing independent federal review. It creates no license to rewrite the selection requirement or decide unrelated state-law sentencing questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- New York v. Ferber, 458 U.S.747: authoritative state construction accepted.
- Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S.1: binding construction of operative state-law terms distinguished from the federal characterization at issue here.

##### Intentional racial selection can aggravate an independently criminal battery

**Controlling proposition:** The First Amendment permits an enhanced penalty for an independently criminal battery when the defendant intentionally selects the victim because of race under the applicable statutory predicate. The State may prove a relevant motive through lawful evidence, but may not punish abstract racist belief, expression or association without its legally relevant connection to the offense or another legitimate issue.

**Authority:** White’s Parts I and II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the selection requirement identifies a feature of the criminal attack, not an independent offense of offensive belief. Barclay permits consideration of racial motivation connected to the murder there; the Dawson decision preserves that distinction while prohibiting irrelevant proof of abstract belief. A legislature may account for the distinct individual and community harms it reasonably attributes to deliberately discriminatory attacks. Hishon and Roberts likewise show that considering discriminatory purpose does not automatically convert regulation of conduct into punishment of protected expression. The R.A.V. decision invalidated substantial reach into protected speech and reserved the broader question of distinctions within unprotected speech categories. This statute instead requires an independently criminal act and intentional selection of its target. Nothing in that reserved question requires invalidation here. The State must prove the actual statutory nexus, and speech evidence remains subject to relevance and other evidentiary limits. No punishment for belief alone or automatic admission of offensive statements is authorized.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dawson v. Delaware, March 9, 1992: entered connection-to-legitimate-issue limit applied; abstract belief remains protected.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S.939: racial motivation connected to the criminal act distinguished from abstract association evidence.
- R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, June 22, 1992: entered substantial-overbreadth holding distinguished; noncontrolling content-selection reasoning is not imported.
- Hishon v. King & Spalding, 467 U.S.69, and Roberts v. United States Jaycees, 468 U.S.609: regulation of discriminatory conduct distinguished from suppression of a protected message.
- Runyon v. McCrary, 427 U.S.160: protection for belief does not confer a right to engage in unlawful discrimination.

##### Speculative future evidentiary use does not establish substantial overbreadth

**Controlling proposition:** The possibility that a person might suppress protected views for fear that those views could later help prove intentional selection in a future crime does not establish substantial overbreadth of this enhancement. The First Amendment permits relevant statements to prove criminal intent, subject to evidentiary safeguards and Dawson’s prohibition on punishment for abstract belief.

**Authority:** White’s Part III; White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proposed chilling chain is too speculative to invalidate the statute on its face. It assumes that a person will withhold protected expression because the person might later commit a covered crime and the expression might then prove the required discriminatory selection. The statute does not prohibit the present expression itself. Haupt establishes that earlier statements may be relevant evidence of criminal intent even though the statements could lawfully have been made. Dawson supplies the necessary boundary: irrelevant belief or association is not a legitimate basis for punishment. Ordinary rules of relevance, reliability and admissibility continue to operate, so the decision does not admit every offensive remark a prosecutor offers. The contrary argument would turn legitimate evidentiary use into a general constitutional obstacle to proving motive. That attenuated concern is unlike a law directly reaching a substantial range of protected speech. It therefore does not sustain this facial challenge to the intentional-selection enhancement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Haupt v. United States, 330 U.S.631: relevant earlier statements may establish criminal intent subject to careful evidentiary scrutiny.
- Dawson, March 9, 1992: the limit on irrelevant abstract-belief evidence preserved.
- R.A.V., June 22, 1992: direct substantial reach into protected expression distinguished from speculative evidentiary chilling.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: R.A.V. supplied substantial-overbreadth law and Dawson required a legitimate connection for belief evidence. After this decision: all nine sustain enhancement for this intentional-selection battery; eight reject the distinct speculative-chill ground in a controlling opinion. The reserved within-unprotected-speech question remains open. No later jury-allocation rule, independent speech crime or blanket admissibility rule enters.

#### Source Notes

[Official report and underlying record description, 508 U.S.476](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508476/usrep508476.pdf); [Wisconsin Supreme Court judgment, 169 Wis.2d153](https://static.case.law/wis-2d/169/html/0153-01.html) supplies the state judgment and practical-effect analysis. Official report 480–484, especially notes 1 and 2, verifies the applicable statute, special verdict, sentence and excluded claims; 485–490 supports the motive/evidence distinction.

Record and statutory descriptions are supported by these sources. The public opinion summaries are paraphrases, not verbatim quotations. The 1989–1990 version governs. The statutory exclusion for crimes already requiring proof of the protected status is distinct from ordinary evidentiary limits. The 1992 amendments and equal-protection/vagueness questions are not adjudicated. No special mandate date or subsequent lower-court outcome is reported.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Wisconsin Supreme Court judgment is reversed and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with the enhancement’s First Amendment validity. The Court removes the constitutional obstacle but does not issue a new sentencing order or decide equal-protection and vagueness claims outside the granted question. The intermediate court found the former waived and rejected the latter; this Court does not alter those unreviewed determinations or erase ordinary evidentiary objections.

Supreme Court review is complete and the Wisconsin proceedings resume on remand. The First Amendment obstacle is removed; no new sentence, acquittal, decision on the unreviewed Fourteenth Amendment claims or subsequent evidentiary ruling is inferred.

### Weiss v. United States, No. 92-1482, including Hernandez under Rule 12.2

Merits decision, January 19, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

510 U.S. 163. Argued November 3, 1993. On review of the Court of Military Appeals, 36 M.J. 224 and 37 M.J. 252, affirming the separate courts-martial. The questions are whether assignment of commissioned officers as trial and appellate military judges requires a second appointment and whether the lack of fixed judicial terms violates due process.

All nine participated at argument and decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

#### Judgment

Both Court of Military Appeals judgments are affirmed unanimously.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appointment objection, Weiss and Hernandez | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | No opposing Justice | Both judgments stand |
| Fixed-term objection, Weiss and Hernandez | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | No opposing Justice | No fixed term, new appointment or retrial ordered |

Weiss's larceny conviction and sentence and Hernandez's drug/conspiracy conviction and sentence remain undisturbed on these institutional objections.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg in full; Scalia and Thomas in Part A and the historical description only | Both affirmances | Part A: appointment; Part B: contextual tenure analysis has seven votes |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Both affirmances | Mandatory germaneness for these assignments; historical ground for tenure |
| Concurrence | Souter | No other Justice | Both affirmances; full Court-opinion join within its limits | Inferior-officer and principal-office boundary |

Ginsburg's independent-constitutional-review qualification is expressed in the Court's opinion; it is not a separate published writing. No Marks inference is necessary.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Commissioned officers assigned germane military judicial duties

**Controlling proposition:** The Appointments Clause applies to military judges, but the already commissioned officers serving as trial and appellate judges in these cases require no second appointment: their judicial duties are germane to the military offices conferred by presidential appointment and Senate confirmation. Assignment by a Judge Advocate General does not itself confer a new, unrelated federal office; the holding does not authorize that result or decide civilian appellate appointments or assignment to principal offices.

**Authority:** Part A of Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; nine Justices support the confined existing-commission and germane-duty proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines the office and duties, not merely Congress's choice of the word detailing. Buckley establishes that persons exercising significant federal authority must hold constitutionally authorized appointments. These judges already possess presidential commissions confirmed by the Senate. Shoemaker permits germane additional responsibilities within a validly conferred office; it does not permit an unauthorized official to fill a distinct federal office by renaming appointment as assignment. Administering military discipline has long belonged to commissioned officers' responsibilities. Congress's judicial qualifications and separation of judging from command make that function more independent without removing its connection to the military office. Articles 26 and 66 accordingly provide for detailing or assignment of qualified officers rather than a second appointment. The constitutional objection fails on that bounded combination of existing appointment and germane duties. Neither administrative convenience nor the initial commission alone would establish authority to assign an unrelated office, and civilian appellate judges are outside the facts before the Court.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Buckley v. Valeo: applied to the officer character of the military judges' duties; military office is not exempt from the Appointments Clause.
- Shoemaker v. United States: applied to germane additional duties within an existing commissioned office; no general authority to fill unrelated offices is recognized.
- Freytag v. Commissioner: its insistence on constitutional appointment of officers is preserved; the present judges already hold constitutionally sufficient commissions.

##### Fixed tenure and impartial military adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** Due process does not require fixed terms for the commissioned military judges in this statutory system merely because their assignments lack a specified duration. Under Middendorf's military-specific framework, the history and actual protections against command influence answer this facial objection; the holding does not require proof of actual bias to challenge a constitutionally intolerable structure or preclude a supported challenge to retaliation, improper reassignment or other command influence.

**Authority:** Part B of Kennedy's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; seven Justices. Scalia and Thomas concur in the judgment on the distinct historical-practice ground and join the historical description, but do not join Part B's contextual sufficiency analysis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court gives substantial weight to Congress's responsibility for military discipline while independently enforcing the right to an impartial tribunal. Middendorf asks whether the considerations supporting an additional protection are extraordinarily weighty against Congress's military judgment. Fixed tenure is one possible protection, but its absence cannot be evaluated apart from this system. Article 26 protects judicial-duty evaluations from the convening authority and commanding officer; Article 37 prohibits improper censure and influence, and statutory review, disqualification and challenge mechanisms provide further protection. Military appellate decisions also forbid retaliation through sentence-based evaluation, transfer or decertification. Tumey and Ward establish that a structurally unacceptable incentive may violate due process without proof that a judge yielded to it. The present arrangement, however, does not establish such a risk merely by omitting fixed terms. Gibson and Concrete Pipe demand genuine neutrality in their different settings, not identical institutional safeguards everywhere. Actual command influence and materially different structures remain reviewable; the Court prescribes no tenure period.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Middendorf v. Henry: applied as the military-specific framework; constitutional review remains independent.
- Tumey v. Ohio and Ward v. Village of Monroeville: preserved for constitutionally unacceptable institutional incentives without a requirement to prove actual capitulation.
- Gibson v. Florida Bar and Concrete Pipe & Products v. Construction Laborers Pension Trust: their context-specific neutrality requirements remain in force; neither compels military fixed terms.
- Medina v. California: no universal rejection of Mathews is inferred from the authority of its California-specific judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. The existing-commission/germane-duty rule and the military-specific tenure ruling preserve constitutional appointment and impartiality requirements in materially different circumstances.

Effective January 19, 1994, these commissioned trial and appellate military assignments require no second appointment and this system need not supply fixed terms. The Court does not decide civilian appointments, an unrelated or principal-office assignment, actual command influence, retaliatory midtrial reassignment, or every possible structural-bias claim. The appointment proposition has nine votes; the contextual due-process explanation has seven, while the two historical concurrences supply no additional mandatory general test.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by Thomas. They join Part A's conclusion because germane duties are necessary when the assigning Judge Advocate General is not a constitutionally authorized appointing actor and are established here. On tenure they rely on the established history of military judging without fixed terms, rather than judicial balancing of the military system's advantages and safeguards. Their agreement with the disposition does not approve an open-ended authority to assess whether Congress selected the best arrangement. They leave inadequately presented principal-versus-inferior-office questions undecided.

Souter concurs, joined by no other Justice. He regards the existing-commission arrangement as sustainable for these inferior officers while preserving the Appointments Clause's accountability requirements. Germane assignment cannot justify promotion into a principal office without the constitutionally required appointment. His explanation does not decide every military judge's classification, authorize civilian assignment without appointment, or change the Court's judgment. He joins both parts of the Court's opinion within their express limits.

#### Source Notes

Military proceedings, commissions, duties and statutory protections: [official United States Reports, 510 U.S. 163](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510163/usrep510163.pdf), 165–181. Articles 26, 37, 66 and 98 supply distinct protections, rather than a finding that pressure never occurs. The judges at issue were commissioned officers; no conclusion about civilian appellate appointments is supplied. No particular judge's unreported promotion history or reason for rotation is assumed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Both military appellate judgments stand. No remand, appointment proceeding, tenure rule or new trial is directed. Neither this decision nor the guilty pleas foreclose distinct claims beyond the institutional objections decided.

**End of entry: Weiss v. United States, merits decision, January 19, 1994.**

### Waters v. Churchill, No. 92-1450

Merits decision, 1994-05-31

#### Chronology and Posture

Reported at 511 U.S. 661. Argued December 1, 1993; decided May 31, 1994. On certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 977 F.2d 1114, which reversed defendants' summary judgment under an actual-speech rule. The questions concern mistaken employer accounts, actual retaliatory cause, and the proper remand.

All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

#### Judgment

The Seventh Circuit's judgment is vacated and the case remanded, 7–2. Defendants' summary judgment remains set aside because the actual ground for discharge is materially disputed.

| Component | Disposition and vote | For | Other position | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical appellate factual-error rule and mandate | Vacate and remand, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Blackmun, Stevens would affirm reversal and remand under actual-speech rule | Defendants' summary judgment remains set aside; genuine reliance/motive and protection of causally relevant other speech remain. |
| Personal immunity, municipal policy/custom and final relief | No final merits disposition on these matters | No immunity or damages award | No revived association claim | Immunity remains available for consideration if necessary; ungranted municipal issue not reached. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion announcing judgment; plurality | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg | Full objective-care framework and conditional application; four joins, no complete Opinion of the Court. |
| Concurrence | Souter | — | Genuine belief, limited endpoint agreements and unraised excessiveness observation. |
| Concurrence in judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Genuine permissible ground; no independent inquiry duty; accepts sufficient safe harbor and remand. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun | Actual protected speech; would affirm and remand; accepts unreasonable-false-protected-speech endpoint. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A genuine, reasonable account of disciplinable speech supplies a limited safe harbor

**Controlling proposition:** A public employer does not violate the employee's substantive speech right by disciplining her because of a genuinely held, objectively reasonable account of speech that the Pickering–Connick balance permits it to discipline, even if that account is later shown mistaken. The protection requires actual reliance on the permissible ground; it does not protect a reasonable investigation used to conceal retaliation for different protected speech.

**Authority:** O'Connor's plurality opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg, adopts this sufficient condition; Scalia's concurrence in the judgment, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, expressly accepts it under their broader genuine-ground rule. Seven Justices agree at this precise level; only four adopt the full objective-inquiry framework.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court's common rule permits reliance on a genuine, reasonable account without requiring perfect reconstruction of a workplace conversation. Pickering balances protected public criticism against effective public service, and Connick permits reasonable forecasts of disruption in the actual employment setting. An employer need not await a breakdown, but the account must describe speech the legal balance permits it to discipline. Givhan protects private expression without eliminating the relevance of its manner, time and place. These principles do not protect an asserted belief that was not the real reason for discharge. Mt. Healthy requires attention to actual causation rather than a reason that merely could have justified the result. Here the reported attack on supervisory authority and discouragement of transfer could permissibly prompt discipline because of its threatened disruption, even assuming some public-concern value. That conditional legal conclusion neither finds the reported words true nor establishes genuine reliance. The separate patient-care criticism and asserted management hostility leave the actual basis for discharge disputed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pickering v. Board of Education, 391 U.S. 563 (1968): applies the public-employee speech balance, adding this limited factual-error permission.
- Connick v. Myers, 461 U.S. 138 (1983): applies contextual public-concern analysis and reasonable prospective assessment of institutional disruption; personal embarrassment alone is insufficient.
- Givhan v. Western Line Consolidated School District, 439 U.S. 410 (1979): preserves private speech protection and the relevance of manner, time and place.
- Mt. Healthy City Board of Education v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274 (1977): preserves actual causation; a potentially permissible ground is not proof of the ground actually used.

##### An unreasonable false account cannot defeat actually protected speech

**Controlling proposition:** Discipline because of an unreasonable and incorrect report of speech that was actually protected violates the substantive speech right when the protected speech bears the required causal relation to the action, subject to distinct causation defenses and remedial immunity. This agreement does not establish liability for every investigative imperfection, for an unreasonable report of actually unprotected speech, or without proof of the underlying protected conduct and causal action.

**Authority:** O'Connor, Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg accept this proposition under objective factual care; Stevens's dissent, joined by Blackmun, expressly accepts it under their broader actual-speech rule. Six Justices agree on this proposition, but the two dissenters do not join the reasonable-error safe harbor or the judgment vacating the appellate rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The controlling agreement preserves protection when a public employer acts against protected speech on an unreasonable false account. Pickering does not permit public-concern criticism to be displaced merely by calling it disruptive; the institutional justification must answer the legally relevant speech. Givhan prevents private delivery from eliminating that protection, while Connick requires attention to content, form and context. The competing concern for workable management cannot justify the combined assumptions of an unreasonable report and actually protected speech that caused the adverse action. At the same time, this proposition concerns substantive suppression, not an employment hearing guaranteed by Roth. A flawed interview alone proves neither protected speech nor causation, and disagreement over witnesses does not itself establish unreasonableness. Mt. Healthy retains the employee's motivating-factor showing and the employer's separate same-decision defense. No damages follow automatically: the facts remain disputed and individual immunity has its own requirements. The Court does not combine this agreement with the separate safe harbor to attribute a complete investigatory theory to all six Justices.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pickering, Connick and Givhan: preserve the substantive protection of the actual public-concern speech within the employment balance.
- Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564 (1972): distinguishes a property-based hearing entitlement from this substantive speech claim.
- Mt. Healthy, 429 U.S. 274 (1977): retains the distinct causation and same-decision requirements; unreasonableness alone is not an automatic recovery rule.

##### The motive dispute requires remand without restoring defendants' summary judgment

**Controlling proposition:** The Seventh Circuit's categorical actual-words rule is vacated, but defendants' summary judgment remains set aside because genuine reliance and retaliation for other protected criticism are materially disputed. On remand the employee must show protected conduct was a substantial or motivating factor; the employer may prove by a preponderance that the same decision would have occurred without it, and individual qualified immunity remains available and, if necessary, must be assessed as a distinct defense under the law relevant to the 1987 conduct.

**Authority:** O'Connor's four-Justice plurality and Scalia's three-Justice concurrence in the judgment support this common vacatur and limited remand. Blackmun and Stevens would affirm the appellate reversal under their different actual-speech rule; their votes are not counted as support for vacatur.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court remands because a permissible reported account does not establish that management actually acted upon it. Churchill's evidence concerning earlier patient-care criticism, cross-training disputes and management hostility supports an inference that protected expression, rather than the reported personal attack, prompted discharge. The Court does not resolve that inference against her on summary judgment. Mt. Healthy requires a proved same-decision defense, not a showing that another ground could have justified dismissal; Givhan preserves that distinction for private criticism. If different statements caused discharge, their protection must be assessed under Pickering and Connick. The competing grounds for vacatur do not require reopening the completed investigation's objective adequacy; genuine reliance and motive remain unresolved under both judgment-supporting approaches. Harlow and Elder preserve a separate inquiry into personal damages immunity and relevant authority, without allowing a newly stated 1994 rule itself to establish notice in early 1987. No damages, reinstatement, final credibility finding or municipal-liability holding is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mt. Healthy, 429 U.S. 274 (1977), and Givhan, 439 U.S. 410 (1979): apply the employee's causal showing and the employer's preponderance same-decision defense.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 (1982): preserves individual qualified immunity as distinct from the substantive speech rule.
- Elder v. Holloway, February 23, 1994: requires considering relevant authority on the preserved immunity issue, without expanding the claim or supplying notice from later law.
- Rankin v. McPherson, June 24, 1987, and Anderson v. Creighton, June 25, 1987: may inform later doctrine but cannot themselves establish notice for the January and February 1987 actions.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Pickering and Connick retain their contextual speech balance, subject to the identified factual-error propositions. Mt. Healthy and Givhan retain causation and the proved same-decision defense. Roth creates no assumed employment-hearing entitlement. Harlow and Elder remain distinct immunity authorities; no later rule supplies notice for early 1987. No precedent is overruled. The different logical overlaps do not establish one complete rationale under Marks.

The controlling propositions are the limited reasonable-and-genuine safe harbor, the six-Justice agreement concerning unreasonable false reports of actually protected speech, and the common motive-based remand. The full objective-inquiry framework has four joins. It does not become a unified majority rationale by combining different directions of agreement. No general constitutional investigation claim, mandatory witness checklist, least-severe-discipline rule, universal post-discharge cure, or automatic recovery for procedural imperfection is adopted. Unreasonable inquiry when the actual words were unprotected is not resolved.

For this completed process, the plurality finds objective adequacy conditionally on genuine reliance; the other three vacating Justices require no independent adequacy showing. The reported personal attack and discouragement of transfer may lawfully be disciplined on a genuine account because the predicted disruption outweighs any assumed public-concern value. No finding establishes that those were the true words or real cause. Different patient-care criticism must be assessed if it actually caused discharge. Mt. Healthy requires the employee's substantial-or-motivating-factor showing and permits the employer to prove the same decision by a preponderance. Individual immunity, the ungranted municipal policy/custom issue, and substantive protection remain distinct.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor announces the judgment and writes a plurality opinion joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter and Ginsburg. Their complete framework permits a genuinely held, reasonably reached account of speech to enter the employment balance even if mistaken. When a reasonable supervisor would recognize a substantial likelihood that the actual speech was protected, the employer must exercise the care a reasonable manager would use for this decision in this setting. Good faith alone does not supply that care. Reliability, known contradictions, obvious available witnesses, practicable clarification, urgency and reasonably feared harm inform the assessment; they are not a mandatory interview checklist. Hearsay and ordinary credibility choices may be reasonable. The Constitution does not demand a trial, confrontation, every witness, perfect accuracy or an employment hearing under Roth.

On the completed sequence here, the initial and confirming Ballew reports, the Perkins-Graham interview, Hopper's opportunity to hear Churchill during the grievance, and the additional check with Ballew satisfy the objective standard if genuinely relied upon. This does not establish that the witnesses were truthful or the reports were the actual reason for discharge. It creates no universal rule that later grievance review cures every earlier injury. The reported personal attack, discouragement of transfer and threatened working relationship furnish sufficient predicted disruption to outweigh any assumed public-concern value in that account. Different patient-care criticism remains a different question. The four reserve no new objective-adequacy issue on remand; they preserve genuine belief, actual motive, protection of other causally relevant speech and available defenses. This full framework has four joins.

Souter also concurs separately. Genuine belief is an independent requirement: an objectively reasonable investigation cannot protect a decisionmaker who does not credit its result or uses it as a pretext. He explains the majority agreements at the two ends of the disputed factual-error question. The reasonable-and-genuine safe harbor is accepted by seven Justices; liability for an unreasonable false report of actually protected speech is accepted by six. Those overlaps do not give the entire plurality framework seven or six joins. He also considers whether management genuinely regarded the selected discipline as appropriate, while noting that no distinct pretext-of-excessiveness claim is raised. That observation establishes no least-severe-sanction rule.

Scalia concurs in the judgment, joined by Kennedy and Thomas. The First Amendment forbids retaliation for protected expression. In their view a genuine decision based on a legally disciplinable account does not violate that right merely because the account was wrong, and the Constitution creates no separate investigation duty. Selective inquiry, implausible explanations and disregard of evidence may prove pretext; inadequacy of an investigation is not itself their constitutional wrong. They accept the plurality's reasonable-and-genuine safe harbor because their rule permits more employer action, not because they adopt objective care as necessary. Honest belief still cannot make legally protected speech punishable, and neither an asserted belief nor a possible lawful ground proves actual causation. They join the remand on the supported motive dispute.

Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun. They would affirm the appellate reversal and remand under the rule that protection follows the speech actually uttered and its causal connection to discipline. Churchill's supported patient-care account must be taken favorably on defendants' summary-judgment motion; that procedural assumption is not a trial finding. Their Connick and Rankin positions protect informed criticism of public operations while retaining Pickering balancing. Mt. Healthy and Givhan preserve causation and a proved same-decision defense, but they would not add bad faith as an element of a claim involving actually protected speech. Reasonable factual error does not extinguish the substantive right. Management's legitimate interests enter the balance, and individual immunity addresses some damages risks without narrowing the right. Burnup & Sims offers an analogy concerning mistaken reports of protected labor activity, not direct application of the NLRA. They expressly agree that an unreasonable false report cannot defeat actually protected speech that caused discipline, while rejecting the broader safe harbor for reasonable mistakes.
