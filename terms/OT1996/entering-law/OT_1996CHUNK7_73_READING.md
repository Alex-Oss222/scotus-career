## Standards and Tests selection: Private enforcement of Adoption Assistance Act reasonable-efforts and reporting clauses

### Private enforcement of Adoption Assistance Act reasonable-efforts and reporting clauses

**Governing question:** Private enforcement of 42 U.S.C. §671(a)(15)'s reasonable-efforts clause and §671(a)(9)'s reporting clause through §1983 or an action implied directly under the Act.

**Current rule:** Read within the Act's plan and administrative-enforcement structure, §671(a)(15) creates no individual right enforceable under §1983 to compel the asserted reasonable efforts to prevent removal or reunify families. An approved plan's commitment, §671(a)(3)'s statewide-operation requirement and caseworker deficiencies do not supply that missing right. Nor does §671(a)(15) imply a separate private action: beneficial purpose does not establish congressional intent to create the requested remedy. Section 671(a)(9) requires a plan to provide reporting to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency when a state agency has reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse or exploitation and the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B. That administrative direction does not create the asserted private §1983 right or an implied action to obtain caseworker relief. The §1983-right and implied-action inquiries remain distinct; failure to imply an action does not bar an otherwise available enacted §1983 remedy.

**Authority by component:**
- No individual §671(a)(15) right: Suter v. Artist M., March 25, 1992, “Section 671(a)(15) supplies no individual reasonable-efforts right enforceable under §1983,” Part II.
- No separate implied (15) action: the same record, “Section 671(a)(15) supplies no separate implied action,” Part III.
- Reporting trigger and absence of asserted §1983 right: the same record, “The asserted §671(a)(9) reporting claim is not enforceable through §1983,” Part IV-A.
- No implied reporting action: the same record, “The asserted §671(a)(9) claim supplies no separate implied action,” Part IV-B. White for O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas supplies six controlling joins for each component.

**Present operation effective:** Suter, March 25, 1992.

**Exceptions and limits:** No general rule makes state-plan provisions unenforceable. Administrative funding review does not necessarily foreclose an otherwise existing §1983 right, and the State's administrative obligations remain. Appropriate relief presupposes a cognizable action and cannot create the missing right or action.

**Open question:** Private enforceability of §671(a)(16), read with §675(1) and §675(5)(B), remains undecided, as do the preserved claim's merits and proper relief.

**Holdings navigation:** Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992) — Child-welfare statutory enforcement.

## Standards and Tests selection: Culpable affirmative assistance to a securities fraud

### Culpable affirmative assistance to a securities fraud

**Governing question:** When the received private §10(b) and Rule 10b-5 action reaches a person assisting another’s fraud.

**Current rule:** A person who knowingly or with severe recklessness substantially assists an underlying securities fraud through affirmative conduct can be privately liable. The defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the activity’s fraudulent character and its conduct’s assisting role. Severe recklessness is an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known to the defendant or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it. An independent disclosure duty is unnecessary for affirmative assistance. The plaintiff must prove the underlying manipulation or deception, qualifying assistance and culpability, legally sufficient reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss by the ordinary civil preponderance standard. Investors need not have known of or relied on concealed assistance itself; no reliance presumption follows merely from this rule.

**Authority by component:**

- Received action, mental state, underlying-fraud reliance and proof: Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A — The received private action includes culpable substantial assistance; Knowledge or severe recklessness in affirmative substantial assistance; Triable issues require further proceedings, not a liability award, 1994-04-19. Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the existence of the confined action and its received-remedy rationale. No fractured-decision synthesis is necessary. Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; all five adopt this confined mental-state and affirmative-assistance rule, not a universal recklessness rule for every securities claim. Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the record application, preserved proof requirements and limited affirmance and remand.

**Present operation effective:** Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A, 1994-04-19.

**Exceptions and limits:** Negligence, valuation concerns alone, professional status, ordinary services without qualifying culpability and assisting effect, and mere duty-free silence are insufficient. Relabeling inaction does not make it affirmative. No primary misrepresentation by the assistant, general investigation duty, liability from default, universal securities scienter rule or comprehensive SEC-enforcement rule follows.

**Open question:** The full rules for duty-based omissions and consciously intended assistance through duty-free inaction remain open. Contribution separately requires established shared liability under Musick, Peeler.

**Holdings navigation:** The received private action includes culpable substantial assistance; Knowledge or severe recklessness in affirmative substantial assistance; Triable issues require further proceedings, not a liability award.

## Standards and Tests selection: Compensatory remedies for intentional Title IX discrimination

### Compensatory remedies for intentional Title IX discrimination

**Governing question:** Whether remedial silence or Spending Clause notice bars compensatory damages in the established Title IX private action.

**Current rule:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing relief does not itself create a new cause of action. Title IX's established private action permits compensatory damages for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation, and compensable loss. The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar that compensation for intentional breach of Title IX's express nondiscrimination condition.

**Authority by component:**

- Appropriate relief in an existing action and availability of Title IX compensation: Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools — Compensatory relief in the established Title IX action, February 26, 1992.
- Rejection of the categorical funding-notice bar: Franklin — Notice does not bar damages for the pleaded intentional violation, February 26, 1992. Both holdings rest on White's opinion joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter; Scalia and Thomas supply judgment votes only on a narrower statutory ground.

**Present operation effective:** *Franklin*, February 26, 1992.

**Open question:** The decision supplies no final recipient-attribution rule, punitive remedy, liability for unintended discriminatory effects, or resolution of genuinely ambiguous funding conditions.

**Holdings navigation:** Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992) — Statutory discrimination remedies.

## Standards and Tests selection: Senate committee evidence-taking in an impeachment trial

### Senate committee evidence-taking in an impeachment trial

**Governing question:** What instructions govern senate committee evidence-taking in an impeachment trial?

**Current rule:** The Senate's use of a committee to receive testimony is not judicially revisable on the objection to that evidentiary allocation when the full Senate receives the record, hears argument and retains deliberation and judgment. This is a nonjusticiability rule for that procedural claim, not constitutional merits approval. Judicial enforcement of the oath, presidential-trial presiding-officer and two-thirds requirements, and review of a purported proceeding abandoning adjudication altogether, remain open.

**Authority by component:**

- The presented committee-evidence challenge is not judicially revisable: Nixon v. United States — The presented committee-evidence challenge is not judicially revisable, 1993-01-13. Stone-Zsela’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Six of nine Justices adopt this exact ground and its limits; White and Blackmun’s merits ground is noncontrolling, and Souter does not formally join the Court’s opinion.

**Present operation effective:** 1993-01-13; Nixon v. United States, for the components identified above.

**Holdings navigation:** Nixon v. United States, 506 U.S. 224 (1993).

#### Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-1488.
**Decided:** March 25, 1992.

For this case’s other questions, see *Suter v. Artist M.*, March 25, 1992, No. 90-1488: whether the separate b.h. consent decree moots or precludes this statutory-enforcement controversy (Federal Courts — Mootness and decree scope).

##### Whether §671(a)(15) creates the asserted individual reasonable-efforts right enforceable through §1983

**Holding:** Section 671(a)(15), read in the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's plan and administrative-enforcement structure, does not confer on these beneficiaries an individual right enforceable through §1983 to compel the asserted reasonable efforts to prevent removal or reunify families. An approved plan's reasonable-efforts commitment, §671(a)(3)'s statewide-operation requirement, and the record's caseworker deficiencies do not supply that missing federal right.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this rule and reverse the §1983 ruling under §671(a)(15).

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not declare every state-plan provision unenforceable or find that administrative funding withdrawal comprehensively forecloses an otherwise existing §1983 right. The absence of an individual right, the existence of an action, and the scope of relief remain separate inquiries.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Seventh Circuit’s judgment is reversed as to §671(a)(15) and (9) enforcement through both asserted routes; the three-working-day assignment/reassignment injunction and ancillary weekly monitoring order are vacated, 6–3. Any blanket resolution of the independent §671(a)(16) claim is vacated and that claim remanded for provision-specific consideration, 9–0, without keeping the existing injunction alive on an unadjudicated ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Maine v. Thiboutot, 448 U.S. 1 (1980) — preserves statutory-right enforcement under §1983; a violation of federal law still must invade an enforceable right.
- Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987) — distinguishes its defined rent and utility entitlement; its enforceability holding and separate foreclosure inquiry remain operative.
- Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990) — distinguishes its substantive reimbursement obligation and supplied benchmarks; neither its Medicaid holding nor its rejection of automatic foreclosure by funding withdrawal is overruled.
- Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman, 451 U.S. 1 (1981) — applies its clear-notice principle to the asserted funding obligation; no categorical immunity for state-plan provisions is created.

##### Whether §671(a)(15) creates a separate implied private action

**Holding:** The Act does not create an implied private cause of action to enforce §671(a)(15)'s reasonable-efforts clause because the claimants have not established congressional intent to create that remedy. This conclusion concerns the distinct action asserted directly under the Act and does not make failure to imply an action a bar to an otherwise available enacted §1983 remedy.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this independently necessary ground for rejecting the second enforcement route sustained below.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Failure to imply a private action does not foreclose an otherwise available enacted §1983 remedy; other statutory provisions require their own examination.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Seventh Circuit’s judgment is reversed as to §671(a)(15) and (9) enforcement through both asserted routes; the three-working-day assignment/reassignment injunction and ancillary weekly monitoring order are vacated, 6–3. Any blanket resolution of the independent §671(a)(16) claim is vacated and that claim remanded for provision-specific consideration, 9–0, without keeping the existing injunction alive on an unadjudicated ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Cort v. Ash, 422 U.S. 66 (1975) — applies its inquiry into an implied private remedy, with congressional intent decisive.
- Transamerica Mortgage Advisors, Inc. v. Lewis, 444 U.S. 11 (1979) — applies its requirement of congressional intent to create the particular remedy asserted; statutory purpose alone is insufficient.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992, decision — preserves the distinction between the existence of a cognizable action and appropriate relief within it; no Title IX holding is narrowed.

##### Whether the asserted §671(a)(9) reporting claim is enforceable through §1983

**Holding:** Section 671(a)(9)'s requirement that a state plan provide for reporting to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency when a state agency has reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse, or exploitation, and the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B, does not confer on these respondents a private federal right enforceable through §1983 on the claim presented. The reporting ground therefore cannot independently sustain the challenged caseworker injunction.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-A, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this separate statutory holding.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The reporting obligation and administrative enforcement remain. Section 671(a)(16)’s case-plan and review claim is not decided through this reporting holding.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Seventh Circuit’s judgment is reversed as to §671(a)(15) and (9) enforcement through both asserted routes; the three-working-day assignment/reassignment injunction and ancillary weekly monitoring order are vacated, 6–3. Any blanket resolution of the independent §671(a)(16) claim is vacated and that claim remanded for provision-specific consideration, 9–0, without keeping the existing injunction alive on an unadjudicated ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Wright, 479 U.S. 418 — applies the need to identify a privately enforceable statutory right; its distinct rent holding remains in force.
- Wilder, 496 U.S. 498 — applies provision-specific examination of a binding individual entitlement; no general rule against enforcement of plan provisions replaces it.

##### Whether §671(a)(9) creates the asserted separate implied action

**Holding:** Section 671(a)(9) does not create an implied private action for these respondents to obtain the challenged caseworker relief: its reporting requirement and the Act's enforcement structure do not establish congressional intent to authorize that action. Rejection of this distinct statutory vehicle does not decide private enforcement of §671(a)(16).

**Proposition-level authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-B, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt the ground rejecting direct implied enforcement of the reporting claim.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 671(a)(16), read with §675(1) and §675(5)(B), remains open as to enforceable right, implied action, violation and additional lawful relief. No constitutional claim is revived or nationwide three-day deadline imposed.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Seventh Circuit’s judgment is reversed as to §671(a)(15) and (9) enforcement through both asserted routes; the three-working-day assignment/reassignment injunction and ancillary weekly monitoring order are vacated, 6–3. Any blanket resolution of the independent §671(a)(16) claim is vacated and that claim remanded for provision-specific consideration, 9–0, without keeping the existing injunction alive on an unadjudicated ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Cort, 422 U.S. 66, and Transamerica, 444 U.S. 11 — apply the congressional-intent requirement to the distinct implied reporting action.
- Franklin, February 26, 1992, decision — applies its action/remedy distinction without changing the established Title IX action or relief available within it.



## Verbatim selected authority: state/holdings/civil-rights.md — Franklin v. Gwinnett

#### Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 90-918.
**Decided:** February 26, 1992.

##### Whether compensatory damages are available in the established Title IX private action for intentional discrimination

**Holding:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing that remedy does not create a new cause of action. In Title IX’s established private action, compensatory damages are available for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remedial rule and application. Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment on a narrower statutory ground.

**Material application:** Franklin’s allegations concern completed sexual harassment and abuse while attending a federally funded school, with alleged official knowledge and inaction. After her departure, backpay and prospective student relief could not compensate the pleaded injury. Congress’s 1986 reference to legal and equitable remedies and subsequent preservation of private enforcement supply no direction barring compensation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Allegations are not findings. No new cause of action, final recipient-attribution rule, punitive remedy, or liability for unintended discriminatory effects is established; the source of Congress’s constitutional power need not be selected.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the categorical damages dismissal and remand for ordinary adjudication of liability, attribution, causation and compensable loss. No amount is awarded or prospective school-supervision order entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677, supplies the established Title IX action and is not reopened. *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, supplies appropriate relief absent congressional limitation. *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, separates authority to sue from remedy. *J. I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426, opposes limiting relief merely because a statute does not enumerate remedies. *Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission*, 463 U.S. 582, supplies intentional-discrimination remedial support without a single unified majority rationale. *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624, supplies analogous monetary-relief support.

##### Whether Spending Clause notice categorically bars compensation for the intentional Title IX violation alleged

**Holding:** The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar compensatory relief for an intentional violation of Title IX’s express prohibition of sex discrimination within its established private action. Ambiguous funding conditions, unintentional effects and the separate requirements for attributing misconduct to a recipient are not resolved.

**Proposition-level authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices reject the separate funding-notice ground for dismissal.

**Material application:** The enacted nondiscrimination condition and established private action defeat a categorical notice objection to compensation for the pleaded intentional discrimination. This conclusion does not establish when the school is responsible for the teacher’s conduct.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The notice objection does not sustain dismissal; compensatory relief may be sought on remand subject to proof of the violation and resulting loss.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1, retains its fair-notice rule for funding conditions but does not bar relief for the intentional violation alleged. *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, informs the identification of sex-based harassment without importing a Title VII attribution formula. *Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624, defeats a categorical monetary-remedy objection based on spending legislation. *Cannon*, 441 U.S. 677, supplies the established enforcement premise.



## Verbatim selected authority: state/holdings/business-and-commercial-law.md — Central Bank of

#### Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A., 511 U.S. 164

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-854.

**Decided:** 1994-04-19.

##### The received private action includes culpable substantial assistance

**Holding:** The established private action under §10(b) and Rule 10b-5 permits recovery from a person who knowingly or with severe recklessness substantially assists an underlying securities fraud through affirmative conduct, subject to proof of that fraud, the defendant’s qualifying participation, legally sufficient reliance, causation and recoverable loss. This is accessory responsibility within the received investor action, not a finding that the assistant made a primary misrepresentation or a rule imposing liability through professional status or control alone.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the existence of the confined action and its received-remedy rationale. No fractured-decision synthesis is necessary.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Superintendent of Insurance v. Bankers Life & Casualty Co., 404 U.S. 6 — applied as recognition of the received private action; it did not itself decide accessory liability.
- Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance of Wausau, 508 U.S. 286 — applied for bounded development of the received action and distinguished as to its actual contribution holding; shared liability must still be established before allocation.
- Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Curran, 456 U.S. 353 — used by analogy for the significance of an established judicial remedy; its different statutory setting does not establish congressional enactment of this action.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green, 430 U.S. 462 — applied: the underlying wrong must involve the required manipulation or deception, not unfairness or fiduciary misconduct alone.
- Pinter v. Dahl, 486 U.S. 622 — distinguished: its construction of express seller liability under §12 does not settle this received §10(b) action; its own statutory holding remains intact.
- Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185 — its reservation of aiding and abetting is resolved here for the confined private affirmative-assistance action; Hochfelder itself did not decide that question.
- Herman & MacLean v. Huddleston, 459 U.S. 375 — its reservation of aiding and abetting is resolved here for the confined private affirmative-assistance action; Huddleston itself did not decide that question.

##### Knowledge or severe recklessness in affirmative substantial assistance

**Holding:** For affirmative substantial assistance, the defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the fraudulent character of the activity and the assisting role of its conduct; severe recklessness means an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known to the defendant or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it. No independent disclosure duty is required for that affirmative participation, but negligence, valuation concerns alone, professional status, ordinary services without qualifying culpability and assistance, or mere silence without a relevant duty do not suffice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; all five adopt this confined mental-state and affirmative-assistance rule, not a universal recklessness rule for every securities claim.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185 — its exclusion of negligence remains controlling; its recklessness reservation is resolved only for this private affirmative-assistance setting.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green, 430 U.S. 462 — its deception-or-manipulation requirement prevents ordinary investment risk and professional error from becoming securities fraud.
- First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. v. Pring, 969 F.2d 891 — its confined affirmative-assistance and extreme-recklessness analysis is adopted for the bank claim; inconsistent knowledge-only or categorical duty requirements for such affirmative assistance cannot govern after this decision. Its separate rulings concerning Pring are outside this review.

##### Triable issues require further proceedings, not a liability award

**Holding:** Viewed favorably to the purchasers at summary judgment, the appraisal warnings, internal concerns and agreement postponing an already demanded independent review until after the offering permit a genuine dispute over severe recklessness and substantial affirmative assistance; the Tenth Circuit’s reinstatement of the pleaded bank claim is affirmed and the case remanded. The purchasers must still prove the underlying violation, the bank’s qualifying assistance and culpability, legally sufficient reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss under the ordinary civil burden; neither the default nor the bonds’ face value establishes fraud damages.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the record application, preserved proof requirements and limited affirmance and remand.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. v. Pring, 969 F.2d 891 — affirmed insofar as it reverses summary judgment on the purchasers’ affirmative-assistance claim against Central Bank; triable issues are not findings of fraud or damages.
- Herman & MacLean v. Huddleston, 459 U.S. 375 — the ordinary preponderance standard is preserved; neither institutional status nor information held by the defendant shifts that burden.
- Basic Inc. v. Levinson, 485 U.S. 224 — its reliance requirement is preserved. Today’s decision establishes no presumption for these municipal bonds and, as part of the retained accessory action, requires reliance on the underlying fraud rather than on the assistant’s concealed participation.
- Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance of Wausau, 508 U.S. 286 — its shared-liability prerequisite and contribution limits remain effective; this remand establishes no entitlement or allocation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide the full rules for duty-based omissions, duty-free inaction, agency attribution, a universal securities recklessness rule, other enforcement or statutory settings, or Stone's additional concurrence formulations. The §20(a) defense for a controller acting in good faith without directly or indirectly inducing the violation remains distinct. Reliance on the underlying fraud is required; investors need not personally rely on concealed assistance, and no reliance presumption is established for these bonds.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Tenth Circuit insofar as it reinstated the pleaded bank claim and remand, 5–4. The bank's summary judgment cannot rest on the rejected categorical grounds or the disputed assistance and culpability record. No liability, damages, automatic indemnity, unpleaded primary claim or adjudication of another defendant's claim is supplied.

## Verbatim selected authority: terms/OT1996/workspace/neutral-projection.md — Abebe-Jira v.

### 1997-01-14 — Abebe-Jira v. Negewo, No. 95-8873; Eleventh Circuit No. 93-9133.

Authority: [Record](../records/Abebe_Jira_v_Negewo_merits_1997-01-14.md).

#### Holdings

#### I. Jurisdiction does not itself establish a damages action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1350 supplies district-court jurisdiction over an alien's civil tort action alleging a violation of the law of nations or a United States treaty; these substantial allegations of official torture fall within that jurisdiction. Jurisdiction alone neither supplies a domestic damages action nor establishes liability, sovereign-immunity waiver or authority for every asserted international claim.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's opinion, joined by all other participating Justices: nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute identifies the forum and category of controversy. Whether domestic law provides the asserted action is a separate question. Suter requires attention to the source of an enforceable right and action, and Meyer distinguishes federal adjudicative authority and consent from substantive liability. Those distinctions do not make a substantial source-of-action dispute a reason to deny jurisdiction over this pleaded category. The plaintiffs are aliens alleging torts violating the established prohibition of official torture. Their allegations establish the statutory kind of controversy; findings and governing law must separately support relief. No sovereign is made a defendant and no immunity issue is decided.

**Precedent treatment:** Suter's source/action distinction and Meyer's separation of jurisdiction, consent and substantive liability are preserved; neither previously decided §1350's present application.

#### II. A confined received action for personally committed official torture

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1350's reception of judicial adjudication of law-of-nations torts, read in its common-law setting, supports a confined domestic civil action against an individual for deliberate participation in or personal supervision of official torture. The actionable norm forbids severe physical or mental suffering intentionally inflicted under official authority for interrogation, punishment or intimidation; international law supplies that specific obligatory prohibition, while the received domestic action supplies authority to enforce it.

**Authority:** Part II of Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas do not join.

**Controlling explanation:** Universal condemnation alone is not domestic remedial authority, and the jurisdictional label alone cannot resolve the action question. Congress expressly provided adjudication of law-of-nations torts against the common-law background in which such wrongs received judicial remedies. That particular statutory function supports this determinate, established personal-tort action. Filartiga supplies persuasive evidence of judicial recognition of the official-torture prohibition and its civil enforcement. Central Bank's confined received-action reasoning and Musick, Peeler's treatment of incidents of an established action offer analogies; neither itself creates an action under §1350. Suter's administrative-plan provisions supply no contrary rule for this received tort, and O'Melveny rejected a new receiver preference rather than this source-specific construction. The contrary argument correctly separates jurisdiction from liability but gives insufficient effect to the statute's particular reception of tort adjudication. The supported trial findings establish Negewo's direct participation and personal supervision concerning Abebe-Jira and Taye, and personal supervision of some torture concerning Demissie. Official title alone is insufficient. The Court sustains the judgment on torture, without adopting a general Lincoln Mills or legislative-history ground, and announces no action for every customary norm, private nonstate violence, corporate liability, absent-superior liability or every cruel, inhuman or degrading act.

**Precedent treatment:** Suter and O'Melveny are distinguished at their source-specific limits; Central Bank and Musick, Peeler supply confined received-action analogies, not existing ATS holdings. Filartiga is persuasive lower-court authority for this established personal tort.

#### III. Compensation for proved injury within that action

**Holding and operative rule:** The recognized action permits compensatory damages for proved injury caused by the individual's qualifying official torture. The established trial findings support categorical compensatory authority for each plaintiff, without substituting allegations or official position for personal responsibility and causation.

**Authority:** Part III of Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Traditional tort reparation compensates an injured person for loss caused by the defendant's wrongful conduct. Franklin preserves appropriate relief within a cognizable action, while requiring the action to be established first. Part II supplies that separate source here. The bench-trial findings establish the personal torture ground; this is not a damages award based solely on the complaint. The source objection therefore does not defeat compensation once the confined action is recognized. Each plaintiff's $200,000 compensatory award remains in place against the granted categorical objection. The Court neither enlarges the judgment nor adjudicates an independent challenge to amount or trial allocation.

**Precedent treatment:** Franklin's action/remedy distinction is applied without turning its Title IX holding into an independent ATS cause or an amount-of-damages ruling.

#### IV. Punitive authority requires an independently justified aggravated-tort remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Within this established action for intentionally inflicted official torture, traditional common-law remedies permit punitive damages for malicious, willful or equivalently reckless aggravated wrongdoing; punitive relief is permitted, not required in every case. The punitive culpability standard does not dilute the underlying action's intentional-official-torture predicate, and the proved deliberate personal torture supports punitive availability here while amount and lawful assessment remain distinct questions.

**Authority:** Part IV of Stevens's opinion is joined in full by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices directly adopt its complete punitive-culpability formulation. Stone-Zsela joins the authority for punitive relief and its application to the proved deliberate personal torture, but does not join the formulation concerning equivalently reckless aggravation; seven directly support the deliberate-torture ground sustaining this judgment. Scalia and Thomas reject relief on this asserted action because they reject its source.

**Controlling explanation:** Punishment cannot be inferred from compensation alone. Arms distinguishes compensation from the traditional punitive response to aggravated personal wrongdoing. Filartiga's remedial decision supplies a persuasive application to official torture. The domestic action's common-law remedial character and that independently supported aggravated-tort remedy authorize punishment here; Franklin expressly left punitive authority open and does not answer this question. The concern that punitive relief has penal and international consequences warrants attention to the source, culpability and remedy, but does not eliminate an ordinary remedy for this established intentional personal tort. No sovereign or population is punished and no criminal conviction is entered. The Court does not import Filartiga's default, its assessment of a foreign prosecution, its choice-of-law findings or its dollar amounts. Each plaintiff's $300,000 punitive award survives the categorical authority objection. TXO and BMW retain their actual constraints and disagreements: this grant supplies no independent amount challenge, wealth finding, assessment formula, required ratio or certification of every possible calculation. The Court authorizes no universal punitive remedy for international wrongs.

**Precedent treatment:** Arms supplies the traditional distinction between reparation and punishment for aggravated wrongdoing; Franklin's punitive reservation remains intact and is not treated as authorization. TXO and BMW remain controlling at their existing procedural and substantive scopes; no new excessiveness test is announced.

#### V. The foreign setting does not make this individual tort a political question

**Holding and operative rule:** Adjudication of these individual official-torture claims is not categorically barred as a political question because it applies a determinate prohibition to personal conduct without deciding the legitimacy of Ethiopia's government, directing United States diplomacy or resolving a constitutionally committed war-powers dispute. A concrete immunity defense or contrary political-branch determination would require its own consideration; none is supplied or decided here.

**Authority:** Part V of Stevens's opinion, joined by all other participating Justices: nine direct joins. Scalia and Thomas's agreement does not supply an action or their support for the damages judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Baker requires a concrete constitutional commitment or obstacle to judicial decision, not merely a foreign setting. Montana and Nixon preserve that issue-specific inquiry. Courts can decide personal conduct under an established torture prohibition and apply ordinary remedial law without choosing a foreign government or supervising diplomacy. Sanchez-Espinoza concerned United States foreign-policy operations and domestic sovereign immunity; its express distinction of suits against foreign officials defeats using it as a categorical prohibition here. The Court does not decide a new immunity question or an unpresented defense. The absence of a political-question bar does not answer the independent source-of-action and remedy inquiries.

**Precedent treatment:** Baker's issue-specific inquiry, as retained in Montana and Nixon, is applied. Sanchez-Espinoza is distinguished at its domestic-immunity and United States foreign-policy setting; its result is not extended to this defendant.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Suter v. Artist M.: its separation of enforceable right, action and relief remains; its administrative-plan ruling does not foreclose this source-specific received action.
- FDIC v. Meyer: jurisdiction and consent remain distinct from a substantive action; no sovereign-immunity rule is enlarged.
- O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC: the rejection of a new receiver preference is distinguished from §1350's particular common-law function.
- Central Bank of Denver v. First Interstate Bank and Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance: their confined received-action reasoning supports analogy, without importing securities liability or independently establishing an ATS action.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools: appropriate remedies presuppose an action; compensation is supported at that scope, and punitive authority is independently decided here.
- Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms: its traditional distinction between compensation and punitive relief for aggravated wrongdoing informs the punitive rule.
- TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources and BMW of North America v. Gore: their actual procedural and substantive punitive limits remain unchanged; no new amount-review rule is adopted.
- Baker v. Carr, United States Department of Commerce v. Montana and Nixon v. United States: their concrete political-question inquiry is applied without a foreign-affairs category bar.
- Filartiga's Second Circuit and damages decisions supply persuasive support for this established official-torture action and its remedies. Sanchez-Espinoza's domestic-immunity and United States foreign-policy setting is distinguished, consistently with its express distinction of foreign-official suits. These lower-court decisions are not treated as prior Supreme Court holdings.

#### Law After Decision

Section 1350's forum grant remains distinct from an actionable norm, a domestic action and the permitted relief. The Court recognizes a confined received action for proved personal official torture, with compensatory relief and independently justified punitive authority. It does not make every customary-international-law norm privately enforceable, impose liability from official title, adjudicate a sovereign's immunity or authorize general supervision of foreign policy. The underlying tort still requires intentional infliction under official authority; the punitive aggravation formulation does not create a cause for merely reckless conduct.

### 1997-01-14 — Atherton v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, No. 95-928.

Authority: [Record](../records/Atherton_v_FDIC_merits_1997-01-14.md).

#### Holdings

#### I. Review of the source-of-law argument

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court may consider the argument that no federal common-law duty exists because it concerns the same restored federal claims and is included within the presented dispute over §1821(k)'s displacement or preservation of those claims. Both sides have addressed that legal question on the existing record; consideration introduces no separate claim, additional factual finding or different requested relief.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight Justices. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees that the merits should be reached but does not join this procedural explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court must distinguish a supporting legal argument from a separate question. Yee permits new arguments for a preserved claim but retains the independent limit imposed by the question presented. United States National Bank permits consideration of necessary governing law after the parties have a fair opportunity to address it. Lebron permits, without requiring, consideration of an included legal argument on an established record. Here the existence of the asserted duty is part of deciding what federal claim survives the statute. Izumi is different: its separate adverse intervention judgment had to be displaced before the presented question could be reached. Merits briefing alone does not enlarge the grant, and the withdrawal of the state claims remains a separate procedural matter. This confined exercise of review creates no universal exception for antecedent questions.

**Precedent treatment:** Yee's argument/question distinction, United States National Bank's governing-law authority and Lebron's discretionary included-argument rule are applied within their limits; Izumi's separate-judgment limit is distinguished and preserved.

#### II. State law supplies the ordinary corporate duty

**Holding and operative rule:** State law supplies the ordinary standard of care for these acquired corporate-governance claims against officers and directors of a federally chartered savings association unless enacted federal law or a specifically demonstrated significant conflict with an identified federal policy displaces it. Federal chartering, insurance, receivership recovery and general administrative uniformity do not establish that conflict here.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by all other participating Justices: nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** O'Melveny applies Erie's rejection of general federal common law and Wallis's requirement of a particular significant conflict with federal policy. Its independently sufficient pre-FIRREA ground does not depend on applying the later statute to earlier conduct. The asserted duties concern lending decisions, fiduciary conduct and corporate governance, subjects ordinarily governed by state law. The receiver identifies no charter command or operational obligation requiring a different rule. Kimbell Foods distinguishes rights created by federal programs and rejects automatic national uniformity even there. A desire to increase recoveries cannot supply the missing conflict. New Jersey law therefore supplies the ordinary duty for any properly available state theory, subject to federal displacement actually established. The Court neither selects New Jersey's precise standard or business-judgment rule nor decides breach, causation, defenses or liability. Congress or a valid federal regulation may prescribe a specific duty; federal enforcement of distinct statutory obligations is not decided.

**Precedent treatment:** O'Melveny's acquired-claim and specific-conflict rules are applied to these corporate duties; Erie and Wallis retain their general-law and conflict limits; Boyle's exceptional-displacement requirements remain available; Kimbell Foods supplies no automatic uniform federal rule.

#### III. Section 1821(k) establishes a federal minimum and preserves other rights

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1821(k), when applicable, permits personal monetary liability of a director or officer of an insured depository institution, whether federally or state chartered, for gross negligence, similar conduct or conduct demonstrating a greater disregard of duty, including intentional tortious conduct, with those culpability terms defined under applicable state law; the action must be by, on behalf of, or at the request or direction of the Corporation, prosecuted wholly or partly for its benefit, with the Corporation acting as receiver or conservator, on a claim purchased from, assigned by or otherwise conveyed by such a receiver or conservator, or on a claim purchased from, assigned by or otherwise conveyed wholly or partly by an insured institution or its affiliate in connection with §1823 assistance. That authorization defeats a more protective state liability shield but preserves rights actually available under other applicable law, including a state duty permitting recovery for less culpable conduct: gross negligence is a federal floor, not a ceiling.

**Authority:** Part III of Scalia's opinion, joined by all other participating Justices: nine direct joins to the textual construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The enacted provision describes insured institutions without excluding federal charters. Its final sentence preserves other applicable rights; restricting that sentence to other provisions of the same federal enactment would deny its ordinary breadth. State law imposing an ordinary-negligence duty can therefore remain effective. Preservation of other law does not manufacture a federal common-law action or restore a withdrawn pleading. The section's authorization, the three routes by which the Corporation acts, the state-law definitions of culpability and the savings sentence each retain their separate work. Permission to impose liability does not establish breach, causation, the absence of defenses or an amount due. The text resolves the construction without reliance on legislative history. O'Melveny's rejection of an additional receiver preference supplies the source-of-law boundary; it did not previously decide this provision's minimum or savings rule. The section's temporal application is unnecessary to resolve the independently defective federal-common-law premise and remains open.

**Precedent treatment:** O'Melveny's conditional FIRREA analysis is preserved without converting it into a retroactivity decision; its source-of-law rule supplies no additional federal action. Landgraf's provision-specific temporal inquiry remains available when application to earlier conduct must actually be decided.

#### IV. Vacatur requires further proceedings under the correct sources

**Holding and operative rule:** The Third Circuit judgment is vacated because it restored the asserted general federal-common-law theories and excluded federally chartered institutions from §1821(k). On remand the lower courts must apply the state-law starting point and the federal provision at its lawful scope, resolve properly available claims and defenses, and determine any liability and relief without treating this decision as reinstatement of withdrawn state claims.

**Authority:** Part IV of Scalia's opinion, joined by all other participating Justices: nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The appellate court reserved the content of its proposed federal standard, including ordinary versus gross negligence. Its judgment is not an adjudication of ordinary-negligence liability. The statutory pleading remains for ordinary adjudication. State claims were treated as withdrawn below; the effect of that treatment, permissible amendment and any properly available state theory remain for the lower courts. Neither the savings sentence nor correction of the federal source resurrects those claims. O'Melveny distinguishes correction of an erroneous federal rule from automatic reinstatement of an earlier judgment. Its same distinction supports this bounded remand. The Court decides neither the precise New Jersey standard nor FIRREA's temporal application, and awards no damages.

**Precedent treatment:** O'Melveny's separation of the governing-law correction from final relief is applied; no liability or temporal rule is added by the mandate.

#### Precedent Treatment

- O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC: its state-law starting point, specific-conflict requirement and separation of source correction from ultimate relief govern; the §1821(k) floor and savings construction is decided here. Its conditional FIRREA analysis remains conditional.
- Erie Railroad v. Tompkins: the absence of general federal common law remains the starting point.
- Wallis v. Pan American Petroleum Corp. and Boyle v. United Technologies Corp.: exceptional displacement still requires the identified federal interest and significant conflict; no such conflict is established here.
- United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.: federal program rights are distinguished, and general uniformity alone remains insufficient.
- Yee v. City of Escondido: supporting arguments and separate questions remain distinct.
- United States National Bank of Oregon v. Independent Insurance Agents: necessary governing law may be considered after a fair opportunity to address it.
- Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.: the Court applies its discretionary included-argument rule without erasing the question-presented limit.
- Izumi Seimitsu v. U.S. Philips Corp.: the separate intervention judgment there distinguishes it from this included source argument.
- Landgraf v. USI Film Products: its provision-specific temporal inquiry remains unchanged; retrospective application is not decided.

#### Law After Decision

The ordinary duty rules for these federally chartered institutions remain state supplied unless valid federal displacement is shown. Section 1821(k) applies to insured institutions of either charter and preserves stricter applicable state duties while establishing its own minimum authorization. Federal chartering and receivership do not alone create a general federal negligence action. The content and procedural availability of New Jersey claims remain open.
