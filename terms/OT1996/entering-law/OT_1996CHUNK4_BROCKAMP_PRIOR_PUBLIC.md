### Commissioner v. Lundy, No. 94-1785

Merits decision, January 17, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

516 U.S. 235. Argued October 31, 1995. On certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, 45 F.3d 856. Lundy had filed no return when the Commissioner mailed a deficiency notice on September 26, 1990. His withholding was deemed paid April 15, 1988; he filed his return later that December. The question is whether the Tax Court may refund that withholding under the hypothetical-claim provision of 26 U.S.C. §6512(b)(3)(B).

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Thomas.

Section 6512(b)(3)(B) does not authorize refund of these withholding payments. The Tax Court may adjudicate the deficiency and remaining computations consistently with that limit; the Court fixes no refund amount or separate remedy.

#### Opinions and Holdings

O'Connor writes for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Thomas dissents, joined by Stevens. The seven-Justice Court opinion controls the rule and explanation below.

##### The notice-date hypothetical selects and measures the payment window

**Operative rule:** Under §6512(b)(3)(B), a hypothetical refund claim filed on the deficiency-notice date both selects and measures the applicable §6511(b)(2) payment lookback. For the ordinary withholding claim here, no return existed at that date, so the two-year window applies; a later return cannot bring earlier payments into that window.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 6511(a) ordinarily requires a claim within three years after filing the return or two years after payment, whichever expires later; if no return is filed, it uses two years after payment. Section 6511(b)(2)(A) limits a claim filed within the three-year period to payments within three years, plus any filing-extension period, before the claim. Subparagraph (B) uses two years for a claim outside that period. Section 6512(b)(3)(B) directs the Tax Court to apply those payment limits as if a claim stating the overpayment ground had been filed at the notice date, whether or not an actual claim was filed.

The hypothetical supplies a claim, not a return. On September 26, 1990, Lundy had filed no return; the hypothetical claim therefore did not fall within a return-based three-year period. His April 15, 1988 payments lie outside the resulting two-year window. Treating December's return as already present in September would change the counterfactual Congress selected.

The contrary construction would preserve a later effective real claim as an alternative. But it separates the choice of period from the notice-date instruction governing the incorporated calculation. A possible advantage in another refund forum does not authorize rewriting this mechanism. United States v. Dalm preserves the connection between refund authority and statutory conditions; United States v. Williams does not remove applicable timing limits when recognizing an eligible payer. Reich's protection of a promised state postpayment remedy supplies no different federal lookback.

This conclusion concerns the ordinary subparagraph (B) route presented. No filing extension, assessment-extension agreement, special-period item, or other statutory route is established. The Court decides neither every nonfiler's refund rights nor whether every tax deadline has the same jurisdictional character.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596:** Applied for the connection between refund authority and its statutory conditions, without classifying every tax limit alike.
- **United States v. Williams, 514 U.S. 527:** Its express-remedy and payer-eligibility holdings leave applicable timing requirements intact; it does not decide the notice-date hypothetical.
- **Reich v. Collins, 513 U.S. 106:** Its protection against withdrawal of a clearly held-out state postpayment remedy remains; it supplies no power to change this federal payment window.

#### Law After Decision

For this ordinary §6512(b)(3)(B) claim, the notice-date hypothetical governs both selection and measurement; the later return does not cure the payment-window defect. Deficiency jurisdiction remains distinct from refund authority. Subparagraph (A)'s treatment of payments after notice and subparagraph (C)'s treatment of qualifying actual claims filed before notice remain separate. No qualifying alternative is established here, and other special periods are not adjudicated.

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by Stevens.** They would affirm consideration of the refund under the actual claim filed with the later return within three years of payment. In their reading, Congress created a hypothetical claim to protect taxpayers who had not made one; it did not direct courts to disregard a real, otherwise effective claim. The incorporated refund provisions can therefore operate through either the actual claim or the protective hypothetical. The majority's notice-date reading turns a benefit into an exclusive restriction without express language requiring that result.

This is a statutory disagreement, not an equitable exception. Dalm's timing conditions remain enforceable, and Williams supports using a remedy Congress actually provided. They would not require an actual claim in every Tax Court case, manufacture a return at the notice date, or give every delinquent filer a three-year window. Their ground concerns the real claim actually filed within three years of payment; the amount recoverable remains subject to ordinary computation.

#### Source Notes

The [official report, 516 U.S. 235–263](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-516/pdf/USREPORTS-516-235.pdf) and the [Fourth Circuit opinion](https://openjurist.org/45/f3d/856/lundy-v-commissioner-of-internal-revenue) supply the filings, payment dates, and judgment below. The [1994 United States Code, title 26](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title26/html/USCODE-1994-title26.htm), §§6511–6513, supplies the governing text. The return's requested amount and the correspondence do not establish a final refund entitlement or binding settlement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit must proceed consistently with the Tax Court's inability to refund these identified withholding payments under §6512(b)(3)(B). Remaining deficiency and tax computations are not displaced. No fixed refund, damages, interest, fees, or additional hearing is ordered.

**End of entry: Commissioner v. Lundy, Merits decision, January 17, 1996.**

### Henderson v. United States, No. 95-232

Merits decision, May 20, 1996

517 U.S. 654. Argued March 19, 1996; October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fifth Circuit, 51 F.3d 574, which affirmed dismissal without prejudice under the Suits in Admiralty Act's direction to serve the complaint forthwith. The questions are whether Rule 4 displaces that timetable and, alternatively, whether the statutory period may be extended.

Henderson filed on April 8, 1993, after administrative disallowance, alleging injury on or about August 27, 1991 aboard the United States-owned DELMONTE. His negligence, unseaworthiness, maintenance, cure and damages claims remain unadjudicated. The Attorney General received the mailed papers May 25, day 47. The original 120-day period expired August 6. The district court signed a fifteen-day service extension August 31; it was filed September 1, and the United States Attorney was served September 3, day 148. The order contains no express finding of good cause, diligence or clerk fault. Counsel's account of problems with forms, seals and mail and the Government's challenge to his diligence remain opposed submissions.

The Government sought dismissal under §742, without a distinct Rule 4 dismissal ground, and described service as properly effected September 3. The district court dismissed under Rule 12(b)(1) in June 1994. The Fifth Circuit treated forthwith service as a jurisdictional condition of consent applying to both recipients and held 148 days insufficient even assuming good cause and a reasonable-time construction. It did not decide whether day-47 receipt independently satisfied the statute. Dismissal without prejudice did not establish that a new suit would be timely.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Justices O'Connor and Thomas, who would affirm dismissal under the surviving statutory service condition.

The action was timely commenced by filing. Neither day-148 service nor day-47 receipt can sustain dismissal under the displaced statutory timetable.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice Scalia delivered the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven Justices directly adopted each holding and explanation below. Justice Thomas dissented, joined throughout by Justice O'Connor.

##### I. The service direction implements an existing consent to suit

**Holding and authority.** Part I holds that §742's direction to serve the filed complaint forthwith regulates procedure in an already authorized Suits in Admiralty Act action. It does not independently condition this waiver of sovereign immunity or remove subject-matter jurisdiction. Congress may make a procedural requirement a jurisdictional condition of consent, but this timetable's placement, function and surrounding provisions establish that Congress did not do so here.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 742 first authorizes the specified nonjury in-personam admiralty proceeding against the United States. Its later sentences direct delivery to the United States Attorney, registered mailing to the Attorney General and a sworn return. These steps notify the designated recipients of the filed action. Consent, venue, service and transfer perform separate functions within the section; proximity to the waiver does not make every direction jurisdictional.

Section 743's instruction to follow principles and practice applicable between private parties reinforces this procedural reading. It does not erase that section's separate prescriptions concerning costs, interest, admiralty review, in-rem principles concerning proceedings against property, or government security. Nor does procedure's subject alone settle the question: Congress may attach jurisdictional consequences to procedure. This holding interprets the particular scheme and announces no categorical immunity exception.

The Government argues that placing the service command in §742, apart from §743's general practice provision, specially conditions consent. That structure gives the service directions work without making their timetable a substantive waiver limit. *Nordic Village* requires clear consent to the particular monetary remedy and distinguishes consent from general jurisdiction; this decision supplies no new monetary remedy. *McNeil* enforces a prefiling exhaustion sequence, and *Keene* a filing-time jurisdictional bar. Neither determines this postfiling direction's function. *Carlisle* likewise distinguishes procedural restrictions from subject-matter jurisdiction without classifying all procedural provisions alike.

The two-year commencement limit and substantive boundaries of consent remain intact. Whether the timetable survives as a nonjurisdictional command is the separate question addressed in Part II.

##### II. Congress's later Rule 4 scheme displaces the competing timetable

**Holding and authority.** Part II holds that, in this timely commenced domestic Suits in Admiralty Act action, the applicable Rule 4 timing scheme, including authorized enlargement, governs service. Section 742 cannot independently require dismissal for failure to serve sooner. Congress's direct enactment of former Rule 4(j) displaces the incompatible procedural timetable without enlarging substantive consent or dispensing with the designated-recipient requirements.

**Controlling explanation.** Congress enacted former Rule 4(j) in Pub. L. 97-462. It required dismissal without prejudice when service was not made within 120 days and good cause was not shown, on motion or on the court's initiative after notice; foreign service under former subdivision (i) was excepted. This extendable scheme governs the same postfiling service event as §742.

The Government argues that prompt service satisfies both texts and that a general outer deadline does not repeal a special command. But physical compliance is not the whole inquiry. A separately fatal, shorter timetable would deny the service allowance and authorized enlargement the later scheme supplies. Former Rule 4(a)'s prompt-service responsibility creates no such independent statutory dismissal condition; the holding nevertheless permits no deliberate disregard of service responsibilities.

Section 2072 authorizes procedural regulation, protects substantive rights and addresses conflicting procedural laws. Its framework supports this construction, but its clause concerning rules prescribed under the Act does not directly nullify earlier law through a rule Congress itself enacted. The operative route is the later congressional enactment, construed with this specific conflict. The Court adopts no second independently sufficient ground based on Court promulgation. Rule 82 is respected because neither substantive consent nor jurisdiction is enlarged.

*Hanna* and *Mississippi Publishing Corp. v. Murphree* support treating service mechanisms as procedure; they do not replace the conflict inquiry. *Walker* and *Ragan* preserve a state-created right's distinct state-law commencement condition: Rule 3's filing provision did not address that different limitations question. Here both provisions govern the same service event. Section 743 reinforces limited displacement without erasing other special Suits in Admiralty Act provisions.

The holding concerns timing, not every service statute, all government/private procedural differences or an unpresented registered-versus-certified-mail dispute. The 1993 amendment's qualified pending-case application remains governed by Part III.

##### III. Remand removes the statutory dismissal without deciding a new service dispute

**Holding and authority.** Part III requires reversal of the statutory dismissal as to both designated recipients and application of the governing Federal Rules to any properly presented issue in ordinary further proceedings. The decision neither validates the existing extension nor orders a new service hearing. Any necessary application of amended Rule 4 must honor its express pending-case qualification.

**Controlling explanation.** September 3 fell outside the original 120 days but within the district court's fifteen-day direction. No distinct Rule 4 defect supplied the dismissal under review. The absence of express good-cause findings does not itself invalidate the order or require renewed factfinding. Any genuinely available later objection remains subject to its own authority, preservation and procedural conditions. Neither recipient's timing sustains the displaced §742 ground; whether either service was forthwith need not be decided.

Former Rule 4(j) governed the April–September 1993 acts. The April 22, 1993 order made amended Rule 4 effective December 1 for new cases and, insofar as just and practicable, proceedings in then-pending cases. That direction controls if a properly presented issue concerning the 1994 dismissal or further proceedings requires choosing the applicable Rule. The Court does not automatically relabel the 1993 order or hold that amended Rule 4(m) necessarily supplies relief.

Under Rule 4(m), after notice, the court must dismiss without prejudice or direct service within a specified time when service exceeds 120 days; good cause requires an appropriate extension. Without good cause, enlargement is permitted, not compelled. Foreign service under subdivisions (f) and (j)(1) is excepted. A limitations bar may support discretion but does not require relief. *Pioneer* preserves flexibility authorized by the governing rule, not a Suits in Admiralty Act tolling power. *Landgraf* requires attention to express temporal direction and does not automatically reopen completed acts.

The district-court Rule 12 waiver contention supplies no independently sufficient reversal ground: the transfer motion's legal effect and appellate preservation are not established. No new waiver ruling, negligence finding, maintenance or cure award, damages judgment, immediate trial entitlement or separate due-process decision follows. Section 745's two-year limit and narrowly dated 1950 savings provision create no general new refiling opportunity.

#### Precedent treatment

- *United States v. Nordic Village, Inc.*, 503 U.S. 30, retains its requirement of clear consent to the actual monetary remedy and its distinction between consent and jurisdiction. Its original §106(c) construction does not classify this service timetable.
- *McNeil v. United States*, 508 U.S. 106, concerns prefiling administrative exhaustion. That statutory sequence and its express alternatives and exceptions remain unchanged.
- *Keene Corp. v. United States*, 508 U.S. 200, concerns a particular filing-time bar; later service presents a different operative event.
- *Carlisle v. United States*, April 29, 1996, supplies the distinction between procedural authority and subject-matter jurisdiction, while preserving rule-consistent discretion, express transition limits and independently supported avenues of relief.
- *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460, supplies recognition of federal service procedure; actual conflict and lawful authority remain necessary.
- *Mississippi Publishing Corp. v. Murphree*, 326 U.S. 438, supports service's procedural role in implementing an existing right without enlarging substantive rights or jurisdiction.
- *Walker v. Armco Steel Corp.*, 446 U.S. 740, concerns state limitations and commencement, unlike the competing provisions governing the same federal postfiling service event here.
- *Ragan v. Merchants Transfer & Warehouse Co.*, 337 U.S. 530, preserves the state-law commencement condition for the state-created right. Rule 3 does not universally displace such conditions.
- *Pioneer Investment Services Co. v. Brunswick Associates Limited Partnership*, 507 U.S. 380, preserves rule-specific enlargement authority and its limits. It supplies neither former Rule 4's good-cause test nor a general extension of Suits in Admiralty Act conditions.
- *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, requires respect for express temporal direction. Neither a pending case nor procedural characterization automatically reopens completed acts.

No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The Fifth Circuit's premise that the forthwith-service timetable remains a jurisdictional condition enforceable independently of Rule 4 is rejected.

#### Law after the decision

Effective May 20, 1996, §742's competing forthwith timetable cannot defeat service allowed by the governing Rule 4 scheme in a timely commenced domestic Suits in Admiralty Act action. This procedural direction does not define substantive consent. Congress retains power to impose jurisdictional procedural conditions, and an enacted command requires a lawful superseding source for displacement.

The designated-recipient requirements, §745's two-year commencement period, substantive waiver limits and compliance with the applicable Rules remain. The extension's validity, other possible service issues, the Clarification Act/PVA classification, maritime liability and relief are undecided. The decision neither extends a surviving statutory deadline nor creates a general refiling period. Further use of amended Rule 4 remains subject to the express just-and-practicable transition and the distinction between permitted and mandatory enlargement.

#### Dissent

Justice Thomas's dissent would affirm. Congress may make procedure a condition of sovereign consent, and the dissent reads §742's particular service command, separately from §743's general private-party practice instruction, as doing so here. *Sherwood*, *Block* and *Nordic Village* require respect for the actual scope of consent; the argument is not that every sentence in a waiver statute is jurisdictional. *Meyer*'s broad agency clause and *Carlisle*'s criminal procedural mechanism do not determine the differently structured Suits in Admiralty Act waiver.

A judicial rule cannot enlarge that consent under §2072(b) and Rule 82. Congress enacted former Rule 4(j) and could change the waiver, but its general dismissal timetable does not sufficiently establish repeal of the retained special condition. Prompt service can satisfy both, and general authorized enlargement need not displace the special statute. The 1993 transition supplies no independent substantive enlargement.

Service on the United States Attorney after 148 days fails the surviving requirement even under a reasonable-dispatch reading and assuming good cause. The Rule 4 extension does not extend that separate condition. No universal number of days, adverse diligence finding or conclusion about day-47 Attorney General receipt is required. *Pioneer* and the *American Pipe*/*McCormick* analogy establish no general authority to extend this consent condition. The dissent creates no new Rule 4 defect and decides neither injury merits, separate due process nor a right to timely refiling. The unestablished Rule 12 waiver route supplies no alternative reversal of the jurisdictional condition. These propositions are noncontrolling.

#### Source notes

The [filed petition, joint appendix and merits submissions in No. 95-232](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0641) supply the procedural record and competing arguments. Petition Appendix 1–9 reproduces the lower decisions; Joint Appendix page 15 contains the fifteen-day order without express good-cause findings. The complaint's injury date remains an allegation; inconsistent incidental recitations establish no different merits finding. The actual earlier venue-transfer motion and appellate preservation needed for a dispositive Rule 12 waiver ruling are not established here.

The Suits in Admiralty Act, 41 Stat. 525, its 1960 amendment, 74 Stat. 912, and Pub. L. 97-462 supply the statutory language and Congress's direct adoption of former Rule 4(j). The [April 22, 1993 order and Rule text, 507 U.S. 1091, 1103–1110](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/507bv.pdf), supply the effective date and qualified application to pending proceedings. The 1993 subdivision (m) note distinguishes discretionary relief from good-cause enlargement. Section 745's amendment history preserves its dated 1950 savings provision; it supplies no general extension for present refiling.

#### Mandate, remedy, and stage

The Fifth Circuit's judgment affirming dismissal under §742's forthwith-service direction is reversed. The case returns through that court for ordinary further proceedings consistent with Parts I–III. The displaced statutory timetable cannot again sustain dismissal. Further proceedings must apply the Federal Rules to any genuinely available service issue and to the injury claims. Any genuine Rule 4 question must meet its own authority, preservation and procedural conditions, with the 1993 transition applied if material.

The Court creates no new service objection and orders no new good-cause hearing, favorable extension decision, immediate merits trial or relief on the merits. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures; no later Supreme Court event is prescribed.

**End of entry: Henderson v. United States, merits decision, May 20, 1996.**

### United States v. Williams, No. 94-395

Merits decision, April 25, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 22, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing Williams v. United States, 24 F.3d 1143 (May 19, 1994). That court reversed a district-court dismissal for lack of refund jurisdiction and remanded. The question is whether 28 U.S.C. §1346(a)(1) permits Williams to sue for money paid to remove federal tax liens from property she claimed as her own when she was not personally liable for the taxes assessed against Rabin. The Government seeks jurisdictional dismissal; Williams seeks permission to proceed with her refund suit.

Williams paid through escrow to free the house she was selling from liens asserted for Rabin's taxes, timely claimed a refund administratively, and sued after denial. No levy or substitute-fund agreement is established. The lower judgment did not award a refund. The parties dispute lien validity, the scope of an asserted concession, and the amount and components of payment; those disputes are not resolved by the jurisdictional ruling.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit's jurisdictional judgment, 7–2. Williams's refund action may proceed under the ordinary administrative-claim, merits, and proof requirements. The Court awards no refund and fixes no amount.

| Statutory availability of Williams's refund action | Affirm jurisdiction, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Kennedy, Thomas | The Ninth Circuit's remand for further proceedings remains in force; no monetary award. The dissent would restore the jurisdictional dismissal. |

The merits of any recovery are not a separate disposition resolved here. The Court leaves lien validity and priority, any binding concession, wrongfulness, proof, and amount for further proceedings as necessary.

Both affirmative grounds control through their own named majorities. Neither Stone-Zsela's nor Scalia's agreement with the judgment supplies a vote for the independent definitional alternative.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Express refund consent includes this lien-backed collection claim

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1346(a)(1) permits a refund suit by a person who, although not personally liable for the assessment against another, paid under the coercive force of a federal tax lien to free property claimed as her own and alleges that the Government wrongfully collected that payment. Section 7422(a)'s duly filed administrative claim and §6511's applicable timing requirements remain mandatory; §6511's taxpayer terminology does not implicitly exclude this timely collection claim from the express waiver.

**Authority:** Justice Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined on this ground by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Breyer: seven direct votes for the stated plaintiff category and jurisdiction-only disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted refund waiver reaches Williams's claim about money collected from her through liens asserted against her property. Section 1346(a)(1) expressly covers wrongful collection as well as erroneous assessment. Nordic Village requires clear monetary consent; this statute supplies it, rather than leaving the remedy to general jurisdiction or fairness. Dalm requires reading the waiver with the administrative-claim and timing provisions, and Williams duly presented a timely claim. Those provisions do not silently remove this collection claim merely because the assessment named someone else. The Government's integrated reading gives the timing provision a claimant restriction that the express waiver does not impose here. Stahmann supports distinguishing a payment to free one's goods from an unsolicited payment for another, while its different tax incidence and collector-action setting prevent treating it as a construction of these statutes. The holding permits adjudication of the alleged wrongful collection. It does not find the liens invalid, fix an amount, excuse an untimely claim, or authorize strangers' and volunteers' suits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., February 25, 1992: applies its demand for unequivocal monetary consent and distinguishes its text permitting nonmonetary readings; the present refund waiver expressly authorizes monetary recovery.
- United States v. Dalm, 494 U.S. 596 (1990): applies its connected claim-and-timing requirements, while distinguishing its untimely action from this timely nonassessed claimant.
- United States v. California, April 26, 1993: preserves its refusal to create a common-law refund remedy; this action rests on the express congressional remedy it left open.
- Stahmann v. Vidal, 305 U.S. 61 (1938): applies its distinction between collection from one's property and a volunteered payment, while retaining its different statutory incidence and collector-action limits.

Section 6511(a) allows, for a tax requiring a return, a claim within three years of filing the return or two years of payment, whichever expires later; if no return was filed, the period is two years from payment. Its separate stamp-tax clause allows three years from payment. No lateness is alleged here. Section 7422(b) permits suit whether or not payment was under protest or duress. The coercive lien and property connection identify the facts and limited plaintiff category decided; they do not create a universal protest or duress prerequisite or decide every other claimant's eligibility.

##### Williams also falls within taxpayer for these refund provisions

**Controlling proposition:** Independently, a person whose claimed property and own payment are subjected to the lien-backed tax collection presented here is a person subject to an internal-revenue tax within §7701(a)(14) for the connected refund provisions, even though she is not personally liable for the assessment. This construction allows her timely administrative refund claim; it neither makes every payer a taxpayer for all Code purposes nor adjudicates the debt or lien's validity.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's separately identified alternative holding, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, and Breyer: five direct votes. Stone-Zsela and Scalia do not join or decide this independent definitional ground. Kennedy and Thomas reject it. The alternative controls through its own five-Justice coalition, without combining distinct opinions under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds, as an independent ground, that the connected taxpayer provisions include Williams in the limited collection setting before it. Section 7701(a)(14) speaks of a person subject to an internal-revenue tax, and the asserted federal liens subjected property she claimed and her own payment to collection. Personal assessment and subjection to that collection are not identical. The Government correctly insists, under Dalm, that §7422's duly filed claim requirement and §6511 be read together; the Court does so without treating the refund machinery as available only to the assessed debtor. Nordic Village's clarity requirement remains satisfied by the express monetary waiver read with this limited definition. The definitions govern title 26 unless otherwise distinctly expressed or manifestly incompatible with statutory intent; this construction concerns these connected refund provisions rather than an unexamined transfer of every Code definition into title 28. The Court does not decide that any payment creates taxpayer status everywhere, that the underlying assessment may be challenged on every ground, or that Williams has proved a refund entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dalm: applies the integrated treatment of waiver, claim, and timing provisions without converting its late-claim holding into a decision about this plaintiff category.
- Nordic Village: applies clear-consent analysis to the actual refund text and its connected definition; no general jurisdictional or equitable label supplies consent.

##### Distinct third-party mechanisms do not foreclose this refund action

**Controlling proposition:** The cited quiet-title, wrongful-levy, and substitute-fund provisions do not make those mechanisms exclusive of this §1346(a)(1) collection claim or require their exhaustion before her duly claimed refund suit. Their own triggers and restrictions remain in force: a lien is not a levy, discharge into a substitute fund is permissive and requires an agreement, and the fund remedies presuppose the corresponding fund.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, joined on this construction by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Breyer: seven direct votes. This ground answers the asserted statutory obstacle; remedial hardship does not independently waive immunity.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government's other cited remedies do not withdraw the express refund route for this claim. Section 2410 permits quiet-title proceedings against a claimed federal lien; it does not automatically become a refund action after payment. Section 7426(a)(1) requires a levy, and none occurred here. Section 6325(b)(3) permits the Secretary to discharge sold property upon an agreement holding proceeds as a fund with the same lien priority. It does not command an agreement or prove that Williams obtained one. Sections 7426(a)(3) and (b)(4) address rights and recovery in an actual substitute fund. Section 7426(c) conclusively presumes the assessment valid in proceedings under that section; this distinct restriction is neither erased nor silently transferred to §1346. Dalm preserves the refund claim prerequisite, but supplies no duty to exhaust different proceedings. California forbids creating a common-law remedy; the present suit uses enacted consent. The Court need not resolve disputes about advice, knowledge, or administrative willingness to recognize the statutes' distinct operation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dalm: preserves the statutory refund-claim requirement without adding an exhaustion duty for quiet-title, levy, or substitute-fund proceedings.
- United States v. California: preserves the distinction between an express remedy and one newly created by a court; the practical limitations of other remedies corroborate statutory construction but do not create consent.

The holding is jurisdictional. Lien validity and priority, purchaser status and consideration, the effect and scope of any binding Government concession, wrongfulness of collection, payment components, and recoverable amount remain for further proceedings as necessary. No interest, fees, damages, or unrestricted challenge to Rabin's underlying assessment is awarded or authorized by this decision.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Nordic Village's unequivocal-consent rule remains intact and is satisfied by the express refund text at the stated scope. Dalm's administrative-claim and timing conditions remain binding; its late-claim holding did not decide this timely nonassessed payer. United States v. California retains its bar against manufacturing a common-law refund right and is distinguished by the express remedy here. Stahmann supports treating payment to free one's own goods as different from a volunteered payment, while its tax-incidence and collector-action setting remain material limits. No precedent is overruled, and no general equitable waiver is created.

The express refund waiver permits the stated lien-backed collection claim after satisfaction of the administrative-claim and timing rules. Independently, the five-Justice taxpayer-definition holding includes this collection relationship within the connected refund provisions. The third-party remedies retain their separate statutory triggers and do not foreclose this suit or add an exhaustion step. Jurisdiction does not establish wrongful collection or entitlement to any money. Volunteers, strangers, universal taxpayer status, and an unrestricted challenge to another person's assessment remain outside the holdings.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia concurs, joining the Court's express-waiver and connected timing analysis and its treatment of the distinct third-party remedies. Nordic Village requires clarity in the scope as well as the existence of a monetary waiver. Here the express wrongful-collection language supplies that consent, and the remote timing provision cannot plausibly impose the significant implicit claimant exclusion urged by the Government. He withholds the independently sufficient conclusion that Williams is a taxpayer under §§6511 and 7701 because that alternative is unnecessary to his disposition. He expresses no contrary view on that definition. Ordinary administrative-claim and timing requirements remain effective, and he joins no equitable exception to sovereign immunity.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the Court's primary ground and judgment. The collection was directed against Williams through a lien on property she claimed, and her payment therefore gives her a concrete collection injury even though another person was assessed. The levy, quiet-title, and substitute-fund mechanisms did not provide an adequate route in these circumstances: no levy occurred, quiet title did not furnish a realistic postpayment recovery, and a discretionary discharge requiring an agreement was not an assured substitute for refund review. He treats that remedial setting as confirmation of the enacted waiver's application, not as an equitable enlargement of consent. His ground remains confined to a compelled payment connected with the claimant's property, leaving volunteers and strangers outside the holding. He does not adopt the separate taxpayer-definition alternative. The refund suit proceeds subject to the administrative claim, merits, and proof requirements, without a present monetary award. His additional explanation does not create a general adequacy-of-remedy test.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice Thomas, dissents. Dalm requires the broad refund language to be read with the duly filed claim requirement, the timing provision's taxpayer language, and §7701's definition. In their view those connected provisions do not unequivocally authorize a person concededly not liable for the tax to invoke the taxpayer refund action. Wrongful collection and personal liability are distinct, but collection against claimed property does not itself make that person the taxpayer Congress identified. Nordic Village requires clear consent for the requested remedy and its scope; inconvenience cannot supply it. The specially drawn third-party statutes reinforce that reading without proving that any alternative actually was available to Williams or had to be exhausted. The dissent does not call a lien a levy, treat a permitted substitute-fund agreement as mandatory, or decide lien validity. Stahmann involved a statute intended to burden the producers and an action against the collector; it does not settle this different waiver. The two would reverse the jurisdictional judgment and restore dismissal of this refund action, leaving other lawfully authorized remedies and the merits unadjudicated. Their claimant construction is noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand remain in force. The district court may adjudicate the refund action under the ordinary claim, merits, and proof requirements, resolving lien validity and priority, any binding concession, wrongfulness, and amount as necessary. The Court orders no immediate payment, interest, fees, or damages and predicts no merits result.
