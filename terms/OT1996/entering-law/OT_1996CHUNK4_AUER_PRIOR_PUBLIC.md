### Thomas Jefferson University v. Shalala, No. 93-120

Merits decision, June 24, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

512 U.S. 504. Argued April 18, 1994; decided June 24, 1994. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit. The questions concern the Secretary's construction of Medicare's educational-cost redistribution provision and the separate community-support ground for denying reimbursement.

The university's teaching hospital operates an approved clinical graduate-medical-education program through affiliated medical-college faculty. A later study allocated to the hospital nonsalary categories previously borne by the college. The claim for the year ending June 30, 1985 totaled $8,806,205 in educational and physician-administrative costs; the original disputed difference was $2,861,247. Particular faculty-compensation and physician-administrative amounts were settled below, so that difference is not a determination of the amount now remaining. The PRRB allowed the documented costs on November 17, 1989; the Administrator modified that decision on January 18, 1990, invoking redistribution, community support and a separate general-administrative allowability ground. The Eastern District of Pennsylvania upheld the denial in a May 1, 1992 memorandum and order, entered May 4. The Third Circuit affirmed without opinion on April 21, 1993, reported at 993 F.2d 879. The hospital seeks reversal and reimbursement; the Secretary seeks affirmance.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. No nonparticipation is recorded.

#### Judgment

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed, 5–4, on the redistribution ground alone.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Challenged educational-cost reimbursement denial | Affirm, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Thomas, Ginsburg | The denial remains in place on redistribution alone; no additional reimbursement or remand is ordered. |

The separate community-support rationale and the Administrator's additional university general-and-administrative-cost ruling are not independently sustained or rejected. The dissenters would reverse the judgment sustaining both §413.85(c) exclusions and remand for lawful review of remaining allowability, with any necessary agency proceedings; they would not order full payment. The settled faculty-compensation amounts and the unadjudicated alternative enforcement claim remain untouched.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Souter | Affirm | Part I: governing own-regulation standard and redistribution construction; Part II: consistency, actual agency ground and application; Part III: bounded affirmance and reservations. All join fully. |
| Dissent | Thomas | Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg | Would reverse and remand | Part I: nonoperative-subsection framework; Part II-A: alternative contextual redistribution construction; Part II-B: alternative net-cost/community-support construction; Part III: remaining-allowability remand. All join fully. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The redistribution provision reasonably limits newly shifted educational costs

**Controlling proposition:** Under the governing rule giving an administering agency's interpretation of its own regulation controlling weight unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with that regulation, the Secretary reasonably construes 42 C.F.R. §413.85(c) to bar increased reimbursement resulting from transferring previously medical-college-borne educational costs to the hospital's patient-care account, even when the underlying clinical education is generally eligible. That construction sustains the challenged denial for the reporting year ending June 30, 1985; it does not establish a universal historic-dollar ceiling, make every newly measured expense ineligible, or decide the separate community-support ground.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Scalia and Souter. Five participating Justices directly adopt this regulatory construction, its application and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that eligibility of an educational activity does not remove every limit on financing it. Seminole Rock and Tallman supply the governing weight for a permissible interpretation of the administering agency's own regulation; Gardebring preserves contrary text and evidence of intent at promulgation, and Martin preserves review for consistency and reasonableness. Section 413.85(c)'s final sentence addresses increased costs from redistribution between educational and patient-care units. Its reference to costs reasonably reaches a financial transfer even when the clinical instruction remains unchanged. The hospital's contextual reading is substantial: the sentence also discusses activities customarily undertaken by providers. But that first clause need not consume the second clause's separate financial restriction. The permissive allowance in subsection (a), the net-cost calculation in subsection (g), and the related-organization cost rule can operate subject to that restriction. The college previously carried the disputed categories; the new study allocated them to the hospital. The Secretary reasonably treated that allocation as redistribution. Complexity alone supplies no authority, and neither community support nor a rule for every later base-year dispute is decided.

**Application and operative qualifications:** Section 413.85(a)(1) provides that allowable cost may include the net cost of approved educational activities under paragraph (g), apportioned for Medicare utilization under §413.50. Permission to include those costs is not an unconditional payment command. Paragraph (g) deducts tuition revenue and includes trainee stipends, teacher compensation and other direct and indirect educational-activity costs under Medicare cost-finding principles. The recodified paragraph (a)(2) directs payment for approved residency programs in medicine, osteopathy, dentistry and podiatry to §413.86 for cost reporting periods beginning on or after July 1, 1985. That later-period exception does not itself disallow the reporting year ending June 30, 1985, and the Court fixes no later-period payment or base-year amount.

Section 413.17(a), subject to the separate exception in paragraph (d), includes the cost of services, facilities and supplies furnished by organizations related through common ownership or control at the related organization's cost, capped at the price of comparable items that could be purchased elsewhere. It does not independently make an otherwise excluded educational cost allowable. No party's entitlement under paragraph (d) is decided, and its separate exception is not a premise of this holding. Section 1395x(v)(1)(A)'s reasonable-cost system takes direct and indirect costs into account within authorized methods and included-item rules, excludes unnecessary costs in efficient delivery, and bars shifting Medicare costs to non-Medicare patients or the reverse. It does not guarantee payment of every asserted actual expense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co., 325 U.S. 410 (1945): applies controlling weight to a permissible agency interpretation of its own regulation; the plainly-erroneous-or-inconsistent limit remains operative.
- Udall v. Tallman, 380 U.S. 1 (1965): applies the same own-regulation standard; an alternative reasonable reading does not by itself displace the Secretary's permissible construction.
- Gardebring v. Jenkins, 485 U.S. 415 (1988): preserves the limit where an alternative reading is compelled by the regulation's plain language or other indications of the Secretary's intent at promulgation; no such compulsion is established here.
- Martin v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission, 499 U.S. 144 (1991): applies its account of reasonable own-regulation interpretation and administrative expertise; its allocation of interpretive authority under the Occupational Safety and Health Act remains specific to that statutory structure.
- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: preserves reimbursement within lawful cost methods and limitations, including authorized exceptions and independent challenges to unlawful methods; actual expenditure alone does not establish entitlement here.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co., June 17, 1994: preserves the requirement to respect operative statutory limits; its mandatory-tariff holding does not invalidate this textually supported regulatory restriction or replace the own-regulation standard.
- Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs v. Greenwich Collieries, June 20, 1994: preserves the distinction between delegated authority and its actual exercise; its treatment of a general reasonable-doubt policy does not render every statement of regulatory intent nonoperative or create a Medicare proof burden.
- Ohio State University v. Secretary, Department of Health and Human Services, 996 F.2d 122 (6th Cir. 1993): rejects its contrary construction insofar as it excludes this transfer of otherwise eligible clinical-education costs from the redistribution provision; its separate community-support analysis is not resolved.

##### The actual agency ground survives the asserted inconsistency

**Controlling proposition:** The Administrator's expressed redistribution ground and the established history of college-borne costs support this denial; a memorandum's silence about redistribution and an intermediary's prior allowance do not, on this record, establish an authoritative contrary interpretation that defeats it. A considered, otherwise permissible interpretive change is not automatically invalid, but contrary text, genuine inconsistency and the required agency explanation must be examined, and a reviewing court may not substitute a new ground or missing finding.

**Authority:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Scalia and Souter. The same five Justices adopt this answer to the consistency and actual-ground objections; it supports application of the redistribution holding rather than furnishing an alternative reimbursement bar.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted prior practice does not defeat the ground the Administrator actually gave. Good Samaritan requires attention to text, delegated authority and reasons; it neither invalidates every interpretive change nor supplies automatic weight to counsel's unsupported position. Boston & Maine permits recognition of an agency's discernible ground and forbids judicial invention of one. The Administrator identified the earlier school-borne categories and rejected the claim that their new allocation was only an accounting refinement. Letter 78-7 conditionally discusses allowable related costs but does not affirmatively exempt those costs from redistribution limits. Its silence is not a contradictory command. The intermediary's earlier clerical-cost allowance likewise does not establish a binding Secretary-level interpretation. The Court does not find that clinical training changed, that every unclaimed dollar was community-financed, or that agency expertise cures contrary text. The disputed allocation supplies the actual financial transfer relevant to this provision. Because that ground suffices, the Court leaves the independently stated community-support and general-administrative allowability grounds unresolved and orders no payment.

**Application and operative qualifications:** Letter 78-7 requires that faculty-service costs would be allowable if incurred directly, be allocated consistently among recipient components, comply with related-organization and comparable-price limits, and satisfy the applicable patient-care relation, documentation, reasonable-allocation and reasonable-amount requirements. Its identified additional nonfaculty costs are limited to direct and space costs. It does not permit duplicative additions where the hospital maintains its own corresponding library, administrative structure, office or clerical support. Those conditions survive; the Court does not read silence about redistribution as an unconditional promise of all overhead. The record does not explain the failure to claim reimbursement before 1974, and the Court does not turn that silence into a finding of community support or an estoppel rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala, June 7, 1993: applies its rule that considered interpretive change is not automatically disqualifying while preserving scrutiny of text, delegation and reasons; unsupported litigation positions receive no automatic controlling weight.
- National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp., March 25, 1992: applies the requirement of a discernible actual agency rationale; the express redistribution ground satisfies it without a new judicial explanation.
- SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80 (1943): preserves review on the agency's own ground rather than a substitute invented on review.
- Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn. v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983): preserves required reasoned agency decisionmaking and the prohibition on appellate rationalizations supplying missing findings.
- Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204 (1988): preserves the rejection of unsupported litigation rationalizations; this decision rests on the Administrator's stated rationale and decides no new retroactive-rulemaking question.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court affirms on redistribution alone. It neither endorses nor rejects the separate construction treating all non-Medicare funding as community support; decides no merits of the Administrator's additional university general-and-administrative-cost ruling under §§413.9, 413.85 and Letter 78-7; and orders no reimbursement, penalty, recoupment, reopening of the settled faculty-compensation amounts, or relief on the unadjudicated alternative enforcement claim. The original $2,861,247 disputed difference is not adjudicated as an amount remaining after settlement. Materially different retrospective applications, later §413.86 calculations and entitlement under §413.17(d) remain unresolved. No independently sufficient alternative holding is adopted, and no fractured-rationale synthesis is needed.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The holding-specific treatments govern. The governing own-regulation standard, lawful-cost-method rules, permissible-interpretive-change rule and actual-agency-ground requirement remain in force. The Court rejects the Sixth Circuit's contrary redistribution reading in Ohio State to the extent stated, without deciding its community-support analysis. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The dissent's two alternative frameworks do not establish precedent treatment by the Court.

Effective June 24, 1994, the Secretary's redistribution construction is sustained as applied to these previously college-borne costs. Generally eligible clinical education and otherwise permissible related-organization cost attribution do not remove this additional restriction. The Court applies the existing own-regulation framework; it creates no new general deference test, burden of proof, permanent cost-history ceiling or right to all actual costs. The two controlling propositions operate together on the single sufficient redistribution ground. Community support, remaining general-administrative allowability, paragraph (d)'s separate exception and later reimbursement calculations remain unresolved.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Thomas dissents, joined by Justices Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg. They would reverse the judgment sustaining the two §413.85(c) exclusions and remand for review of the remaining actual allowability issues, including any necessary administrative proceedings. Their first independently sufficient framework treats subsection (c)'s descriptions of program intent and community responsibility as explanatory policy, not the asserted substantive exclusions. Deference cannot create a categorical reimbursement bar that the agency's operative regulation has not established. Greenwich Collieries preserves that distinction between authority to adopt a rule and adoption of the rule; it does not decide this regulation automatically.

Their second framework assumes that subsection (c) has operative force and gives separate answers to each agency ground. As to redistribution, the entire final sentence must be read with the approved-activity allowance in subsection (a), the direct-and-indirect net-cost provisions in subsection (g), and the related-organization rule. In their view, it prevents moving an educational institution's own activities not customarily or traditionally undertaken by providers into patient-care reimbursement. It does not forfeit otherwise eligible costs of this hospital's approved clinical training merely because the college formerly carried them or the hospital improved its measurement. As to community support, subsection (a) refers the net-cost calculation to subsection (g), which deducts tuition; the Secretary cannot add every non-Medicare source as an additional subtraction or permanent bar. Both branches are needed to reject both asserted grounds under this alternative framework.

The dissent accepts that the regulation says allowable cost may include approved education, rather than commanding payment of every expense. Related costs remain subject to their cost basis, comparable-price cap and separate paragraph (d) exception, which the dissent does not adjudicate. Letter 78-7 retains its directness, documentation, allocation, amount and nonduplication requirements. Good Samaritan permits lawful methods and considered interpretive change, but does not establish that these exclusions were adopted. Earlier administration informs interpretation and explanation without establishing automatic estoppel or an agreed finding of a settled twenty-year policy reversal. Boston & Maine requires treatment of the Administrator's actual additional general-and-administrative-cost ground. The dissent therefore does not direct payment of the whole claim, decide the remaining allowable amount, reopen the faculty-compensation settlement, or adjudicate the alternative enforcement claim. These are four-Justice positions, not additional holdings of the Court.

### Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., including Walsh’s employee action, No. 93-1197 (513 U.S. 30)

Merits decision, November 14, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 3, 1994, on writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit.

The question is whether PATH, the Port Authority’s bistate railway subsidiary, is an arm of New York and New Jersey entitled to Eleventh Amendment immunity from these employees’ federal suits. Hess and Walsh alleged separate workplace injuries and brought separate FELA actions; Hess also invoked the Boiler Inspection Act. The district courts dismissed under the Third Circuit’s arm-of-State precedent and the unmet one-year condition attached to the States’ consent. The reviewed appellate disposition left those dismissals in place. The two actions remain distinct; this decision resolves their common immunity premise.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Third Circuit’s judgment and remands both employee actions.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hess action: dismissal on PATH’s claimed state immunity | Reverse and remand, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Remove the Eleventh Amendment bar; allow further proceedings subject to ordinary defenses and merits adjudication. |
| Walsh action: dismissal on PATH’s claimed state immunity | Reverse and remand, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | The same removal of the immunity bar, with the separate action’s ordinary defenses and merits reserved. |

The dissenting Justices would affirm both dismissals because PATH is an arm, neither plaintiff satisfied the one-year consent condition, and FELA does not itself remove the federal-forum immunity. No Justice resolves negligence, damages or an independent nonimmunity time bar.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reverse and remand both actions | Entire entity-specific status holding, application, limits and remedy. |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Thomas | Would affirm both dismissals | State-control ground for arm status, unmet conditional consent and absence of FELA abrogation; no universal treasury guarantee. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### PATH’s actual compact and financial structure does not confer the States’ immunity

**Controlling proposition:** A bistate compact entity does not acquire the States’ Eleventh Amendment immunity merely by exercising governmental power: its actual legal, financial and institutional relationship must supply good reason to conclude that the States structured it as their arm and Congress concurred, with state-treasury exposure highly probative but not categorically indispensable. PATH is a distinct, financially self-supporting enterprise responsible for its obligations; these employee suits impose no judgment obligation on either State and do not treat either State itself as a defendant, so PATH lacks that immunity.

**Authority:** Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six of nine participants directly support this entity-specific rule and its application to both employee actions. O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas dissent; no fragmented-rationale analysis is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that PATH’s actual relationship to New York and New Jersey does not make these suits actions against the States. Lake Country Estates requires examining the compact structure, and Mt. Healthy distinguishes separate public entities from the State itself. Gubernatorial appointment and veto, legislative restrictions and public transportation responsibilities favor PATH’s position, but do not overcome this enterprise’s separate identity, revenues, assets and obligations. Article XV requires equal state appropriations only while operating revenues are inadequate, only for salaries, office and other administrative expenses, in amounts recommended by the Authority and approved by both governors, with each State obligated only up to $100,000 annually. It is no promise to pay judgments. Financial independence therefore carries great weight in this mixed setting, without making treasury liability immunity’s sole condition. Feeney assumed status when deciding consent; it did not resolve the antecedent question now presented. Because PATH is not an immune arm, its conditional consent is unnecessary to overcome an Eleventh Amendment bar. The Court leaves ordinary defenses and liability for the alleged injuries to proceedings on remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Lake Country Estates, Inc. v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency*, 440 U.S. 391 (1979), applies its entity-specific compact inquiry: exercising state power does not itself make a bistate corporation the States, and the compact’s actual structure matters.
- *Mt. Healthy City School District Board of Education v. Doyle*, 429 U.S. 274 (1977), preserves the distinction between a separate public entity and an arm of a State; a governmental function alone supplies no immunity.
- *Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp. v. Feeney*, 495 U.S. 299 (1990), is distinguished as deciding federal-forum consent while assuming, rather than deciding, PATH’s arm status. Its consent ruling is not an earlier holding that PATH is immune.
- *Hilton v. South Carolina Public Railways Commission*, 502 U.S. 197 (1991), preserves state-court FELA coverage without constructive waiver of federal-forum immunity; no abrogation or waiver rule is enlarged here.
- *Puerto Rico Aqueduct & Sewer Authority v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc.*, 506 U.S. 139 (1993), preserves immediate review of a qualifying immunity denial without establishing that a particular entity is an immune arm. Its appealability holding does not decide PATH’s status.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not declare every compact entity nonimmune, make a debt disclaimer alone dispositive, decide a materially different entity’s practical dependence, abolish state sovereign immunity or hold that FELA abrogates federal-forum immunity. Negligence, causation, damages, the Boiler Inspection Act merits, every independently applicable nonimmunity limitations defense and hypothetical consent exceptions remain undecided.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective November 14, 1994, PATH is not an arm entitled to the States’ Eleventh Amendment immunity from these employee actions. Lake Country’s compact inquiry is clarified through the actual separate legal and financial structure, with treasury exposure carrying great weight but no universal sole-treasury test. The Third Circuit’s contrary PATH-status rule cannot sustain these dismissals. FELA coverage, consent of an entity that actually is immune, immunity abrogation and collateral-order appealability remain separate and unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas, dissents. She would treat PATH as an arm of the States because the governors and legislatures retain substantial control over its commissioners, actions, projects and spending. Financial exposure matters, but the protection of a State from federal suit also safeguards its institutional authority. A self-supporting state enterprise need not lose that protection merely because it can pay judgments from its own revenues. Lake Country Estates permits an entity-specific finding of arm status; it does not compel treating PATH’s state controls as equivalent to the more local structure there. Puerto Rico Aqueduct recognizes protection from suit for qualifying arms while leaving the qualification question separate.

The dissent does not read Article XV as an unlimited state guarantee. Its equal, conditional administrative appropriations, recommended by the Authority and approved by both governors, bind each State only up to $100,000 annually while operating revenues are inadequate. The claimed sovereign relationship rests on the combined legal controls, not an invented obligation to pay judgments. Having found arm status, the dissent treats consent as a separate requirement. Feeney’s federal-forum consent did not eliminate its conditions; the district courts found both actions outside the one-year period running from accrual. Hilton’s state-court FELA holding does not revive constructive waiver in federal court. The dissent therefore would affirm these dismissals without deciding liability, damages or a new exception to the consent legislation. This position does not control the Court’s status ruling or remand.

#### Source Notes

The separate records are [Hess, 809 F. Supp. 1172](https://static.case.law/f-supp/809/cases/1172-01.json), and [Walsh, 813 F. Supp. 1095](https://static.case.law/f-supp/813/cases/1095-01.json). The compact’s relevant financial qualifications and the prior status rationale appear in [Port Authority PBA, 819 F.2d 413](https://static.case.law/f2d/819/cases/0413-01.json); chronology and the reviewed posture are documented at [513 U.S. 30](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep513/usrep513030/usrep513030.pdf). Injury and negligence remain allegations. No unpresented exception to the consent legislation or universal accrual rule is decided.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Both federal employee actions may proceed against PATH, subject to ordinary defenses and adjudication of the underlying claims. The Eleventh Amendment premise of dismissal is removed. The Court awards no damages and decides no negligence, Boiler Inspection Act merits or independent nonimmunity limitations question.

**End of entry: Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., including Walsh’s employee action, Merits decision, November 14, 1994.**

### Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, No. 94-12

Merits decision, March 27, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, No. 94-12; 517 U.S. 44. October Term 1995. Argued October 11, 1995; decided March 27, 1996. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing its January 18, 1994 immunity judgment, 11 F.3d 1016.

The Tribe sued Florida and Governor Lawton Chiles in his official capacity under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. The district court denied immunity dismissal on June 18, 1992; the court of appeals reversed and directed dismissal. The questions concern congressional authorization of a prospective direct-State action under the Indian Commerce Clause and a prospective officer action under Ex parte Young. Alabama is not a party to this docket.

A separate September 22, 1993 merits summary judgment for Florida and the Governor and its stayed Eleventh Circuit appeal, No. 93-5256, remain outside this immunity review. The complaint alleges a January 29, 1991 negotiation request and September 19 filing; the United States describes a December filing. Either interval exceeds 180 days, but the discrepancy establishes no amendment history. The actual prayer for a compact within sixty days, later mediation and a game-scope declaration is distinct from the government's narrower proposed good-faith negotiation or declaratory relief.

#### Judgment

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Operative effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Governor Chiles | Reversed and remanded, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Remove the categorical bar to the connected officer action; retain IGRA's conditions and lawful prospective limits. |
| Florida | Immunity-dismissal direction set aside and remanded, 5–4 as to this common relief | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Remove that appellate command without a majority holding establishing State amenability. |

The Florida component contains three distinct primary positions: Stone-Zsela and Stevens would reverse and sustain the direct State action; Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate without deciding amenability; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm. The first five share removal of the dismissal direction and remand, not a constitutional or avoidance rationale. Stone-Zsela expressly disagrees with stopping short of sustaining the State action.

The State claim is not terminated by the immunity ruling. This mandate supplies no majority determination establishing jurisdiction over Florida and no finding that jurisdiction is absent. Any State merits adjudication requires a lawful jurisdictional basis. The three Justices' additional avoidance sequencing is not a Court instruction. The separate September 1993 merits judgment and stayed appeal are not vacated, decided or treated as an undecided district-court merits action. No bad faith, compact, gaming entitlement, license, damages or taxing power is awarded.

No aggregation of conditional votes or broader separate reasons supplies a controlling State-amenability rule.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The statutory judicial decision is not categorically advisory

**Controlling proposition:** Adjudicating IGRA's federal good-faith negotiation dispute is a judicial function where the judgment conclusively determines the parties' legal relations and activates constrained statutory consequences. The prospect of later mediation and executive action, and the absence of a guaranteed compact, do not make the court's decision merely advisory.

**Authority:** Part I has seven direct supporters: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens's serious reservation is not an eighth join; Stone-Zsela supplies no independent join.

**Controlling explanation:** A court decides an existing dispute over a federal duty and, upon the requisite finding, changes the parties' legal obligations and access to the next statutory stage. That supplies effective partial relief even though neither the desired compact nor every desired game is assured. The decision's legal operation matters; the answer does not rest solely on whether the Secretary can edit individual findings.

Aetna and Nashville reject a universal requirement that judicial relief take the form of an immediately executable coercive decree. Brimson supports judicial adjudication in aid of administration, while its actual enforceable-order setting prevents treating it as an answer to every intermediate proceeding. Ferreira and the relevant Gordon and Hayburn materials require attention to whether the purported judgment has legal effect or remains subject to executive revision. IGRA's binding determination and prescribed consequences satisfy that concern here.

Wisconsin's partial-redress rule concerns the judicial capacity to afford meaningful relief. Morgan Stanley and Plaut address Congress's ability to establish a new rule requiring renewed adjudication of specified completed proceedings without dictating the judicial resolution of underlying claims. Neither line determines whether IGRA's conclusive adjudication of a good-faith negotiation dispute ceases to be judicial merely because subsequent mediation or executive action remains. The Court neither determines every later preclusion question nor manufactures coercive remedies or a new cause of action to make the statutory decision judicial.

##### II. IGRA permits this connected officer action within its own limits

**Controlling proposition:** Ex parte Young permits a prospective official-capacity action against Governor Chiles to enforce an ongoing violation of IGRA's good-faith negotiating duty, subject to the statute's full sequence and remedial limitations. IGRA's State-directed wording and detailed procedure do not exclude that route in this case.

**Authority:** Part II has five direct supporters: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The common rule is specific to IGRA; it does not adopt the three Justices' additional general clear-indication theory.

**Controlling explanation:** Young provides the established route against a responsible officer for an ongoing federal violation. The Governor's representatives participated in negotiations, supplying the required connection to the alleged failure. Rejecting categorical dismissal does not require proof that he alone can bind Florida to every compact term. Mandatory good faith differs from discretion over those terms.

Congress imposed a federal negotiating duty and prescribed how its enforcement progresses. References to the State do not, in this arrangement, exclude the official through whom its obligations are performed. The scheme limits the officer action rather than authorizing a free-standing injunction with greater remedies. This construction answers the objection that an officer could evade Congress's chosen restraints: both sixty-day periods, mediation, selection, consent and Secretarial conditions remain effective. It does not treat remedial detail as irrelevant or establish that every statutory right carries officer relief.

The State's involvement in negotiations establishes neither waiver nor unlimited compacting authority. Incorporation of state gaming law into the federal obligation does not itself convert the claim into a Pennhurst demand to enforce state law. Hafer's individual-capacity damages rule supplies no basis for this action, and Edelman's retrospective-relief boundary remains.

The actual request for a compact and declaration does not establish entitlement to either. A court cannot prescribe its preferred compact, enlarge the good-faith duty, bypass mediation or recast accrued compensation as an injunction. The statutory progression, rather than an added coercive order compelling agreement, governs failure to reach agreement. The categorical officer dismissal must therefore be reversed while the remaining merits and lawful relief receive their proper treatment.

##### III. Removing the officer bar does not establish the predicates for compacting relief

**Controlling proposition:** The remand permits only relief authorized by IGRA upon the actual statutory predicates. It neither resolves good faith or gaming scope nor disturbs the distinct merits judgment and appeal. The following conditions govern any relief through the recognized officer route.

**Authority:** Part III has the same five direct supporters. The four opposing the route preserve the same enacted conditions in their dissent; recognizing those conditions does not imply their agreement to reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** A valid duty, an available enforcement defendant and entitlement to a particular remedy are different questions. IGRA makes adverse findings, successive waiting periods and specified mediation and administrative steps do separate work. Removing immunity cannot substitute for any of them. Nor does the actual prayer enlarge the relief Congress supplied. The September 1993 merits judgment and its stayed appeal must receive their own lawful disposition; this immunity mandate decides neither. The Court accordingly preserves the statutory progression and independent gaming conditions below without ordering that progression to begin as though bad faith were already established.

**Complete statutory conditions and limits:**

- Under 25 U.S.C. §2710(d)(3)(A), a qualifying request by the Tribe with jurisdiction over the relevant Indian lands triggers the State's duty to negotiate in good faith, not a duty to accept preferred terms. A failure-to-negotiate suit under (d)(7)(A)(i) may begin only after 180 days. The effective-compact enforcement action under (A)(ii) and Secretary's enforcement action under (A)(iii) remain distinct.
- Under (B)(ii), tribal evidence that no compact exists and that the State failed to respond or respond in good faith precedes the State's burden to prove good faith. Nonagreement alone does not establish bad faith. An actual adverse finding triggers (B)(iii)'s order to the State and Tribe to conclude a compact within the first sixty-day period. The court **may** consider public interest, public safety, criminality, financial integrity and adverse economic effects on existing gaming; it **shall** consider a demand for direct taxation of the Tribe or Indian lands as evidence of bad faith.
- If no agreement follows during that period, (B)(iv) requires each side's last-best proposal to a court-appointed mediator. The mediator selects the proposal best comporting with IGRA, other applicable federal law, and the court's findings and order. Submission to both parties under (B)(v) begins a **second sixty-day period** for State consent. Consent makes the proposal an agreed compact under (B)(vi).
- Nonconsent during that second period triggers notice to the Secretary under (B)(vii). The Secretary **shall** prescribe procedures, consulting the Tribe and acting consistently with the selected proposal, IGRA and relevant state law. The express findings/order selection criterion appears in (iv), not verbatim in (vii). The Secretary lacks unrestricted discretion to withhold all action after the enacted predicates occur.
- State nonparticipation does not automatically select the Tribe's proposal. The government's qualified prediction supplies no textual default. The adopted construction adds neither contempt nor an order compelling agreement merely because agreement or participation during the ordered period fails; statutory progression is the consequence. No rule concerning every separate obstruction or disobedience sanction is decided.
- Section (d)(4) grants no new state taxing power while preserving regulatory-cost assessments agreed under (d)(3)(C)(iii). That exception is distinct from the mandatory treatment of direct-tax demands as bad-faith evidence. Absence of other taxing authority does not justify refusing negotiation.
- Class III gaming still requires approved tribal legislation, a State permitting such gaming for some person, organization or entity, and an effective compact subject to applicable remedial provisions; licensing and regulation remain separate. Under (d)(8), disapproval may rest on IGRA violations, other federal law **not relating to jurisdiction over gaming on Indian lands**, or federal trust obligations. Forty-five-day inaction produces approval only insofar as consistent with IGRA; Federal Register publication remains necessary.
- Section 2721's severability provision preserves qualifying remaining law but does not satisfy a finding, waiting period, mediation, selection or nonconsent condition and creates no alternative program. The distinct asserted Secretary substitute remedy remains unadjudicated. The Court neither endorses an automatic bypass nor reverses the lower court's separate substitute-remedy determination. Immunity dismissal alone satisfies none of the enacted predicates.

##### IV. Direct-State intent is agreed upon, but State amenability is unresolved

**Majority-supported statutory proposition:** IGRA unmistakably authorizes a direct action naming the State. Its specified State-party action, State evidentiary burden, order to State and Tribe and successive State-directed steps satisfy the clear-statement requirement. General federal-question jurisdiction, §1362 or scattered references to States alone would not suffice.

**Authority and force:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens and the four dissenters actually adopt this statutory construction across their writings. Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer accept it only if their alternative State route must be reached. The six-position agreement establishes only the exact statutory-authorization proposition stated above; it creates no common State-amenability opinion. Statutory intent and constitutional authority remain separate: no controlling holding sustains or rejects Florida's amenability, and no majority opinion decides the constitutional question. It also supplies no sequencing rule requiring abrogation to be decided before officer relief.

**Common judgment, without common State rationale:** Five support setting aside the immunity-dismissal direction and remand. Two support affirmative direct-State relief; three support nonmerits vacatur; four oppose either change. Avoidance is not a logical subset of the two Justices' affirmative constitutional grounds. No narrower controlling constitutional rationale can be extracted by treating their different reasons as interchangeable.

**Other limits:** No express waiver, automatic reciprocal tribal/state consent, constructive waiver by negotiating, or delegation of the United States' immunity exemption establishes this suit; the United States is an amicus. The Court does not reach the distinct Tenth Amendment defense first raised on appeal and not decided below. The question-scope limitation is prudential: X-Citement Video permits an appropriate respondent defense without requiring resolution of this distinct constitutional issue. No universal forfeiture rule, jurisdictional incapacity or independent mediator-appointments invalidation is announced.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Ex parte Young:** Applied to this connected officer and ongoing federal statutory duty. The recognized route carries IGRA's limits and establishes no universal rule that detailed statutes preserve officer equity.
- **Edelman:** Its prospective/retrospective boundary remains. An injunction cannot be used to obtain accrued treasury compensation; prospective compliance costs alone do not decide that classification.
- **Pennhurst:** Its restriction on federal orders enforcing state law remains. Incorporation of state gaming law as part of the federal IGRA obligation does not itself turn this action into a state-law enforcement claim.
- **Union Gas:** Not overruled. Its actual damages judgment, statutory holding and five-vote constitutional conclusion remain precedent within their decided scope. The fractured rationale neither erases that conclusion nor makes every plurality sentence binding. Four Justices would overrule the constitutional conclusion; their dissent changes no law. Three unused constitutional alternatives cannot be aggregated with two affirmative State votes to create a present holding.
- **Hans:** Not overruled. The separate common-law accounts of its nontextual immunity do not become a Court holding, and the State set-aside does not abolish its rule.
- **Fitzpatrick:** Its distinct Fourteenth Amendment authority is unaffected; the decision establishes no new Reconstruction ground.
- **Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227:** Applied for the judicial character of specific, conclusive declaratory relief in an actual dispute over present rights. The declaratory statute is procedural; it creates neither a constitutional controversy nor an otherwise missing substantive action. The absence of a damages or injunction award is not alone disqualifying.
- **Nashville, Chattanooga & St. Louis Railway v. Wallace, 288 U.S. 249:** Applied for the proposition that an adverse, conclusive declaratory adjudication can be judicial without execution or coercive process. Its actual state-tax controversy does not establish that a declaratory label alone supplies legal effect or an IGRA remedy.
- **ICC v. Brimson, 154 U.S. 447:** Applied as support for judicial adjudication assisting administration, while preserving its ordinary adversarial proceeding, conclusive judgment and judicially enforceable order. Contempt there followed disobedience of the court's order, not merely the agency's request. It neither establishes an IGRA contempt remedy nor alone answers whether a differently structured intermediate judgment is judicial.
- **United States v. Ferreira, 54 U.S. 40:** Distinguished. The judge acted as a claims commissioner in a process lacking ordinary suit, parties, process and court record; no debt arose without Treasury approval, and the Secretary could revise the award. IGRA's conclusive adjudication and prescribed legal consequences differ. Ferreira's reserved appointments question supplies no mediator ruling; the appended Yale Todd account is a later inferential report, not a recorded reasoned holding.
- **Gordon v. United States, 69 U.S. 561, and the later appendix at 117 U.S. 697:** The actual disposition dismissed for want of appellate jurisdiction without a contemporaneous reasoned opinion. Taney's prepared paper, considered after his death and later printed, was not a delivered majority opinion; the contemplated Court opinion was never prepared. Its execution language informs the historical concern about legal effect contingent on another branch, but is not an unqualified holding requiring execution in every judicial case.
- **Hayburn's Case, 2 U.S. 409:** The reported Supreme Court proceeding produced no merits decision. The circuit judges' communications printed in the note explain objections to executive suspension and legislative revision of purported judicial decisions. They remain relevant historical materials, not a reasoned Supreme Court holding deciding IGRA's constrained later process.
- **Wisconsin v. City of New York:** Its concrete partial-redress holding supplies a limited analogy. It did not decide whether IGRA assigns a judicial function, eliminate Stevens's doubt or guarantee the claimant's preferred ultimate result.
- **Morgan Stanley/Plaut:** Article III permits Congress to require reinstatement of qualifying completed private securities limitations dismissals under §27A(b)'s objectively defined procedure while courts independently determine eligibility and adjudicate the claims. The holdings do not establish an unrestricted power to reopen completed merits judgments and do not determine whether IGRA's conclusive adjudication ceases to be judicial because mediation or executive action follows.
- **Puerto Rico Aqueduct:** Applied to the immediate appeal of the conclusive immunity denial. Appealability resolves neither immunity nor waiver, abrogation or the officer route's merits.
- **Hess:** Its entity-specific arm-of-State analysis does not resolve Florida's own immunity or congressional abrogation.
- **Hafer:** Its personal-capacity damages holding remains separate from the official-capacity prospective action recognized here.
- **New York v. United States:** Its compulsory regulation-or-ownership rule and distinct permitted inducements remain effective. It does not resolve the separate Tenth Amendment defense left unadjudicated here.
- **Voting Rights Coalition:** Its election-power grounds and United States enforcement posture supply no general Indian Commerce abrogation or state-command rule.
- **X-Citement Video:** Its allowance for an appropriate prevailing-respondent defense is preserved without compelling adjudication of this distinct Tenth Amendment issue. The Court's question-scope limit remains prudential.

#### Law After Decision

From March 27, 1996, the controlling rules reject the categorical advisory-function objection to this legally effective IGRA adjudication and permit the connected prospective officer action within the complete statutory sequence. No general rule that every detailed scheme preserves or excludes Young is adopted. Six actual writers agree that IGRA clearly authorizes a State action, but the judgment creates no controlling rule on Congress's power to subject Florida to that action.

The State-removal judgment binds the parties without a controlling State-amenability rationale. Union Gas remains precedent; the decision neither adopts a general Article I abrogation power nor establishes the dissent's general Article I prohibition. Statutory duty, cause, defendant, constitutional power, merits and remedy remain separate. The judgment supplies no direct-State jurisdiction by default, Secretary bypass, new damages action or gaming authorization.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins the officer holding and statutory limits and supports removal of Florida's dismissal direction, but would affirmatively sustain Congress's clearly enacted prospective direct-State action. Indian Commerce authority, the ongoing federal duty and unmistakable authorization support that route. Prospective compliance costs do not become accrued treasury compensation merely because payment is needed. Congress's express enforcement channel and the Tribe's continuing-duty claim warrant retaining a forum. Young independently reaches the connected officer, with the same statutory restraints. Neither route permits a preferred compact, bypassed mediation, enlarged duty or disguised retrospective damages; lawful relief should avoid double or inconsistent commands.

He expressly refuses to treat officer availability as sufficient reason to stop short of sustaining the State action. He retains Union Gas's actual damages judgment without adopting every broader abrogation rationale. He reserves materially new compensatory, punitive or restitutionary liability, compulsory state-court actions and different statutory schemes, while recognizing that already-decided Union Gas questions remain settled within its scope. He supplies no new waiver or Reconstruction ground.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins Parts II–III and would sustain both routes. Outside the Eleventh Amendment's actual textual restriction, Hans immunity is judicial doctrine subject to displacement by valid congressional legislation. IGRA supplies the affirmative direction absent from Hans; Congress cannot amend the Constitution or exceed Article III. His rationale is not confined to prospective Indian Commerce litigation and does not require overruling Hans's actual result. He retains Union Gas's constitutional conclusion and leaves the textual issue concerning out-of-state or foreign-citizen plaintiffs open; the Tribe is not treated as such a plaintiff. His own remedial-intent analysis supports the constrained officer action, and he accepts lawful officer-only resolution as a narrower sufficient alternative without abandoning his affirmative State position.

He nevertheless retains serious doubt whether this intermediate judicial step, particularly if separate executive relief could make it dispensable, is fully judicial under Article III. That is a reservation, not a vote to dismiss independently. Wisconsin's partial-redress holding does not settle this different judicial-function issue. He does not formally join Souter's entire separate theory.

**Souter, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, respecting the State claim in Part IV.** They prefer the established officer route and vacatur of Florida's immunity-dismissal direction without deciding abrogation. Intricate remedies and State wording alone do not clearly exclude established prospective equity; Congress may limit statutory enforcement, but they find no sufficient indication of exclusion here. This is distinct from inventing a personal damages action. They would address whether State adjudication is necessary in light of lawful officer relief while preserving lawful jurisdiction before State merits adjudication or a proper nonmerits resolution. The Tribe might forgo its State claim or adequate officer relief might support denying duplicative relief, but neither fact is found. They prescribe no automatic abandonment, mootness, dismissal or indefinite stay. Their additional sequence is not the Court's mandate.

If Young were rejected and Florida's still-live immunity claim adjudicated, they would recognize the direct-State clear statement and uphold congressional displacement of Hans immunity under valid Article I power. They regard that nontextual immunity as federal common law, retained by stare decisis absent deliberate legislative displacement, rather than constitutional immunity Congress can never alter. Constitutional text, limited prior holdings and legislative control of common law independently support that account; it is not a wholesale adoption of Brennan's Union Gas reasoning. Their view is not confined to prospective relief or commerce, but decides no damages or unrelated statute. Because Young succeeds here, these constitutional views remain conditional alternatives, not actual joins in a State-amenability holding.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join the judicial-character ruling and find unmistakable direct-State intent, but would affirm both dismissals. Their constitutional rule is broad: original Article I powers cannot overcome the constitutional immunity limiting federal judicial power over a nonconsenting State. Prospective relief does not cure the defect; neither Indian/interstate commerce nor proprietary/governmental distinctions justifies retaining Union Gas. They would expressly overrule its constitutional conclusion while leaving its statutory construction and Fitzpatrick's later Fourteenth Amendment authority intact.

They acknowledge Union Gas's five-vote constitutional conclusion rather than declaring it never precedent. In their view its conflict with the constitutional immunity structure, fractured rationale and deficient integration with prior immunity decisions supply the special justification for correction. They reject the opposing distinction between a common-law default and congressional displacement, without assuming zero reliance or proof that clear-statement rules are empirically unworkable. Their premise does not adjudicate every other statute or remedy, grant immunity to every public entity, foreclose United States enforcement or eliminate ordinary Young relief.

Independently, they read IGRA as selecting the State-party action, reciprocal State/Tribe order and limited subsequent progression. An ordinary officer injunction risks judicial coercion Congress withheld, but their objection is not limited to unlimited contempt: even an officer action copying the deadlines changes Congress's selected defendant and enforcement form. The sovereign-compacting arrangement reinforces that inference. They recognize that Congress can authorize constrained officer remedies; they conclude it did not do so here. They establish no universal detailed-scheme bar, no categorical affirmative-duty exception to Young, and no power to rewrite the Act merely because enforcement otherwise has a gap.

#### Record and Statutory Context

The immunity decisions are 11 F.3d 1016 and 801 F. Supp. 655. The separate merits judgment and stayed appeal establish no finding of good faith in this review. The differing filing-date accounts establish no amendment history. The record does not resolve every game-scope issue or the Governor's complete state-law authority to bind Florida; a party's account of a possible administrative substitute is not enacted authority.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for proceedings consistent with the officer holding, shared State set-aside and preserved statutory conditions. The separate September 22, 1993 merits judgment and stayed appeal No. 93-5256 require their own lawful treatment; this decision supplies neither reversal of that judgment nor a finding of bad faith. The State claim remains unterminated by the immunity disposition, with no majority determination of State amenability and no mandatory avoidance sequence imposed by the Court.

No immediate compacting order, mediator appointment, default proposal, Secretarial prescription, damages, game-scope declaration or license issues from this decision. The distinct asserted Secretary substitute remedy, independent administrative authority, complete state-law compacting authority, Tenth Amendment question and mediator-appointments issue remain unadjudicated. No special mandate date or continuing Supreme Court supervision is ordered.
