## Standards and Tests selection: ERISA equitable categories and nonfiduciary compensation

### ERISA equitable categories and nonfiduciary compensation

**Governing question:** Available equitable categories and individual redress under ERISA §502(a)(3).

**Current rule:** Section 502(a)(3)'s appropriate equitable relief means traditionally equitable categories, not compensatory damages merely because equity could award them in trust litigation. Compensation from an admitted nonfiduciary for plan losses is unavailable under that provision. The rule does not prohibit every equitable monetary remedy or decide Mertens’s expressly disclaimed antecedent nonfiduciary cause-of-action question; restitution is not reopened. Sections 409 and 502(a)(2)’s plan-centered remedy does not categorically bar §502(a)(3)’s appropriate individual equity for an actual §404 violation. Adequate relief under another ERISA provision normally makes further equity unnecessary and inappropriate, without an absolute prohibition governing every alternative pleading. Relief must redress an actual ERISA or plan obligation; veil piercing alone supplies no freestanding violation.

**Authority by component:**

- The reviewed compensatory award is not appropriate equitable relief: Mertens v. Hewitt Associates — The reviewed compensatory award is not appropriate equitable relief, 1993-06-01. Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.
- Varity — Part III, individual equity and qualified adequacy principle, 1996-03-19; Souter, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer (five); Stone-Zsela’s stricter condition is noncontrolling.
- Peacock — ERISA enforcement requires an independently violated statutory or plan obligation, 1996-02-21; Thomas, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer (seven).

**Present operation effective:** Varity, March 19, 1996, resolving the categorical individual-fiduciary-redress question; Mertens’s equitable-category limit survives.

**Holdings navigation:** Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248 (1993); Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489; Peacock v. Thomas, 516 U.S. 349.

**Exceptions and limits:** Restoration supplies existing membership rights, not immutable benefits. The controlling rule does not classify every substituted monetary item as equitable or adopt all trust-law compensation. An adequate benefits remedy is not established merely because that statutory route exists in the abstract.

## Standards and Tests selection: Fiduciary benefit communications and deliberate disloyal deception

### Fiduciary benefit communications and deliberate disloyal deception

**Governing question:** When an employer’s benefit communication is ERISA administration and breaches loyalty.

**Current rule:** Fiduciary responsibility under §3(21)(A) follows the particular exercise of discretionary plan administration, including benefit information communicated in that capacity. Authority over administration, the occasion and linked benefit content may establish that function; general employer status, forecasting or participant perception alone does not. An administrator acting in that capacity violates §404(a)(1) by knowingly and significantly misleading participants about benefit security to induce surrender of existing plan interests for the employer’s benefit. Lawful amendment or termination power does not authorize that deliberate disloyal deception.

**Authority by component:**

- Varity — Parts I–II, functional administration and loyalty breach, 1996-03-19; Souter, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer (six).

**Present operation effective:** Varity, March 19, 1996.

**Open question:** No general duty to volunteer information, answer every inquiry, disclose every forecast or avoid negligent misstatement is decided; not every corporate prediction is fiduciary administration. The distinct retirees’ assignment determination establishes no new substantive assignment rule.

**Holdings navigation:** Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489.

## Standards and Tests selection: Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments

### Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments

**Governing question:** Sufficiency and implementation of a welfare plan’s reservation of amendment power to a corporation.

**Current rule:** A written reservation of amendment to the Company can satisfy ERISA §402(b)(3)’s procedure and authorized-person requirements because §3(9) includes corporations and applicable corporate law identifies who can bind them. A list of named officers is not invariably required. Any additional procedure the plan actually adopts remains binding. Sufficiency of the reservation does not prove that an authorized actor adopted an amendment: actual express or implied authority, approval and any legally effective ratification, with its timing and consequences, require separate determination.

**Authority by component:**

- The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3): The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3), 1995-03-06. Souter’s unanimous opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; all nine adopt the statutory construction.
- A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action: A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action, 1995-03-06. Souter’s unanimous opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; all nine adopt this required remand and its limits.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-03-06; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** A summary plan description is not proof of authorized amendment. Sponsor amendment power remains distinct from fiduciary duties, written-instrument and disclosure requirements. No universal procedural-violation remedy, vesting rule or amendment immunity follows.

**Holdings navigation:** The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3); A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action.

## Standards and Tests selection: ERISA sponsor design, fiduciary implementation and genuine benefit payments

### ERISA sponsor design, fiduciary implementation and genuine benefit payments

**Governing question:** Whether plan amendment or release-conditioned retirement benefits establish a prohibited transaction.

**Current rule:** An employer designing and adopting pension-plan amendments acts as sponsor, not fiduciary. Actual implementation is a separate functional inquiry; party-in-interest status alone does not satisfy §406(a)(1)’s requirement that a fiduciary cause a transaction knowing or having reason to know that it is prohibited, subject to §408’s statutory exceptions. The asset-disposition branch does not acquire an extra discretion requirement. Payment of genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan is not prohibited by §406(a)(1)(D) merely because required employment-related releases benefit the employer. This assumes fiduciary implementation for that classification and creates neither a §408 exemption nor a general net-benefit defense.

**Authority by component:**

- Spink — Part I, sponsor capacity and separate implementation, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg for all nine.
- Spink — Part II, genuine release-conditioned benefit payments, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas (seven); Souter and Breyer reserve the question.

**Present operation effective:** Spink, June 10, 1996.

**Exceptions and limits:** Amendment procedures, participation, funding, vesting, loyalty, anti-inurement and independently applicable §406(b) duties survive. No sham transfer, kickback, unlawful plan or unenforceable release is immunized. Particular release validity under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law remains open. Surplus does not become employer property.

**Related current rules:** “Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments” governs actual authorization and amendment procedure; “Discharge of pension funding obligations as an exchange” governs the distinct property-for-release transaction.

**Holdings navigation:** Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882.

#### Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1935.
**Decided:** March 6, 1995.

##### The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3)

**Holding:** In this employer-sponsored welfare plan, a written reservation of amendment to the Company provides an amendment procedure and identifies an authorized person under ERISA §402(b)(3), because §3(9) includes corporations and applicable corporate law identifies actors empowered to bind the Company. The statute does not invariably require a list of individual officers; actual additional procedures adopted by the plan remain binding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, 503 U.S. 318 — ordinary legal meaning informs ERISA where the enacted text supplies no contrary rule; no employee-classification test is imported into amendment authority.
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank (December 13, 1993) — status and function remain separate from breach and relief; procedural sufficiency establishes no final amendment or immunity.

##### A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action

**Holding:** A legally sufficient reservation of amendment power does not establish adoption of the challenged amendment. The lower courts must determine who had express or implied authority, whether an authorized actor approved the 1983 cutoff, and, if necessary, whether later ratification was legally effective, including its timing and consequences, before entering a conforming benefits judgment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248 — the reviewed §502(a)(3) compensation limit remains; it does not bar benefits due under §502(a)(1)(B).
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank — the distinction between legal status and ultimate liability is preserved; no new breach or remedy follows merely from identifying amendment power.

**Limits and questions not reached:** ERISA §402(b)(3), 29 U.S.C. §1102(b)(3), requires every plan to provide a procedure for amending it and for identifying the persons with amendment authority. Section 3(9), §1002(9), includes a corporation among persons, as well as an individual, partnership, joint venture, mutual company, joint-stock company, trust, estate, unincorporated organization, association, and employee organization. The plan Constitution reserves to the Company the right at any time and from time to time to modify or amend any or all provisions, in whole or in part, and separately reserves termination. Delaware §141(a) places corporate business and affairs under the board’s management or direction except as otherwise provided by that chapter or the certificate of incorporation. No charter, bylaw, delegation, or approval is established as a fact here. Section 402(a)(1)’s written-instrument and named-fiduciary requirements remain distinct from sponsor amendment authority. Sections 1022 and 1024 retain actual summary, disclosure, and instrument-access duties; an SPD is not proof of authorized amendment. Section 502(a)(1)(B) benefits enforcement is distinct from §502(a)(3)’s appropriate-equitable-relief category.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Third Circuit is reversed and the case remanded to identify authorized corporate actors, determine actual approval and legally effective ratification, including timing and consequences, and enter a conforming benefits judgment. Actual termination and amounts are not decided; rejected lifetime vesting is not automatically reopened.



## Verbatim selected authority: state/holdings/labor-and-employment.md — Lockheed Corp.

#### Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882

**Docket or dockets:** 95-809
**Decided:** June 10, 1996

##### Does sponsor adoption of pension amendments create fiduciary prohibited-transaction liability?

**Holding:** Designing and adopting these pension-plan amendments is sponsor conduct, not ERISA fiduciary conduct. Subject to §408's statutory exceptions, §406(a)(1) requires a fiduciary to cause a transaction the fiduciary knows or should know is prohibited; employer party-in-interest status alone is insufficient, and actual implementation must be assessed separately under §3(21)(A)'s functional terms, including asset-disposition control without an additional discretion requirement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), Part I, unanimous; Part IV-A's independent-claims directions likewise unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Amendment power establishes neither compliance with amendment procedures nor lawful administration. Surplus remains plan property. The sponsor-capacity rule forecloses §404 liability resting solely on design or adoption, while actually preserved fiduciary-implementation and §403(c)(1) anti-inurement grounds retain their own predicates and remedies.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The reviewed adverse judgment is reversed and remanded, 9–0 overall. The theory resting solely on amendment is dismissed; preserved independent Count III alternatives receive ordinary consideration without a new allegation, automatic liability or unconditional dismissal of Count III. Count V, refusal of offensive collateral estoppel and withdrawn Count IV remain outside the relief. Appellate fees are vacated for reconsideration because their stated grounds included the rejected substantive rulings; the fee rationale has eight Associate joins, with Stone-Zsela agreeing in that disposition only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Curtiss-Wright's sponsor-amendment role is applied to a pension plan while its procedural requirements remain distinct. Varity separates sponsorship from administration and preserves its qualified equitable-remedy rule. John Hancock keeps asset status, function, breach and remedy separate. Mertens and Peacock retain the distinct predicate and remedy requirements for any preserved claim.

##### Does employer benefit from a required release alone make enhanced retirement payments prohibited transfers?

**Holding:** Genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan are not prohibited transactions under §406(a)(1)(D) merely because employment-related releases benefit the employer. The rule assumes fiduciary implementation for this inquiry and does not immunize sham transfers, kickbacks, an independently unlawful plan or an unenforceable release.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven. Souter and Breyer would reserve the question, not decide the payments unlawful.

**Material application:** The accepted complaint allegations describe participant benefits rather than diversion solely because releases have employer value. No sham or kickback is alleged in the decided issue.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No §408 exemption or general net-benefit defense is created. Independent participation, funding, vesting, loyalty and anti-inurement duties survive. Section 406(b)'s distinct prohibitions and particular waiver enforceability under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law are not decided; no loss, deception or hidden diversion is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Keystone's transfer of property to discharge an enforceable funding obligation is distinguished; valuable consideration alone does not equate that transaction with these participant benefits.

##### Does OBRA require credit for Spink's earlier lawful exclusion from pension participation?

**Holding:** OBRA §§9201–9202 do not require credit for service during Spink's earlier lawful nonparticipation. Section 9204(a)(1) applies the new accrual protections to plan years beginning on or after January 1, 1988 and employees with an hour of service in a covered year; a smaller total benefit caused by earlier uncredited service is not itself a reduced accrual rate during a covered year.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part III, all eight Associate Justices; Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment only. Dismissal of Counts I–II is restored, 9–0.

**Material application:** The relevant plan year began December 25, 1988. The later calculation date does not turn prior lawful nonparticipation into credited service or establish a separate violation earlier in that calendar year.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 9204(b) separately applies §9203's participation amendments to covered plan years and service performed on or after January 1, 1988; that added service-date condition does not enter subsection (a). Under §9204(a)(2), benefits pursuant to and persons covered by employer–employee-representative collective agreements ratified before March 1, 1986 use the first plan year beginning on or after the earlier of January 1, 1990 or the later of January 1, 1988 and termination of the last qualifying agreement, disregarding extensions after February 28, 1986; no allegation invokes that transition. Section 9204(c) permits formal amendment to await the first plan year beginning on or after January 1, 1989 only if interim operations comply and amendment covers that interval retroactively. Operation under that provision alone does not make benefits or contributions indeterminable or the plan inconsistent with its terms, and does not delay substantive compliance. No general prohibition on using past facts or new default temporal rule is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Landgraf and Rivers require application of enacted temporal direction first; that inquiry resolves the claim without a new default presumption.



## Verbatim selected authority: state/holdings/labor-and-employment.md — Varity Corp.

#### Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-1471.
**Decided:** March 19, 1996.

##### The integrated benefit-security presentation was fiduciary administration

**Holding:** ERISA §3(21)(A) attaches fiduciary responsibility to the particular exercise of discretionary plan administration, including plan information communicated in that capacity. Varity exercised that function when it used administrative authority, benefit comparisons and linked assurances of benefit security to induce the class's transfer; employer status, corporate forecasting or participant perception alone would not suffice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: six Justices.

**Material application:** Current-benefit descriptions and deliberately linked security assurances formed one administrative presentation. Warnings that future conditions depended on MCC's success and that changes might occur did not displace the unchallenged trial findings that the combined presentation was knowingly misleading.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Every employment statement or corporate-solvency prediction is not thereby fiduciary. Corporate restructuring and plan-amendment powers remain distinct from administration.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** John Hancock's particular-function inquiry is applied while keeping status, breach and remedy separate. Curtiss-Wright preserves sponsor amendment authority but distinguishes retained power from authorized exercise; it creates no immunity for this administrative act.

##### Deliberate disloyal deception violates §404

**Holding:** An administrator acting as a fiduciary violates §404(a)(1)'s loyalty duty by knowingly and significantly misleading participants about benefit security to induce surrender of existing plan interests for the employer's benefit. Varity's found deception and the class's reliance meet that rule despite reserved lawful amendment or termination powers.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by the same six Justices as Part I.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This intentional-deception holding decides no general duty to volunteer information, answer every inquiry, disclose every forecast or avoid negligent misstatement. It supplies no substantive rule for the ten retirees' different assignment claim and no permanent-benefits promise.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Firestone's trust-law guidance explains loyalty within ERISA's statutory function, without replacing that threshold. Curtiss-Wright's amendment rule supplies no permission to deceive participants into surrendering present membership.

##### Section 502(a)(3) permits appropriate individual equitable redress for fiduciary breach

**Holding:** Sections 409 and 502(a)(2) do not impose a categorical plan-only bar on §502(a)(3)'s express authorization of appropriate individual equitable relief for a §404 violation. Relief must be traditionally equitable; where another ERISA provision adequately remedies the injury, further equity normally is unnecessary and inappropriate, without an absolute rule governing every alternative pleading.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part III, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela's stricter adequate-remedy condition supports the class's judgment but is not the Court's rule.

**Material application:** The class sought restoration after fiduciary deception procured surrender of membership. Rejection of its lifetime-benefits contract theory established neither an adequate §502(a)(1)(B) remedy for that injury nor an automatic bar to restoration.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not classify each monetary item as equitable, including replacement-insurance premiums or all unpaid benefits. It neither revives the rejected $7.6 million and $712,332 compensatory awards nor permits general compensation through trust law. Section 510 and estoppel remain unadopted alternatives.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The clarified restoration decree is affirmed. Reinstatement confers only the rights of the M-F plan as it now exists. Past entitlement may be adjusted for intervening events, with relevant additional evidence and a conforming decree. Future lawful changes remain possible subject to applicable law and preserved retaliation or discrimination objections. No new payment amount or immutable lifetime entitlement is entered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Russell's plan-centered §409 construction is preserved while its distinct §502(a)(3) question is answered. Mertens's equitable-category limit applies; its reserved antecedent action issue is not treated as an earlier action bar. Peacock's statutory-or-plan-violation requirement is met by the proved §404 breach; its later new-debtor jurisdictional holding does not govern this original action. Firestone supplies guidance within enacted limits.

##### The ten retirees' independent assignment-breach ground was not adequately challenged

**Holding:** The categorical plan-only objection does not defeat the ten retirees' individual equitable recovery, and their distinct lower-court assignment-breach determination remains undisturbed because the petition did not sufficiently present a challenge to it. Affirmance decides no new substantive assignment rule, precise monetary classification or categorical unavailability of §502(a)(1)(B) to those retirees.

**Proposition-level authority:** Souter's Part IV, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: five Justices. Stone-Zsela provides a sixth judgment vote on the distinct assignment component without a ground.

**Material application:** The ten had retired before the purported assignment and did not consent. Their unauthorized-administration ground did not depend on the class's presentation or voluntary departure; arguments concerning misrepresentation and lifetime contractual benefits did not identify error in that separate ground.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the clarified decree for this group on the same existing-plan and adjustment terms stated above, without entering a new amount or reviving terminated-employee, punitive, lifetime-contract, settled-disability or pension claims.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Curtiss-Wright does not transform an amendment reservation into proof of an authorized assignment or permanent vesting; Mertens's monetary-category limits remain without a new classification decision.

### Varity Corp. v. Howe, No. 94-1471

Merits decision, March 19, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Varity Corp. v. Howe, No. 94-1471, 516 U.S. 489. Argued November 1, 1995; decided March 19, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, 36 F.3d 746, as clarified at 41 F.3d 1263. The questions concern individual equitable relief for fiduciary breach under ERISA §502(a)(3) and liability for the challenged benefit communications despite reserved amendment and termination powers.

The 83-member Retired Class transferred employment to MCC in reliance on an integrated benefits presentation and retired before MCC's receivership. Ten other respondents had already retired from M-F when their benefit obligations were assigned without their knowledge or consent. The district court's unchallenged findings establish deliberate deception and reliance for the class; the ten retirees' independent claim concerns unauthorized assignment. MCC stopped paying benefits, while the M-F plan remained in existence.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The benefit presentation was fiduciary administration

**Controlling proposition:** Under ERISA §3(21)(A), fiduciary responsibility attaches to the particular exercise of discretionary plan administration, including plan information communicated in that capacity. Varity performed that function when it used its administrative authority, benefit comparisons and linked assurances of benefit security to induce the class's transfer; general employer status, corporate forecasting or participant perception alone would not establish it.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; six Justices directly support this rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court identifies what Varity was doing, rather than treating every act of a plan administrator as fiduciary. John Hancock requires attention to the particular statutory function, separately from breach and remedy. Here, administrators used their authority over benefit information to explain the proposed transfer's consequences for participants' existing protection. The descriptions of current benefits and the assurances of continued security formed one administrative presentation. Accurate statements that MCC would initially maintain the same terms do not require treating the deliberately linked assurances as unrelated corporate speech. The speakers' authority, the occasion and the communication's content identify actual administration; employees' understanding is relevant context, not an independent statutory test. The record also includes warnings about MCC's success and possible future changes. The unchallenged findings establish that the combined presentation was nonetheless knowingly misleading. Curtiss-Wright preserves sponsor amendment powers but distinguishes possession of a power from its authorized exercise. Neither that power nor the employer's ability to act in two capacities supplies immunity for this different administrative act. The holding does not classify every employment statement or prediction of corporate solvency as fiduciary conduct.

##### II. Deliberate disloyal deception breaches §404

**Controlling proposition:** An administrator violates §404(a)(1)'s loyalty duty when, acting in its fiduciary capacity, it knowingly and significantly misleads participants about benefit security to induce surrender of existing plan interests for the employer's benefit. Varity's found deception and the class's reliance satisfy that rule notwithstanding the reservation of lawful amendment or termination powers.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by the same six Justices as Part I; direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory duty requires administration solely in participants' and beneficiaries' interests. Firestone's use of trust principles helps explain why deliberate deception in that relationship violates loyalty; it does not replace the statutory function requirement established in Part I. The findings show that Varity used benefit explanations and assurances to procure transfers while knowing MCC's poor prospects and seeking to remove benefit costs. The Court therefore decides intentional disloyal conduct, not whether the plan promised permanent benefits. A power to make a lawful future change does not authorize knowingly misleading participants into surrendering present membership. The warnings and reservation language remain part of the evidence but do not overturn the accepted findings about the full communication. No general duty to volunteer information, answer every inquiry, disclose every forecast or avoid negligent misstatement is decided. This communication holding supplies no new rule governing the ten retirees' separate assignment claim.

##### III. Section 502(a)(3) permits appropriate individual equitable redress

**Controlling proposition:** Sections 409 and 502(a)(2) do not impose a categorical plan-only bar on §502(a)(3)'s express authorization of appropriate individual equitable relief for a §404 violation. Such relief must remain within traditionally equitable categories; where another ERISA provision supplies adequate relief for the injury, further equitable relief normally is unnecessary and therefore inappropriate, without an absolute rule governing every alternative pleading.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt this proposition and its application. Stone-Zsela supports the class's judgment on the distinct, stricter condition explained in his concurrence, without joining this Part.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 502(a)(2) provides the plan-centered remedy specified by §409. Section 502(a)(3) separately permits participants and beneficiaries to redress statutory and plan violations through appropriate equity. Giving that express grant work for personal injury does not change the recipient of §409 recovery or imply an omitted damages remedy. Russell confined the latter provision and expressly left §502(a)(3) open. Mertens likewise reserved its disclaimed antecedent action question and instead enforced the equitable-category limit. That limit remains binding here; trust law does not authorize general compensation. Peacock requires an actual statutory or plan violation, which the class's proved §404 breach supplies, rather than equitable relief at large.

The class seeks restoration after administrative deception procured its surrender of membership. Failure of its different lifetime-benefits theory does not adjudicate an adequate remedy for that injury under §502(a)(1)(B). The specific benefits remedy remains effective on its own terms, but its existence does not itself defeat this restoration. Adequate statutory redress ordinarily makes further equity unnecessary; the Court does not decide every overlap or pleading combination. The equitable character of the particular monetary items is outside the questions presented, so affirmance neither reinstates the rejected compensatory awards nor classifies replacement-insurance premiums or all unpaid benefits as restitution. The clarified decree restores the existing relationship without creating additional vested rights.

##### IV. The ten retirees' distinct breach remains undisturbed

**Controlling proposition:** The common categorical plan-only objection does not defeat the ten retirees' reviewed individual equitable recovery, while their distinct lower-court assignment-breach determination remains undisturbed because the petition did not sufficiently present a challenge to that ground. Affirmance preserves the clarified decree without deciding a new substantive assignment rule, the precise monetary classifications, or whether §502(a)(1)(B) is categorically unavailable to these retirees.

**Authority:** Souter's Part IV, joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the presentation ground and its remedial limits. Stone-Zsela supplies a sixth judgment vote without adding a ground on the distinct assignment component.

**Controlling explanation:** These respondents had retired before the transfer and did not consent to assignment of their benefit obligations. Their claim does not depend on receiving the class's presentation or voluntarily leaving the M-F plan. Petitioner's reply contests recovery, but its reliance on absence of misrepresentation and lifetime contractual rights does not identify error in the different unauthorized-administration ground. The petition's communications question does not adequately put that ground before the Court. The individual-action question does apply to both groups, and Part III rejects the categorical statutory obstacle. The Court thus preserves the lower determination rather than deciding assignment liability anew. Curtiss-Wright does not convert an amendment reservation into proof of an authorized assignment, while reinstatement creates no permanent vested entitlement. The rejected contract theory proves neither an adequate alternative remedy nor the categorical absence of one. No separate monetary-category ruling is required to affirm the clarified judgment. Section 510 and equitable estoppel remain unadopted alternatives; the Court revives no terminated-employee, punitive, lifetime-contract, settled disability or pension claim.

#### Precedent Treatment

* **Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S. 134:** Its plan-centered §409 construction is preserved. The Court answers the distinct §502(a)(3) individual-action question Russell expressly reserved.
* **Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248:** Its traditionally equitable-category limit is applied without converting the reserved antecedent action issue into a prior rejection. Its unchallenged restitution disposition and reservation of other monetary equity remain intact.
* **John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank, 510 U.S. 86:** Its particular-function inquiry is applied to administration. The insurance-asset holding and the reservation concerning agency litigation positions remain separate; no deference ruling is needed here.
* **Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen, 514 U.S. 73:** Its distinction between retained amendment power and authorized exercise is preserved. The decision supplies neither an immunity for administrative deception nor lifetime vesting.
* **Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101:** Trust-law guidance informs fiduciary loyalty within ERISA's actual text. The Court does not replace ordinary benefits-review rules with a general fiduciary remedy.
* **Peacock v. Thomas, 516 U.S. 349:** Its requirement of a statutory or plan predicate is satisfied by the identified §404 breach; its later new-debtor jurisdictional limit does not govern this original fiduciary action. Its broader monetary reservations remain open.

No earlier holding is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

ERISA fiduciary status remains tied to the function performed. The Court now applies that requirement and the loyalty duty to the deliberate use of plan administration to mislead participants into surrendering membership. It resolves the previously open categorical individual-action question in favor of appropriate equitable redress under §502(a)(3), preserving the distinct plan remedy, benefits remedy and equitable-category limit. The controlling adequacy principle is qualified: adequate statutory relief normally makes further equity unnecessary. Stone-Zsela's stricter condition does not become the Court's rule.

The ten retirees' lower breach determination remains effective in this case without establishing a new general assignment rule. The decision establishes no universal disclosure duty, categorical overlap prohibition, new financial-remedy classification or immutable benefits entitlement.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins the holdings on fiduciary administration and deliberate loyalty breach. Varity used its administrative role to explain existing benefit security and induce surrender through materially false assurances; employer restructuring powers do not excuse that separate function. He would permit individual equitable redress only when no other ERISA provision adequately remedies the same injury. Sections 502(a)(1)(B)–(3) must retain their separate tasks, and Russell's reserved question permits the express equitable action without weakening Mertens's prohibition on converting it into general legal damages. He therefore supports the class's restoration within the clarified decree while excluding legal or punitive damages and duplicate relief through another adequate statutory channel. His condition is stronger than the Court's statement that adequate statutory relief normally makes further equity inappropriate; he does not join Part III. He concurs in affirmance of the ten retirees' distinct assignment component without adding a ground and does not join Part IV. He supplies no finding that they voluntarily left their plan, no categorical denial of a benefits action and no new classification of the monetary items.

**Thomas, dissenting, joined by O'Connor and Scalia.** The dissent reads §§409 and 502(a)(2) as the specific statutory route for fiduciary-breach relief, with recovery directed to the plan. In its view, §502(a)(3)'s residual language cannot remove that deliberate recipient limitation for the same violation; the provision still applies to other statutory and plan obligations. Russell expressly reserved this question, but its respect for ERISA's differentiated enforcement structure supports that construction. Mertens decided the remedy category and reserved its antecedent action question, so it supplies no prior action bar. The dissent reaches its construction independently rather than treating either precedent as already dispositive.

The dissent also rejects the class's fiduciary-capacity theory. Accurate descriptions of current benefit terms do not, in its view, turn corporate viability forecasts into discretionary administration merely because the employer also administers a plan. It accepts the findings of deliberate deception but disputes their ERISA classification; participant understanding and general fiduciary status cannot replace the statutory function. This ground does not dispose of the ten retirees' different assignment claim. Assuming their undisturbed breach, the dissent applies its individual-action objection to both groups. Reserved amendment power does not establish a valid assignment or erase the findings. It would reverse the reviewed fiduciary-breach recovery and remand for any preserved §510 or estoppel ground, without guaranteeing recovery, dismissing the entire action, reviving closed claims or deciding the unpresented monetary classifications.

### 1997-02-18 - California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Construction, N.A., Inc.; No. 95-789; OT1996, chunk 3, matter 32.

Authority: [Record](../records/California_Division_of_Labor_Standards_Enforcement_v_Dillingham_merits_1997-02-18.md).

#### Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, Parts I-III, is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Each proposition below has nine direct rationale joins; no narrower-opinion calculation is needed.

##### I. The wage criterion does not refer to ERISA plans

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the challenged public-works wage application as a reference to ERISA plans: the lower apprentice rate turns on state approval of an apprenticeship program, not on its ERISA status or the benefit content of a covered plan. The Court accepts this program's covered status for purposes of deciding preemption without holding that every apprenticeship or training arrangement is an ERISA plan.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers distinguishes state laws whose legal operation identifies covered plans or uses their benefits as a benchmark from laws operating independently of that status. Board of Trade supplies the contrasting case: its replacement-coverage obligation used a covered plan's benefit content as the mandatory measure. California's criterion concerns approval of apprenticeship programs as such, and programs subject to that criterion need not all be ERISA plans. Its operation therefore does not depend on the existence of a covered plan.

The contrary argument begins with this program's covered status and the fact that approval conditions affect its operation. But a law does not acquire an ERISA-specific reference merely because a covered plan encounters it. That would replace the statutory inquiry with the presence of an ERISA litigant. The particular program may be assumed covered without making that assumption the legal measure of the State's wage rule. The Court does not decide every requirement in California's apprenticeship system or the classification of other training arrangements.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers is applied for independently operating state cost rules. Board of Trade is distinguished because its mandatory covered-plan benchmark is absent; its holding remains intact.

##### II. The wage choice does not establish a preempted plan command

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, §514(a) does not preempt California's requirement that contractors pay prevailing journeyman wages when they do not use apprentices from an approved program. The approval-linked wage incentive neither dictates covered-plan benefits, funding, structure, administration or remedies nor is shown to have economic effects so acute that the retained wage choice becomes an effective plan command; a direct command or demonstrated coercive application remains outside this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers requires examination of a law's actual operation. Its hospital-payment holdings preserve ordinary economic influence while reserving effects that operate as substantive coverage commands or effectively restrict choice. A contractor here may retain an unapproved program and pay the prevailing journeyman wage. The State's incentive favors approved training, but no supported finding shows that the higher wage eliminates that alternative or requires a change in a covered plan's internal terms. Later approval of the program was not retroactive and does not erase the dispute over the earlier wages.

Dillingham's strongest contention is that the wage differential pressures programs to conform to substantive state training standards. Influence is real, but it does not by itself establish the compulsion Travelers requires. Treating every substantial cost consequence as preemption would discard that decision's distinction. Traditional state responsibility for wages and apprenticeship informs the statutory setting; it does not immunize every public-works measure or establish a percentage safe harbor. The Court applies the concrete command-and-effects inquiry, not a formula under which any verbal relationship to a covered plan suffices. It neither creates a competing ERISA enforcement remedy nor sustains regulation of every plan merely because the State acts through a contractor.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers's indirect-effects rule and acute-effects reservation are applied without change. Board of Trade remains controlling when plan benefits supply the mandatory legal benchmark, which this wage application does not use.

##### III. Reversal of the ERISA ground leaves independent issues for remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's ERISA-preemption judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with the absence of §514(a) preemption of this wage application. The mandate removes that federal obstacle to enforcement without directing final judgment on the independently unresolved NLRA theory, contingent §1983 damages, any properly remaining fee issue, nonpreemption defenses or the underlying wage calculation.

**Controlling explanation:** The District Court granted judgment to the state defendants on the ERISA and NLRA theories. The Ninth Circuit reversed on ERISA, rejected the Fitzgerald Act saving theory, and did not decide the independent NLRA issue or its contingent damages consequence. Removing the ERISA ground therefore does not supply a decision on those unreviewed questions. The separate rejection of the particular §1988 fee request based on an asserted ERISA right not sought to be enforced under §1983 is not automatically reversed, and the State's contractual-estoppel contention, declined below because it was first raised on appeal, is not revived.

The contrary remedial path would reinstate final judgment on every claim merely because the sole appellate preemption ground fails. That would decide more than the granted ERISA question and eliminate issues the Court of Appeals has not reached. Conversely, remand does not authorize a new challenge to the settled absence of §514(a) preemption on these facts. Because that provision does not reach this application, the Court need not decide §514(d) or whether the Fitzgerald Act would save an otherwise preempted rule. Nor does the State obtain blanket immunity merely from public ownership of the project.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers governs the ERISA ground at its actual scope. Neither Boston Harbor nor Chamber of Commerce v. Reich supplies an automatic public-contract immunity or decides the independent NLRA and damages issues left for remand.

#### Precedent Treatment

- New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co., April 26, 1995: applied to the concrete difference between ordinary economic influence and a direct or effective plan command. Its acute-effects reservation and unresolved direct-self-funded questions remain intact; no numerical threshold or general-applicability immunity is added.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguished because California's wage criterion does not use covered-plan benefit content as a mandatory measure. The covered-plan benchmark holding remains binding.
- Building & Construction Trades Council v. Associated Builders & Contractors (Boston Harbor), March 8, 1993: its protection of the lawful proprietary labor agreement supplies no blanket ERISA immunity for every public-works condition. The Court does not resolve the independent NLRA claim by applying a procurement label.
- Chamber of Commerce v. Reich, January 14, 1997: its particular limit on federal procurement authority does not decide this ERISA application or the separate NLRA claim and associated remedies. Its refusal to make a procurement label dispositive remains undisturbed.

#### Law After Decision

California may apply the reviewed wage consequence without the ERISA bar imposed below, subject to independently unresolved issues and ordinary enforcement requirements. State approval of apprenticeship programs as such is not a reference to ERISA status, and this record's wage incentive is not an actual or effective plan command. Direct plan regulation, an ERISA-specific legal benchmark, competing enforcement remedies and demonstrated acute compulsion remain outside the holding. No categorical immunity for wage laws, apprenticeship laws, public purchasing or indirect economic effects is adopted.
