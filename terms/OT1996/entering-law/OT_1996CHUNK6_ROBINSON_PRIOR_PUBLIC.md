### Lee v. Weisman, No. 90-1014

Merits decision, June 24, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 6, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, reviewing [908 F.2d 1090](https://static.case.law/f2d/908/cases/1090-01.json) (July 23, 1990), which affirmed the District of Rhode Island's prospective injunction, [728 F.Supp. 68](https://static.case.law/f-supp/728/cases/0068-01.json) (January 9, 1990). The question is whether school officials may select clergy and include clergy-led invocations and benedictions in official public secondary-school graduation ceremonies. Officials seek reversal; the Weismans seek preservation of the injunction. No companion or consolidation is involved. Procedural background source locator: [505 U.S. 577](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505577/usrep505577.pdf).

Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 6–3.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutional challenge to official clergy-led graduation prayers and prospective injunction | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | White, Scalia, Thomas | The First Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The injunction against school officials' authorizing or encouraging the challenged clergy-led graduation prayer practice remains in force. It reaches the official program and does not prohibit private prayer or genuinely private forum speech. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Blackmun | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter in full; Kennedy in Part I only | Affirmance | Part I: continued prospective exposure and justiciability. Part II: official sponsorship as an independently sufficient merits ground; Kennedy does not join Part II. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Kennedy | Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Affirmance; independent majority-supported ground | Entire writing: school-directed graduation prayer creates impermissible pressure on objecting adolescents. Does not join the sponsorship-only ground. |
| Concurrence | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor | Affirmance | Entire writing: broader historical and neutrality account; all-religion endorsement, coercion not a universally necessary element, and accommodation must lift a discernible burden. |
| Dissent | Scalia | White, Thomas | Would reverse | Entire writing: historical ceremonial accommodation; religious coercion requires law-backed compulsion; sectarian endorsement remains distinct. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Continued exposure supplies a live controversy

**Controlling proposition:** A student who remains in the same public-school system and faces the challenged official prayer policy at her forthcoming high-school graduation has a live claim for prospective protection even though her earlier graduation is over. Deborah Weisman's continued exposure permits review and preservation of the injunction without deciding her father's taxpayer standing.

**Authority:** Part I of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices expressly agree at this threshold level.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the claim remains live because the challenged policy continues to bear directly on Deborah's own education. She attended the middle-school ceremony after temporary relief was denied and now attends Classical High School within the same system. The stipulated record makes a clergy-led invocation and benediction at her later graduation likely. Her request therefore concerns an identified future application of the policy, not merely disagreement with a completed ceremony. Engel and Schempp supply the school-prayer interest for which prospective protection is sought; deciding that interest requires no conclusion about an unrelated taxpayer's right to sue. The officials' merits argument that attendance is optional does not erase the event's expected occurrence or Deborah's concrete relation to it. The Court preserves review of this ongoing practice and leaves taxpayer standing unresolved. It announces no exception permitting advisory decisions whenever an expired school dispute might recur.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — supply the substantive school-prayer interest; neither is expanded into a new taxpayer-standing rule.

##### Officially organized graduation worship is forbidden

**Controlling proposition:** Public secondary-school officials violate the Establishment Clause when they select clergy, request invocations and benedictions, guide the prayers' religious content, and incorporate that worship into the official graduation program. Neither nonsectarian wording nor formally optional attendance cures that official sponsorship; no separate showing of punishment or quantified social pressure is required to invalidate this practice.

**Authority:** Part II of Blackmun's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Kennedy does not join Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the school may not organize worship as part of its own graduation ceremony. Engel forbids governmental composition and sponsorship of school prayer even when the words are denominationally neutral and observance is voluntary. Schempp likewise rejects excusal from school devotional exercises as a cure. Lemon's prohibition on a primary effect advancing religion applies directly: the principal selected a rabbi, requested prayer, supplied civic-prayer guidance and placed the exercise in the official program. Asking that the prayer be broadly acceptable still makes religious presentation an undertaking of the State. The officials' interest in solemnity does not authorize that undertaking; a secular ceremony can mark the achievement without selecting an approved devotional expression. Marsh concerns legislative prayer in a distinct institutional and historical setting. Mergens protects private religious activity under equal access, not school-directed worship. The Court preserves those distinctions, leaves general accommodation doctrine intact, and decides no question about funding, denominational aid or genuinely private religious speech.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 — applied to official school-organized worship at graduation; denominational neutrality and voluntary observance do not cure sponsorship.
- School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — applied: permission to abstain does not validate the official devotional exercise.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 — applied through its prohibition on a primary effect advancing religion; no replacement test and no separate decision under its purpose or entanglement prongs.
- Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783 — distinguished: legislative prayer does not authorize this school-directed graduation exercise.
- Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 — distinguished: private religious activity under equal access is materially different from official sponsorship.

##### The school-directed ceremony also imposes impermissible religious pressure

**Controlling proposition:** The Establishment Clause independently forbids school-directed clergy prayer at a public secondary-school graduation when the school's control and the ceremony's importance place objecting students under substantial pressure to participate, appear to participate, or forgo the occasion. On this record, formally optional attendance and an opportunity to stand or remain silent do not remove that unconstitutional pressure; mere offense alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Kennedy's concurrence in part and in the judgment, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter; five Justices expressly adopt this independently sufficient rationale. It controls by direct majority support, without a Marks inference. Stone-Zsela does not join this writing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the school may not use the importance and discipline of graduation to press students into a religious exercise. Engel and Schempp recognize the distinctive constitutional dangers of prayer organized for schoolchildren. Here officials selected the religious participant and controlled the ceremony in which classmates stood together. An objecting adolescent could reasonably understand standing or silence during the collective prayer as her participation or approval. Requiring her instead to protest publicly or miss a singular educational occasion does not preserve a meaningful choice. The absence of a disciplinary penalty therefore does not answer the constitutional injury. Marsh rests on an adult legislative setting where attendees can enter and leave without the same educational and social consequences. The Court does not equate every disagreeable message or ordinary demand for courtesy with religious coercion. It leaves the treatment of mature adult audiences and other religious accommodations open; the holding concerns this State-directed exercise and the pressures attending public secondary-school graduation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 — applied to indirect religious pressure in the secondary-school graduation setting; formal excusal is not dispositive.
- Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783 — distinguished by the adult legislative setting and freedom to leave without the same consequences.
- Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668 — its minimum prohibition on coerced religious support or participation is applied, without extending its display holding.
- County of Allegheny v. ACLU, 492 U.S. 573 — the present decision does not reopen the display judgments or settle every dispute about accommodation.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, 1992, official organization of the identified secondary-school graduation worship violates the Establishment Clause independently of a separate showing of coercion, and the school's indirect pressure supplies a second independently sufficient ground. Before this decision Engel, Schempp and Lemon governed school worship, but their precise application to this graduation practice was unresolved. Both stated applications now control, each with five Justices. Each ground has express majority support, so no inference under Marks is necessary to identify controlling law.

No general replacement establishment test, rule against mere offense, quantitative pressure scale, funding rule or universal accommodation rule is adopted. Lemon remains in force; its purpose and entanglement prongs are not independently adjudicated. Adult legislative prayer and genuinely private religious speech remain governed by their own authorities.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter, joined by Stevens and O'Connor, adds a broader explanation that the Establishment Clause reaches nonpreferential religious endorsement and that coercion is not universally necessary; his further accommodation account requires a discernible burden on religious exercise. Those broader propositions are not additional holdings of this decision.

Scalia, joined by White and Thomas, would reverse: the historical tradition of nonsectarian ceremonial prayer and absence of law-backed compulsion distinguish graduation from mandatory classroom worship. The dissent treats respectful silence as civility, not compelled observance, while preserving a separate objection to sectarian governmental endorsement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The First Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The existing prospective injunction against school officials' authorizing or encouraging the challenged clergy-led graduation prayer practice remains in force. It reaches the official program and does not prohibit private prayer or genuinely private forum speech.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. No further Supreme Court hearing, remand program, altered institutional practice or retained enforcement proceeding is ordered. Future disputes require actual filings; no such filing is established.

**End of entry: Lee v. Weisman, merits decision, June 24, 1992.**

### Lee v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc., No. 91-339

Plenary merits decision, June 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 25, 1992, with No. 91-155. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Second Circuit, [925 F.2d 576](https://static.case.law/f2d/925/cases/0576-01.json). The Port Authority seeks reversal of the judgment affirming declaratory and injunctive relief against its continuous or repetitive distribution prohibition in publicly accessible, controlled terminal interiors. The District Court had invalidated both solicitation and distribution restrictions; the Second Circuit distinguished them. This case concerns distribution, not an unrestricted privilege to sell or collect immediate payment. The separately decided International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc. v. Lee, No. 91-155, addresses the limited solicitation-and-receipt restriction on the same date; neither companion has priority over the other.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision. Thomas took his oath on October 23, 1991, before argument.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the judgment invalidating the complete literature-distribution prohibition.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Complete prohibition of continuous or repetitive literature distribution in the Authority's controlled common terminal interiors | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | White, Scalia, Thomas | The declaration and injunction against the prohibition remain; no new remedial program |

The judgment does not decide the validity of particular rules against obstruction or litter, or particular location rules. It creates no categorical right to immediate payment, merchandise sales, obstruction of queues or distribution in every operational area.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judgment of the Court | Per curiam | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter | Affirms distribution relief | Six judgment votes; no unified rationale |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports affirmance | Assumes nonpublic status and finds the complete ban unreasonable in the actual setting; reserves classification |
| Separate opinion concerning the companions | O'Connor | No other Justice | Concurs in this judgment | Classifies the terminals as nonpublic and rejects the distribution ban under contextual reasonableness |
| Opinion concerning the companions | Kennedy | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter join Part I only | Part I supports distribution relief | Part I classifies the common areas as public forums and rejects the distribution prohibition. Part II's separate solicitation and literature-sales analysis has no additional joins |
| Opinion concerning the companions | Souter | Blackmun, Stevens | Concurs in this judgment; dissents from solicitation affirmance in No. 91-155 | Joins Kennedy Part I and supports the public-forum analysis |
| Dissent | White | Scalia, Thomas | Would reverse and sustain the distribution ban | Nonpublic-forum reasonableness |

No opinion has five joins. The per curiam judgment does not make the companion writings' separate reasons an Opinion of the Court.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Judgment without a controlling invalidity rationale

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter affirm the invalidation of the Authority's complete literature-distribution prohibition. Stone-Zsela applies nonpublic-forum reasonableness on an assumption; O'Connor reaches unreasonableness after classifying the terminals as nonpublic. Kennedy, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter apply public-forum protections. The declaration and injunction bind these parties, but neither forum classification nor one constitutional invalidity rationale receives five joins.

**Authority and limits:** Under Marks v. United States, 430 U.S. 188, a common result is not enough to create a controlling opinion. The two contextual-reasonableness writings cannot be mechanically combined with the four public-forum votes: a restriction can fail the more protective public-forum test while remaining reasonable in nonpublic property. Conversely, the forum predicates of the published writings are different. No adopted logical-subset rule with majority support is established here. The per curiam expressly preserves the judgment without a unified rationale rather than treating stronger scrutiny as an implicit join in a different test. There is no controlling alternative holding.

**Limits.** The judgment preserves relief against the complete distribution prohibition in the common terminal areas actually at issue. It supplies no categorical license to sell merchandise, solicit immediate personal payment, obstruct circulation, or enter private or secure operational space. Particular viewpoint-neutral conduct and location regulations await their own assessment. No majority doctrine governing sales of literature is established.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Perry and Cornelius remain controlling forum doctrine; no majority airport classification is added. Ward remains the public-forum time, place and manner standard; four Justices' application does not displace nonpublic reasonableness. Schneider's public-street litter rule and Taxpayers for Vincent's distinct treatment of attached signs retain their settings. Jews for Jesus remains authority against a comprehensive airport speech ban; this judgment supplies no broader unified rule. Kokinda's distinction between immediate monetary transactions and taking a leaflet remains relevant through its own published authority, without conversion of its plurality into a majority. Marks is applied to prevent an unsupported synthesis. No precedent is overruled.

From June 26, 1992, the Port Authority remains subject to the declaration and injunction against this complete distribution prohibition. The Court adds no controlling airport classification or reusable constitutional invalidity rationale. Existing forum, reasonableness, viewpoint-neutrality and tailoring rules remain in force. The companion's distinct limited solicitation judgment does not itself validate or invalidate distribution rules.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela finds the ban unreasonable even assuming nonpublic status. A passerby can accept or decline a leaflet without completing a financial exchange. The terminal's ordinary circulation and commercial activities undercut a total claim of incompatibility. Litter, occasional obstruction and the need to distinguish leafletting from solicitation can support appropriate regulation, but on this record do not justify wholesale exclusion. This is contextual reasonableness, not a demand that officials prove every alternative would fail.

O'Connor reaches the same distribution judgment after finding nonpublic status. The Authority's actual multipurpose operation, rather than a statement that transport is its sole purpose, governs the fit of its restriction. She distinguishes the disruption inherent in collecting money from passive distribution.

Kennedy's Part I, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, treats these accessible common areas as public forums based on physical character, public use and compatibility with expression. On that ground the complete distribution ban lacks adequate tailoring and alternative channels. Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, separately supports that public-forum analysis and this judgment while opposing the solicitation result.

White, joined by Scalia and Thomas, would sustain the distribution prohibition under nonpublic reasonableness. They consider cumulative congestion, conversations prompted by literature, litter and monitoring costs sufficient managerial grounds. They do not equate every leaflet with an immediate-payment transaction; their disagreement concerns how much operational justification reasonableness requires.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review of No. 91-339 is complete. Ordinary mandate leaves the Second Circuit's distribution relief in place. No new injunction term, remedial program, administrative rule, further Supreme Court hearing, retained jurisdiction or special mandate-issuance date is ordered. A future challenge to a particular rule would require its own proceeding. The companion supplies the separate limited solicitation-and-receipt disposition.

**End of entry: Lee v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc., plenary merits decision, June 26, 1992.**

### Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette, No. 94-780

Merits decision, June 29, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 26, 1995; decided June 29, 1995. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, which affirmed the district court’s cross-access judgment, 30 F.3d 675. The question is whether the Establishment Clause justifies Ohio’s refusal to permit the applicants’ privately sponsored religious display in Capitol Square’s public forum. The Court also resolves the effect of the permit’s expiration.

All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Stone and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No recusal or vacancy affects the decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Sixth Circuit and issues the opinions identified below.

| Component | Disposition | Justices |
|---|---|---|
| Expiration and jurisdiction | Reach the merits, 9–0 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |
| Cross-access judgment | Affirm, 7–2 | Affirm: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer. Reverse: Stevens, Ginsburg. |

The Sixth Circuit’s judgment is affirmed. The district court’s adjudication after its Rule 65(a)(2) merits trial of Count One stands at its original scope: a permit for December 21–24, 1993. That period has expired. No new permit, extension, mandatory sign term, removal order, damages award or remand is entered. Unappealed rally, damages and other matters are outside this judgment.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing and part | Author and joins | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Part I: reviewability | Stone; Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer join | Opinion of the Court, 9 |
| Parts II–III: protected private access and rejection of Ohio’s categorical defense | Stone; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer join | Opinion of the Court, 7 |
| Part IV: contextual attribution | Stone; O’Connor, Souter, Breyer join | Four-Justice reasoning; not an additional holding of the Court |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment: private equal access | Scalia; Kennedy and Thomas join | Three-Justice separate ground |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment: less restrictive attribution alternatives | Souter; O’Connor and Breyer join | Three-Justice separate ground |
| Dissent from the merits judgment | Stevens; no joins | Agrees with Part I only |
| Dissent from the merits judgment | Ginsburg; no joins | Agrees with Part I only |

The shared holding in Parts II–III controls at its stated level. Neither the four-Justice contextual explanation nor the three-Justice private-access explanation is declared controlling under Marks. One is not shown to be a logical subset of the other.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The expired permit dispute remains reviewable

**Controlling proposition:** A dispute over a short seasonal permit remains live when its duration prevents full review and there is a reasonable expectation that the same claimant will again face the challenged restriction. These applicants meet both requirements; recurrence affecting other speakers alone would not suffice.

**Authority:** Stone, Part I, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine votes for this threshold holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that expiration of the December 1993 permit does not end this controversy. Weinstein v. Bradford requires both a dispute too brief for full litigation and a reasonable expectation that the same complaining party will again face it. A holiday display lasting only days cannot ordinarily complete appellate review before its permit expires. These organized applicants continued to pursue access in an annual forum dispute, and their amended pleading sought relief without the original holiday limitation. Those circumstances support recurrence for these claimants, rather than merely the possibility that someone else might seek a religious display. A broader prayer alone would not prove future exposure, and a newspaper account of possible plans is unnecessary to the conclusion. The Court decides the challenged 1993 restriction. Neither the recurrence exception nor the amended prayer enlarges the judgment into an order governing every future application.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Weinstein v. Bradford: applies both short duration and same-claimant recurrence; the exception does not extend to every recurring public issue.

##### 2. Ohio’s categorical Establishment Clause defense cannot sustain this exclusion

**Controlling proposition:** Ohio may not exclude this genuinely private religious display from Capitol Square’s actual, generally available forum on the categorical ground that its religious message and proximity to the Statehouse necessarily establish religion. The judgment concerns the proposal presented, including its offered private-attribution safeguard; it neither finds the later sign adequate nor creates an unconditional right to every unattended display.

**Authority:** Stone, Parts II–III, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer: seven votes for this bounded speech and access holding. Those seven do not adopt one comprehensive Establishment Clause test.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State’s asserted necessity for excluding this proposal fails. Widmar v. Vincent protects religious expression within a forum actually opened to comparable private uses, and Lamb’s Chapel forbids exclusion of a religious perspective from such permitted expression. Capitol Square is a traditional public forum with diverse private uses; the cross would be privately owned, financed and sponsored. Ohio’s unsuccessful vote to bar unattended displays did not close that forum. The State therefore cannot rely on a general display prohibition that never took effect. Compliance with the Establishment Clause remains a legitimate constitutional obligation, but that obligation does not establish that exclusion is necessary here. The applicant offered readable clarification of private sponsorship, with negotiable wording. The Court rejects the claim that the governmental location makes any such private presentation impermissible. It does not decide the effectiveness of the sign later used, treat the offer as an express injunction condition, or resolve government-sponsored displays, actual religious favoritism, or future permit rules.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Widmar v. Vincent: applies protection of religious expression in a genuinely open forum, while preserving examination of an actual establishment defense.
- Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District: applies its controlling protection against exclusion of a religious perspective from comparable private access; its contextual reasoning is not converted into categorical forum immunity.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman and County of Allegheny v. ACLU: not overruled; the Court does not replace existing establishment law with a comprehensive test in this case.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Weinstein supplies the threshold limits; Widmar and Lamb’s Chapel supply protected private access. Lemon and Allegheny are not overruled. Marks supplies no controlling composite rationale here.

The protected-access holding binds later courts within its actual predicates. Private religious speech remains protected; a genuine establishment concern still requires examination rather than assertion. The Court adopts no categorical immunity for every nominal public forum and no universal disclaimer requirement. Lemon and existing contextual establishment law remain in force. A neutral identification rule may be considered for comparable private displays, but none is ordered or adjudicated here. The proposal, the later physical sign and the injunction’s express terms remain distinct. No holding finds that Ohio approved final sign wording or that the actual sign was adequate.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone’s Part IV, joined by O’Connor, Souter and Breyer.** Context determines whether private expression appears to be governmental religious speech. The forum’s actual history, genuinely private ownership and sponsorship, and effective means of explaining attribution defeat Ohio’s claim that this proposal must be excluded because of its location. An informed reasonable observer knows the setting’s general private uses without being presumed to know every permit rule or newspaper story. For the three joining Justices, adequate dissociation from the State remains material to their application. The generic, negotiable sign offer is part of the proposed display, not a finding that a completed sign worked or that the decree expressly required it. This reasoning does not approve public worship funding, government-sponsored displays, selective religious favoritism, a captive religious exercise, or a purported forum whose circumstances actually attribute the message to the State. It does not impose a constitutional label requirement on all religious speech.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.** Genuinely private religious speech in a publicly announced traditional or designated forum actually open to all on equal terms is not an establishment merely because an observer mistakes its message for the State’s. Public ownership and proximity alone do not convert ordinary permission into religious sponsorship. Widmar’s protection of private access and Lamb’s Chapel’s prohibition of religious viewpoint exclusion support the result; an endorsement-based veto does not supply their sufficient ground. An adequate disclaimer is not a prerequisite to their votes. Actual governmental selection, adoption, religious favoritism, unequal access or a sham forum would change the premises. Kennedy’s agreement does not make coercion the only possible establishment violation. Thomas does not add a factual holding that this particular application was exclusively political. Neutral identification measures remain possible, subject to their actual terms; no unproduced regulation is adjudicated.

**Souter, joined by O’Connor and Breyer.** Even if an unattended cross near the Statehouse can suggest governmental affiliation, complete exclusion is unnecessary where effective clarification can address the concern. A sufficiently clear and prominent disclaimer, or an appropriately identified private-display area, offers a less restrictive alternative. These are alternatives, not cumulative requirements. The contextual concern survives a formal forum label; an ineffective disclaimer or other indications of actual governmental adoption could change the result. The applicants offered clarification, but the sign was not yet written at the hearing, did not name the Klan, and its later effectiveness is not adjudicated. Souter reads the offered attribution measure as part of the proposal before the Court, without adding a condition absent from the decree or assuming Ohio agreed to final wording. This narrower ground rejects Ohio’s insistence that the location makes exclusion necessary regardless of clarification.

**Stevens, dissenting.** The unattended cross at the seat of government conveys governmental religious affiliation even though a private party owns it and other private expression is permitted. The objection concerns what the State’s setting communicates to ordinary viewers, including nonadherents; it does not depend on a finding that every religious speaker becomes the government. Lamb’s Chapel involved identifiable private use in a materially different setting, and Widmar does not automatically establish a right to erect every unattended structure. Identification does not cure the State’s apparent decision to place a potent religious symbol in this governmental setting. Stevens would reverse and remand consistently with that conclusion. His ground is broader than Ginsburg’s objection to the actual injunction’s terms.

**Ginsburg, dissenting.** An offer to use a disclaimer does not establish that the injunction secures an adequate one. The order required access without an express effective-attribution condition, and the record does not settle whether the sign later used adequately dissociated the unattended cross from Ohio. She therefore would reverse the unconditioned access judgment and remand. Her objection does not adopt Stevens’s view that no adequate sign can cure this setting. She reserves whether a materially different, properly conditioned order would satisfy the Establishment Clause; that reservation is not a promised vote to uphold such an order. No removal of an absent cross or categorical exclusion from future seasons follows from her position.

#### Source Notes

The judgment below is Pinette v. Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board, 30 F.3d 675 (6th Cir. 1994). The [docket’s Records and Briefs collection](https://archive.org/details/us-supreme-court) supplies the petition, both merits briefs, reply and joint appendix for No. 94-780. The stipulated application and proposed attribution offer, amended prayer (JA75), hearing testimony concerning the unfinished sign, and final order and notice of appeal (JA178–180) distinguish the proposal from the decree. The order contains no express disclaimer condition. The later sign’s alleged defects and an outside-record press account of future plans are not adopted as findings. The invalid November 18 vote supplies no operative forum closure. The exact later sign’s effectiveness and a complete independently adjudicated permit-code defense are outside this decision. No new factual purpose is inferred from the applicant’s identity.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appellate affirmance completes review of the December 1993 Count One access judgment. Its holiday command remains expired; the mandate affirms without remand or further display instructions. The Court does not decide later rally or damages proceedings, future applications, fees, contempt or bond liability. Future neutral operational or attribution rules require their own lawful application.

**End of entry: Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette, merits decision, June 29, 1995.**

### Chabad-Lubavitch of Georgia v. Miller, No. 93-1047

Merits decision, June 29, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 24, 1995; decided June 29, 1995, after Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette. On certiorari to the en banc United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, 5 F.3d 1383. The questions are whether Georgia may exclude Chabad’s otherwise eligible privately sponsored menorah from the Rotunda’s display forum because of its religious viewpoint, and whether the Establishment Clause requires that exclusion. Only the appealed Rotunda claim, Count II, is before the Court.

All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Stone and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No recusal or vacancy affects either milestone.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the en banc judgment on the Rotunda claim and issues the opinions identified below.

| Component | Action | Justices |
|---|---|---|
| Recurrence, reviewability and Count II scope | Reach the merits; Count I remains outside review, 9–0 | Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |
| En banc reversal of summary judgment for Georgia on Count II | Affirm, 8–1 | Affirm: Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Reverse and restore State summary judgment: Stevens. |
| Additional express attribution inquiry before a future decree | Proposed by four; not added to the Court’s mandate | O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join that added requirement; Stevens would deny this proposal on his distinct ground. |

The Eleventh Circuit’s judgment reversing State summary judgment on Count II is affirmed. The case returns through the ordinary mandate for further proceedings and lawful implementation of equal access, not a new Supreme Court permit. Georgia must provide access on the same neutral terms governing comparable private expression; the Court neither prescribes an actual future decree nor guarantees an unconditional display. The State may apply lawful neutral physical, safety, scheduling or identification rules and may prospectively close or redefine its designated forum through lawful viewpoint-neutral rules. It may not preserve comparable secular access while excluding an otherwise eligible religious viewpoint. No retrospective 1991 permit, fixed sign, permanent seasonal entitlement, damages amount or compulsory forum closure is ordered. Count I remains undisturbed.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing and part | Author and joins | Status |

Direct joins support Parts II and III at their stated levels. No Marks aggregation converts the three-Justice private-access ground and four-Justice remedial inquiry into a different majority rule.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The recurring access dispute remains live, and only the Rotunda claim is reviewed

**Controlling proposition:** A short annual display dispute remains reviewable when full litigation cannot finish within the holiday and the same applicant reasonably expects to seek access again. Chabad’s repeated requests and stated intention to reapply satisfy that rule; this appeal reaches only Count II concerning the Rotunda, not the conceded outdoor-plaza claim.

**Authority:** Souter, Part I, joined by Stone, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: nine votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that passage of Chanukah 1991 does not make this access controversy moot. Weinstein v. Bradford and Murphy v. Hunt require both a dispute too brief for complete review and a reasonable expectation of recurrence for the same applicant. Chabad sought access in successive years and confirmed in the appellate proceedings that it would apply again. The eight-day holiday cannot realistically accommodate the request, trial and appellate process. This is a concrete recurring denial to the same speaker, not merely interest in what might happen to someone else. Pinette applies that same claimant-specific principle without reviving an expired permit. The Court also confines review to the judgment actually appealed. Chabad conceded the State’s right to judgment on the outdoor claim because its unattended-object rule was neutrally enforced there. That rule did not apply inside the Rotunda. Nothing in this decision reopens Count I or grants a retrospective holiday permit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Weinstein v. Bradford and Murphy v. Hunt: apply short duration and same-party recurrence; the exception remains claimant-specific.
- Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette: applies the earlier decision’s limited reviewability rule without extending an expired permit.

##### 2. Georgia’s categorical defense cannot sustain summary judgment excluding the private religious display

**Controlling proposition:** A State that actually opens a designated forum to comparable private unattended expression may not exclude an otherwise eligible religious viewpoint merely by asserting that its Capitol location necessarily establishes religion. On this record that categorical defense does not justify judgment for Georgia; lawful access must be provided on the neutral terms governing comparable private expression, with the actual form of relief left for proceedings below.

**Authority:** Souter, Part II, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight votes for this bounded judgment and speech holding; Stevens dissents from its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that summary judgment for Georgia cannot stand. Perry Education Association recognizes a forum the State has actually opened to private expression, and Widmar protects religious expression within the access granted to comparable speakers. Lamb’s Chapel forbids exclusion of a religious perspective from such permitted expression. The Rotunda admitted diverse private uses on neutral terms, including sizeable unattended exhibits for several days. Chabad’s proposed menorah is privately owned and sponsored. The outdoor prohibition cannot justify an indoor exclusion where it does not apply. Pinette rejects a categorical establishment defense grounded merely in religious content and governmental proximity; the interior setting must be considered without making it conclusive. Affirming the appellate judgment removes Georgia’s summary judgment, not every unresolved question about an eventual decree. The Court does not endorse all the en banc opinion’s broader reasoning, command an unconditional display, or prescribe sign wording. Neutral size, safety, scheduling and attribution rules remain subject to their actual lawful terms.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association: applies the actual designated-forum status and preserves lawful neutral management and prospective forum changes.
- Widmar v. Vincent and Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District: apply protection of comparable private religious expression and the prohibition on religious viewpoint exclusion.
- Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette: applies the bounded rejection of a categorical establishment defense, not a universal forum exemption.

##### 3. Interior location does not itself make clearly private expression governmental religion

**Controlling proposition:** Neutral access does not establish religion merely because a genuine private-expression forum lies inside a state capitol, when the actual context makes clear that the religious expression remains private rather than governmental. Actual selection, financing, control, adoption, preferential treatment or obscured private sponsorship can change that conclusion; neither a forum label nor any unspecified sign automatically resolves attribution.

**Authority:** Souter, Part III, joined by Stone, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: five votes for this contextual rule. The additional decree-specific inquiry proposed in Part IV has only O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; it is not a five-Justice mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that attribution depends on the actual governmental relationship to the expression. County of Allegheny requires attention to the setting, and Pinette preserves that inquiry while rejecting exclusion based solely on governmental proximity. The central interior location and the State’s own uses of the Rotunda matter; so do its established private uses and the identity of the private sponsor. A reasonable informed observer need not know every permit record to distinguish a genuinely private exhibit from the State’s religious message. Effective clarification may explain that distinction without treating all religious expression as governmental worship. The identifying greeting names Chabad but does not expressly disclaim State sponsorship, and the offer of an appropriate disclaimer is not a finding that a completed safeguard works. The Court therefore adopts no universal sign requirement or presumption that any sign cures governmental adoption. This rule defeats the claim that location alone requires exclusion while reserving actual future relief and display configurations.

**Precedent treatment:**

- County of Allegheny v. ACLU: preserves contextual examination of apparent governmental affiliation, rather than title alone.
- Capitol Square Review and Advisory Board v. Pinette: preserves its shared access holding and distinct attribution rationales; no categorical immunity is drawn from its separate opinions.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman: remains in force; no comprehensive replacement establishment test is adopted.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Perry preserves actual designated-forum status and lawful prospective management; Widmar and Lamb’s Chapel protect comparable private religious speech. Pinette’s bounded holding is applied, and its separate opinions remain distinct. Allegheny and Lemon continue to govern contextual establishment analysis. Lee and Kiryas Joel are not transformed into rules approving governmental religious adoption; Zobrest supplies no aid-merits holding. Hurley and McIntyre do not predecide the constitutionality of an unproduced display-identification regulation.

The Rotunda’s actual designated-forum status and the holding against this categorical exclusion control. The Court’s five-Justice contextual rule makes interior location relevant but not dispositive; actual private attribution and absence of governmental adoption matter. Neither private title nor a forum label alone supplies universal immunity. The separate four-Justice requirement for an additional attribution inquiry is not promoted into a majority mandate, and Stone’s permission for neutral identification rules remains permission. No sign wording, universal label duty, preferential religious access or obligation to keep the forum open is created. Lemon remains in force. Pinette is applied at its actual shared holding and separately identified rationales.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter’s Part IV, joined by O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer.** The State has not shown that complete exclusion is necessary when effective attribution alternatives remain available. A clear and prominent disclaimer or an adequately identified private-display area could address the concern; these are alternatives, not cumulative conditions. The district court did not separately determine why all such measures would fail. The four would require actual relief proceedings to determine and secure adequate dissociation from government before compelling this interior presentation. They do not find that the existing greeting performs that function, that the proposed disclaimer already exists, or that any unproduced wording will be effective. They also do not reopen the established forum classification, invent a new cause of action, or authorize perpetual access. Their insistence on an express additional inquiry receives four votes and is not a new condition in the Court’s mandate. The Court’s five-Justice contextual rule and other governing constitutional requirements remain applicable to actual future relief.

**Stone, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** Viewpoint neutrality and actual attribution govern. Georgia opened the Rotunda to private unattended displays and may not preserve access for secular perspectives while excluding a comparable religious one. The menorah is privately owned and sponsored. The forum’s actual private uses, neutral admission and identification of the private speaker make this proposed access distinct from governmental worship or adoption of doctrine. Indoor location matters but is not conclusive. Identification or a suitable disclaimer can make the private sponsorship plain, without a ten-factor formula or a new general sign mandate. Stone joins the Court’s contextual rule and bounded judgment, but does not turn his permission for neutral identification measures into Part IV’s additional compulsory adequacy inquiry. The State must provide equal access under lawful comparable terms. It need not maintain the forum, grant preferential placement or duration, disregard physical or safety limits, or supply governmental assistance. He reserves a State-selected, designed, financed, controlled or adopted religious exhibition, a pretextually open forum, and obscured private sponsorship coupled with governmental adoption. The yellow greeting identifies Chabad; it is not described as an already adjudicated express governmental disclaimer.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Genuine private expression in a designated forum actually open on equal terms does not become the State’s religion from indoor location or mistaken observer attribution alone. Pinette’s private-access concurrence supplies their sufficient ground, alongside Widmar and Lamb’s Chapel’s protection of comparable private religious speech. The Rotunda’s actual neutral admissions and comparable unattended exhibits distinguish it from an official religious presentation. A disclaimer is not a constitutional prerequisite to their votes. They join the bounded judgment but refuse Part III’s endorsement methodology and Part IV’s added inquiry before access. Ordinary proceedings may implement lawful access and neutral rules; they need not impose a fresh endorsement condition on unchanged genuinely private and equal-access predicates. State selection, privilege, adoption or a sham forum would be different. Kennedy does not make coercion the sole possible establishment violation, and Thomas does not recast the menorah as exclusively secular. No universal right to all government interiors or ruling on an unquoted identification rule follows.

**Ginsburg, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** Pinette involved an already entered injunction commanding access without an express effective-attribution condition. An offer to negotiate a disclaimer did not secure one in that decree. The judgment here merely reverses summary judgment for Georgia. It does not itself command an unconditioned display, so affirming it leaves room to settle actual lawful relief. Georgia’s categorical exclusion cannot be sustained without showing why appropriate dissociation measures could not address the asserted affiliation. This distinction preserves, rather than abandons, her Pinette objection. Before an injunction compels the interior display, she would require enforceable adequate attribution on actual terms. Neither a holiday greeting naming Chabad nor a promise to negotiate proves that requirement met. She does not promise approval of any particular disclaimer and does not adopt Stevens’s conclusion that identification can never cure this presentation. Her decree-specific requirement is part of the four-Justice Part IV, while she joins the Court’s five-Justice contextual rule at its stated conditional level.

**Stevens, dissenting; no joins.** The prominent, isolated religious symbol in the Capitol’s central interior conveys governmental religious affiliation notwithstanding private title and ordinary admission of other expression. This presentation is more closely associated with the government’s own institutional setting than the outdoor forum in Pinette. He accepts Pinette’s limited holding but distinguishes this interior use, where the State also speaks in its own name. The greeting does not disclaim sponsorship, and he concludes that the offered identification alternative does not cure the particular governmental association. That is his contextual legal conclusion, not a finding that an installed sign was tested and failed. Widmar and Lamb’s Chapel protect private expression without requiring every unattended presentation at the seat of government. He would reverse and restore summary judgment for Georgia on Count II, without banning all religious expression, deciding Count I or ordering the Rotunda closed. A materially different presentation would require its own examination.

#### Source Notes

The [en banc decision, 5 F.3d 1383](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/005/5.F3d.1383.92-8008.html), reversed State summary judgment on Count II. The [prior panel decision, 976 F.2d 1386](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/976/976.F2d.1386.92-8008.html), includes the district court’s order and Judge Kravitch’s separate discussion of the offered appropriate disclaimer and Chabad’s intention to reapply. The sources establish an identifying yellow holiday greeting, not an already approved express disclaimer of governmental sponsorship. No exact future sign text, dimensions, placement or effectiveness finding is supplied. The State’s planned Christmas tree would arrive after the relevant Chanukah period; no combined secularizing presentation is assumed. The lower judgments establish the difference between the outdoor unattended-object rule and the indoor forum. The original Supreme Court petition, briefs and a detailed final access decree are not supplied. Those limits do not establish an adverse fact about an uncreated sign or permit.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment affirming the Eleventh Circuit’s reversal issues on Count II. Further proceedings settle and implement actual lawful relief consistently with the Court’s holdings, without an already entered Supreme Court access injunction. No expired holiday period is revived. Count I, damages amounts, fees and unpresented future applications are not decided. Georgia may adopt lawful neutral forum rules or a genuinely prospective viewpoint-neutral closure or redefinition; the Court orders none.

### Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, No. 94-329

Merits decision, June 29, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 1, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, which sustained denial of the publication’s printing benefit on Establishment Clause grounds after identifying viewpoint discrimination. The federal speech and establishment questions concern this program.

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Fourth Circuit and requires removal of the religious-viewpoint exclusion from the generally available printing benefit.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Viewpoint exclusion and asserted Establishment justification | Reversed, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Viewpoint-neutral eligibility on otherwise lawful program terms; remand |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |

| Concurrence | O’Connor | Unjoined | Full bounded Court join; safeguards and compelled-fee challenge reserved |
| Concurrence | Thomas | Unjoined | Full Court join; separate civic-benefit/history explanation |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would affirm because of direct devotional-production support |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Religious viewpoint in the private publication program

**Controlling proposition:** The University may not exclude an otherwise eligible private student publication from its generally available printing benefit because its editorial viewpoint is religious. The rule applies to this privately edited, independently attributed publication program on its otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms; it does not require funding every religious activity or every subject.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas; five Justices adopt the program-specific viewpoint rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the University’s decision to support independent student expression does not make each publication its own speech. Perry permits definition of a limited forum and its subjects, but not discrimination against a religious perspective on otherwise included subjects. Lamb’s Chapel and Widmar supply religious-viewpoint equality in genuinely opened private-expression channels. Here the broad publication program leaves editing with the students, requires independence disclaimers and pays approved outside printing expenses. The denial treats primarily religious belief or manifestation as a forbidden activity while allowing comparable student perspectives. That operation excludes viewpoint rather than neutrally defining a government message. The Court does not erase the distinction between recognition, facilities, distribution and money, or equate every expenditure with a speech forum. Nor does it find the organization ineligible under a separate organizational rule. The religious-viewpoint exclusion must be removed; other lawful program terms remain. Actual payment amounts and properly available independent defenses remain subject to the federal viewpoint holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association: preserves limited-forum definition while forbidding viewpoint discrimination.
- Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches; Widmar v. Vincent: apply private religious-viewpoint equality without assuming that facility access decides every subsidy.
- Regan v. Taxation With Representation: distinguishes definition of a government financing program from discriminatory exclusion within this opened private-publication arrangement.

##### Permissible printing support on the actual program terms

**Controlling proposition:** Providing this neutral printing benefit to the otherwise eligible private religious publication does not itself violate the Establishment Clause. Its secular student-expression purpose, broad neutral availability, independent editorial attribution and controlled vendor payments matter together; neutrality alone is not a universal authorization of devotional financing or governmental sponsorship of worship.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas join this concrete program conclusion. Lemon remains governing law; no new universal framework is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the publication’s religious message remains private under the actual safeguards of this program. The fund serves broad student expression rather than government worship, and officials approve printing expenses without adopting the students’ editorial choices. Required disclaimers and independent organizations separate the private message from the University. Vendor payments provide the defined printing service rather than unrestricted grants to churches. Witters and Mueller supply the relevance of neutral benefits and private direction, without making beneficiary neutrality conclusive in every setting. Under the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry, this arrangement does not become governmental religious speech merely because one publication includes devotion and proselytizing. Kiryas Joel’s denominational allocation of governmental power is different; its neutrality limits and retention of Lemon remain. The contrary direct-financing objection gives too little weight to the concrete private-expression structure. Zobrest’s actual procedural remand supplies no aid-merits approval. Compelled-fee refund claims, unrestricted religious support and any particular monetary award remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lemon v. Kurtzman: retains the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry; no universal replacement is adopted.
- Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind; Mueller v. Allen: apply neutral-benefit and private-direction distinctions within the actual program, not neutrality alone.
- Board of Education of Kiryas Joel v. Grumet: distinguishes denominational governmental authority and retains religious-neutrality limits.
- Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills: preserves the actual antecedent-law remand; supplies no religious-aid merits holding here.
- Lee v. Weisman: preserves limits on governmental religious participation and coercion; this private printing benefit compels no student worship.
- Church of Scientology v. Clearwater: distinguishes RFRA burdens and exemptions from constitutional financing authority.

The denial concerned religious activity, defined by the Guidelines as primarily promoting or manifesting belief in or about deity or ultimate reality, rather than an established finding of religious-organization ineligibility. The organization rule separately concerns devotion as organizational purpose. Political restrictions separately exclude principal-purpose lobbying and candidate electioneering while permitting incidental or insubstantial advocacy, nonpartisan study/research and ideological views; those permissions are not exceptions to the religious-activity rule. Permitted ability-to-perform membership limits likewise remain distinct. Abandoned free-exercise/state individual-rights claims and unchallenged equal-protection intent findings are not reopened.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Lemon remains governing; Zobrest is procedural rather than an aid-merits holding. No denominational political power, compulsory worship or unrestricted religious subsidy is authorized.

Otherwise eligible private student publications receive viewpoint-neutral access to this defined printing benefit. Independent expression and the program’s safeguards do constitutional work; this is no rule that every public expenditure creates a forum or every formally neutral religious subsidy is lawful.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor concurs and joins the Court in full at its program-specific level. Organizational independence and disclaimers, approved vendor payments, a vigorous competing forum and the availability of a properly presented objection by students who oppose compelled fees provide safeguards; the last is a possible challenge, not an existing opt-out or refund award. Neutrality alone is insufficient. Thomas also concurs, joining the Court while explaining his own view that equal participation in broadly available civic benefits should not turn solely on a financial-versus-in-kind classification; that broader historical theory is not adopted by the Court. Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents. Mandatory fees pay for devotional production, and routing money to the printer does not turn that support into mere facility access. Ball and Nyquist distinguish direct support of religious activity; the beneficiary’s own editorial choice is not an independent third party’s selection under Witters and Mueller. The dissent does not exclude all religious viewpoints or invalidate every neutral private religious benefit.

#### Source Notes

The asserted Virginia funding defense is distinct from abandoned affirmative state-rights claims. Its applicability and available remedies remain unresolved; no payment amount is guaranteed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit is reversed and the case remanded. The University shall remove the religious-viewpoint exclusion and apply otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms. Any properly available independent Virginia funding defense must be examined consistently with the federal viewpoint holding; no applicability, validity or revival of forfeited defenses is declared. No full requested amount, damages, immunity, fee or compelled-fee refund determination is made.
