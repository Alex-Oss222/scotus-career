### 1997-01-06 — DeAngelis v. El Paso Municipal Police Officers' Association; Fifth Circuit No. 93-8700, 51 F.3d 591; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied

Authority: [Record](../records/DeAngelis_v_El_Paso_Association_merits_1997-01-06.md).

#### Holdings

#### I. The credited course of publication does not meet Title VII's objective threshold

**Holding and operative rule:** Sex-based harassment violates Title VII when the complete circumstances establish conduct sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter employment conditions, objectively hostile or abusive and actually perceived as abusive; no physical advance, superior-subordinate relationship, psychological injury or measurable work loss is indispensable. Crediting this verdict record and favorable reasonable inferences, the supported course of newsletter commentary falls below that objective threshold, so the Association's hostile-environment judgment is affirmed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; six direct votes support this rule and application. Stone-Zsela's judgment vote supplies no additional statutory join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Harris's all-circumstances inquiry, which preserves an objective boundary while rejecting injury and productivity prerequisites. Meritor supplies the requirement that discriminatory harassment alter working conditions. The ten reproduced columns must be considered together, including general comments about women, the references associated with DeAngelis, extensive workplace circulation, humiliation and alleged interference with command. General comments are not excluded merely because they do not name her.

The jury's favorable inferences receive their proper force. Even on that account, the irregular sequence, degree, limited direct targeting, editorial setting and actual workplace responses establish offensive intermittent commentary rather than the required abusive alteration. Broad circulation and repeated ridicule matter, but do not eliminate that statutory distinction. Good ratings, upheld discipline and supportive responses are contextual evidence, not indispensable defenses. Neither an invitation to respond nor the membership's protection of the author's anonymity creates consent or a numerical safe harbor.

The lower opinion's suggestions that equal opportunity must be destroyed or work performance palpably deteriorate are disapproved. Citation to Harris alone would not cure those suggestions. The Court instead independently applies the correct standard and sustains the judgment on that ground. It neither disbelieves DeAngelis's testimony nor requires physical threats, advances or supervisory harassment. Because the statutory ground is sufficient, the Court does not decide the preserved First Amendment defense. It adopts no newsletter immunity and no general rule that offensive expression cannot constitute harassment.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris is applied without adding a prerequisite; Meritor's alteration-of-conditions boundary remains controlling. No constitutional speech precedent is extended by this holding.

#### II. The complaint-related publications establish no actionable retaliatory consequence here

**Holding and operative rule:** Protected activity and hostile commentary do not alone establish Title VII retaliation without a cognizable adverse consequence. The mocking EEOC reference, routine minutes reporting the complaint and announced intention to sue if the action proved groundless establish no denial of an employment or Association benefit, concrete imposed penalty or comparably supported material disadvantage on this record; judgment for the Association is therefore affirmed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part II, joined by all eight other Justices; unanimous direct authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court distinguishes opposition to a discrimination complaint from a proved retaliatory consequence. The publications reveal disparagement and contemplated conditional litigation, but do not establish lost pay, promotion, assignment, authority, membership, representation or a comparable imposed disadvantage caused by the complaint. No actual retaliatory lawsuit or unreported sanction is found. The same record can raise distinct hostile-environment and retaliation questions; disagreement on the former does not supply the missing consequence for the latter.

This record-specific conclusion does not confine all actionable retaliation to ultimate employment decisions. It does not hold that threats, harassment or public shaming can never establish a materially adverse consequence on stronger facts, and it decides no comprehensive standard for every retaliation claim. The Court does not infer failure from missing research materials or convert the opportunity to answer the newsletter into a condition of protection.

**Precedent treatment:** Harris's distinction between offensive treatment and legally sufficient discrimination is preserved at its hostile-environment scope; its injury rule is not converted into a comprehensive retaliation test.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Harris v. Forklift Systems: applies the objective and subjective totality inquiry and rejects mandatory work-loss, injury, advance and hierarchy requirements; the present application adds no such requirement.
- Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson: applies the requirement of discriminatory alteration of employment conditions; extreme examples remain illustrations.
- Wisconsin v. Mitchell, Hurley and R. A. V.: their existing holdings remain intact. The Court does not reach the expressive defense, adopt R. A. V.'s separate content-selection rationale, or decide a general immunity for association publications.

#### Law After Decision

Effective January 6, 1997, the particular newsletter record is insufficient under Harris even with testimony and reasonable verdict-favorable inferences credited. Title VII retains its contextual severe-or-pervasive test, with no numerical publication threshold or mandatory tangible injury. Retaliation fails here for lack of a supported material consequence; the decision leaves broader retaliation categories open. No controlling First Amendment rule is added. Stone-Zsela's expressive ground and Stevens and Ginsburg's proposed hostile-environment remand remain noncontrolling.

### 1997-01-14 — Walters v. Metropolitan Educational Enterprises, Inc., No. 95-259; Equal Employment Opportunity Commission v. Metropolitan Educational Enterprises, Inc., No. 95-779.

Authority: [Record](../records/Walters_v_Metropolitan_Educational_Enterprises_merits_1997-01-14.md).

#### Holdings

#### I. Count genuine employment relationships on each working day

**Holding and operative rule:** For Title VII's requirement of fifteen or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year, count an individual on every working day during an existing employment relationship, whether or not that person worked or was paid that day. Payroll ordinarily evidences that relationship but is not conclusive: a person not yet hired, already separated, an independent contractor or within an express statutory exclusion does not become an employee merely by appearing on a list.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion, joined by all other participating Justices: nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute counts employees the employer has, not daily units of compensated labor. Employment continues across ordinary unpaid days, part-time schedules and leave. The daily requirement still does real work: an employee must have the relationship on each pertinent working day, so days before hiring or after separation do not count. Metropolitan's alternative counted paid leave; it is therefore a compensation rule, not simply an actual-work rule. But compensation on a particular day does not follow from Congress's choice of the employment relationship. Darden supplies the common-law agency approach to a circular employee definition, considering all relevant incidents without a single decisive factor. Town & Country's compatible-service and actual-control analysis supports attention to the real relationship within each statute's terms. Neither case had already decided Title VII's daily-counting language. Payroll offers ordinary administrable evidence without displacing legal status. The Court adopts no exclusion or worker-status dispute absent from this stipulated record, and does not decide whether numerosity is jurisdictional.

**Qualifications:** The industry-affecting-commerce requirement and employer-agent language remain. Employer exclusions remain for the United States, wholly United States-owned corporations, Indian tribes, District of Columbia departments or agencies subject by statute to competitive-service procedures, and bona fide private membership clubs other than labor organizations exempt under §501(c). The first-year-after-March-24-1972 transition for employers with fewer than twenty-five employees does not govern this period. The separate employee exclusions remain for elected state or local public officers, their chosen personal staff, policymaking appointees and immediate advisers concerning the office's constitutional or legal powers; those exclusions do not cover employees subject to applicable state or local civil-service laws. Section 2000e(f)'s inclusion of United States citizens employed abroad does not alter this domestic count. No exclusion is found to cover the two part-time employees here.

**Precedent treatment:** Darden's genuine-relationship framework is applied by analogy at its common-law scope; Town & Country's attention to compatible employment and enacted exclusions is preserved without transplanting NLRA exclusions into Title VII.

#### II. The stipulated minimum satisfies the 1990 numerical requirement

**Holding and operative rule:** Under relationship counting, the stipulated 1990 record establishes at least thirty-eight weeks in which Metropolitan had the required employees on each working day, exceeding the twenty-week requirement. That minimum resolves the 1990 numerical objection to the retaliatory-discharge claim without a remand to recompute an exact total or litigate an unsupported worker-status dispute.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by all other participating Justices: nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The pertinent years for the retaliatory-discharge claim are 1990 and 1989. The stipulated failure to reach the threshold in 1989 remains, but success in 1990 suffices. The original count identified forty-seven weekly payroll counts without ensuring presence on every working day in each week. Even excluding all nine weeks potentially affected by midweek employment changes leaves forty-seven minus nine, or thirty-eight, qualifying weeks. No larger exact total is needed, and the Court does not decide whether an excluded week might also qualify. The separate nine-week figure under the rejected compensation method describes a different calculation; the record does not identify those as the same weeks. Metropolitan disputed the relationship criterion rather than the use of payroll evidence to establish the stipulated relationships. The legal possibility that payroll can be rebutted elsewhere therefore supplies no present factual contest. This application does not classify every possible payroll entry or establish continuous threshold employment throughout all fifty-two weeks.

**Precedent treatment:** Darden and Town & Country preserve the distinction between legal status and an evidentiary list; neither requires relitigation of these stipulated relationships.

#### III. The unpresented promotion-year issue receives no relief

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court declines to review the separate coverage-year discrepancy for Walters's September 1989 promotion claim because she raised no objection to it in her petition or merits brief and no supported exceptional reason warrants review. The existing promotion dismissal remains undisturbed on that prudential basis, without deciding its substantive correctness, an unknown 1988 count or intentional waiver.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct joins. Stone-Zsela agrees only with the disposition leaving the promotion dismissal undisturbed and does not join this explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory current-or-preceding-year formula identifies 1988 and 1989 for the September 1989 promotion claim. The District Court instead used 1989 and 1990. That mismatch is a separate claim-specific issue; the supported omission from both petition and merits brief is an actual filing omission, not an inference from unavailable papers. Yee preserves the independent question-presented limit. Williams permits review of a question actually passed upon in its particular circumstances; the district court's choice of years does not establish such appellate consideration. Zafiro shows that declining review may leave a judgment undisturbed without a merits rejection or intentional-waiver finding. United States National Bank and Lebron permit consideration of included legal questions on established records, but do not require it or authorize automatic restoration of a claim after the Court declines review. The retaliation counting question can be answered without a 1988 finding. The Court neither treats the missing count as evidence against Walters nor uses its absence to reopen the promotion judgment. It does not hold that 1990 is a proper year for that claim, that the promotion was lawful or that review is jurisdictionally barred.

**Precedent treatment:** Yee and Zafiro support bounded nonreview and its undisturbed-judgment consequence; Williams, United States National Bank and Lebron retain their permissive, circumstance-specific scope and supply no mandatory promotion remand.

#### IV. The remand concerns retaliation, with the numerical objection resolved

**Holding and operative rule:** The compensation-based judgment is reversed within the retaliatory-discharge dispute in the consolidated cases, and that dispute is remanded for unresolved liability, genuinely remaining defenses and appropriate relief under the correct counting rule. The 1990 numerical objection is resolved, while the promotion dismissal remains undisturbed and this mandate supplies no authority to reopen its calendar years, preservation or unknown 1988 count.

**Authority:** Part IV of Souter's opinion has nine direct joins concerning reversal and further retaliation proceedings; its promotion-disposition explanation has the same eight-Justice coalition as Part III, with Stone-Zsela agreeing only to that disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Meeting the statutory count establishes no retaliation, discrimination, causation, damages or ultimate liability. Those questions remain for ordinary adjudication. Conversely, the established minimum requires no remand merely to determine whether thirty-eight exceeds twenty. The mandate must respect the claim-specific limit stated in Part III: nonreview leaves an existing dismissal in place without a new affirmance of its substantive correctness. The Court expresses no view about an independently available procedure outside this mandate. The lower courts' description of dismissal as jurisdictional remains procedural history, not a new Supreme Court classification of numerosity.

**Precedent treatment:** Zafiro's distinction between nonreview and merits adjudication governs the promotion limit; Darden and Town & Country provide no automatic liability or remedial award from employee classification.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden: its common-law agency approach to a circular employee definition informs genuine relationship counting; no individual status is inferred from a payroll label alone.
- NLRB v. Town & Country Electric: its compatible-service and actual-control analysis is used at its stated NLRA scope; Title VII's own exclusions govern here.
- Yee v. City of Escondido: the distinction between an included argument and a separate question remains, together with the prudential question-presented limit.
- United States v. Williams: permission to review a question actually passed upon below does not require review of this omitted promotion-year issue.
- Zafiro v. United States: prudential nonreview can leave the existing judgment undisturbed without adjudicating merits, jurisdictional character or intentional waiver.
- United States National Bank of Oregon and Lebron: their permissive governing-law and included-argument rules do not mandate review or restoration of the promotion claim.

#### Law After Decision

Title VII's daily employee count follows genuine employment relationships, ordinarily shown by payroll, with hiring, separation, legal status and enacted exclusions preserved. Payment on every working day is not required. The stipulated minimum resolves this employer's 1990 numerical objection for retaliation; it does not resolve ultimate liability or the jurisdictional character of numerosity. The separate September 1989 promotion claim receives no reopening through this mandate.

### 1997-02-18 - Robinson v. Shell Oil Co.; No. 95-1376.

Authority: [Record](../records/Robinson_v_Shell_Oil_merits_1997-02-18.md).

#### Holdings

##### Former employees fall within this retaliation protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Title VII §704(a)'s protection of employees includes former employees alleging postemployment retaliation by their former employer because they engaged in Title VII-protected activity. Former-employee status therefore cannot alone support dismissal of Robinson's claim; inclusion within the protected category does not establish retaliatory conduct, motive, causation, actionable harm or a remedy.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Title VII's definition identifies an employment relationship but does not supply one temporal answer for every statutory use. Section 704(a) contains no current-employment qualifier. Its protection for making a charge or participating in enforcement must be read with the discharge and remedial provisions: discharge complaints ordinarily continue after employment ends, and §706(g)'s reinstatement of employees confirms that the noun can include people no longer employed. References and blacklisting can punish that continued resort to enforcement. This enacted context resolves the temporal meaning; desirable policy does not override an unambiguous exclusion.

The express applicants category does not require the opposite result. Applicants need never have had an employment relationship, whereas former employees did; the separate category does not transform the latter into strangers. Nor does a uniform statutory definition require one time of reference. Walters counts existing relationships on each specified working day under §701(b); separated workers remain excluded from subsequent daily counts. Section 704(a) contains no corresponding daily-count requirement. Darden's worker-status inquiry and Town & Country's compatible-service analysis likewise require attention to each statute's own work, rather than a universal temporal definition.

The complaint alleges a false, unfavorable reference after Robinson's EEOC charge. Those allegations have not been adjudicated. The Court reverses the categorical coverage dismissal and remands for unresolved conduct, prohibited reason, causation, legally required harm, defenses and relief. It defines no general actionable-harm standard, new employment-nexus element, universal postemployment tort or deference rule. The separate discriminatory-discharge merits are not reopened.

**Precedent treatment:** Walters's genuine-relationship daily count is preserved at its §701(b) scope; Darden and Town & Country supply attention to legal relationship and statutory context without deciding the temporal reach of §704(a).

#### Precedent Treatment

- Walters v. Metropolitan Educational Enterprises, Inc.: distinguished by the §701(b) daily time units; its exclusion of separated workers from later daily employer-size counts remains intact and its reservation of retaliation liability is respected.
- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden: preserves its ERISA common-law agency status inquiry; no universal rule terminating statutory protections at separation is inferred.
- NLRB v. Town & Country Electric, Inc.: its attention to actual compatible employment and each statute's exclusions supports context-sensitive interpretation; its NLRA holding does not itself decide Title VII temporal coverage.

#### Law After Decision

Former employees are within §704(a)'s retaliation protection at the scope stated. Employment separation does not itself defeat that coverage; it still ends later daily relationship counts under Walters. Coverage remains distinct from proving a prohibited retaliatory act and obtaining relief. No general standard of actionable postemployment harm, causation rule, liability finding or new remedy is adopted.

### 1997-01-06 — DeAngelis v. El Paso Municipal Police Officers' Association; Fifth Circuit No. 93-8700, 51 F.3d 591; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** He joins the retaliation holding but rejects statutory insufficiency on hostile environment. The repeated sex-derogatory message, distribution to more than seven hundred colleagues, connections to DeAngelis, recurring comments and workplace incidents, humiliation, claimed loss of authority, promotion deterrence and refusal to identify the columnist permit a rational jury's severe-or-pervasive finding under Harris. Physical threats, supervisory power, reduced performance and the written medium are not statutory barriers. He therefore reaches the preserved expressive defense.

In his view, this award punishes protected editorial opinion or satire solely for its sex-derogatory viewpoint. Such civil liability is content based and is not narrowly tailored here to the compelling interest in eradicating sex discrimination. The publication itself is the independently asserted wrong; the record supplies no separate discriminatory employment act, true threat, authority-backed directive or denial of employment or associational rights to which the expression is integral or evidentiary. Hurley preserves the expressive inquiry, and Mitchell distinguishes punishment of protected belief from use of expression to establish an independently unlawful act. He would prohibit Title VII damages resting solely on such protected opinion or satire, while preserving liability for discriminatory decisions, quid pro quo demands, true threats, authority-backed directives and expression integral to or used as evidence of independently unlawful employment conduct. His rule is confined to this expressive application and does not declare that speech cannot itself be workplace harassment. That constitutional ground alone supports his hostile-environment affirmance. On retaliation, he finds no material employment or membership disadvantage caused by the complaint and leaves stronger threats, harassment and public-shaming cases open.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Ginsburg.** They join Part II but would reverse the hostile-environment portion and remand. Harris protects disadvantageous conditions without proof of lost output. The lower opinion's reliance on destroyed opportunity, palpable performance deterioration, missing advances and absence of a superior harasser materially narrowed its review of the jury verdict. Its correct abstract recitation of Harris and the substantial contrary contextual evidence do not cure that error. Repeated sex-based attacks, broad circulation, mocking, humiliation and alleged impairment of command warrant review without those added thresholds. Ginsburg's Harris explanation makes professional disadvantage relevant without measurable productivity loss. They find a review error, not liability as a matter of law. Mitchell and Hurley keep the preserved expressive defense distinct; it and any remaining properly preserved grounds must be resolved before liability could stand. They would restore neither damages nor a particular allocation and reject both automatic newsletter immunity and automatic loss of protection for an offensive editorial.
