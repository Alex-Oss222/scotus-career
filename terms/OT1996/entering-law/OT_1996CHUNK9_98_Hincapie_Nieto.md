## Standards and Tests selection: Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct

### Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct

**Governing question:** How courts decide whether new legislation governs events preceding enactment.

**Current rule:** First determine whether Congress prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. If it did, that direction governs subject to independent constitutional limits. Otherwise ask whether applying the provision would attach new legal consequences to completed events by impairing existing rights, increasing liability for past conduct or imposing new duties concerning completed transactions. Such operation requires clear congressional intent. Pending litigation alone does not answer the question. Procedural, jurisdictional and prospective-relief labels are not substitutes for examining the provision’s actual operation. Legislative correction of a judicial construction, or a purpose to restore an earlier understanding, does not by itself establish retrospective reach; this legislative inquiry differs from the temporal operation of judicial decisions.

**Authority by component:**

- Sequential temporal inquiry: Landgraf v. USI Film Products — Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s general-framework portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this organizing rule. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur on a distinct relevant-activity approach, not as additional joins at this level of generality.
- Corrective purpose and distinction from judicial interpretation: Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc — Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, controls by five votes at this explanatory level. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the judicial/legislative distinction through their separate textual method, not through a presumed full join.

**Present operation effective:** Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc, 1994-04-26.

**Holdings navigation:** Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences; Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach.

**Exceptions and limits:** An express congressional direction concerning the temporal operation of a statute resolves the threshold statutory question, subject to applicable constitutional limits. Such a direction does not alone determine whether Congress may constitutionally require reconsideration of an already completed Article III judgment. Landgraf concerns statutory consequences for earlier conduct in litigation properly before the courts and does not decide that separate final-judgment issue.

**Related current rules:** Congressional reopening of completed private federal judgments supplies Morgan Stanley's and Plaut's separate Article III rule for §27A(b).

## Standards and Tests selection: Supreme Court original habeas after an authorization-review bar

### Supreme Court original habeas after an authorization-review bar

**Governing question:** The retained original-writ channel and extraordinary showing following §2244(b)(3)(E).

**Current rule:** The statutory ban on ordinary certiorari review of a circuit’s successive-application authorization decision does not repeal §2241’s distinct Supreme Court original habeas authority. An original petition receives independent consideration rather than appellate review of that authorization judgment. Circuit permission to file in district court is not a prerequisite to filing here. Sections 2244(b)(1)–(2) inform original-writ discretion; whether they bind it directly remains unresolved. Rule 20 separately requires an explanation for not seeking district relief, how and where state remedies were exhausted or §2254(b) otherwise satisfied, exceptional circumstances warranting the Supreme Court’s discretionary exercise and lack of adequate relief in another form or court. Retained jurisdiction alone does not warrant the writ, further proceedings or an execution stay. Section 1651 aids independently held jurisdiction rather than supplying a freestanding new source.

**Authority by component:**

- Separate jurisdiction and original-writ discretion: The withdrawn review route and retained original jurisdiction; Successive restrictions and original-writ discretion; The separate constitutional innocence claim and original relief, 1996-06-28; Felker Parts I-A, II and IV-B, all nine.

**Present operation effective:** Felker, 1996-06-28.

**Exceptions and limits:** Felker’s as-applied constitutional rejection rests on actual independent consideration and an adequate traditional original-writ ground, not approval of eliminating every meaningful forum. Constitutional innocence’s universal merits threshold, other supervisory routes, and the constitutionality of all AEDPA restrictions remain open.

**Holdings navigation:** Felker v. Turpin, 518 U.S. 651 (1996).

#### Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244

**Docket or dockets:** No. 92-757.

**Decided:** 1994-04-26.

For the other questions in Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 1994-04-26, No. 92-757, see Civil Rights — Section 102 does not add its new damages to this preenactment conduct; Civil Rights — The jury election depends on an available statutory damages claim.

##### Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences

**Holding:** When Congress has prescribed a civil statute’s temporal reach, courts apply that direction subject to constitutional limits; absent sufficient direction, they ask whether applying the provision would impair rights possessed when a party acted, increase liability for past conduct, or impose new duties concerning completed transactions. If it would, the provision does not govern those events without clear congressional intent; a pending case or reference to earlier facts alone does not establish such an effect, and prospective relief, jurisdiction, procedure and collateral matters require examination of what the particular provision actually changes rather than an automatic reopening of completed acts.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens’s general-framework portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this organizing rule. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur on a distinct relevant-activity approach, not as additional joins at this level of generality.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp. v. Bonjorno, 494 U.S. 827 (1990): applied to make statutory temporal direction the starting inquiry.
- Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204 (1988): applied to the presumption against retrospective legal burdens; no automatic civil constitutional prohibition is inferred.
- Bennett v. New Jersey, 470 U.S. 632 (1985): applied to protect substantive obligations fixed under earlier law.
- Bradley v. School Board of Richmond, 416 U.S. 696 (1974): retained in its collateral litigation-fee setting and reconciled with the new-consequence inquiry, not overruled.
- Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation, 509 U.S. 86 (1993): distinguished as judicial retroactivity; its equal-application rule remains unchanged.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The rule is provision-specific, not a declaration that the whole 1991 Act is inapplicable in pending cases. Section 402(a)'s enactment-date clause, §109(c)'s later-conduct limitation for extraterritorial amendments, and §402(b)'s exception for disparate-impact complaints filed before March 1, 1975 with initial decisions after October 30, 1983 remain distinct. Judicial retroactivity under Harper does not control legislative reach. An express congressional direction concerning the temporal operation of a statute resolves the threshold statutory question, subject to applicable constitutional limits. Such a direction does not alone determine whether Congress may constitutionally require reconsideration of an already completed Article III judgment. The present case concerns statutory consequences for earlier conduct in litigation properly before the courts and does not decide that separate final-judgment issue.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Fifth Circuit judgment is affirmed, 8–1. No new damages trial, award or remand is ordered, and the existing equitable-remedy judgment and unreviewed constructive-discharge findings remain intact.

#### Felker v. Turpin, 518 U.S. 651 (1996)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 95-8836; associated application A-890.
**Decided:** June 28, 1996.

##### Whether §2244(b)(3)(E) bars review of circuit authorization while preserving original habeas

**Holding:** Section 2244(b)(3)(E) bars ordinary certiorari review of a court of appeals' grant or denial of authorization to file a second or successive district-court habeas application; the present certiorari request must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The provision does not repeal the distinct original habeas authority retained by §2241, which permits independent consideration of this petition without affirming or reviewing the circuit's authorization judgment.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part I-A, unanimous.

**Material application:** Section 2241 still names the Court and its Justices; §2244(b)(3)(A) concerns advance authorization for district filing, and the amended transfer rule does not name this Court. Direct filing is procedurally original but constitutionally appellate; §1651 aids existing jurisdiction rather than creates it.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Unpresented certification, interlocutory and supervisory channels are not decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Ordinary certiorari dismissed for want of jurisdiction, 9–0, without affirming the authorization judgment.

##### When the new authorization-review bar governs

**Holding:** The authorization and review proceedings begun after AEDPA's April 24, 1996 enactment are governed by its specified authorization-review bar. That conclusion does not decide the temporal reach of every Title I provision or apply a new substantive restriction backward merely because a petition is pending.

**Proposition-level authority:** All nine support the channel result; O’Connor’s Part I-B has Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, six. Thomas, Scalia and Kennedy use a distinct relevant-activity approach.

**Material application:** Landgraf’s provision-specific temporal sequence governs the majority reasoning; a procedural/jurisdictional label alone does not answer retroactivity. The May authorization and subsequent review proceedings began after April 24.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No holding determines every Title I provision’s temporal reach or Georgia’s chapter 154 qualification. Chapter 154’s express pending-case direction does not automatically govern other amendments.

##### What successive restrictions and discretionary conditions govern an original petition

**Holding:** The district-court authorization mechanism does not govern filing this original application. Sections 2244(b)(1)–(2) inform the Court's consideration of repeated applications, but whether those substantive provisions bind this Court directly is reserved. Retained jurisdiction does not itself entitle an applicant to an original writ.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion, Part II, unanimous.

**Material application:** For statutory second/successive §2254 applications, (b)(1) dismisses previously presented claims. New claims under (b)(2) require either a previously unavailable new constitutional rule made retroactive by this Court, or both previously undiscoverable facts despite due diligence and facts which, if proved with the whole evidence, clearly and convincingly establish that but for constitutional error no reasonable factfinder would find guilt. A request to announce a new rule is not the first exception. Before district filing, (b)(3) requires circuit permission by a three-judge panel upon prima facie showing within thirty days; the determination permits no appeal, rehearing or certiorari. Under (b)(4), district dismissal is required unless the applicant actually establishes the conditions, despite authorization.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Whether (b)(1)–(2) bind this Court directly remains reserved; they inform the Court’s consideration. Rule 20 separately requires reasons for bypassing district court, exhaustion or satisfaction of §2254(b), exceptional circumstances and unavailability of adequate relief in another form or court. Section 2254(b)(1) permits exhaustion, absence of available state corrective process or circumstances rendering it ineffective; (b)(2) permits, but does not require, denial on merits despite nonexhaustion; counsel’s express waiver is necessary under (b)(3). No exhaustion, process adequacy, waiver or affirmative diligence-failure fact is decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Original relief denied, 9–0, on the exceptional-original-showing ground; no return, transfer or hearing is ordered.

##### Whether the channel and successive scheme suspend meaningful review or dictate an unlawful outcome here

**Holding:** The specified review restriction and consideration of the operative successive scheme do not suspend constitutionally sufficient habeas review or prescribe a forbidden judicial outcome as applied to these claims. The original petition receives independent examination and fails on an adequate traditional original-writ ground, without deciding that every future statutory exclusion or every nominal surviving remedy is constitutional.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s opinion, Part II, unanimous.

**Material application:** The retained original route supplies actual independent claim examination and a sufficient traditional denial ground. Klein distinguishes changed law from a command to falsify facts or decide an unchanged rule in a prescribed manner.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No approval of every nominal forum, future exclusion of otherwise sufficient innocence claims, all AEDPA, §§2254(d)–(e), or unpresented supervisory restriction.

##### Whether the submitted medical evidence establishes the Schlup gateway

**Holding:** This submission does not establish Schlup's gateway: reliable new evidence, assessed with all probative evidence, making it more likely than not that no reasonable, properly instructed juror would convict. That conclusion does not adjudicate the separately pleaded constitutional innocence theory or make the gateway and AEDPA's successive conditions interchangeable.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part IV-A, all eight Associates; Stone-Zsela withholds this additional application.

**Material application:** Detailed new three-to-five-day estimates and physiological criticisms are considered with Whitaker’s independently favored earlier death estimate, his acknowledgment that the later interval was possible, and the disappearance, car, call and clothing evidence. The submission does not eliminate reasonable adverse assessment at Schlup’s probability threshold. No affidavit is declared false and other-offense evidence retains its motive/intent/state-of-mind limit.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Gateway passage would permit consideration of a trial claim, not prove it, establish statutory (b)(2)(B), or decide a distinct constitutional innocence entitlement.

##### Whether the separately pleaded constitutional innocence claim warrants exceptional original relief

**Independent alternative holding:** Even assuming a possible constitutional claim based on actual innocence, this considered submission does not establish exceptional circumstances warranting the Court's original habeas intervention. Original relief is denied without establishing a universal freestanding-innocence burden, rejecting every such constitutional claim, or requiring an additional return merely because new expert opinions are offered.

**Proposition-level authority for alternative holding:** O’Connor’s Part IV-B, unanimous; independently sufficient original-writ ground.

**Material application:** Herrera supplies a particular-submission insufficiency holding, not one general constitutional innocence burden. The full medical dispute receives consideration; the conviction and circuit refusal alone do not replace examination.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal freestanding-innocence threshold, categorical denial of every such right, presumed adequate Georgia remedy or mandatory further return from new expert opinions.

##### Whether A-890 warrants protection after claim-specific consideration is complete

**Holding:** Execution's irreversibility does not alone establish entitlement to a stay. Because the independently considered claims show no sufficiently substantial basis for relief through the available proceedings and no identified unfinished legally required process warrants protection, application A-890 is denied.

**Proposition-level authority:** O’Connor’s Part V, unanimous, in the reasoned combined threshold decision.

**Material application:** Bowersox’s substantial-ground assessment remains distinct from Lonchar’s protection of legally required process and McFarland’s counsel/preparation predicates. Neither the petition’s existence nor retained jurisdiction supplies relief by itself.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No automatic lateness penalty, execution date, altered prior stay, exhaustion of all other possible avenues or execution legality determination.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The pending stay request is denied, 9–0, after independent examination; no unfinished identified required process or sufficiently substantial relief basis remains.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Ex parte McCardle:** Applied to the specified withdrawn appellate route; no conclusion follows that every separate habeas source is repealed.
- **Ex parte Yerger and Ex parte Bollman:** Applied to the retained statutory habeas channel and its constitutional appellate character. They do not make issuance of an original writ automatic.
- **Landgraf and Rivers:** Their controlling provision-specific temporal inquiry remains in force; the application here concerns post-enactment authorization/review proceedings, not all pending habeas litigation.
- **McCleskey v. Zant:** Its recognition of lawful successive/abuse restrictions supports consideration of the new standards; it does not eliminate their separate textual requirements or settle their mandatory reach here.
- **United States v. Klein:** Its jurisdiction/rule-of-decision distinction is preserved; the present channel and standards do not direct a factual result under unchanged law.
- **Victor and Sandoval:** The whole-charge reasonable-likelihood test is applied by the eight Associates, with its existing limits on isolated terminology and automatic cures intact.
- **Sullivan:** Its structural-error rule is preserved; the Court finds no constitutional vitiation of the proof standard and conducts no harmless-error substitute.
- **Lisenba:** Its fundamental-fairness inquiry governs the presented medical-testimony trial claim. **Ake, Ford and Eddings** do not supply the proposed automatic physician-only testimony requirement.
- **Schlup:** Its gateway remains distinct from a substantive innocence claim and the new statutory conditions; the eight Associates find the present showing insufficient under its governing probability standard.
- **Herrera:** Its particular-submission holding is preserved without converting any separate theory into a general Court rule. The present original denial likewise assumes the possible constitutional claim without deciding its universal merits threshold.
- **Bowersox, Lonchar and McFarland:** Their distinct substantial-ground, required-process and counsel/preparation rules retain their actual triggers. None guarantees additional proceedings or a stay from filing alone.

#### Reno v. Flores, 507 U.S. 292 (1993)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-905.

**Decided:** March 23, 1993.

##### Statutory authority for the juvenile release regulation

**Holding:** Section 1252(a)(1) permits the Attorney General to use the family-custodian and discretionary-release arrangements in 8 C.F.R. §242.24 for temporary juvenile custody pending deportation proceedings; the regulation is not facially beyond that authority because it does not mandate release to every willing responsible adult. Custody remains subject to the statute's reasonable-dispatch limitation, its habeas remedy upon the requisite conclusive showing of unnecessary delay, the regulation's release avenues and the existing conditions agreement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion for the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six votes for this rule. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on a narrower statutory and facial-review ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952): applies discretion bounded by the immigration statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody.
- National Center for Immigrants' Rights, December 16, 1991, decision: applies general immigration-release authority with preserved individual safeguards; does not enlarge that decision into universal detention authority.
- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): applies facial-review limits to the whole-regulation remedy.

##### The facial substantive-due-process challenge fails

**Holding:** The Constitution does not give a juvenile without an available parent, guardian or close adult relative a fundamental right to placement with a willing unrelated adult instead of temporary, nonpunitive government care under this regulation and the binding conditions agreement. On this facial record, the family preferences and other release avenues reasonably serve care and attendance interests; individual unconstitutional confinement and enforcement of the agreement remain open.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. O'Connor and Souter also explain the substantial liberty interest in avoiding institutional confinement in a separate concurrence; Stone-Zsela does not join this formulation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the distinction between protective juvenile custody and punishment.
- Parham v. J. R., 442 U.S. 584 (1979): applies the special circumstances of juvenile care; does not extinguish a child's liberty interest.
- Youngberg v. Romeo, 457 U.S. 307 (1982): preserves constitutional limits on institutional care.
- Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992, decision: distinguishes psychiatric confinement after its predicate ends; no universal custody test is inferred.

##### Request-based review is not facially inadequate

**Holding:** The described examination, notice and opportunity to obtain immigration-judge review on request do not facially require replacement with an automatic hearing for every detained juvenile. That result depends on the available procedures and the absence of a record establishing general inability to invoke them; it does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela agrees with setting aside the universal automatic-hearing remedy but reserves a distinct Mathews-based implementation inquiry.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Fare v. Michael C., 442 U.S. 707 (1979): applies the recognition that juveniles can make a knowing waiver; no universal finding of comprehension follows.
- The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86 (1903): applies aliens' entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings.

##### The challenged classifications do not establish facial unequal treatment

**Holding:** The regulation's distinction between juveniles with available family custodians and those without them survives the facial equal-protection objection because family availability bears a rational relation to safe placement and attendance. The separate federal juvenile-delinquency release scheme does not require identical release rules for immigration custody, and this ruling does not resolve unequal administration in a particular case.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on this component and adds no ground.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the legitimate relevance of protective care in juvenile custody; no general exemption from equal protection follows.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Subsection (b)(1) requires release in order of preference to a nondetained parent, legal guardian, or adult brother, sister, aunt, uncle or grandparent unless detention is required to secure timely appearance before the Service or immigration court, or ensure the juvenile's safety or that of others. If the only listed custodian is detained, (b)(2) provides discretionary case-by-case consideration of simultaneous release. Under (b)(3), a parent or guardian detained or outside the United States may designate another capable and willing person by sworn affidavit before an immigration or consular officer; that person must agree to care for the child and ensure presence at all future proceedings. Subsection (b)(4) permits other agreeing adult custodians only in unusual and compelling circumstances, at the district director's or chief patrol agent's discretion. These are distinct release avenues, not one undifferentiated exception.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The 1987 conditions agreement remains enforceable and is not a finding that each facility complies. Custody review under §1252(a)(1) permits habeas upon a conclusive showing of failure to proceed with reasonable dispatch warranted by the particular facts. For a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure, the described 24-hour process uses a different examiner unless no qualified alternative is available; English/Spanish notice allows review on request and withdrawal of waiver. No universal ruling on voluntary-departure safeguards, 30-day custody cap or approval of defective notice, ineffective assistance, delayed review or unconstitutional individual/systemic implementation follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the facial juvenile-release and automatic-hearing injunctions, 7–2 in judgment, and remand consistently with the six-Justice rationale. No particular child’s release is ordered; conditions enforcement and properly supported implementation challenges remain.

### 1996-11-13 — INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang, No. 95-938; October Term 1996.

Authority: [Record](../records/INS_v_Yueh_Shaio_Yang_merits_1996-11-13.md).

#### Holdings

#### I. Review under the corrected 1996 transition provisions

**Holding and operative rule.** Review of this existing deportation proceeding remains available under former INA §106 and 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) on November 13, 1996. Corrected IIRIRA §306(c)(1) gives new §242(g) reach across past, pending and future proceedings when that provision commences; it does not advance commencement of the new jurisdictional rule from April 1, 1997, and the other review restrictions establish no present bar on this record.

**Authority.** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies an eight-Justice controlling rationale. Stone-Zsela concurs only in this threshold resolution and does not join its reasons.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court reads the enacted timing instructions together. Section 309(a) sets April 1, 1997 as the general commencement date, expressly excepting §309 and §§303(b)(2), 306(c), 308(d)(2)(D) and 308(d)(5). Corrected §306(c)(1) applies the amendments as provided in §309, then separately extends §242(g) to old as well as new proceedings. The immediate-operation argument relies on those exceptions and the unrestricted reference to pending claims. But the exceptional clause specifies covered proceedings without supplying a replacement commencement date. Its present operation as an applicability instruction has independent work: after commencement, §242(g) reaches proceedings otherwise protected by grandfathering. This reading does not presume that jurisdiction can never change during litigation.

Corrected §309(c)(1), subject to its succeeding provisions, preserves the earlier proceedings and judicial review. No paragraph (2) or (3) agency election is established. Paragraph (4) requires a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996; it lists §§212(c), 212(h), 212(i), 244 and 245. The 1995 posture supplies no new triggering order, and that list omits former §241(a)(1)(H). Permanent §242(a)(2)(B)(i) instead lists §§212(h), 212(i), 240A, 240B and 245; clause (ii) separately addresses other statutorily discretionary authority, except §208(a) grants. Neither permanent clause governs this event.

Section 306(c)(2) protects existing §106 judgments and orders from invalidation or compelled reconsideration by paragraph (1); it does not independently establish appellate jurisdiction. Section 306(d)'s AEDPA-related criminal amendment supplies no bar without a covered conviction, which this record does not establish. The Court leaves open §242(g)'s scope after commencement, including its three subjects—commencing proceedings, adjudicating cases and executing removal orders—and its exception for review provided in §242. *Landgraf* and *Rivers* supply attention to Congress's actual temporal directions, not a new unanimous adoption of every earlier temporal rationale.

#### II. Eligibility does not exclude the continuing fraud pattern from discretion

**Holding and operative rule.** Former INA §241(a)(1)(H), 8 U.S.C. §1251(a)(1)(H), permits the Attorney General to consider a continuing pattern of immigration fraud when choosing among eligible waiver applicants, even though particular acts are connected with entry. Eligibility does not erase that conduct or compel a waiver; the Court neither permits automatic denial to every eligible applicant because of the qualifying fraud nor decides whether the minimum qualifying fraud alone could sustain a particular individualized denial.

**Authority.** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls unanimously.

**Controlling explanation.** The statute makes eligibility and favorable discretion distinct. It reaches covered entry fraud or misrepresentation, whether willful or innocent. An applicant must be the spouse, parent, son or daughter of a citizen or lawful permanent resident, have possessed an immigrant visa or equivalent document, and otherwise have been admissible at entry. The exceptions for labor-certification and immigrant-documentation grounds under §§1182(a)(5)(A) and (7)(A) apply only when those grounds directly resulted from the fraud. The separate exclusion for assistance in Nazi persecution or participation in genocide remains. An actually granted waiver also reaches entry-inadmissibility grounds directly resulting from the fraud. These provisions define eligibility and the consequence of relief; they do not remove conduct from the discretionary decision.

*Jay v. Boyd* supplies the distinction between statutory eligibility and discretionary selection. *INS v. Errico* construed a mandatory predecessor; its family-preservation concern does not convert the later discretionary provision into an entitlement. *National Center for Immigrants' Rights* supports discretion exercised within the statute while preserving required individual consideration.

The Board accepted eligibility and expressly disregarded the initial entry fraud. It considered the wife's false-identity scheme and sham marital arrangements, the later naturalization fraud, further marital arrangements and lack of candor, together with favorable residence, business, property, family and health considerations and Taiwan connections. Those are the Board's findings and assessments. The Ninth Circuit allowed dishonest testimony to count but wrongly excluded collaboration and naturalization conduct because of their connection with entry. The argument that waiver discretion must remain meaningful therefore does not justify its categorical rule. A continuing pattern distinguishes applicants without making every eligible applicant lose. The Court does not reweigh the balance.

#### III. Permissible factors remain subject to reasoned and consistent administration

**Holding and operative rule.** Statutory permission to consider a factor does not excuse arbitrary decisionmaking, pretext, disregard of a binding rule, or an unexplained departure from a genuinely settled agency policy. Here the Board rationally distinguished continuing immigration fraud from the initial entry fraud it disregarded; the record supplies no policy reversal requiring a new administrative balance.

**Authority.** Part III of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls unanimously.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court separates statutory authority from the lawfulness of its exercise. *Smiley*, applying the administrative-change principles preserved from *Good Samaritan*, requires examination of the agency's actual reasons, prior guidance and legitimate reliance. A court cannot supply a litigation explanation for an unexplained change. An agency may revise a policy openly with adequate explanation, subject to binding law; the existence of discretion alone supplies no answer.

The Board retained its initial-fraud distinction and explained the significance of the continuing pattern. Its actual reasons defeat the claim that this decision irrationally abandoned that distinction. The Court neither finds a different binding policy nor commands a new explanation for an unproved reversal. The mandate removes the Ninth Circuit's compelled reweighing based on categorical exclusion. Any genuinely remaining preserved objection is for ordinary proceedings within the record and mandate; the Court directs neither a waiver nor its denial, reopening, a new hearing, or a fresh Board balance. This decision concerns the statutory immigration function presented, not classifications outside it.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Landgraf v. USI Film Products* and *Rivers v. Roadway Express*: applied to the actual congressional timing instructions; their distinct temporal rationales are not combined into a broader unanimous framework.
- *Jay v. Boyd*, 351 U.S. 345: applied for eligibility's separation from discretionary selection; it remains authority for that distinction.
- *INS v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214: distinguished because its mandatory predecessor differs from the discretionary text here; its own construction is not overruled.
- *INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights* (December 16, 1991): applied by analogy for statutory boundaries and legally required individual consideration; its release-condition holding is not transformed into a waiver decision.
- *Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala* and *Smiley* (June 3, 1996): applied for reasoned administrative interpretation and explained treatment of settled policy and reliance; statutory discretion does not displace those limits.
- *INS v. Doherty* (January 15, 1992): its fractured disposition supplies no controlling substantive reopening rationale for this waiver question; no separate rationale is promoted to Court law.

#### Law After Decision

Effective November 13, 1996, courts may not exclude a continuing immigration-fraud pattern from former §241(a)(1)(H) discretion merely because its acts are connected with entry. The eligibility conditions, the distinct consequence of a granted waiver, meaningful individual discretion and ordinary review of actual agency reasons remain operative. The Court leaves the minimum-fraud-only individualized case unresolved and permits no universal eligibility-based denial. The specific transition construction preserves review at this event; it decides no post-April §242(g) scope question. No waiver is awarded or denied by this Court, and the agency balance is not judicially redone.

### 1997-05-12 — Reyes-Hernandez v. INS; Seventh Circuit No. 95-3677, 89 F.3d 490; Supreme Court docket unassigned.

Authority: [Record](../records/Reyes_Hernandez_v_INS_merits_1997-05-12.md).

#### Holdings

##### I. Section 242(g) does not withdraw review of these completed-adjudication claims

**Holding and operative rule:** After its April 1, 1997 commencement, §242(g) reaches past, pending and future proceedings but withdraws review of the identified executive decisions or actions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases or execute removal orders, rather than every legal challenge arising from completed adjudication. These challenges to temporal eligibility, the legality of the completed waiver denial and treatment of a supplemental brief remain outside that withdrawal and may use the otherwise saved former §106 review route.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, has eight direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees only with the threshold result.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies *Yang*'s April 1 commencement holding and construes the scope it reserved. Corrected §306(c)(1) applies the general amendments through §309 while extending §242(g) across old and new proceedings. Corrected §309(c)(1) preserves earlier proceedings and review subject to the stated transition rules: paragraph (2) permits election of new procedures only before the evidentiary hearing has commenced, with notice at least thirty days before that hearing; paragraph (3) permits termination and reinitiation only before a final administrative decision. Neither predicate exists after this completed 1995 adjudication. The October 1995 final administrative order does not meet §309(c)(4)'s requirement of an order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996; no agency election or later final order is established. Section 306(c)(2) protects existing §106 judgments from invalidation or required reconsideration by paragraph (1), but creates no jurisdiction.

The contrary construction stresses the broad reference to any claim notwithstanding other law and the exception for review provided in §242. But making the three enumerated executive choices encompass every adjudicated legal issue would erase the detailed saving scheme beyond Congress's stated exceptions. Review of a completed legal result differs from challenging the executive action to adjudicate. Former §106 review is not declared to be review textually provided in §242; it survives through §309 only for claims outside the specified withdrawal. This construction does not preserve every discretionary or constitutional claim. *Felker* requires identification of the particular channel withdrawn and retained; neither a hypothetical §2241 remedy nor §1651 supplies the jurisdiction here.

##### II. The two AEDPA restrictions do not attach these new consequences to the completed concession

**Holding and operative rule:** On the uncontested premise that Reyes surrendered a colorable defense to deportability before enactment in order to pursue the then-available waiver-and-review path, §440(d) does not remove eligibility for that original waiver claim, and §440(a) does not eliminate ordinary review of its lawful denial, because neither clearly prescribes those new consequences for that completed choice. A genuine defense is necessary to this lost-option rationale, not a universal element of retroactivity analysis or an exemption for every preenactment conviction, pending case or later reopening request.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, has six direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies *Landgraf* separately to each provision: first examine enacted temporal direction, then the new legal consequence attached to the completed act. Section 440(f) directs the reach of subsection (e), not either subsection at issue. Section 440(d) would remove eligibility that formed part of Reyes's completed surrender. Section 440(a) would separately remove the then-available opportunity to correct an unlawful denial through ordinary review after that surrender. Discretionary relief was never guaranteed, but the absence of an entitlement to mercy does not answer whether a real legal option has acquired a new disability.

The strongest objection treats §440(a) solely as regulation of present judicial power. *Landgraf* does not let the jurisdictional label replace examination of the actual consequence. This record supplies a completed concession, more than mere pendency or a general hope that a forum will persist. *Felker* applied its review restriction to authorization proceedings begun after enactment, a different temporal event. The Government did not dispute the colorable-defense predicate; the Court invents no particular defense and orders no factfinding to reopen that accepted premise. An utterly insubstantial defense would not establish this surrendered-option theory. Other temporal predicates remain open, and no universal subjective-reliance requirement, constitutional invalidation, habeas holding or promise of favorable discretion is adopted. The IIRIRA dual-offense technical correction supplies no additional conviction finding.

##### III. Eligibility does not compel a favorable waiver balance

**Holding and operative rule:** An eligible §212(c) applicant remains subject to a reasoned individualized exercise of lawful discretion, and the BIA may adopt an immigration judge's adequate reasons without independently repeating the entire balance. Here consideration of rehabilitation and family hardship against serious, recent and repeated drug offenses lawfully supports denial; the reviewing court may not replace that balance merely because the favorable equities are substantial.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, has six direct votes. The jurisdictional dissenters do not reach this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies *Yang*'s distinction between eligibility, permissible factors and reasoned administration. Permission to exercise discretion does not permit pretext, disregard of binding rules or an unexplained departure from genuinely settled policy. Those defects are not established here. The immigration judge addressed family hardship and rehabilitation, yet gave greater weight to repeated serious drug violations; the BIA adopted those actual reasons. Adoption of an adequate explanation is not absence of analysis. Reyes's stronger-equities argument asks for a fresh judicial weighing, not correction of a demonstrated legal error. The Court sustains this denial without creating a presumption against every eligible drug offender or foreclosing review of an actual policy departure.

##### IV. A supplemental appellate brief did not require original factfinding or automatic reopening

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, the BIA could require the established motion to supplement, remand or reopen before considering new post-hearing evidence offered in an appellate brief, rather than treating that brief as a different motion or conducting original appellate factfinding. The decision does not permit disregard of material evidence already admitted or decide the eligibility, timeliness, merits or disposition of any later motion.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, has six direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court distinguishes refusing to receive new evidence through an improper vehicle from ignoring evidence already in the record. The supplemental brief described later employment, sobriety and volunteer work, but the agency had not admitted that material. It required the established procedural route and expressly identified reopening as remaining possible. *Abudu* supplies the governing reopening framework; *Doherty*'s fractured result supplies no controlling general rationale. The argument that several years of developments could affect the equities shows why an evidentiary route matters, not why the Board had to convert a merits brief into a new proceeding. On the actual record that requirement was lawful. The Court neither guarantees a successful motion nor decides a later proceeding.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang*: Applies its April 1 commencement holding, decides the previously reserved §242(g) scope at the stated boundary, and applies its separation of eligibility from reasoned discretion.
- *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*: Applies its provision-specific temporal inquiry to the completed surrender of an actual legal option; neither pendency nor a jurisdictional label controls by itself.
- *Felker v. Turpin*: Preserves channel-specific review and distinguishes its post-enactment authorization proceedings; no presumed alternative remedy replaces the actual preserved route.
- *INS v. Abudu*: Preserves the existing reopening framework and distinction between record review and presentation of new material.
- *INS v. Doherty*: Its fractured disposition still supplies no general controlling reopening rationale; no separate writing is elevated into law.

#### Law After Decision

Effective May 12, 1997, §242(g)'s cross-temporal reach does not eliminate these legal challenges to a completed adjudication within the saved former review scheme. The separate AEDPA review and eligibility restrictions do not attach the specified retrospective consequences to this uncontested surrender of a colorable defense. That limited temporal protection supplies review and eligibility, not a waiver, automatic reopening or a guarantee that agency discretion will favor the applicant. The completed waiver denial and ordinary new-evidence procedure stand. Other convictions, temporal predicates, later motions, actual habeas scope and constitutional review requirements remain unresolved.

                instituted under this paragraph must be filed no later 
                than 60 days after the date the challenged section, 
                regulation, directive, guideline, or procedure described 
                in clause (i) or (ii) of subparagraph (A) is first 
                implemented.
                    ``(C) Notice of appeal.--A notice of appeal of an 
                order issued by the District Court under this paragraph 
                may be filed not later than 30 days after the date of 
                issuance of such order.
                    ``(D) Expeditious consideration of cases.--It shall 
                be the duty of the District Court, the Court of Appeals, 
                and the Supreme Court of the United States to advance on 
                the docket and to expedite to the greatest possible 
                extent the disposition of any case considered under this 
                paragraph.
            ``(4) Decision.--In any case where the court determines that 
        the petitioner--
                    ``(A) is an alien who was not ordered removed under 
                section 235(b)(1), or
                    ``(B) has demonstrated by a preponderance of the 
                evidence that the alien is an alien lawfully admitted 
                for permanent residence, has been admitted as a refugee 
                under section 207, or has been granted asylum under 
                section 208, the court may order no remedy or relief 
                other than to require that the petitioner be provided a 
                hearing in accordance with section 240. Any alien who is 
                provided a hearing under section 240 pursuant to this 
                paragraph may thereafter obtain judicial review of any 
                resulting final order of removal pursuant to subsection 
                (a)(1).
            ``(5) Scope of inquiry.--In determining whether an alien has 
        been ordered removed under section 235(b)(1), the court's 
        inquiry shall be limited to whether such an order in fact was 
        issued and whether it relates to the petitioner. There shall be 
        no review of whether the alien is actually inadmissible or 
        entitled to any relief from removal.

    ``(f) Limit on Injunctive Relief.--
            ``(1) In general.--Regardless of the nature of the action or 
        claim or of the identity of the party or parties bringing the 
        action, no court (other than the Supreme Court) shall have 
        jurisdiction or authority to enjoin or restrain the operation of 
        the provisions of chapter 4 of title II, as amended by the 
        Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 
        1996, other than with respect to the application of such

        provisions to an individual alien against whom proceedings under 
        such chapter have been initiated.
            ``(2) Particular cases.--Notwithstanding any other provision 
        of law, no court shall enjoin the removal of any alien pursuant 
        to a final order under this section unless the alien shows by 
        clear and convincing evidence that the entry or execution of 
        such order is prohibited as a matter of law.

    ``(g) Exclusive Jurisdiction.--Except as provided in this section 
and notwithstanding any other provision of law, no court shall have 
jurisdiction to hear any cause or claim by or on behalf of any alien 
arising from the decision or action by the Attorney General to commence 
proceedings, adjudicate cases, or execute removal orders against any 
alien under this Act.''.
    (b) Repeal of Section 106.--Section 106 (8 U.S.C. 1105a) is 
repealed.
    (c) Effective <<NOTE: 8 USC 1252 note.>> Date.--

SEC. 309. EFFECTIVE DATES; TRANSITION.

    (a) In <<NOTE: 8 USC 1101 note.>> General.--Except as provided in 
this section and sections 303(b)(2), 306(c), 308(d)(2)(D), or 308(d)(5) 
of this division, this subtitle and the amendments made by this subtitle 
shall take effect on the first day of the first month beginning more 
than 180 days after the date of the enactment of this Act (in this title 
referred to as the ``title III-A effective date'').

    (b) Promulgation of Regulations.--The Attorney General shall first 
promulgate regulations to carry out this subtitle by not later than 30 
days before the title III-A effective date.
    (c) Transition for Aliens in Proceedings.--
            (1) General rule that new rules do not apply.--Subject to 
        the succeeding provisions of this subsection, in the case of an 
        alien who is in exclusion or deportation proceedings as of the 
        title III-A effective date--
                    (A) the amendments made by this subtitle shall not 
                apply, and
                    (B) the proceedings (including judicial review 
                thereof) shall continue to be conducted without regard 
                to such amendments.

[[Page 110 STAT. 3009-626]]

            (2) Attorney general option to elect to apply new 
        procedures.--In a case described in paragraph (1) in which an 
        evidentiary hearing under section 236 or 242 and 242B of the 
        Immigration and Nationality Act has not commenced as of the 
        title III-A effective date, the Attorney General may elect to 
        proceed under chapter 4 of title II of such Act (as amended by 
        this subtitle). The Attorney General shall provide notice of 
        such election to the alien involved not later than 30 days 
        before the date any evidentiary hearing is commenced. If the 
        Attorney General makes such election, the notice of hearing 
        provided to the alien under section 235 or 242(a) of such Act 
        shall be valid as if provided under section 239 of such Act (as 
        amended by this subtitle) to confer jurisdiction on the 
        immigration judge.
            (3) Attorney general option to terminate and reinitiate 
        proceedings.--In the case described in paragraph (1), the 
        Attorney General may elect to terminate proceedings in which 
        there has not been a final administrative decision and to 
        reinitiate proceedings under chapter 4 of title II the 
        Immigration and Nationality Act (as amended by this subtitle). 
        Any determination in the terminated proceeding shall not be 
        binding in the reinitiated proceeding.
            (4) Transitional changes in judicial review.--In the case 
        described in paragraph (1) in which a final order of exclusion 
        or deportation is entered more than 30 days after the date of 
        the enactment of this Act, notwithstanding any provision of 
        section 106 of the Immigration and Nationality Act (as in effect 
        as of the date of the enactment of this Act) to the contrary--
                    (A) in the case of judicial review of a final order 
                of exclusion, subsection (b) of such section shall not 
                apply and the action for judicial review shall be 
                governed by the provisions of subsections (a) and (c) of 
                such in the same manner as they apply to judicial review 
                of orders of deportation;
                    (B) a court may not order the taking of additional 
                evidence under section 2347(c) of title 28, United 
                States Code;
                    (C) the petition for judicial review must be filed 
                not later than 30 days after the date of the final order 
                of exclusion or deportation;
                    (D) the petition for review shall be filed with the 
                court of appeals for the judicial circuit in which the 
                administrative proceedings before the special inquiry 
                officer or immigration judge were completed;
                    (E) there shall be no appeal of any discretionary 
                decision under section 212(c), 212(h), 212(i), 244, or 
                245 of the Immigration and Nationality Act (as in effect 
                as of the date of the enactment of this Act);
                    (F) service of the petition for review shall not 
                stay the deportation of an alien pending the court's 
                decision on the petition, unless the court orders 
                otherwise; and
                    (G) there shall be no appeal permitted in the case 
                of an alien who is inadmissible or deportable by reason 
                of having committed a criminal offense covered in 
                section 212(a)(2) or section 241(a)(2)(A)(iii), (B), 
                (C), or (D) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (as 
                in effect as of the

[[Page 110 STAT. 3009-627]]

                date of the enactment of this Act), or any offense 
                covered by section 241(a)(2)(A)(ii) of such Act (as in 
                effect on such date) for which both predicate offenses 
                are, without regard to their date of commission, 
                otherwise covered by section 241(a)(2)(A)(i) of such Act 
                (as so in effect).
            (5) Transitional rule with regard to suspension of 
        deportation.--Paragraphs (1) and (2) of section 240A(d) of the 
        Immigration and Nationality Act (relating to continuous 
        residence or physical presence) shall apply to notices to appear 
        issued before, on, or after the date of the enactment of this 
        Act.
            (6) Transition for certain family unity aliens.--The 
        Attorney General may waive the application of section 212(a)(9) 
        of the Immigration and Nationality Act, as inserted by section 
        301(b)(1) of this division, in the case of an alien who is 
        provided benefits under the provisions of section 301 of the 
        Immigration Act of 1990 (relating to family unity).
            (7) Limitation on suspension of deportation.--The Attorney 
        General may not suspend the deportation and adjust the status 
        under section 244 of the Immigration and Nationality Act of more 
        than 4,000 aliens in any fiscal year (beginning after the date 
        of the enactment of this Act). The previous sentence shall apply 
        regardless of when an alien applied for such suspension and 
        adjustment.

    (d) Transitional References.--For purposes of carrying out the 
Immigration and Nationality Act, as amended by this subtitle--
            (1) any reference in section 212(a)(1)(A) of such Act to the 
        term ``inadmissible'' is deemed to include a reference to the 
        term ``excludable'', and
            (2) any reference in law to an order of removal shall be 
        deemed to include a reference to an order of exclusion and 
        deportation or an order of deportation.

    (e) Transition.--No period of time before the date of the enactment 
of this Act shall be included in the period of 1 year described in 
section 212(a)(6)(B)(i) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (as 
amended by section 301(c) of this division).

                                 An Act


 
  To extend the authorized period of stay within the United States for 
          certain nurses. <<NOTE: Oct. 11, 1996 -  [S. 2197]>> 

    Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the 
United States of America in Congress assembled,

SECTION 1. <<NOTE: 8 USC 1182 note.>>  EXTENSION OF AUTHORIZED PERIOD OF 
            STAY FOR CERTAIN NURSES.

    (a) Aliens Who Previously Entered the United States Pursuant to an 
H-1A Visa.--
            (1) In general.--Notwithstanding any other provision of law, 
        the authorized period of stay in the United States of any 
        nonimmigrant described in paragraph (2) is hereby extended 
        through September 30, 1997.
            (2) Nonimmigrant described.--A nonimmigrant described in 
        this paragraph is a nonimmigrant--
                    (A) who entered the United States as a nonimmigrant 
                described in section 101(a)(15)(H)(i)(a) of the 
                Immigration and Nationality Act;
                    (B) who was within the United States on or after 
                September 1, 1995, and who is within the United States 
                on the date of the enactment of this Act; and
                    (C) whose period of authorized stay has expired or 
                would expire before September 30, 1997 but for the 
                provisions of this section.
            (3) Limitations.--Nothing in this section may be construed 
        to extend the validity of any visa issued to a nonimmigrant 
        described in section 101(a)(15)(H)(i)(a) of the Immigration and 
        Nationality Act or to authorize the re-entry of any person 
        outside the United States on the date of the enactment of this 
        Act.

    (b) Change of Employment.--A nonimmigrant whose authorized period of 
stay is extended by operation of this section shall not be eligible to 
change employers in accordance with section 214.2(h)(2)(i)(D) of title 
8, Code of Federal Regulations (as in effect on the day before the date 
of the enactment of this Act).
    (c) Regulations.--Not later than 30 days after the date of the 
enactment of this Act, the Attorney General shall issue regulations to 
carry out the provisions of this section.
    (d) Interim Treatment.--A nonimmigrant whose authorized period of 
stay is extended by operation of this section, and the spouse and child 
of such nonimmigrant, shall be considered as having continued to 
maintain lawful status as a nonimmigrant through September 30, 1997.
