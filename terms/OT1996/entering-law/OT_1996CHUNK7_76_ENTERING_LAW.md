## Standards and Tests selection: Announcement before dwelling entry

### Announcement before dwelling entry

**Governing question:** The role of knock-and-announce in Fourth Amendment reasonableness.

**Current rule:** Common-law announcement of identity and authority forms part of the reasonableness inquiry when officers enter a dwelling. Supported countervailing circumstances, including danger, futility, escape or threatened evidence destruction, may justify entry without announcement. Justification depends on the actual circumstances; the rule is not an inflexible requirement and does not itself decide exclusion or another remedy.

**Authority by component:**

- Announcement and reasonable home entry: Announcement and reasonable home entry, 1995-05-22. Thomas's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-05-22; the component authorities identified above.

**Holdings navigation:** Announcement and reasonable home entry.

### Announcement before home entry

#### Wilson v. Arkansas

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-5707.
**Decided:** May 22, 1995.

##### Announcement and reasonable home entry

**Holding:** Common-law announcement of identity and authority forms part of Fourth Amendment reasonableness when officers enter a dwelling. Supported countervailing circumstances, including danger, futility, escape or threatened destruction of evidence, may justify unannounced entry; actual justification and remedy remain for the state court.

**Proposition-level authority:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join this proposition.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The exact quantum of suspicion required for each exception and whether every violation requires suppression remain undecided. The federal entry statutes' every detail is not constitutionalized.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Miller v. United States, 357 U.S. 301: supplies common-law announcement background; federal statutory particulars are not all constitutionalized.
- Sabbath v. United States, 391 U.S. 585: supplies federal statutory entry analysis without deciding this constitutional application.
- Ker v. California, 374 U.S. 23: applies state-entry reasonableness with circumstance-dependent excuses.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand for the state court to evaluate the actual entry, record-supported excuses and remedy. No violation, automatic suppression or final vacation of the conviction is established.

### Appellate review of suspicion and probable cause

#### Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690

**Docket or dockets:** 95-5257; Seventh Circuit Nos. 94-3349 and 94-3350
**Decided:** May 28, 1996

##### Which suppression questions remain preserved for appellate review through these conditional pleas?

**Holding:** Review encompasses both ultimate reasonable suspicion for the initial stop and ultimate probable cause for the warrantless search, as preserved in these conditional pleas. Agreement between the parties about a proposed review standard neither determines that legal standard nor expands review to unpreserved discovery or suppression-merits issues.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor), Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; unanimous controlling rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 11(a)(2) permits a written reservation of specified adverse pretrial determinations with the court's approval and the Government's consent. The selected orders establish preservation of both thresholds here; incompleteness of other documents does not establish litigant default. The Court reviews the allocation of appellate responsibility, not a newly presented database-discovery question or the ultimate suppression outcome.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Success in obtaining the proper review standard does not itself establish a right to withdraw the pleas. The ordinary requirement of prevailing on the reserved issue remains.

##### How must appellate courts review reasonable suspicion and probable cause for a warrantless search?

**Holding:** Appellate courts independently determine the ultimate constitutional sufficiency of reasonable suspicion for a stop and probable cause for a warrantless search under the objective totality of the circumstances. Historical facts, credibility determinations and genuinely factual subsidiary inferences receive clear-error protection, while grounded contextual inferences drawn by resident judges and experienced officers receive genuine due weight without deference to the ultimate constitutional conclusion.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor), Parts II-III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight-Justice majority. Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** Independent review develops workable constitutional standards and permits comparison of recurring circumstances. The reviewing court must nevertheless respect what occurred, the trial court's credibility role, local context and grounded police experience. Miller v. Fenton and Thompson v. Keohane support allocating mixed questions by function rather than a single label; First Options preserves separate legal and factual review. Brinegar, Gates, Sokolow and White supply the substantive totality inquiry but do not decide who finally applies it on appeal. Gates's substantial-basis review of a magistrate's warrant decision concerns a different institutional task. Mere acknowledgment of an officer's inference is insufficient due weight, but experience cannot manufacture an unproved fact or entitle the officer's ultimate conclusion to a presumption of correctness.

**Limits and questions not reached:** There is no numerical weighting formula, intermediate standard, new evidentiary fact or police-correctness presumption. Pierce and Cooter & Gell retain deference for their task-specific discretionary determinations; Salve Regina's treatment of legal review remains. The Court imports neither habeas burdens from Thompson nor a general solution to every mixed question; Markman's reserved appellate issue remains open.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**
- Miller v. Fenton and Thompson v. Keohane supply functional allocation of legal and factual review; Thompson's habeas setting does not transfer its burdens here.
- First Options preserves independent legal review and protection of historical findings.
- Brinegar, Gates, Sokolow and White retain objective totality-of-circumstances substantive standards.
- Gates's warrant-issuance substantial-basis rule is distinguished from independent review of a warrantless search.
- Pierce and Cooter & Gell retain their task-specific deference; Salve Regina supports independent legal review.
- Markman does not decide the reserved appellate-review question by analogy alone.

##### What follows for these two appeals and the district court's suppression findings?

**Holding:** Both appellate judgments must be vacated and reconsidered under this allocation, without automatically ordering suppression or reopening the evidentiary record. Under section 636(b)(1)(B), the district judge independently determines specifically objected-to portions of the magistrate's recommendation and may receive evidence or recommit; that duty does not invariably require a new hearing.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (O'Connor), Part III, joined by the same eight-Justice majority.

**Controlling explanation:** The district court accepted the credited loose-panel finding; rust was rejected, scratches were inconclusive, and actual NADDIS identification was unproved. Independent legal review cannot improve those facts by invoking experience. Raddatz permits the district judge's specified independent determination without an invariably repeated hearing; its concern about rejecting credibility findings is not triggered by the acceptance here. Section 636(b)(1)(A) does not set the ultimate suppression standard or the appellate allocation.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court assumes the circuit's limited-consent premise: consent did not authorize dismantling the panel. It adopts no general consent rule and does not revive waived inevitable discovery. The allocation also governs authorized Government suppression appeals, but section 3731's actual conditions remain: the statutory restriction after jeopardy attaches and before verdict, required certification of no delay and substantial material proof, the thirty-day and diligence requirements, and double-jeopardy limits. No independent Government appeal is authorized here.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Both Seventh Circuit judgments, reported at 16 F.3d 714 and 52 F.3d 328, are vacated and remanded, 8-1. The Court orders reconsideration of both constitutional thresholds, not suppression, plea withdrawal, release, a new evidentiary hearing or a particular ultimate result.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Raddatz preserves the distinction between a required independent judicial determination and an invariably new hearing; its reserved credibility-rejection question remains untriggered.

### 1996-11-18 — Ohio v. Robinette; No. 95-891

Authority: [Record](../records/Ohio_v_Robinette_merits_1996-11-18.md).

#### Holdings

##### I. Review of the interwoven federal and state grounds

**Holding and operative rule:** When a state judgment fairly appears primarily federal or interwoven with federal law and does not clearly state an independently sufficient state ground, the Court may review its federal basis. Ohio's intertwined constitutional explanation supplies no clear independent ground barring review here; Ohio remains free to supply greater protection on genuinely independent state-law grounds.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part I, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, controls by six direct joins. Stone-Zsela agrees to exercise review without joining this explanation; Ginsburg and Stevens accept review in their separate writings.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ohio's reference to its own Constitution does not end the jurisdictional inquiry. Michigan v. Long supplies the rule for opinions that rest on federal law or interweave federal and state law without a clear independent basis. Arizona v. Evans retains that rule. The Ohio opinion invokes both provisions and develops the suppression analysis with federal authorities; it does not announce that an independent Ohio ground alone sustains the judgment. Federal review therefore lies. Deciding the federal question neither erases the Ohio provision nor prevents the Ohio courts from adopting a clearly independent state rule in further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:** Long and Evans are applied without changing the independent-state-ground rule or creating an exception to the ordinary final-judgment requirement.

##### II. Objective legality during and after a traffic stop

**Holding and operative rule:** An officer's uncommunicated investigative motive or private decision to issue only a warning does not invalidate an objectively lawful traffic stop or an exit order while lawful traffic business is being completed. Once that justification ends, further detention requires independent legal grounds; the court must examine the objective circumstances to determine whether the person could terminate the encounter, and return of a license is neither an automatic end of seizure nor an automatic license to continue it.

**Authority:** Part II.A's rule concerning motive and the exit order has six direct joins: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. Part II.B's distinct continued-detention rule has those six plus Stone-Zsela, seven. Stone-Zsela supplies no additional reason for Part II.A.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an objectively justified beginning and a lawful continuation are separate questions. Whren rejects subjective purpose as an extra condition for an ordinary probable-cause traffic stop while preserving limits on manner, scope and duration. Mimms permits an exit order during a lawful traffic stop without a separate justification for that direction. The Ohio court could not deem the direction unlawful merely because the deputy privately intended a warning or hoped to ask unrelated questions.

That correction does not establish that detention lawfully continued through the later consent request. Bostick requires an objective assessment of whether the person could decline the request or terminate the encounter. The returned license and completed warning bear on that inquiry, but so do the immediacy and actual wording of further questioning, tone, positioning and continuity of official authority. Neither the officer's secret willingness to permit refusal nor Robinette's subjective impression alone decides the seizure question. Supported trial findings matter; the Court does not replace the record with a categorical conclusion in either direction. Ornelas's distinction between historical fact and ultimate constitutional sufficiency remains applicable within its decided scope; this decision announces no universal appellate-review rule for every mixed or consent question. The encounter's classification remains for proper application below.

**Precedent treatment:** Whren's objective initial-stop rule and independent duration limits remain intact; Mimms is confined to lawful traffic business. Bostick's actual-circumstances inquiry is applied. Ornelas is not extended into a general rule for all consent issues.

##### III. No indispensable federal release warning; voluntary consent and attenuation remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fourth Amendment does not invariably require an officer to give a free-to-go warning before seeking consent to search. Consent must be voluntary under all the circumstances, and when an unlawful detention preceded or accompanied it, voluntariness alone does not establish that the consent was sufficiently independent of that illegality to permit use of the evidence.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, controls by seven direct joins. Ginsburg and Stevens separately reject an indispensable federal script; neither additional agreement is needed to establish the Court's rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a useful warning is not automatically an indispensable constitutional condition. Schneckloth requires voluntary consent under the total circumstances and treats knowledge of the right to refuse as relevant without making advice of that right universally mandatory. A fixed release formula would replace that inquiry rather than perform it. The absence of a clear release signal nevertheless remains important evidence; rejecting a script does not make every post-warning request consensual.

The supported trial findings concerning the manner, wording and positions of the parties, Robinette's education and the conclusion of traffic business must be considered with the returned license and actual continuation words. His testimony includes both an initial belief that he could leave and a later feeling that he could not refuse. Neither fragment decides voluntariness by itself. The Court holds neither that this consent was valid nor that it was invalid.

Royer and Brown keep consent's voluntariness separate from its causal independence of unlawful detention. If the court finds an unsupported continued seizure, it must address that additional question; voluntary consent does not automatically erase the illegality, and illegality does not establish that every later consent is incurably tainted. Suppression remains available when the applicable inquiry fails. Nothing in federal law prevents Ohio from requiring clearer warnings under independent state law or police policy.

**Precedent treatment:** Schneckloth is applied without a new warning element. Royer and Brown preserve the separate inquiry into the effect of prior illegality; no automatic cure or automatic suppression rule is created.

##### IV. The remedy corrects the federal rules without deciding admissibility

**Holding and operative rule:** Rejection of the warning rule and the motive-based exit-order premise requires further proceedings on the actual objective detention, voluntary consent and any necessary attenuation question. It does not itself establish the search's validity, require admission of the evidence or reinstate the conviction.

**Authority:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer directly join Part IV; Stone-Zsela joins its stated inquiries and limits while selecting vacatur instead of reversal. Ginsburg separately supports reversal with the same unresolved-application boundary. Seven Justices support the Court's formal reversal and remand; the holdings do not merge Stone-Zsela's different remedy into that coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ohio's two grounds must be addressed separately. The federal script requirement fails, and the detention ruling cannot rest on undisclosed motives during lawful traffic business. Yet those errors do not decide whether the later encounter was consensual, lawfully justified or unlawfully extended, or whether consent was both voluntary and independent of any illegality. The state courts must apply the rules to the supported findings and complete encounter. They may consider genuinely independent state law. A new hearing is not commanded merely by this remand, and the Court enters no final evidence-admission or conviction order.

**Precedent treatment:** The rule/application distinction retained in Evans and the separate consent and causation inquiries in Schneckloth, Royer and Brown govern the scope of remand without enlargement.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Michigan v. Long:** Applies the presumption permitting review of interwoven federal grounds absent a plain independent-state-law basis.
- **Arizona v. Evans:** Retains that jurisdictional rule and the distinction between correcting a federal rule and deciding its unresolved factual application; its court-record-error exclusion rule is not extended here.
- **Whren v. United States:** Applies objective legality to the initial traffic business and preserves independent limits on manner, scope and duration.
- **Pennsylvania v. Mimms:** Applies the permission to order a driver out during a lawful stop; no indefinite post-stop detention authority follows.
- **Florida v. Bostick:** Requires assessment of the actual objective capacity to decline requests or terminate the encounter, without a categorical seizure or release rule.
- **Ornelas v. United States:** Preserves its historical-fact/ultimate-sufficiency distinction within its actual scope; no general review rule for all consent questions is added.
- **Schneckloth v. Bustamonte:** Applies total-circumstances voluntariness and treats knowledge as relevant rather than an indispensable warning requirement.
- **Florida v. Royer:** Preserves the need to consider whether consent follows or remains a product of unlawful detention.
- **Brown v. Illinois:** Preserves attenuation as distinct from voluntariness; neither a voluntary response nor the occurrence of illegality alone answers the remedial question.

#### Law After Decision

Federal law imposes no universal release script, but the objective seizure, voluntary-consent and necessary attenuation inquiries remain distinct and enforceable. The decision supplies no categorical rule that a returned license ends a seizure, that a traffic offense authorizes later unrelated detention, or that a polite request ensures admissibility. The actual search result remains unresolved under the corrected federal standards, and genuinely independent Ohio protection remains available.


### 1997-02-19 - Maryland v. Wilson; No. 95-1268; OT1996, chunk 4.

Authority: [Record](../records/Maryland_v_Wilson_merits_1997-02-19.md).

#### Holdings

#### Passenger-exit directions during a lawful traffic stop

**Holding and operative rule:** During a lawful traffic stop, an officer may direct a passenger to leave the vehicle without additional passenger-specific suspicion as a limited safety measure incident to the stop. This authority concerns the exit direction itself; it does not establish categorical forcible detention for the entire stop, permission to frisk or search, or power to prolong detention after the lawful traffic business ends.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, counting its author within that six-Justice coalition; six direct rationale votes control.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court extends Mimms's comparison of officer safety and incremental intrusion to the passenger's immediate relocation during an independently lawful traffic stop. A passenger may conceal a weapon and threaten an officer even though the passenger committed no traffic offense. The safety risk does not disappear when the potentially dangerous occupant sits beside the driver.

The passenger has a stronger initial liberty argument because the driver's offense supplies the reason for the stop. But the direction changes the passenger's location during an interruption already lawfully occasioned by the vehicle's stop. At that limited scope, the safety interest outweighs the additional intrusion. Terry and Brignoni-Ponce require attention to the nature of the intrusion and its justification; their particularized-ground rules for different intrusions remain. The Court does not resolve whether every passenger was already seized at the instant the vehicle stopped or whether a passenger may be forcibly held for its entire duration.

Whren permits an objectively justified ordinary initial stop while preserving execution limits. Robinette likewise separates lawful traffic measures from continued detention after their justification ends. Neither supplies automatic search or frisk authority. References in Michigan v. Long to occupants leaving cars and in the Rakas concurrence do not constitute an earlier controlling passenger-exit holding; the Court decides that question here. Because Maryland presented only categorical authority, the Court neither relies on the observed movements as a distinct justification nor eliminates preservation limits. The contrary categorical federal premise is reversed, leaving ordinary further proceedings rather than a final evidence-admission order.

**Precedent treatment:** Mimms's safety/incremental-intrusion rule extends to this passenger-exit direction. Terry, Brignoni-Ponce, Whren and Robinette retain their distinct predicates and limits; no frisk, search or prolonged-detention rule is displaced.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Pennsylvania v. Mimms supplies the safety and incremental-intrusion comparison and is extended from drivers to the limited passenger-exit direction.
- Terry v. Ohio and United States v. Brignoni-Ponce retain their requirements for the distinct seizures and protective intrusions they govern; this decision does not make every additional police measure categorical.
- Whren v. United States preserves objective authority for the ordinary initial traffic stop and independent constraints on manner, scope and duration.
- Ohio v. Robinette preserves the distinction between measures during lawful traffic business and unsupported continuation after it ends, together with genuinely independent state law.
- Michigan v. Long and the Rakas concurrence are not treated as having already decided the passenger-exit question. Ornelas retains its independent ultimate-suspicion/probable-cause review and protection of historical facts without becoming a universal review rule.

#### Law After Decision

Federal law permits the limited passenger-exit command during a lawful traffic stop without an additional passenger-specific reason. The decision adds that authority while leaving other seizure, duration, search, frisk and independent-state-law questions at their existing scopes. It does not authorize every subsequent command merely because the vehicle was lawfully stopped.


### 1997-04-15 — Chandler v. Miller, No. 96-126

Authority: [Record](../records/Chandler_v_Miller_merits_1997-04-15.md).

#### Holdings

**Authority for all holdings:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies nine votes for each rule and qualification below.

**1. Candidacy does not waive bodily privacy.** Government-required urine collection and chemical analysis are Fourth Amendment searches. Choosing to seek public office does not waive that protection; candidacy's public setting may inform reasonableness but does not itself authorize a compelled bodily search.

**Controlling explanation:** Skinner establishes the search character of compelled urinalysis, and Vernonia preserves that conclusion even where participation in an activity is voluntary. A candidate's exposure to public scrutiny of qualifications is different from consent to bodily collection and medical analysis. Georgia's use of a personal physician and initial disclosure to the candidate reduce the intrusion, but do not eliminate the protected interest. The Court evaluates those actual safeguards and attributes no unsupported public disclosure, observed collection, police use or unrelated medical testing to the program.

**2. Georgia has not supplied a special need sufficient to justify this candidate-testing program.** A suspicionless search justified by a need beyond ordinary law enforcement must be reasonable in light of the actual governmental need, the program's operation, and the affected privacy interests. General desires for drug-free officials and public confidence do not supply the substantial, concrete need required for this broad advance-scheduled candidate program; its reduced intrusion cannot make up that deficiency.

**Controlling explanation:** Skinner and Von Raab permit special-needs searches when the protective or integrity interests and the program's operation justify the departure from individualized suspicion. Vernonia sustains its particular school-athletic program through custodial responsibility, contextual privacy and protective need. Political candidates do not share that setting. Georgia's covered offices have no common duty supplying a comparable protective risk, and the record identifies neither a concrete drug problem nor another substantial operational need connecting this broad class to the search. Sound judgment, integrity and deterrence remain legitimate interests, but do not explain why a negative certificate from one predictable test, scheduled within thirty days before qualification, adequately answers the asserted concern about future service. Advance scheduling limits what the certificate establishes; it does not make every scheduled test useless or unconstitutional. Nor must a State wait for a catastrophe, quantify efficacy, exhaust every alternative, or show safety-sensitive duties in every conceivable valid program. The holding rests on this program's combined deficiencies, not a mandatory checklist of missing facts. Georgia's strongest argument—the importance of trustworthy officials and the modest intrusion—therefore fails because a modest bodily search still needs an adequate governmental justification.

**3. The live prospective challenge requires relief against enforcement, without altering past elections.** Chandler's stated and uncontested intention to run again preserves the controversy despite completion of the 1994 election. The judgment sustaining the challenged requirement is reversed, and the case is remanded for declaratory and appropriate prospective relief barring its enforcement.

**Controlling explanation:** The continuing qualification condition affects Chandler's future candidacy, so resolving that live Fourth Amendment dispute does not require setting aside a completed election. The Court prescribes no replacement testing regime or new candidate qualification, and decides no damages, drug use, test result or individualized disqualification. The holding leaves materially different programs to their own supported special-needs justification. Independent First Amendment or state-law qualification claims, medical-fitness examinations, financial disclosure, particularized-suspicion testing, security screening tied to an actual safety risk, and private-sector testing are not decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association: Applies the rule that compelled urine collection and analysis are searches and its attention to substantiated protective need and actual privacy safeguards.
- Treasury Employees v. Von Raab: Preserves contextual special-needs analysis, including integrity interests; the doctrine is not confined to preventing a physical catastrophe.
- Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton: Applies the search and no-waiver holding and distinguishes the actual school-athletic merits holding on custodial, privacy and protective facts. Its five-Justice program rule, eight-Justice facial-prescription ruling and unanimous search rule remain distinct and unchanged.
- New Jersey v. T.L.O.: Its school-responsibility rationale does not transfer wholesale to adult political candidates. No earlier special-needs decision is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Georgia's challenged candidate-testing condition violates the Fourth Amendment. Suspicionless testing remains available under the existing contextual special-needs doctrine when a materially different program has an adequate supported justification. Public prominence and reduced intrusion alone do not provide it; neither a proven catastrophe nor exhaustion of all alternatives becomes a universal condition.


### 1996-11-18 — Ohio v. Robinette; No. 95-891

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He would vacate the Ohio judgment and remand. He joins the no-script rule, the objective continued-seizure inquiry, and the Court's requirements to examine voluntariness and any necessary causal relationship to unlawful detention. A State cannot convert uninterrupted official restraint into consent merely by describing it as a request. Immediate questioning, documents, tone, positioning and the absence of an objective release signal bear on whether the motorist remained seized and whether the choice was real. A clear warning is strong evidence and may be required by state law or policy. He reaches no final admissibility result and preserves suppression where the applicable inquiries fail. He agrees to federal review and to setting aside the motive-based exit-order ground without adding a reason on those components; his chosen formal disposition remains vacatur, not reversal.

**Justice Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She accepts federal review under governing Long despite her earlier preference in Evans for a different jurisdictional presumption. The Ohio opinion's interwoven analysis supplies no clearly independent state basis. She agrees that federal law requires no universal release script and that actual consent must be assessed under the total circumstances. Her agreement does not decide that Robinette's search was valid or that no detention continued. She emphasizes that Ohio may use its own Constitution to require a clear warning, provided it rests the rule on an independent state ground. Federal correction of this judgment does not displace that authority. She does not join Stevens's conclusion that this record already requires suppression.

**Justice Stevens, dissenting.** He accepts review and rejects an indispensable federal warning formula, but would affirm suppression on the actual encounter. His Bostick dissent's attention to the practical force of police authority, together with Royer and Brown's separation of consent from unlawful detention, leads him to treat the continued official direction after completion of traffic business as restraint requiring an independent justification. The deputy's question before departure continued that restraint; no separate suspicion or intervening circumstance supplies a lawful extension or breaks the connection to consent. Returning the license and Robinette's initial sense of freedom do not decide the effect of the subsequent direction. The deputy's courtesy, lack of a drawn weapon and Robinette's education bear on voluntariness, but furnish no intervening break from the detention. Stevens does not invalidate the initial stop or a lawful exit order because of hidden motive, and he proposes no automatic suppression rule after every illegality. His disagreement is with the Court's refusal to affirm suppression on this record, not with Ohio's freedom to require warnings under independent state law.


### 1997-02-19 - Maryland v. Wilson; No. 95-1268; OT1996, chunk 4.

Authority: [Record](../records/Maryland_v_Wilson_merits_1997-02-19.md).

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Justice Stevens.** Stevens and Kennedy would affirm because the passenger's added restraint needs an objective circumstance connected to the actual stop. Mimms's driver committed the traffic offense and controlled the vehicle; a passenger did neither. Aggregate danger does not justify an otherwise arbitrary command to every innocent passenger. Officers retain practical latitude to act on supported safety circumstances, but an explanation tied to those circumstances must precede the exercise of additional control rather than depend on the officer's grace after categorical authority is conferred. The movements and nervousness are not denied; the State did not preserve them as an independent justification, so they supply no basis to concur in reversal.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** He would permit an exit direction when any objective circumstance reasonably supports it for safety, facilitating a lawful search or conducting the stop. The showing is modest and may arise from movements, numbers, location, conduct or other practical facts; neither probable cause nor a formal suspicion label is required. Mimms's driver-offense predicate does not by itself justify an added command to a passenger. Whren's ordinary stop authority should therefore remain subject to a reason connected to the actual stop, rather than general statistics or administrative ease. These observations might have supplied such a reason if properly preserved, but the Court cannot replace the State's categorical case. He would leave suppression in force and order no remand for a new justification. He reserves commands to remain in a vehicle and preserves lawful-search control and arrest on probable cause.

Justice White
delivered the opinion of the Court.
A “protective sweep” is a quick and limited search of premises, incident to an arrest and conducted to protect the safety of police officers or others. It is narrowly confined to a cursory visual inspection of those places in which a person might be hiding. In this case we must decide what level of justification is required by the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments before police officers, while effecting the arrest of a suspect in his home pursuant to an arrest warrant, may conduct a warrantless protective sweep of all or part of the premises. The Court of Appeals of Maryland held that a running suit seized in plain view during such a protective sweep should have been suppressed at respondent’s armed robbery trial because the officer who conducted the sweep did not have probable cause to believe that a serious and demonstrable potentiality for danger existed. 314 Md. 151, 166, 550 A. 2d 79, 86 (1988). We conclude that the Fourth Amendment would permit the protective sweep undertaken here if the searching officer “possesse[d] a reasonable belief based on ‘specific and articulable facts which, taken together with the rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warranted]’ the officer in believing,” Michigan v. Long, 463 U. S. 1032, 1049-1050 (1983) (quoting Terry v. Ohio, 392 U. S. 1, 21 (1968)), that the area swept harbored an individual posing a danger to the officer or others. We accordingly vacate the judgment below and remand for application of this standard.
I
On February 3, 1986, two men committed an armed robbery of a Godfather’s Pizza restaurant in Prince George’s County, Maryland. One of the robbers was wearing a red running suit. That same day, Prince George’s County police obtained arrest warrants for respondent Jerome Edward Buie and his suspected accomplice in the robbery, Lloyd Allen. Buie’s house was placed under police surveillance.
On February 5, the police executed the arrest warrant for Buie. They first had a police department secretary telephone Buie’s house to verify that he was home. The secretary spoke to a female first, then to Buie himself. Six or seven officers proceeded to Buie’s house. Once inside, the officers fanned out through the first and second floors. Corporal James Rozar announced that he would “freeze” the basement so that no one could come up and surprise the officers. With his service revolver drawn, Rozar twice shouted into the basement, ordering anyone down there to come out. When a voice asked who was calling, Rozar announced three times: “this is the police, show me your hands.” App. 5. Eventually, a pair of hands appeared around the bottom of the stairwell and Buie emerged from the basement. He was arrested, searched, and handcuffed by Rozar. Thereafter, Detective Joseph Frolich entered the basement “in case there was someone else” down there. Id., at 14. He noticed a red running suit lying in plain view on a stack of clothing and seized it.
The trial court denied Buie’s motion to suppress the running suit, stating in part: “The man comes out from a basement, the police don’t know how many other people are down there. He is charged with a serious offense.” Id., at 19. The State introduced the running suit into evidence at Buie’s trial. A jury convicted Buie of robbery with a deadly weapon and using a handgun in the commission of a felony.
The Court of Special Appeals of Maryland affirmed the trial court’s denial of the suppression motion. The court stated that Detective Frolich did not go into the basement to search for evidence, but to look for the suspected accomplice or anyone else who might pose a threat to the officers on the scene. 72 Md. App. 562, 571-572, 531 A. 2d 1290, 1295 (1987).
“Traditionally, the sanctity of a person’s home — his castle — requires that the police may not invade it without a warrant except under the most exigent of circumstances. But once the police are lawfully within the home, their conduct is measured by a standard of reasonableness .... [I]f there is reason to believe that the arrestee had accomplices who are still at large, something less than probable cause — reasonable suspicion — should be sufficient to justify a limited additional intrusion to investigate the possibility of their presence.” Id., at 575-576, 531 A. 2d, at 1297 (emphasis in original).
The Court of Appeals of Maryland reversed by a 4-to-3 vote. 314 Md. 151, 550 A. 2d 79 (1988). The court acknowledged that “when the intrusion is slight, as in the case of a brief stop and frisk on a public street, and the public interest in prevention of crime is substantial, reasonable articulable suspicion may be enough to pass constitutional muster,” id., at 159, 550 A. 2d, at 83. The court, however, stated that when the sanctity of the home is involved, the exceptions to the warrant requirement are few, and held: “[T]o justify a protective sweep of a home, the government must show that there is probable cause to believe that ‘“a serious and demonstrable potentiality for danger”’ exists.” Id., at 159-160, 550 A. 2d, at 83 (citation omitted). The court went on to find that the State had not satisfied that probable-cause requirement. Id., at 165-166, 550 A. 2d, at 86. We granted certiorari, 490 U. S. 1097 (1989).
II
It is not disputed that until the point of Buie’s arrest the police had the right, based on the authority of the arrest warrant, to search anywhere in the house that Buie might have been found, including the basement. “If there is sufficient evidence of a citizen’s participation in a felony to persuade a judicial officer that his arrest is justified, it is constitutionally reasonable to require him to open his doors to the officers of the law.” Payton v. New York, 445 U. S. 573, 602-603 (1980). There is also no dispute that if Detective Frolich’s entry into the basement was lawful, the seizure of the red running suit, which was in plain view and which the officer had probable cause to believe was evidence of a crime, was also lawful under the Fourth Amendment. See Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U. S. 321, 326 (1987). The issue in this case is what level of justification the Fourth Amendment required before Detective Frolich could legally enter the basement to see if someone else was there.
Petitioner, the State of Maryland, argues that, under a general reasonableness balancing test, police should be permitted to conduct a protective sweep whenever they make an in-home arrest for a violent crime. As an alternative to this suggested bright-line rule, the State contends that protective sweeps fall within the ambit of the doctrine announced in Terry v. Ohio, 392 U. S. 1 (1968), and that such sweeps may be conducted in conjunction with a valid in-home arrest whenever the police reasonably suspect a risk of danger to the officers or others at the arrest scene. The United States, as amicus curiae supporting the State, also argues for a Terry-type standard of reasonable, articulable suspicion of risk to the officer, and contends that that standard is met here. Respondent argues that a protective sweep may not be undertaken without a warrant unless the exigencies of the situation render such warrantless search objectively reasonable. According to Buie, because the State has shown neither exigent circumstances to immediately enter Buie’s house nor an unforeseen danger that arose once the officers were in the house, there is no excuse for the failure to obtain a search warrant to search for dangerous persons believed to be on the premises. Buie further contends that, even if the warrant requirement is inapplicable, there is no justification for relaxing the probable-cause standard. If something less than probable cause is sufficient, respondent argues that it is no less than individualized suspicion — specific, articulable facts supporting a reasonable belief that there are persons on the premises who are a threat to the officers. According to Buie, there were no such specific, articulable facts to justify the search of his basement.
Ill
It goes without saying that the Fourth Amendment bars only unreasonable searches and seizures, Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives’ Assn., 489 U. S. 602 (1989). Our cases show that in determining reasonableness, we have balanced the intrusion on the individual’s Fourth Amendment interests against its promotion of legitimate governmental interests. United States v. Villamonte-Marquez, 462 U. S. 579, 588 (1983); Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U. S. 648, 654 (1979). Under this test, a search of the house or office is generally not reasonable without a warrant issued on probable cause. There are other contexts, however, where the public interest is such that neither a warrant nor probable cause is required. Skinner, supra, at 619-620; Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U. S. 868, 873 (1987); New Jersey v. T. L. O., 469 U. S. 325, 340-341 (1985); Terry v. Ohio, 392 U. S., at 20.
The Terry case is most instructive for present purposes. There we held that an on-the-street “frisk” for weapons must be tested by the Fourth Amendment’s general proscription against unreasonable searches because such a frisk involves “an entire rubric of police conduct — necessarily swift action predicated upon the on-the-spot observations of the officer on the beat — which historically has not been, and as a practical matter could not be, subjected to the warrant procedure.” Ibid. We stated that there is “‘no ready test for determining reasonableness other than by balancing the need to search . . . against the invasion which the search . . . entails.’” Id., at 21 (quoting Camara v. Municipal Court of San Francisco, 387 U. S. 523, 536-537 (1967)). Applying that balancing test, it was held that although a frisk for weapons “constitutes a severe, though brief, intrusion upon cherished personal security,” 392 U. S., at 24-25, such a frisk is reasonable when weighed against the “need for law enforcement officers to protect themselves and other prospective victims of violence in situations where they may lack probable cause for an arrest.” Id., at 24. We therefore authorized a limited patdown for weapons where a reasonably prudent officer would be warranted in the belief, based on “specific and articulable facts,” id., at 21, and not on a mere “inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or ‘hunch,’” id., at 27, “that he is dealing with an armed and dangerous individual,” ibid.
In Michigan v. Long, 463 U. S. 1032 (1983), the principles of Terry were applied in the context of a roadside encounter: “[T]he search of the passenger compartment of an automobile, limited to those areas in which a weapon may be placed or hidden, is permissible if the police officer possesses a reasonable belief based on ‘specific and articulable facts which, taken together with the rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant’ the officer in believing that the suspect is dangerous and the suspect may gain immediate control of weapons.” Id., at 1049-1050 (quoting Terry, supra, at 21). The Long Court expressly rejected the contention that Terry restricted preventative searches to the person of a detained suspect. 463 U. S., at 1047. In a sense, Long authorized a “frisk” of an automobile for weapons.
The ingredients to apply the balance struck in Terry and Long are present in this case. Possessing an arrest warrant and probable cause to believe Buie was in his home, the officers were entitled to enter and to search anywhere in the house in which Buie might be found. Once he was found, however, the search for him was over, and there was no longer that particular justification for entering any rooms that had not yet been searched.
That Buie had an expectation of privacy in those remaining areas of his house, however, does not mean such rooms were immune from entry. In Terry and Long we were concerned with the immediate interest of the police officers in taking steps to assure themselves that the persons with whom they were dealing were not armed with, or able to gain immediate control of, a weapon that could unexpectedly and fatally be used against them. In the instant case, there is an analogous interest of the officers in taking steps to assure themselves that the house in which a suspect is being, or has just been, arrested is not harboring other persons who are dangerous and who could unexpectedly launch an attack. The risk of danger in the context of an arrest in the home is as great as, if not greater than, it is in an on-the-street or roadside investigatory encounter. A Terry or Long frisk occurs before a police-citizen confrontation has escalated to the point of arrest. A protective sweep, in contrast, occurs as an adjunct to the serious step of taking a person into custody for the purpose of prosecuting him for a crime. Moreover, unlike an encounter on the street or along a highway, an in-home arrest puts the officer at the disadvantage of being on his adversary’s “turf.” An ambush in a confined setting of unknown configuration is more to be feared than it is in open, more familiar surroundings.
We recognized in Terry that “[ejven a limited search of the outer clothing for weapons constitutes a severe, though brief, intrusion upon cherished personal security, and it must surely be an annoying, frightening, and perhaps humiliating experience.” Terry, supra, at 24-25. But we permitted the intrusion, which was no moré than necessary to protect the officer from harm. Nor do we here suggest, as the State does, that entering rooms not examined prior to the arrest is a de minimis intrusion that may be disregarded. We are quite sure, however, that the arresting officers are permitted in such circumstances to take reasonable steps to ensure their safety after, and while making, the arrest. That interest is sufficient to outweigh the intrusion such procedures may entail.
We agree with the State, as did the court below, that a warrant was not required. We also hold that as an incident to the arrest the officers could, as a precautionary matter and without probable cause or reasonable suspicion, look in closets and other spaces immediately adjoining the place of arrest from which an attack could be immediately launched. Beyond that, however, we hold that there must be articulable facts which, taken together with the rational inferences from those facts, would warrant a reasonably prudent officer in believing that the area to be swept harbors an individual posing a danger to those on the arrest scene. This is no more and no less than was required in Terry and Long, and as in those cases, we think this balance is the proper one.
We should emphasize that such a protective sweep, aimed at protecting the arresting officers, if justified by the circumstances, is nevertheless not a full search of the premises, but may extend only to a cursory inspection of those spaces where a person may be found. The sweep lasts no longer than is necessary to dispel the reasonable suspicion of danger and in any event no longer than it takes to complete the arrest and depart the premises.
IV
Affirmance is not required by Chimel v. California, 395 U. S. 752 (1969), where it was held that in the absence of a search warrant, the justifiable search incident to an in-home arrest could not extend beyond the arrestee’s person and the area from within which the arrestee might have obtained a weapon. First, Chimel was concerned with a full-blown search of the entire house for evidence of the crime for which the arrest was made, see id.,- at 754, 763, not the more limited intrusion contemplated by a protective sweep. Second, the justification for the search incident to arrest considered in Chimel was the threat posed by the arrestee, not the safety threat posed by the house, or more properly by unseen third parties in the house. To reach our conclusion today, therefore, we need not disagree with the Court’s statement in Chimel, id., at 766-767, n. 12, that “the invasion of privacy that results from a top-to-bottom search of a man’s house [cannot be characterized] as ‘minor,’” nor hold that “simply because some interference with an individual’s privacy and freedom of movement has lawfully taken place, further intrusions should automatically be allowed despite the absence of a warrant that the Fourth Amendment would otherwise require,” ibid. The type of search we authorize today is far removed from the “top-to-bottom” search involved in Chimel; moreover, it is decidedly not “automatic],” but may be conducted only when justified by a reasonable, articulable suspicion that the house is harboring a person posing a danger to those on the arrest scene.
V
We conclude that by requiring a protective sweep to be justified by probable cause to believe that a serious and demonstrable potentiality for danger existed, the Court of Appeals of Maryland applied an unnecessarily strict Fourth Amendment standard. The Fourth Amendment permits a properly limited protective sweep in conjunction with an in-home arrest when the searching officer possesses a reasonable belief based on specific and articulable facts that the area to be swept harbors an individual posing a danger to those on the arrest scene. We therefore vacate the judgment below and remand this case to the Court of Appeals of Maryland for further proceedings not inconsistent with this opinion.
It is so ordered.
Buie suggests that because the police could have sought a warrant to search for dangerous persons in the house, they were constitutionally required to do so. But the arrest warrant gave the police every right to enter the home to search for Buie. Once inside, the potential for danger justified a standard of less than probable cause for conducting a limited protective sweep.
The State’s argument that no level of objective justification should be required because of “the danger that inheres in the in-home arrest for a violent crime,” Brief for Petitioner 23, is rebutted by Terry v. Ohio, 392 U. S. 1 (1968), itself. The State argues that “[ojfficers facing the life threatening situation of arresting a violent criminal in the home should not be forced to pause and ponder the legal subtleties associated with a quantum of proof analysis,” Brief for Petitioner 23. But despite the danger that inheres in on-the-street encounters and the need for police to act quickly for their own safety, the Court in Tory did not adopt a bright-line rule authorizing frisks for weapons in all confrontational encounters. Even in high crime areas, where the possibility that any given individual is armed is significant, Terry requires reasonable, individualized suspicion before a frisk for weapons can be conducted. That approach is applied to the protective sweep of a house.
We reject the State’s attempts to analogize this case to Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U. S. 106 (1977) (per curiam), and Michigan v. Summers, 452 U. S. 692 (1981). The intrusion in Mimms —requiring the driver of a lawfully stopped vehicle to exit the car — was “de minimis,” 434 U. S., at 111. Summer's held that a search warrant for a house carries with it the authority to detain its occupants until the search is completed. The State contends that this case is the “mirror image” of Summers and that the arrest warrant carried with it the authority to search for persons who could interfere with the arrest. In that case, however, the search warrant implied a judicial determination that police had probable cause to believe that someone in the home was committing a crime. Here, the existence of the arrest warrant implies nothing about whether dangerous third parties will be found in the arrestee’s house. Moreover, the intrusion in Summers was less severe and much less susceptible to exploitation than a protective sweep. A more analogous ease is Ybarra v. Illinois, 444 U. S. 85 (1979), in which we held that, although armed with a warrant to search a bar and bartender, the police could not frisk the bar’s patrons absent individualized, reasonable suspicion that the person to be frisked was armed and presently dangerous. Here, too, the reasonable suspicion standard — “one of the relatively simple concepts embodied in the Fourth Amendment,” United States v. Sokolow, 490 U. S. 1 (1989)—strikes the proper balance between officer safety and citizen privacy.
Our reliance on the cursory nature of the search is not inconsistent with our statement in Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U. S. 321 (1987), that “[a] search is a search,” id., at 325, or with our refusal in Hicks to sanction a standard less than probable cause on the ground that the search of a stereo was a “cursory inspection,” rather than a “full-blown search,” id., at 328. When the officer in Hicks moved the turntable to look at its serial number, he was searching for evidence plain and simple. There was no interest in officer safety or other exigency at work in that search. A protective sweep is without question a “search,” as was the patdown in Terry, supra, at 16; they are permissible on less than probable cause only because they are limited to that which is necessary to protect the safety of officers and others.

Today the Court holds that reasonable suspicion, rather than probable cause, is necessary to support a protective sweep while an arrest is in progress. I agree with that holding and with the Court’s opinion, but I believe it is important to emphasize that the standard applies only to protective sweeps. Officers conducting such a sweep must have a reasonable basis for believing that their search will reduce the danger of harm to themselves or of violent interference with their mission; in short, the search must be protective.
In this case, to justify Officer Frolich’s entry into the basement, it is the State’s burden to demonstrate that the officers had a reasonable basis for believing not only that someone in the basement might attack them or otherwise try to interfere with the arrest, but also that it would be safer to go down the stairs instead of simply guarding them from above until respondent had been removed from the house. The fact that respondent offered no resistance when he emerged from the basement is somewhat inconsistent with the hypothesis that the danger of an attack by a hidden confederate persisted after the arrest. Moreover, Officer Rozar testified that he was not worried about any possible danger when he arrested Buie. App. 9. Officer Frolich, who conducted the search, supplied no explanation for why he might have thought another person was in the basement. He said only that he “had no idea who lived there.” Id., at 15. This admission is made telling by Officer Frolich’s participation in the 3-day prearrest surveillance of Buie’s home. Id., at 4. The Maryland .Court of Appeals was under the impression that the search took place after “Buie was safely outside the house, handcuffed and unarmed.” 314 Md. 151, 166, 550 A. 2d 79, 86 (1988). All of this suggests that no reasonable suspicion of danger justified the entry into the basement.
Indeed, were the officers concerned about safety, one would expect them to do what Officer Rozar did before the arrest: guard the basement door to prevent surprise attacks. App. 5. As the Court indicates, Officer Frolich might, at the time of the arrest, reasonably have “look[ed] in” the already open basement door, ante, at 334, to ensure that no accomplice had followed Buie to the stairwell. But Officer Frolich did not merely “look in” the basement; he entered it. That strategy is sensible if one wishes to search the basement. It is a surprising choice for an officer, worried about safety, who need not risk entering the stairwell at all.
The State may thus face a formidable task on remand. However, the Maryland courts are better equipped than are we to review the record. See, e. g., 314 Md., at 155, n. 2, 550 A. 2d, at 81, n. 2 (discussing state-law rules restricting review of the record on appeal of suppression decisions); cf. United States v. Hasting, 461 U. S. 499, 516-518 (1983) (Stevens, J., dissenting) (This Court should avoid undertaking record review functions that can “better be performed by other judges”). Moreover, the Maryland Court of Special Appeals suggested that Officer Frolich’s search could survive a “reasonable suspicion” test, 72 Md. App. 562, 576, 531 A. 2d 1290,1297 (1987), and the Maryland Court of Appeals has not reviewed this conclusion. I therefore agree that a remand is appropriate.

Justice Brennan,
Justice Brennan,
with whom Justice Marshall joins, dissenting.
Today the Court for the first time extends Terry v. Ohio, 392 U. S. 1 (1968), into the home, dispensing with the Fourth Amendment’s general requirements of a warrant and probable cause and carving a “reasonable suspicion” exception for protective sweeps in private dwellings. In Terry, supra, the Court held that a police officer may briefly detain a suspect based on a reasonable suspicion of criminal activity and may conduct a limited “frisk” of the suspect for concealed weapons in order to protect herself from personal danger. The Court deemed such a frisk “reasonable” under the Fourth Amendment in light of the special “need for law enforcement officers to protect themselves and other prospective victims of violence” during investigative detentions, id., at 24, and the “brief, though far from inconsiderable, intrusion upon the sanctity of the person.” Id., at 26.
Terry and its early progeny “permitted] only brief investigative stops and extremely limited searches based on reasonable suspicion.” United State v. Place, 462 U. S. 696, 714 (1983) (Brennan, J., concurring in result). But this Court more recently has applied the rationale underlying Terry to a wide variety of more intrusive searches and seizures, prompting my continued criticism of the “‘emerging tendency on the part of the Court to convert the Terry decision’ ” from a narrow exception into one that “ ‘swallow[s] the general rule that [searches] are “reasonable” only if based on probable cause.’” Place, supra, at 719 (Brennan, J., concurring in result) (citations omitted).
The Court today holds that Terry's “reasonable suspicion” standard “strikes the proper balance between officer safety and citizen privacy” for protective sweeps in private dwellings. Ante, at 335, n. 2. I agree with the majority that officers executing an arrest warrant within a private dwelling have an interest in protecting themselves against potential ambush by third parties, see ante, at 333, but the majority offers no support for its assumption that the danger of ambush during planned home arrests approaches the danger of unavoidable “on-the-beat” confrontations in “the myriad daily situations in which policemen and citizens confront each other on the street.” Terry, supra, at 12. In any event, the Court’s implicit judgment that a protective sweep constitutes a “minimally intrusive” search akin to that involved in Terry markedly undervalues the nature and scope of the privacy interests involved.
While the Fourth Amendment protects a person’s privacy intérests in a variety of settings, “physical entry of the home is the chief evil against which the wording of the Fourth Amendment is directed.” United States v. United States District Court, Eastern District of Michigan, 407 U. S. 297, 313 (1972). The Court discounts the nature of the intrusion because it believes that the scope of the intrusion is limited. The Court explains that a protective sweep’s scope is “narrowly confined to a cursory visual inspection of those places in which a person might be hiding,” ante, at 327, and confined in duration to a period “no longer than is necessary to dispel the reasonable suspicion of danger and in any event no longer than it takes to complete the arrest and depart the premises.” Ante, at 335-336. But these spatial and temporal restrictions are not particularly limiting. A protective sweep would bring within police purview virtually all personal possessions within the house not hidden from view in a small enclosed space. Police officers searching for potential ambushers might enter every room including basements and attics; open up closets, lockers, chests, wardrobes, and cars; and peer under beds and behind furniture. The officers will view letters, documents, and personal effects that are on tables or desks or are visible inside open drawers; books, records, tapes, and pictures on shelves; and clothing, medicines, toiletries and other paraphernalia not carefully stored in dresser drawers or bathroom cupboards. While perhaps not a “full-blown” or “top-to-bottom” search, ante, at 336, a protective sweep is much closer to it than to a “limited patdown for weapons” or a “ ‘frisk’ of an automobile.” Ante, at 332. Because the nature and scope of the intrusion sanctioned here are far greater than those upheld in Terry and Long, the Court’s conclusion that “[t]he ingredients to apply the balance struck in Terry and Long are present in this case,” ibid., is unwarranted. ' The “ingredient” of a minimally intrusive search is absent, and the Court’s holding today therefore unpalatably deviates from Terry and its progeny.
In light of the special sanctity of a private residence and the highly intrusive nature of a protective sweep, I firmly believe that police officers must have probable cause to fear that their personal safety is threatened by a hidden confederate of an arrestee before they may sweep through the entire home. Given the state-court determination that the officers searching Buie’s home lacked probable cause to perceive such a danger and therefore were not lawfully present in the basement, I would affirm the state court’s decision to suppress the incriminating evidence. I respectfully dissent.
The Court has recently relied on Terry to relax the warrant and probable-cause requirements for both searches of places, e. g., New York v. Class, 475 U. S. 106 (1986) (search of car interior); Michigan v. Long, 463 U. S. 1032 (1983) (same); and seizures of personal effects, e. g., New Jersey v. T. L. O., 469 U. S. 325 (1985) (search of student’s purse); United States v. Place, 462 U. S. 696 (1983) (seizure of luggage).
Individual police officers necessarily initiate street encounters without advance planning “for a wide variety of purposes.” Terry v. Ohio, 392 U. S., at 13. But officers choosing to execute an arrest warrant in the suspect’s house may minimize any risk of ambush by, for example, a show of force; in this case, at least six armed officers secured the premises. And, of course, officers could select a safer venue for making their arrest.
Here the officers’ arrest warrant for Buie and their probable cause to believe he was present in the house authorized their initial entry. But, as the majority concedes, “[o]nce he was found . . . the search for him was over,” and “Buie had an expectation of privacy in those remaining areas of his house.” Ante, at 333. The fact that some areas were necessarily exposed to the police during Buie’s arrest thus does not diminish his privacy interest in the remaining rooms. See Chimel v. California, 395 U. S. 752, 767, n. 12 (1969) (“[W]e can see no reason why, simply because some interference with an individual’s privacy and freedom of movement has lawfully taken place, further intrusions should automatically be allowed despite the absence of a warrant that the Fourth Amendment would otherwise require”).
The protective sweep in this case may have exceeded the permissible temporal scope defined by the Court. The Court of Appeals of Maryland expressly noted that “at the time of the warrantless search, Buie was safely outside the house, handcuffed and unarmed.” 314 Md. 151, 166, 550 A. 2d 79, 86 (1988). On remand, therefore, the state court need not decide whether the “reasonable suspicion” standard is satisfied in this ease should it determine that the sweep of the basement took place after the police had sufficient time to “complete the arrest and depart the premises.” Ante, at 336.
Indeed, a protective sweep is sufficiently broad in scope that today’s ruling might encourage police officers to execute arrest warrants in suspects’ homes so as to take advantage of the opportunity to peruse the premises for incriminating evidence left in “plain view.” This incentive runs directly counter to our central tenet that “in [no setting] is the zone of privacy more clearly defined than when bounded by the unambiguous physical dimensions of an individual’s home — a zone that finds its roots in clear and specific constitutional terms.” Payton v. New York, 445 U. S. 573, 589 (1980).
The Court’s decision also to expand the “search incident to arrest” exception previously recognized in Chimel v. California, supra, allowing police officers without any requisite level of suspicion to look into “closets and other spaces immediately adjoining the place of arrest from which an attack could be immediately launched,” ante, at 334, is equally disquieting. Chimel established that police officers may presume as a matter of law, without need for factual support in a particular case, that arrestees might take advantage of weapons or destroy evidence in the area “within [their] immediate control”; therefore, a protective search of that area is per se reasonable under the Fourth Amendment. Chimel, supra, at 763. I find much less plausible the Court’s implicit assumption today that arrestees are likely to sprinkle hidden allies throughout the rooms in which they might be arrested. Hence there is no comparable justification for permitting arresting officers to presume as a matter of law that they are threatened by ambush from “immediately adjoining” spaces.
