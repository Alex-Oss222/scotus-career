* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated *Beadle v. ## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Notice and prima facie case

* **Reasoning Below:** The majority required notice before the letters were sent and held general knowledge of Chalmers’s faith insufficient. The dissent viewed Title VII’s text as requiring proof that the adverse action was because of a religious practice and treated notice and accommodation as fact dependent.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Chalmers argues that Tulon knew of her evangelical practice, immediately recognized the letters as religious, discharged her because of them, and had time before discharge to consider a warning or other accommodation; Title VII contains no categorical requirement that every exact practice be forecast in advance.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** Tulon argues that general knowledge of strong beliefs does not notify an employer that a supervisor will send personal admonitions to coworkers’ homes, and that accommodation cannot prevent harm already inflicted without advance notice.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may reject a categorical advance-notice bar while recognizing that lack of notice bears on feasibility, remedy, and whether an employer had a fair opportunity to accommodate.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** Religious motivation does not immunize completed misconduct or require an employer to tolerate recurrence merely because it learns of the motive before discipline.

### Issue 2: Hardship and summary judgment

* **Reasoning Below:** The majority found the letters inherently unaccommodable; the dissent identified factual disputes about their meaning, effect, workplace disruption, and available prospective responses.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Chalmers argues that no written policy prohibited the letters, the subordinate denied lasting offense or work impairment, the supervisor’s letter contained no specific accusation, and a warning against future targeted letters could have protected coworkers without discharge.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** Tulon argues that a supervisor intruded into a subordinate’s medical and intimate life, the letters caused actual distress and marital conflict, and forcing coworkers to receive individualized religious correction would impose a material third-party and managerial burden.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** Title VII does not require coworkers or subordinates to submit to coercive, repeated, authority-backed, or materially harmful religious admonition.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

A lawful path reverses summary judgment because the Fourth Circuit resolved disputed notice, meaning, disruption, and accommodation facts against Chalmers and effectively shifted the statutory hardship burden. The Court can reject both extremes: general knowledge of religion is not automatic notice of every act, but Title VII does not categorically require an employee to forecast the exact practice before the employer may consider accommodation. Trial may determine whether Tulon had sufficient notice before discharge, whether the letters caused actual material hardship, and whether a neutral prospective restriction or lesser discipline was an effective accommodation. The disparate-treatment theory remains abandoned.

## G. RECONCILED QUESTIONS, LAW, AND AVAILABLE PATHS

Notice must concern a religious practice and the conflict requiring a response; knowledge of a worker’s religious identity does not automatically reveal every future communication. Conversely, the absence of advance notice before the first act does not necessarily resolve what the employer knew before discharge or whether a prospective accommodation was possible. Beadle supplies the actual Hardison burden and permits evidence-based prediction without mandatory experimentation. It does not decide a universal advance-notice rule for this expression. The employer relies on specific personal harm and workplace relationships; Chalmers relies on known practice, disputed effects, lack of a clear rule and alternatives to immediate discharge. Religious motive does not erase actual coercion or material third-party burdens, and disagreement with religious content alone does not prove hardship.
