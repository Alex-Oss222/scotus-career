## Standards and Tests selection: Limited drug testing of interscholastic athletes

### Limited drug testing of interscholastic athletes

**Governing question:** Fourth Amendment treatment of a documented school-athletic urinalysis program.

**Current rule:** Collecting and chemically analyzing a student’s urine is a search; enrollment and voluntary athletic participation do not waive protection. In the documented interscholastic setting, objective seasonal and random testing may be reasonable without individualized suspicion where the protective safety need, athletic privacy expectations, limited collection and drug information, confidentiality and restricted counseling or athletic consequences together justify the intrusion. The holding does not authorize testing all students, unrelated medical investigation or ordinary police use.

**Authority by component:**

- I. Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver: I. Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver, 1995-06-26. Part I of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine Justices adopt the search and no-waiver rule.
- II. This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable: II. This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable, 1995-06-26. Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the rule and application.
- III. The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy: III. The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy, 1995-06-26. Part III of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices adopt this limited facial rule. Stone-Zsela does not join it. It independently answers this objection, not the entire testing challenge.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-06-26; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** The upheld program used a concealed-identity student draw with adult witnesses, initial seasonal testing and weekly random selection of about ten percent of athletes, leaving no individualized official selection discretion. Boys remained clothed facing away from a monitor without genital viewing; girls used enclosed stalls with an outside monitor. Drug testing covered amphetamines, marijuana and cocaine and occasional LSD, not alcohol or steroids. Anonymous laboratory numbers, restricted school access, one-year retention and no police transmission, suspension, expulsion or general discipline limited use. Initial refusal excluded only the current season.

**Sequence or decisionmaker:** Prescription verification allows a prescription copy or doctor’s authorization, not compulsory personal delivery to teachers or coaches. Missing verification plus a positive result invokes positive-test procedures, not exclusion for missing verification alone. The written policy permits a second test and requires no action if it is negative; confirmation was supplied by testified operation, not a mandatory written retest rule. Confirmed positives lead to parental notification and a hearing. First offense allows counseling with six weekly tests or current-and-next-season athletic suspension. Written second-offense consequences are current-and-next-season suspension, with a counseling/testing alternative described in practice. Third offense excludes the current and next two seasons; testing precedes the next eligible season.

**Open question:** The facial prescription ruling does not approve every form, nonprescription disclosure, physician-name demand or actual disclosure practice. Materially different programs and the independent state constitutional question remain open. No universal numerical efficacy requirement or suspicion-impracticability prerequisite is adopted.

**Holdings navigation:** I. Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver; II. This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable; III. The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy.

#### Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton

**Docket or dockets:** No. 94-590.
**Decided:** June 26, 1995.

For the other questions in Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton, decided June 26, 1995, No. 94-590. see Federal Courts — Scope of appellate disposition (Federal correction requires reconsideration of the Oregon ground).

##### Urine testing is a search; athletic participation is no waiver

**Holding:** Collection and chemical analysis of a student's urine are Fourth Amendment searches. Enrollment or voluntary athletic participation does not waive that protection; the school and athletic setting instead informs the reasonableness inquiry.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part I of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine Justices adopt the search and no-waiver rule.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Voluntary participation informs expectations but is not consent dispensing with constitutional reasonableness. Unrelated medical investigation and ordinary police use are not authorized.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association, 489 U.S. 602: applied; urine collection and chemical analysis each constitute searches.
- New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325: applied; school warrant and probable-cause adjustments preserve constitutional reasonableness rather than extinguishing protection.

##### This interscholastic-athlete testing program is reasonable

**Holding:** In this documented school-athletic setting, objective seasonal and random urinalysis is reasonable without individualized suspicion because the protective safety need, athletic privacy expectations, limited collection and drug information, confidentiality and restricted counseling or athletic consequences together justify the intrusion. The holding concerns this interscholastic program and record; it creates no authority to test every student, conduct unrelated medical searches or use results for ordinary law enforcement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices adopt the rule and application.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The actual policy qualifications below bound this interscholastic-athlete holding. All-student programs, other extracurricular populations, materially more intrusive practices and other uses remain undecided. No universal exhaustion of less intrusive alternatives, confirmed drug catastrophe or quantified efficacy showing is required. Stone-Zsela's necessity/proportionality proposal and the suspicion-centered dissent are not law.

**Material application:** The challenged policy concerns interscholastic athletes, not all extracurricular participants or the separate voluntary all-student program. Testing occurs initially each season and by weekly random selection of about 10 percent of competing athletes. A student draw with adult witnesses conceals selected identities and leaves no individualized official selection discretion. Boys remain clothed with backs toward a monitor, whose presence permits hearing and sometimes observing production but not viewing genitals. Girls produce in enclosed stalls with a monitor outside who can hear. These are actual privacy intrusions. Initial refusal excludes the athlete for the current season.

The basic laboratory targets are amphetamines, marijuana and cocaine; occasional District-requested LSD testing also occurs. Alcohol and steroids are not tested. Anonymous laboratory numbers, restricted school-result access, one-year retention and no police transmission or suspension, expulsion or general discipline confine the use. Prescription verification permits a prescription copy OR doctor's authorization. Failure to verify combined with a positive result triggers positive-test procedures, not automatic exclusion merely for missing verification. The written policy permits a second test and directs no action if it is negative; testified operation supplies confirmation of positive results. The Court does not rewrite written permission into a mandatory statutory retest rule. Parents are notified and a hearing follows confirmed positives. First offense permits counseling with six weekly tests OR current-and-next-season athletic suspension. Written second-offense consequences are current-and-next-season suspension, while testimony describes a counseling/weekly-testing alternative in practice. Third offense excludes the current and next two seasons. Retesting occurs before the next eligible season.

The trial findings establish increased drug use and discipline difficulties, athletes leading drug groups, observed impaired safety performance and previously attempted educational and deterrent responses. A suspected drug-related wrestling injury is not a toxicologically established causal finding. No finding establishes that Acton uses drugs, that every athlete caused the problem, or that the program has a quantified efficacy.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325: applied for school responsibilities and contextual reasonableness; its reservation of suspicionless searches is resolved only for this program.
- Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association, 489 U.S. 602: applied; a substantiated protective need can justify suspicionless testing after attention to actual privacy burdens and safeguards.
- National Treasury Employees Union v. Von Raab, 489 U.S. 656: applied; special-needs balancing is contextual, not confined to a universally required catastrophic-risk category.

##### The facial prescription-verification objection does not invalidate the policy

**Holding:** The prescription-verification provision permits a prescription copy or a doctor's authorization and does not require personal delivery to teachers or coaches. On this facial policy challenge the Court may consider a confidential means of compliance and does not presume the most intrusive implementation; the rule does not approve every specimen form, nonprescription disclosure, physician-name demand or actual personal-disclosure practice.

**Proposition-level authority:** Part III of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices adopt this limited facial rule. Stone-Zsela does not join it. It independently answers this objection, not the entire testing challenge.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A confidential method, including sealed laboratory delivery, is textually possible, not found to have been used. Confidentiality does not erase the information-privacy intrusion. The complete specimen form is not established and actual more intrusive implementation remains undecided; no separate surviving disclosure claim is created.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Association, 489 U.S. 602: applied at the narrow medication-information context; no general approval of unrelated medical disclosure follows.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Vacate the Ninth Circuit judgment and remand for independent reconsideration of the Oregon constitutional question after correction of its federal premise. No unconditional reinstatement of final dismissal, athletic-eligibility remedy, damages, refund, drug finding, program redesign or reopening of the rejected school-authority issue follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Ginsburg concurs. She joins the Court's opinion because the actual competitive athletic setting, safety findings and bounded safeguards justify this program. She emphasizes that the decision does not approve compulsory testing of every student or every extracurricular participant. Athlete influence on classmates cannot, without more, extend the rationale to a population with materially different privacy expectations and activity risks. Her caution is a limit on the proposition adopted, not judgment-only agreement or a refusal to join the Court's five-Justice program rule. The prescription holding remains facial and confined; actual intrusive disclosure demands and materially different protocols receive no approval.

Justice O'Connor dissents, joined by Stevens and Souter. They would affirm the federal invalidation while recognizing that children remain subject to legitimate school supervision and that the District faced a genuine drug and safety problem. T.L.O. relaxed warrant and probable-cause demands but did not approve suspicionless testing. Skinner and Von Raab establish exceptions, not an absolute rule requiring suspicion in every conceivable school setting; the dissent disputes whether this record establishes enough need to dispense with workable individualized suspicion. The District's observations identified suspected users and misconduct. Neutral drawing reduces arbitrary selection, but does not itself justify obtaining bodily samples from an innocent child. Collection remains an intrusion even with limited observation, drug-specific analysis and restricted use. Athlete participation is no constitutional waiver. Stevens's concern that school constitutional safeguards teach civic values reinforces, rather than replaces, the required legal justification. The dissent does not demand a universally catastrophic risk, deny every protective school interest, or prohibit reasonable-suspicion testing. It joins the limited facial prescription-verification holding because the policy permits a confidential laboratory method; it does not base invalidation on a demand that prescriptions necessarily be delivered to school personnel. Different evidence and a narrower program remain open.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents. He would affirm the Ninth Circuit's invalidation because the District has not established that individualized reasonable-suspicion testing or materially less intrusive measures are impracticable. Urine collection and chemical analysis are searches. A genuine non-law-enforcement safety need permits adjustment of warrant and probable-cause defaults, but recurring bodily and medical searches of a broad class require a proportionate justification for dispensing with individualized cause. Coaching, fitness examinations, communal changing and athletic rules do not waive excretory or medical privacy. Random selection, some disclosure limits and noncriminal uses reduce burdens without answering whether suspicionless testing is justified. The findings concerning athletes leading drug groups establish serious concern, not that athletes as a whole caused the problem or that observation and suspicion-based testing cannot address sport-specific risks. He would permit ordinary disciplinary rules, individualized reasonable-suspicion testing, testing after a specific safety incident, fitness-directed examinations and materially narrower safety programs supported by evidence that individualized methods cannot address the risk. He does not authorize law-enforcement use or unrelated medical testing. His medical-information concern does not establish or adjudicate a separate surviving disclosure claim. His proposed necessity rule is noncontrolling; no other Justice joins this writing.
