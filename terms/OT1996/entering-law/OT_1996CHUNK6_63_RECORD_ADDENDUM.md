## Competing standing paths, without deciding them

The plaintiffs' strongest supported path rests on Feldman's personally encountered City display and recurring municipal bill, their personally directed objection to the City's own ongoing seal use, and the district's actual posttrial findings. The challenged communications originate with the defendant City; an injunction against the identified official use would address that asserted exposure. The current record differs factually from a distant observer relying solely on a general interest in constitutional compliance. Residence and employment provide context but need not carry the entire argument.

The City did not develop an independent standing challenge in these filings. Its petition argues on the merits that plaintiffs have unusually strong sensitivities, had lived or worked in Edmond for years before noticing the cross, and remain free to practice their religions (Petition pp.3–5, 13; Appendix 6a). An analytical jurisdictional counterargument, not invented City briefing, can question whether the proved individual injury amounts to more than disagreement or offense, whether current prospective exposure is adequately established, and what legal work the municipal-taxpayer finding performs. It must confront the actual direct-encounter and monthly-bill material and the posttrial findings rather than assert that the record contains only residence and work. Conversely, noncontest and counsel's broad characterization cannot turn every plaintiff's exposure into an independently proved fact.

Lujan's controlling concrete-injury, causation, redressability and litigation-stage proof requirements remain in the paired slice. Ingebretsen's March 19, 1997 standing holding applies those requirements to a supported imminent personal school-event exposure; it neither creates general taxpayer standing nor adjudicates injury from a municipal seal. Its merits holdings concern official school worship and pressure, with distinct coalitions and limits. Neither legal immunity for every municipal symbol nor automatic invalidity of every government religious image follows. This recovered record is for the modeling context's separate application of existing law.

## Material merits and scope clarifications

**A completed trial, with opposed views.** The case follows a May 24–25, 1994 bench trial at which eight witnesses testified, not a complaint-only or summary-judgment merits record. The district found a secular historical/identification purpose, no impermissible effect, and no religious entanglement; the Tenth Circuit reversed on effect and left purpose and entanglement undecided. Its Appendix 9a–10a n.7 declined to choose between clear-error and independent review because it concluded the judgment failed either standard. Those are lower-court positions, not current Court findings or a stipulated standard of review.

**Historical purpose is contested.** Petition pp.3–5 calls a purely historical purpose undisputed and says no complaint arose before 1992. Opposition pp.9–13 contests both descriptions through the cited trial testimony, original contest materials, and Feldman's 1988 complaint. The district actually found historical purpose and said the seal went unquestioned until 1992 (Appendix 21a, 23a); the contradiction must remain visible rather than silently rewriting that finding or calling the City's account undisputed.

The reproduced 1965 council minutes authorize the contest, public prize funding, and selection of judges, but do not impose an exclusively historical subject requirement (Opposition Appendix 8a–9a). Newspaper contest materials mention the land run, college, culture, commerce, agriculture and friendliness and describe entries' church-related character; they also state an official-stationery/flag purpose (Appendix 10a–14a). Opposition pp.10–12 cites designer Bryan's testimony describing historical and contemporary meanings (Tr.317–334), and quotes former councilmember Priest's testimony connecting the school and churches in Edmond's development (Tr.366). Those sources make the interpretive dispute concrete; they do not independently establish an unconstitutional purpose. Bryan's religious affiliation reported in the newspaper is not proof that a church controlled adoption. The district found no evidence of church participation in adoption or retention (Appendix 25a).

**Government authorship and use have documentary support.** The City's design-selection process and use on official property, personnel, communications and utility bills appear in the lower opinions (Petition Appendix 4a–5a, 21a). Opposition Appendix 3a–5a reproduces examples of a property sticker, City-funded lapel pin, police shoulder patch and welcome sign; Appendix 6a–7a reproduces the March 26, 1993 state trademark registration, identifying the City as registrant for municipal services. These support governmental attribution; the examples do not establish that every plaintiff personally saw each item. Private-forum access precedents do not convert the official seal into a privately sponsored display. The cross's comparative visibility and the resulting constitutional message remain contested merits issues; the parties' design comparisons are not neutral perceptual measurements.

**Other claims and relief remain distinct.** The original complaint sought declaratory and injunctive relief plus nominal damages (Petition Appendix 5a). Individual-capacity claims had been dismissed; the Free Exercise claim's dismissal was not appealed (Appendix 5a–6a and n.4). The plaintiffs' admission that they remained free to practice religion supported the separate Free Exercise fee determination; it is not a finding that they had no personal contact with the seal or no Establishment Clause claim. Fees and costs had a separate appeal, and the opposition contends that their reversal was not included in the City's certiorari questions (pp.2–3 n.3). The present simulation grant controls scope. No nominal-damages award, individual liability, immunity ruling, restored state-law claim, or fee disposition is inferred from the supplied merits questions.
