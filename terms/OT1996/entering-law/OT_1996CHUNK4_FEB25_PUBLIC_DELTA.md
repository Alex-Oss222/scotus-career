# Dunn v. Commodity Futures Trading Commission; No. 95-1181.

Authority: [Public decision](../records/Dunn_v_CFTC_merits_1997-02-25.md).

## Event

Dunn v. Commodity Futures Trading Commission, No. 95-1181. Argued November 13, 1996; decided February 25, 1997. On certiorari to the Second Circuit's judgment affirming appointment of a temporary equity receiver. The question is whether the Treasury Amendment excludes the off-exchange foreign-currency options from Commodity Exchange Act coverage. Investors supplied funds for trades in defendants' names; options or participations were not sold directly to those investors. The reviewed proceedings have not finally adjudicated fraud liability.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Second Circuit's judgment and remands. Justice Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Relief dependent on CFTC authority over these statutorily excluded transactions must be vacated. The lower courts may address a genuinely independent lawful ground for a claim or remedy in its proper procedural posture. The Court makes no final fraud finding, orders no restitution and does not direct blanket dismissal of every possible claim.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court. The complete rationale and remedy are joined by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, with the author included in that coalition. No separate opinion is filed.

## Holdings

### Off-exchange foreign-currency options are within the Treasury Amendment's exclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** Options to buy or sell foreign currency are transactions in foreign currency within the Treasury Amendment, so these off-exchange trades are excluded from the Commodity Exchange Act's coverage. The statutory proviso restores coverage only for transactions involving a sale for future delivery **and** conducted on a board of trade; no bank-only or dealer-only restriction is added to the exclusion.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the rule, explanation and limited remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** A currency option is a transaction whose object is the underlying currency. The contract derives its substance from the currency and its fluctuating value; the statutory words do not postpone that relationship until exercise. The CFTC's distinction between trading currency and trading a right to transact in currency is too narrow. Applied consistently, that distinction would also unsettle the recognized treatment of futures and deprive the express future-delivery proviso of its natural work.

Congress separately listed foreign currency, security warrants, security rights, resales of installment loan contracts, repurchase options, government securities, and mortgages and mortgage purchase commitments. It then stated a particular exception. The Court applies that language and structure without adding a counterparty condition or deciding every listed category's boundary. Both future delivery and trading on a board of trade are required by the proviso; the off-exchange transactions here do not satisfy it.

The distinct regulatory trade-option exemption does not define this statutory exclusion. Its quoted conditions require the offeror reasonably to believe the option is offered to a producer, processor, commercial user or merchant solely for purposes of its business as such. The CFTC's representation below that interbank options qualify under that exemption is preserved at its stated scope; it establishes neither universal regulatory coverage nor qualification of every trade here. A permissible regulatory interpretation cannot create authority withheld by the statute. Auer's own-regulation decision concerns a different inquiry, and the Court need not decide the competing deference contentions to apply this clear statutory boundary.

The alleged fraud cannot supply absent authority. Exclusion from this Act does not protect fraud under other law. Because this is an interlocutory receiver appeal, reversal removes the rejected coverage premise and requires implementation below, without a final liability determination or automatic rejection of genuinely independent grounds.

**Precedent treatment:** The Second Circuit's American Board of Trade option/exercise distinction is rejected at this statutory scope; Salomon Forex's contrary inclusion of off-exchange currency options accords with the rule adopted here. Auer remains confined to its own-regulation setting and supplies no expansion of the enacted exclusion.

## Precedent Treatment

- CFTC v. American Board of Trade, Inc., 803 F.2d 1242, cannot sustain the contrary distinction between an option transaction and its exercise under this exclusion.
- Salomon Forex, Inc. v. Tauber, 8 F.3d 966, is consistent with inclusion of off-exchange foreign-currency options; the Court does not adopt every statement concerning other transactions or counterparties.
- Auer v. Robbins governs permissible interpretation of an agency's own regulation within lawful authority. The present statutory holding neither changes that rule nor decides a broader agency-deference dispute.

## Law After Decision

The Treasury Amendment excludes these off-exchange currency options. Coverage under its proviso requires both future delivery and conduct on a board of trade. The statute and the separate trade-option regulation remain distinct. Enforcement need alone cannot expand the CFTC's statutory authority, and other antifraud laws retain their independent reach.
