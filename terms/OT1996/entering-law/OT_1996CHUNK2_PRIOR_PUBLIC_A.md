### Foucha v. Louisiana, No. 90-5844

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1991; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Louisiana Supreme Court, 563 So.2d 1138 (June 14, 1990), which affirmed continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity acquittal. The question is whether the State may continue that confinement on dangerousness and the acquittee's failure to prove safety after disclaiming present mental illness.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the judgment sustaining Foucha's continued psychiatric confinement and remands for implementation.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Continued custody under Louisiana's challenged insanity-acquittee provision | Reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | End confinement on that basis; remand for implementation. No indefinite continuation while seeking another rationale. Independently lawful custody requires its own authority and procedures. |

The equal-protection theory supplies no separate claim for additional relief and no majority holding. The Court does not find Foucha harmless or prescribe conditions for every possible alternative preventive statute.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The insanity judgment no longer authorizes this psychiatric confinement

**Controlling proposition:** When the State concedes that an insanity acquittee is no longer mentally ill, it may not continue his psychiatric confinement under the existing insanity-acquittee authority solely because he cannot prove that he will be harmless. The original acquittal supports neither a suspended criminal sentence nor indefinite psychiatric custody after its illness predicate disappears.

**Authority:** White's Part II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this proposition and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Louisiana must identify a present lawful basis for the restraint it imposes. Jones v. United States permits initial commitment because an insanity verdict supports an inference of illness and dangerousness; its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling rests on those continuing conditions. O'Connor v. Donaldson establishes that a permissible commitment cannot continue after its supporting basis disappears. Jackson v. Indiana requires the nature and duration of custody to remain reasonably related to its purpose. Louisiana no longer claims that Foucha is mentally ill. His antisocial personality and the doctor's unwillingness to guarantee safety do not restore that conceded absence. The State's substantial interest in protecting the public therefore cannot perpetuate this particular psychiatric commitment. The original criminal conduct does not make the acquittal a conviction authorizing punishment. The Court neither finds Foucha harmless nor sets the maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment. Any separate lawful restraint must rest on its own established authority and procedures.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the continuing illness-and-danger basis of insanity commitment; initial verdict-based commitment and rejection of an automatic hypothetical-sentence ceiling remain intact.
- O'Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563 (1975): applied to the requirement that confinement end when its authorizing basis disappears; the Court does not equate a conditional-release recommendation with a finding of harmlessness.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the required relationship between confinement's nature, duration and purpose.

##### 2. Psychiatric custody requires appropriate grounds and procedures

**Controlling proposition:** Once the insanity-acquittal basis for holding Foucha as a mental patient has ended, Louisiana cannot preserve that psychiatric custody without constitutionally adequate proceedings establishing the grounds required for it. If the State invokes ordinary civil commitment, it must establish current mental illness and dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence; additional procedure cannot supply an absent substantive ground.

**Authority:** White's Part II-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: five of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this section's separate Vitek-based procedural characterization.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a hearing about dangerousness alone cannot authorize confinement as a mental patient after the State disclaims mental illness. Vitek v. Jones recognizes that placement in a psychiatric institution imposes a distinct deprivation requiring appropriate justification and procedure, even for someone already lawfully confined. Jackson v. Indiana requires a new lawful basis and the protections belonging to it when the original commitment purpose no longer supports custody. Addington v. Texas supplies the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment. Louisiana instead requires Foucha to disprove danger, although its conceded facts cannot presently establish the illness required for that civil route. The State's reliance on a counseled release hearing does not answer this defect: fairness in deciding the wrong statutory condition does not establish the missing condition. Baxstrom v. Herold reinforces that an expired original basis does not remove the safeguards of the new proceeding. The Court does not prescribe a universal burden for every possible preventive regime or disturb the distinct rules governing initial insanity commitment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Vitek v. Jones, 445 U.S. 480 (1980): applied to psychiatric institutionalization's distinct liberty deprivation and need for appropriate grounds and procedures.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the need for an independently authorized commitment proceeding after the initial basis expires.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979): applied unchanged to the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment; that burden is not made universal for every preventive custody channel.
- Baxstrom v. Herold, 383 U.S. 107 (1966): relied on for the ordinary protections attending a new civil commitment after the prior detention basis ends; no separate majority equal-protection holding is created here.

##### 3. Public safety does not sustain this unbounded dangerousness regime

**Controlling proposition:** Louisiana's scheme permitting indefinite psychiatric confinement of a no-longer-mentally-ill acquittee because he has not proved nondangerousness violates due process. A legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of a carefully limited substantive authority; the Court leaves materially narrower preventive regimes undecided.

**Authority:** White's Part II-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient rejection of Louisiana's asserted alternative preventive justification. The holding adopts no new general scrutiny tier or universal dangerousness proof standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exceptional power to prevent danger cannot be detached from the limits that justify the particular restraint. United States v. Salerno sustained a narrowly focused pretrial scheme with an identified threat, government proof, an adversarial hearing, review and strict temporal limits. Louisiana's rule instead permits confinement without an endpoint while requiring the acquittee to prove safety. The doctor's reluctance to certify Foucha as harmless is enough under that rule, although the State concedes that the psychiatric illness has ended. A fair procedure for predicting danger does not supply substantive authority for an otherwise unauthorized restraint. The original verdict supplies no punitive authority under Jones. If later conduct constitutes a crime, ordinary criminal proceedings remain available; the Court makes no new finding that a particular assault occurred or warrants conviction. A better hearing alone cannot cure this regime's substantive defect. The Court reserves narrowly drawn alternatives rather than converting today's holding into a universal prohibition of preventive detention.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): distinguished because its confined pretrial detention authority and safeguards do not authorize Louisiana's indefinite post-acquittal psychiatric restraint.
- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the absence of authority to punish an insanity acquittee as a convicted offender.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, Jones's distinct initial-commitment rule remains in force, but Louisiana cannot continue this psychiatric confinement after conceding the end of illness on the acquittee's failure to prove safety. Five Justices additionally require appropriate psychiatric grounds and procedures; ordinary civil commitment retains Addington's clear-and-convincing burden. Six reject the indefinite dangerousness alternative under this scheme. These are applied and clarified channel limits, not a new universal scrutiny tier or dangerousness standard. No majority adopts the plurality's equal-protection rule. Maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment, materially narrower preventive regimes, and the scope of state insanity defenses remain undecided. No Marks inference is needed because each controlling due-process proposition has at least five direct joins.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, concludes in Part III that Louisiana also denies equal protection by withholding ordinary commitment safeguards from a sane acquittee while not permitting comparable indefinite dangerousness confinement for other persons whose original custody basis has ended. The four would require a particularly convincing justification for that disparity; this is a plurality position, not law.

O'Connor emphasizes that the holding concerns this indefinite psychiatric regime. She leaves open detention carefully limited in nature and duration to substantial safety concerns and questions extended confinement beyond what a conviction could have permitted, without deciding that question. No constitutional requirement that a State provide an insanity defense is adopted.

Stone-Zsela agrees that the psychiatric channel's ended predicate requires relief. He reserves independent equal protection, a new general scrutiny tier, universal proof rules and narrowly defined alternative regimes. Implementation cannot allow indefinite continuation while the State looks for a new justification; separate custody requires its own established authority and procedures.

Kennedy would affirm because the insanity adjudication follows proof of criminal conduct and belongs to a distinct legal channel. He views state definitions of criminal insanity and incapacitation as constitutionally relevant and rejects automatic transfer of ordinary civil-commitment rules to this setting.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm because acquittees differ from ordinary civil committees, the State may attach continued safety-based custody to that status, and periodic counseled review is adequate here. They reject an expansive new substantive right against this confinement and distinguish its lack of a fixed endpoint from a refusal ever to provide release review. Neither dissent controls.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Louisiana courts must implement reversal by ending custody under the challenged insanity-acquittee basis. The mandate permits no indefinite continuation of that custody while the State searches for a substitute rationale. Any independently lawful custody requires its own established authority and procedures; the Court orders neither automatic release from a separate lawful restraint nor a new civil commitment. No future hearing date, factual diagnosis, new charge, compliance finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is created.

### Heller v. Doe, No. 92-351

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-22; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Sixth Circuit, Doe v. Cowherd, 965 F.2d 109 (1992).

**Question:** Whether Kentucky’s different burdens of proof and family-participation rules for involuntary commitment of persons with intellectual disability and persons with mental illness violate equal protection, and whether family party status violates procedural due process.

**Material record:** Kentucky required proof of dangerousness, likely benefit from treatment, and a least restrictive placement predicate in the relevant commitment proceedings. For the category described in the then-operative statute as “mental retardation,” proof was clear and convincing and guardians or immediate family could participate as parties. For mental illness, proof was beyond a reasonable doubt and that party status was not supplied. The class challenged these differences. The medical and developmental distinctions offered as rationales concern typical onset, diagnostic history, prediction, and the information held by families; they are not individual findings that any class member is dangerous or incompetent.

**Relief sought:** Kentucky seeks reversal of the challenged federal rulings. The committed class seeks affirmance. Other district-court grounds outside the questions under review remain separate.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. Unequal proof burdens invalidated, 5–4; categorical invalidation of family-party participation reversed, 6–3.

| Family/guardian party status: equal protection and categorical due-process invalidation | Reverse categorical invalidation (6–3) | Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Party participation may continue with counsel, effective contest and independent adjudication. Blackmun, Stevens and Souter would invalidate on equal protection and do not reach the independent due-process question. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The lower overall proof protection lacks a rational relation to the disadvantage imposed

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky’s clear-and-convincing burden for involuntary commitment of adults with intellectual disability, compared with proof beyond a reasonable doubt for mental illness, violates equal protection on the preserved rational-basis challenge: the offered diagnostic, prediction and treatment differences do not rationally justify assigning this class the greater risk of mistaken confinement. Addington’s federal minimum remains unchanged; the remedy must address lawful equalization and severability rather than impose a national reasonable-doubt standard or order every class member released.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory distinction must relate to the particular procedural disadvantage. Cleburne permits relevant distinctions between conditions but does not allow a label to substitute for that relationship. A longer history may make diagnosis or some predictions easier. Commitment, however, also requires dangerousness, likely treatment benefit and a least restrictive placement; the interests at stake remain physical liberty and protection from erroneous confinement. Addington explains the allocation of that error risk and supplies a constitutional minimum, not immunity from an equality challenge when a State provides greater protection to another class. The asserted difference in the intrusiveness of treatment likewise does not adequately explain the lower overall safeguard against confinement. Beach does not demand formal findings or perfect empirical fit, and the Court imposes neither requirement. It rejects the proffered relationship, not legislative generalization as such. The appropriate limited remedy must respect severability and the State’s lawful choices, while every continuing confinement still needs an independent lawful basis.

**Precedent treatment:**

- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: rational relationship to the actual disadvantage required; no new scrutiny tier adopted.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S.418: the clear-and-convincing minimum survives; unequal safeguards remain separately reviewable.
- FCC v. Beach Communications, June 1, 1993: no formal-findings or empirical-certainty requirement imposed; its specific economic justification does not decide this classification.
- Foucha, May 18, 1992: current lawful confinement predicates preserved; noncontrolling equal-protection reasoning is not promoted to law.

##### Family-party participation has a distinct rational basis

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky may distinguish the two commitment proceedings by allowing guardians and immediate family members to participate as parties for adults with a lifelong developmental disability. Continuing family knowledge rationally supports that participation; the availability of testimony or a less extensive form of participation does not by itself defeat ordinary rational-basis review.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that gathering useful information is different from assigning a person less protection against erroneous confinement. A developmental condition and sustained family involvement plausibly give relatives and guardians knowledge of abilities, daily functioning and available care that assists the proceeding. Parham recognizes the value of family information without giving a relative authority to decide whether an adult should be confined. Cleburne requires a rational relationship, and that informational relationship supports the participation distinction here. Kentucky need not show that every family has useful knowledge or that no less extensive procedure could work. Nor does the existence of mental illness within some families eliminate the permissible general distinction. The Court does not presume that family members always share the adult’s interests. Their preferences or convenience establish none of the statutory grounds for confinement, and the independent adjudicator must evaluate their evidence under the lawful burden. This holding sustains party participation as a category, not every application of it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cleburne, 473 U.S.432: relevant differences may support a particular procedural distinction.
- Parham v. J.R., 442 U.S.584: family information may be useful; adult confinement is not delegated to relatives.
- Schweiker v. Wilson, 450 U.S.221: ordinary review does not require the least restrictive legislative alternative.

##### Party participation does not itself deprive the adult of fair adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** Allowing relatives or guardians to participate as parties does not categorically violate procedural due process where the adult has counsel and an effective opportunity to contest adverse evidence before an independent decisionmaker. Relatives may neither supply a substitute for the statutory commitment grounds nor control the decision or suppress the adult’s position.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged party status must be examined within the actual procedural safeguards. Mathews requires consideration of the private interest, the risk of error and value of safeguards, and the government’s interests. The liberty interest is substantial. Kentucky nevertheless provides counsel, professional examination, an opportunity to testify and present evidence, cross-examination and independent adjudication. Relevant family information can improve accuracy when tested through those procedures. The possibility of adverse family interests requires such testing; it does not establish that adding a party inherently makes an accurate decision impossible. Parham does not permit the State to call an adult’s involuntary placement voluntary merely because a relative requested it. The adjudicator must find the statutory grounds under the lawful burden, regardless of the family’s preference. Proceedings that defeat effective contest or independent decisionmaking remain challengeable. The Court reverses the categorical due-process invalidation without determining that every class member’s hearing was fair.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S.319: private interest, error risk and safeguards, and government interests applied separately from equality review.
- Parham, 442 U.S.584: informational participation distinguished from final decisional control.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, the proof distinction is invalid under rational-basis review, with severability and lawful equalization left for remand. The family distinction and party status survive the categorical challenges subject to independent adjudication and effective contest. Addington, the substantive confinement predicates and the unresolved heightened-scrutiny question remain distinct. Kentucky’s repealed voluntary-admission rule and other unreviewed grounds are not decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, would sustain the proof distinction. They regard easier diagnosis, a longer behavioral history supporting prediction, and generally less intrusive treatment as independently sufficient rational grounds. Addington’s minimum is met, and ordinary review permits imperfect generalizations. Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would also invalidate family-party status: useful information can be obtained through witnesses, while party status adds an adversary able to call and cross-examine witnesses and appeal a refusal to confine. They find no rational connection between that additional burden and the different condition and therefore do not reach due process. Blackmun separately preserves his view that disability classifications and substantial restraints on liberty warrant heightened review, while accepting that the proof distinction fails the ordinary standard used here.

Supreme Court review of this merits event is complete. Determine severability and lawful state choices to equalize safeguards; no national reasonable-doubt rule or blanket release. Party participation may continue with counsel, effective contest and independent adjudication. Blackmun, Stevens and Souter would invalidate on equal protection and do not reach the independent due-process question. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Receipt-specific causation under the return-year exclusion

**Controlling proposition:** Under the applicable § 104(a)(2), a taxpayer must satisfy both the regulation's tort-or-tort-type-right requirement and the statute's independent requirement that the particular damages be received on account of personal injuries or sickness; amounts attributable to deductions allowed under § 213 in a prior year remain excepted from the exclusion. Assuming the ADEA claim satisfies the first requirement, these backpay and willfulness-based liquidated damages fail the second and must be included in gross income.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; five directly support the causal holding. Scalia concurs only in the judgment and supplies no vote for this explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these receipts are taxable because their compensating purpose does not satisfy the statutory causal condition. Section 61 begins with inclusion; § 104(a)(2) and Treasury Regulation § 1.104-1(c) require both a qualifying claim and qualifying injury-related receipts. The nature of a legal wrong cannot alone settle what each payment redresses. The backpay here replaces earnings lost through the employment deprivation, rather than wages lost because a demonstrated personal injury prevented work. Thurston supplies the ADEA liquidated amount's punitive purpose; Hazen Paper makes knowledge or reckless disregard the willfulness trigger. That multiplier responds to the employer's conduct, not the extent of personal harm. Missel's compensatory treatment of a different FLSA provision does not control this ADEA award. Discrimination may cause dignitary or emotional injury, but that possibility does not identify these payments as compensation for it. The Court assumes tort-type rights and leaves their general ADEA classification open. It does not exclude all wage-measured injury damages or decide a general punitive-damages tax rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Burke — its fractured judgment remains; no general remedial-range rule is treated as controlling and no new tort-classification alternative is adopted.
- Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston, 469 U.S. 111 — applied for the punitive purpose of ADEA liquidated damages.
- Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins — applied for knowing or reckless willfulness; it does not itself decide tax treatment.
- Overnight Motor Transportation Co. v. Missel, 316 U.S. 572 — distinguished because its FLSA compensatory account does not characterize this ADEA multiplier.
- McKennon v. Nashville Banner Publishing Co. — preserved as distinguishing liability and employment remedy; it supplies no tax-exclusion holding.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Burke supplies its bounded fractured judgment, not a controlling general remedial-menu rule. Thurston and Hazen Paper determine the ADEA multiplier's purpose and trigger; Missel's FLSA rule remains distinct. McKennon governs liability and remedies, not this exclusion.

Both components are included under ordinary allocation and computation rules. The statute governing this return concerns personal injuries or sickness. Its prior-§ 213-deduction exception remains. The Court assumes tort-type rights and decides no categorical ADEA classification, physical/mental-health-only test, general punitive-damages rule or distinct state/common-law settlement allocation. Damages genuinely compensating qualifying physical, medical or emotional injury on another adequate statutory and factual record are not decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Thomas, treats age discrimination as an invasion of a personal statutory right and regards the wage remedy as causally connected to that wrong. The use of earnings as a measure does not, in her view, destroy the traditional tort analogy. Her regulatory discussion, joined also by Souter, accepts the reasonable longstanding regulation as defining the ambiguous exclusion rather than adding a separate arbitrary condition. Souter does not join the personal-right discussion merely by joining this regulatory objection. They would affirm exclusion of both allocated components. Scalia concurs in the judgment without a separate opinion.

#### Source Notes

The return and settlement posture is supported by the lower proceedings and the opposing merits briefs in the [Archive docket collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0559), including respondent’s brief pp. 1–3 and the Commissioner’s brief pp. 5–10. The respondent’s release mentions state and common-law claims, but that wording establishes no separate compensatory allocation beyond the identified ADEA components. The governing legal sources are the applicable §104(a)(2), Treasury Regulation §1.104-1(c), and the authorities discussed above.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Remand for any necessary allocation and tax calculation consistent with inclusion of the identified ADEA components. No exact deficiency, refund, withholding amount or new settlement valuation is ordered. The proceedings on the merits in this Court are complete; lawful tax implementation remains.

### A. St. P. C. v. B. C., No. 94-7810

Merits decision, November 20, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

On merits review of State in the Interest of A.C., 643 So. 2d 743 (La. 1994), Louisiana No. 93-CA-1125, on rehearing October 17, 1994. The question is whether procedural due process permits a bare-preponderance finding of parental sexual abuse to trigger La. Rev. Stat. 9:364(D)'s prohibition of all visitation and contact, or requires clear and convincing proof.

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

#### Judgment

**Louisiana Supreme Court judgment affirmed on the federal proof-burden ground, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, who would reverse that ground and remand.

The mandate returns the matter for state proceedings consistent with the heightened-proof requirement. It does not impose a new visitation arrangement, compel treatment or an admission, or decide whether abuse occurred. The Louisiana judgment separately invalidated, under state separation of powers, the command to give the abused child's therapist greater weight than other visitation testimony when that therapist is a licensed mental-health professional with training, experience and expertise in treating sexual-abuse victims; that state ruling remains undisturbed. Independently lawful child-protection measures remain available; no unrestricted contact is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The five join the heightened statutory trigger, bounded affirmance and protective limits. Justice O’Connor dissents, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; they reject the categorical intermediate-proof requirement and would reverse that ground and remand without approving the old notice defect.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Clear and convincing proof must precede this statutory all-contact restriction

**Operative rule:** Before La. Rev. Stat. 9:364(D)'s severe restriction may be imposed, it must be proved by clear and convincing evidence that the parent sexually abused his or her child or children. A bare-preponderance finding cannot trigger mandatory loss of all visitation and contact until a contradictory (adversarial) hearing establishes both successful completion of a program designed for sexual abusers and that supervised visitation is in the children's best interests. The statutory treatment definition requires evaluation and psychotherapy specifically designed for perpetrators of family violence and conducted by licensed mental-health professionals.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five Justices directly adopt the rule, application and limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews requires examination of the actual loss, the risk of error and the State's protective interest. The consequence here is complete exclusion from a child's life with no fixed endpoint, followed, if two conditions are met, only by supervised contact. Santosky explains why ordinary preponderance may allocate too much risk to a parent facing a grave destruction of family relations. Although the legal relationship survives here, the scope and potentially indefinite duration of this particular exclusion warrant intermediate proof before it begins. The Court extends that method to this statutory consequence, not to every custody or visitation decision.

Protection of the child remains essential. Existing authority for investigation, emergency intervention, temporary separation and supported supervised restrictions permits protective action under the requirements governing each measure while the severe statutory predicate is established. Clear and convincing proof is not a prerequisite to every protective act. The contrary view correctly identifies danger from erroneous permission, but it does not explain why imposing this complete, potentially indefinite regime requires only ordinary preponderance when those independent protections remain available. Foucha and Heller reinforce attention to the grounds and reliability of serious deprivation within their distinct commitment-law settings; they do not already prescribe this parental-contact rule.

The earlier bare-preponderance finding, entered without notice of these additional consequences, cannot supply the required predicate. That conclusion is not a finding that abuse did not occur. The Louisiana judgment is affirmed on this proof-burden ground; the Court neither rewrites the statute nor orders contact. Lawful state implementation, including any available conforming construction, remains for the state courts. No general periodic-review schedule, restoration procedure, treatment-admission condition, fee ruling or proof standard for lesser restrictions is created.

**Precedent treatment:** Mathews is applied to the specific deprivation; Santosky's error-allocation method is extended to this statutory cutoff while its termination holding remains intact. Foucha and Heller are applied only by the stated analogy; their distinct operative rules are unchanged.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319: applied to the actual private interest, error risks, value of heightened proof and protective government interest.
- Santosky v. Kramer, 455 U.S. 745: its error-allocation method is extended to §9:364(D)'s severe all-contact consequence; no general visitation rule replaces its termination holding.
- Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, May 18, 1992: its requirement of lawful grounds and adequate protections informs the analysis without exporting a universal confinement burden.
- Heller v. Doe, 509 U.S. 312, June 24, 1993: its attention to unjustified risk of mistaken commitment is relevant by analogy; its equality and family-participation holdings remain distinct and unchanged.

#### Law After Decision

Due process now requires clear and convincing proof for the abuse finding triggering §9:364(D)'s complete-contact cutoff. Ordinary investigations, emergency intervention, temporary protection and lesser custody or supervised-visitation measures remain subject to their existing legal requirements. State courts retain lawful implementation choices; no finding of nonabuse, entitlement to unsafe contact or universal parental-contact burden follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor dissents, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. The dissent would reverse the categorical requirement of clear and convincing proof and remand for proper consideration of the protective procedure. Santosky concerned complete and irrevocable termination; here parental status survives, and treatment followed by a judicial best-interests determination permits supervised contact. Under Mathews, the process must be assessed as a whole, including danger to a child from an erroneous decision permitting renewed exposure. The severity or duration of a restriction demands adequate process but does not automatically import termination's proof burden.

The dissent distinguishes Foucha's expired confinement predicate and Heller's unjustified disparity between commitment classes. Neither supplies this new categorical rule. O'Connor's position preserves scrutiny of actual duration and adequate review; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas likewise reject transferring a proof rule across distinct protective settings. All four recognize the serious contact loss and reject immediate reliance on the old finding entered without notice of these additional consequences. They do not validate every indefinite restriction, make periodic review a cure for every deprivation, find that abuse occurred, or direct enforcement on the defective notice premise. Their proposed reversal would leave those questions and lawful protective measures for further state proceedings.

#### Source Notes

[State in the Interest of A.C., 643 So. 2d 743 (La. 1994), rehearing](https://law.justia.com/cases/louisiana/supreme-court/1994/93-ca-1125-6.html) supplies the lower judgment and quoted statutory terms; [Santosky v. Kramer, 455 U.S. 745](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep455/usrep455745/usrep455745.pdf) supplies the termination-proof framework. The treatment-admission evidence concerns the actual plan described by the state court, not every treatment program. No later contact order, separate fee entitlement or new abuse finding is decided.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Return to the Louisiana courts for lawful implementation consistent with the federal proof requirement and available child protection; no further Supreme Court event is set.

**End of entry: A. St. P. C. v. B. C., Merits decision, November 20, 1995.**

### Baby Richard, by his guardian ad litem, v. Kirchner (In re Petition of Kirchner)

Decision, June 21, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Decided June 21, 1996, on review under 28 U.S.C. section 1257 of the Illinois Supreme Court's turnover judgment, No. 78101, 164 Ill. 2d 468, 649 N.E.2d 324. The order issued January 25, 1995; the explanatory opinion issued February 28, 1995.

The question is whether the child has an independent due-process interest requiring consideration of serious avoidable harm from the manner of implementing a lawful custody transfer. Richard entered the Does' home four days after birth and has lived there for years. Kirchner was deceived into believing his child had died, discovered the truth after fifty-seven days and promptly asserted his claim. The adoption was invalidated without a finding that he was unfit. His lawful custody entitlement remains fixed. Richard remains with the Does and initial transfer is pending.

The state judgment rejected the child's federal-interest claim and required immediate turnover. The narrower implementation argument falls within that claim. Federal review of implementation does not reopen the state-law entitlement to custody; the domestic-relations exception to diversity jurisdiction does not bar this appellate review.

#### Judgment

**Vacated in part and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm the reviewed federal judgment.

The vacatur removes the categorical rejection of the child's implementation claim and the mandatory forthwith requirement only as necessary to permit meaningful, prompt consideration before compulsory transfer. The adoption's invalidation and Kirchner's lawful custody entitlement remain intact. The Court orders no new custody comparison, fixed stay, period of residence, compulsory long-term contact or return transfer. It finds neither serious injury nor a need for delay.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivered the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II, joined in full by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Their joins cover the protected-interest rule, procedure, limited vacatur and remand. O'Connor dissents, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; they would affirm on the grounds explained below.

#### Holdings

##### I. The child's own interest limits the manner of compulsory implementation

**Operative rule:** When the State abruptly severs an established caregiving relationship to implement an otherwise lawful custody transfer, the child has an independent liberty interest against serious, avoidable injury from the manner of that compulsory implementation. That interest permits a claim directed to concrete transition harm and practicable means of avoiding it; it confers no right to retain an invalid adoption, remain indefinitely with nonparents or defeat a fit father's custody entitlement through a best-interests comparison.

**Authority:** Part I of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct joins support the entire rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The protected interest concerns the child's personal security during affirmative government enforcement. It does not arise merely because a loss is grievous or because the child prefers an existing home. Years of caregiving identify the relationship whose abrupt state-compelled severance may cause the serious injury asserted; they do not themselves prove injury or create permanent custody rights.

Smith recognizes the significance of caregiving ties but assumes the asserted interest and emphasizes the natural family's claim to restoration. The Court respects that limit by preserving Kirchner's entitlement. Parham establishes that children possess liberty interests of their own even when parental authority is legitimate, although its institutional-confinement holding does not decide this case. These principles support a bounded development: the child's own security is not exhausted by deciding which adult is legally entitled to custody. Neither lawful entitlement nor state action alone establishes a constitutional injury; the claim must concern serious avoidable harm attributable to the chosen manner of enforcement.

Stanley and Lehr protect a parent's opportunity to assume responsibility. Deception did not establish Kirchner's abandonment, and protecting the child during implementation cannot confiscate that relationship by delay. Santosky supplies principles concerning family deprivation, not a right to preserve this invalid adoption. Michael H. did not decide every independent interest of a child.

The contrary argument correctly distinguishes parental restoration from institutional confinement. It does not justify treating evidence of serious avoidable injury from compulsory implementation as categorically irrelevant. Flores's distinct custody scheme remains intact. DeShaney and Collins reject general protection guarantees; Pinder preserves the distinction between private nonprotection and attributable affirmative deprivation without deciding this claim. The Court neither guarantees an ideal placement nor constitutionalizes ordinary disappointment. Recognition of this limited interest precedes, and is not created by, the procedural inquiry.

##### II. The required opportunity and relief must fit the actual transition claim

**Operative rule:** Before compulsory implementation in these circumstances, the child's representative must have a meaningful, prompt opportunity to present concrete evidence of serious transition harm and practicable measures to avoid it, and the father must have an opportunity to respond; the state court must consider that issue under Mathews and give a reasoned implementation decision. Procedures and any protective measures must fit the actual showing and preserve the father's entitlement: unsupported requests may be rejected, immediate implementation may follow adequate consideration, and no automatic evidentiary hearing, fixed delay or renewed custody contest is required.

**Authority:** Part II of the same Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the complete procedure and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews weighs the identified private interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation and value of additional procedures, and the government's interests and burdens. Here categorical exclusion prevents the child from showing whether the State's chosen implementation threatens serious avoidable injury. A focused opportunity to present evidence can expose that risk and identify a lawful means of avoiding it. The father must be able to contest the showing; his right and the child's need for a settled home make expedition essential.

The required inquiry concerns implementation, not which household is better. It must address competent evidence of the child's actual circumstances without presuming constitutionally decisive trauma from length of residence. Parham supports procedures fitted to the actual decision rather than an invariant trial. A. St. P. C. illustrates attention to a particular family deprivation, but its clear-and-convincing abuse predicate does not govern this claim. Heller and Foucha likewise supply no universal family-hearing or proof requirement.

The state court may use procedures sufficient to the concrete proffer and actual disputes. The Court prescribes no full trial, appointed clinician, recurring professional approval, number of visits or particular burden of proof. A supported brief transition may be permissible when necessary to avoid the serious implementation harm shown; it is not mandatory. Unsupported requests may be rejected, and adequate consideration may support immediate transfer. Independently lawful emergency protection remains available; no emergency is found here.

The categorical federal rejection and inconsistent forthwith requirement must therefore be vacated to permit the opportunity before compulsory implementation. That limited relief supplies no fixed Supreme Court stay or residence period. It leaves the adoption's invalidation and lawful parental entitlement untouched, and determines neither actual injury nor a necessary transition plan.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Smith v. Organization of Foster Families for Equality & Reform, 431 U.S. 816:** Preserves its assumed-interest analysis and natural-family qualification. Its recognition of caregiving ties informs this new implementation rule but establishes no right to defeat the fit father.
- **Parham v. J.R., 442 U.S. 584:** Applies the principle that the child has an independent liberty interest and that process fits the actual decision; distinguishes its institutional-admission interest and procedures.
- **Stanley v. Illinois, 405 U.S. 645, and Lehr v. Robertson, 463 U.S. 248:** Preserve parental relationship and responsibility protections; the implementation inquiry cannot reopen Kirchner's lawful entitlement.
- **Santosky v. Kramer, 455 U.S. 745:** Preserves its termination and error-allocation principles without importing its proof burden or establishing a right to retain this adoption.
- **Michael H. v. Gerald D., 491 U.S. 110:** Preserves its reservation concerning a child's independent filial claim; its rejection of two legal fathers does not resolve these implementation safeguards.
- **Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319:** Applies only after the bounded liberty interest is identified; requires consideration of that interest, error risk and procedural value, and governmental burdens.
- **Reno v. Flores, March 23, 1993:** Distinguishes the sustained temporary immigration-custody scheme and its actual review avenues. Its facial holdings establish neither this transition rule nor categorical immunity from individual implementation claims.
- **A. St. P. C. v. B. C., November 20, 1995:** Uses its attention to the specific deprivation while preserving its distinct clear-and-convincing rule for Louisiana's complete-contact cutoff.
- **Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992, and Heller v. Doe, June 24, 1993:** Preserve their separate confinement and family-participation rules; neither provides a child-transition proof burden or hearing schedule.
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services, 489 U.S. 189, and Collins v. Harker Heights, February 26, 1992:** Preserve the absence of a general protective-services or safe-environment guarantee and distinguish the present challenge to affirmative compelled implementation.
- **Pinder v. Johnson, May 21, 1996:** Preserves the distinction between private nonprotection and attributable affirmative deprivation and its reserved constitutional merits. Its immunity judgment does not decide this prospective implementation claim.
- **Ankenbrandt v. Richards, June 15, 1992:** Preserves its limited exception to diversity jurisdiction; it does not bar section 1257 review of this decided federal claim.

No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The child now has the bounded liberty interest and opportunity described in Parts I-II when challenging serious avoidable injury from the State's manner of severing an established caregiving relationship through compulsory transfer. The rule develops existing principles; no earlier case had already imposed this particular transition inquiry.

The lawful parental entitlement remains controlling as to permanent custody. Ordinary grief, a preferable-home claim or an unsupported demand for delay supplies no right to renewed custody litigation. Protective measures require support in the actual circumstances and must remain necessary to lawful implementation. The earlier termination, institutional-custody, abuse-proof and individual-immunity rules retain their own scopes.

#### Separate Writings

Justice O'Connor dissents, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. The dissent accepts the distinction between implementation and permanent custody but concludes that the established relationship and possible trauma do not supply the new constitutional liberty predicate. Smith did not establish the asserted foster-family interest and gave special weight to restoration of the natural family. Parham and Flores address institutional restraint, a materially different deprivation from placing a child with his fit father.

Mathews selects process for an established interest; the practicality of a modest inquiry cannot establish that interest. Neither state compulsion, the seriousness of possible loss nor a carefully limited remedy is enough. A. St. P. C.'s particular proof requirement and Heller and Foucha's commitment rules cannot be transferred to this setting. DeShaney and Collins caution against turning concern for safety into a general due-process guarantee. Pinder did not decide the outer affirmative-injury merits and supplies no rule resolving them against every claimant.

The dissent would affirm the reviewed federal judgment while leaving humane state-law implementation measures available. It does not infer that Richard will be unharmed, endorse every abrupt transfer, require a factually identical historical precedent, or foreclose a different independently supported constitutional injury. Its common rationale does not adopt Michael H.'s most-specific-tradition method. The disagreement is with the protected-interest predicate here, not with the father's obligation to comply with otherwise lawful child-protection measures.

#### Source Notes

The [Illinois opinion, 164 Ill. 2d 468](https://static.case.law/ill-2d/164/html/0468-01.html), supplies the turnover proceedings and addresses the child's federal interest at pages 499-501. McMorrow's orderly-transition discussion at page 550 is a dissent's argument, not a clinical finding. The [petition preserved in the Records and Briefs collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386012_1537), pages 26 and 28-29, expressly seeks mitigation of abrupt-transfer harm even when transfer is proper; its report of the state submissions remains distinct from those submissions themselves.

The present dispute concerns a pending initial transfer. The record supplies no current diagnosis, quantified risk, necessary visits or professional transition plan. The parties' disputed accounts of the Does' conduct are not converted into findings, and absence of a clinical finding establishes neither serious harm nor harmlessness.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Illinois Supreme Court for prompt proceedings confined to implementation. Before compulsory transfer, the child's representative must receive the required opportunity and the father an opportunity to respond; the court must consider the actual showing and explain its implementation decision. It may reject an unsupported proffer or direct immediate transfer after adequate consideration. Any supported protective measure must preserve the father's custody entitlement and avoid indefinite nonparent custody.

No fixed timetable, particular professional appointment, continuing visitation arrangement or additional Supreme Court event is ordered. The Court's judgment establishes no prior stay, actual injury, completed transfer or need for a return transfer.

**End of entry: Baby Richard, by his guardian ad litem, v. Kirchner (In re Petition of Kirchner), Decision, June 21, 1996.**
