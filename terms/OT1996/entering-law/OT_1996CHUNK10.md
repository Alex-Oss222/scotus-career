## Standards and Tests selection: Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

### Concrete personal interests and procedural citizen-suit standing

**Governing question:** Article III standing for a citizen-suit plaintiff alleging a deprivation of procedure, including ESA §11(g) enforcement.

**Current rule:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury; a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct rather than to independent third parties' own decisions; and a likelihood, not mere speculation, that the requested relief will redress the injury. Authorization for any person to sue does not supply standing for only the public's undifferentiated interest in lawful administration. A procedural right must protect the plaintiff's own threatened concrete interest; when that connection exists, the plaintiff need not prove that the required procedure will certainly alter the ultimate agency decision. Aesthetic, recreational or professional wildlife interests may qualify, but past visits and indefinite return intentions without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure do not establish threatened interference with personal use. At summary judgment the plaintiff must support challenged standing elements with specific facts by affidavit or other evidence, with favorable reasonable inferences available; allegations cannot replace missing evidence.

**Authority by component:**
- Stage-specific evidentiary requirement and existing injury, traceability and redressability elements: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, “1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage”, June 12, 1992; Scalia Part II, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas (five).
- Prospective personal-use boundary: Lujan, “2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part III-A, same five-Justice opinion; Stone separately supports the record-specific conclusion.
- Citizen-suit and procedural-right boundary: Lujan, “3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest,” June 12, 1992; Scalia Part IV, same five Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, June 12, 1992, for the identified citizen-suit and procedural-right clarification; ordinary Article III elements and evidentiary burdens are retained.

**Exceptions and limits:** No purchased-ticket or particular-document requirement is adopted. The redressability application to absent agencies, binding force of the challenged regulation upon those agencies, effect of particular funding changes and ESA §7(a)(2)'s foreign reach are not controlling holdings.

**Holdings navigation:** Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992) — Standing and personal injury.

## Standards and Tests selection: Private enforcement of Adoption Assistance Act reasonable-efforts and reporting clauses

### Private enforcement of Adoption Assistance Act reasonable-efforts and reporting clauses

**Governing question:** Private enforcement of 42 U.S.C. §671(a)(15)'s reasonable-efforts clause and §671(a)(9)'s reporting clause through §1983 or an action implied directly under the Act.

**Current rule:** Read within the Act's plan and administrative-enforcement structure, §671(a)(15) creates no individual right enforceable under §1983 to compel the asserted reasonable efforts to prevent removal or reunify families. An approved plan's commitment, §671(a)(3)'s statewide-operation requirement and caseworker deficiencies do not supply that missing right. Nor does §671(a)(15) imply a separate private action: beneficial purpose does not establish congressional intent to create the requested remedy. Section 671(a)(9) requires a plan to provide reporting to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency when a state agency has reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse or exploitation and the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B. That administrative direction does not create the asserted private §1983 right or an implied action to obtain caseworker relief. The §1983-right and implied-action inquiries remain distinct; failure to imply an action does not bar an otherwise available enacted §1983 remedy.

**Authority by component:**
- No individual §671(a)(15) right: Suter v. Artist M., March 25, 1992, “Section 671(a)(15) supplies no individual reasonable-efforts right enforceable under §1983,” Part II.
- No separate implied (15) action: the same record, “Section 671(a)(15) supplies no separate implied action,” Part III.
- Reporting trigger and absence of asserted §1983 right: the same record, “The asserted §671(a)(9) reporting claim is not enforceable through §1983,” Part IV-A.
- No implied reporting action: the same record, “The asserted §671(a)(9) claim supplies no separate implied action,” Part IV-B. White for O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas supplies six controlling joins for each component.

**Present operation effective:** Suter, March 25, 1992.

**Exceptions and limits:** No general rule makes state-plan provisions unenforceable. Administrative funding review does not necessarily foreclose an otherwise existing §1983 right, and the State's administrative obligations remain. Appropriate relief presupposes a cognizable action and cannot create the missing right or action.

**Open question:** Private enforceability of §671(a)(16), read with §675(1) and §675(5)(B), remains undecided, as do the preserved claim's merits and proper relief.

**Holdings navigation:** Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992) — Child-welfare statutory enforcement.

## Standards and Tests selection: Circumstantial proof of unconstitutional motive despite qualified immunity

### Circumstantial proof of unconstitutional motive despite qualified immunity

**Governing question:** Whether qualified immunity requires direct evidence when motive is an element of the claim.

**Current rule:** Qualified immunity imposes no categorical direct-evidence requirement for unconstitutional motive. The plaintiff must establish the actual constitutional elements, personal responsibility and causation through legally competent direct or circumstantial evidence under ordinary procedural burdens. Specific nonconclusory support, lawful discovery limits and the clearly-established-law defense remain available; the rule abolishes neither all heightened specificity requirements nor a burden supplied by the underlying claim.

**Authority by component:**

- Circumstantial proof remains available in motive-dependent claims: Circumstantial proof remains available in motive-dependent claims, 1995-06-12. Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer; nine votes support the rule and its operative limits.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-06-12; the component authorities identified above.

**Holdings navigation:** Circumstantial proof remains available in motive-dependent claims.

## Standards and Tests selection: Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct

### Statutory temporal reach and new consequences for completed conduct

**Governing question:** How courts decide whether new legislation governs events preceding enactment.

**Current rule:** First determine whether Congress prescribed the statute’s temporal reach. If it did, that direction governs subject to independent constitutional limits. Otherwise ask whether applying the provision would attach new legal consequences to completed events by impairing existing rights, increasing liability for past conduct or imposing new duties concerning completed transactions. Such operation requires clear congressional intent. Pending litigation alone does not answer the question. Procedural, jurisdictional and prospective-relief labels are not substitutes for examining the provision’s actual operation. Legislative correction of a judicial construction, or a purpose to restore an earlier understanding, does not by itself establish retrospective reach; this legislative inquiry differs from the temporal operation of judicial decisions.

**Authority by component:**

- Sequential temporal inquiry: Landgraf v. USI Film Products — Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s general-framework portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this organizing rule. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas concur on a distinct relevant-activity approach, not as additional joins at this level of generality.
- Corrective purpose and distinction from judicial interpretation: Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc — Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach, 1994-04-26. Stevens’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Souter and Ginsburg, controls by five votes at this explanatory level. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas accept the judicial/legislative distinction through their separate textual method, not through a presumed full join.

**Present operation effective:** Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc, 1994-04-26.

**Holdings navigation:** Determine statutory temporal reach before applying the presumption against new retrospective consequences; Corrective legislative purpose does not itself give an amendment retrospective reach.

**Exceptions and limits:** An express congressional direction concerning the temporal operation of a statute resolves the threshold statutory question, subject to applicable constitutional limits. Such a direction does not alone determine whether Congress may constitutionally require reconsideration of an already completed Article III judgment. Landgraf concerns statutory consequences for earlier conduct in litigation properly before the courts and does not decide that separate final-judgment issue.

**Related current rules:** Congressional reopening of completed private federal judgments supplies Morgan Stanley's and Plaut's separate Article III rule for §27A(b).

## Standards and Tests selection: Religious accommodation and demonstrated operational hardship

### Religious accommodation and demonstrated operational hardship

**Governing question:** An unresolved work conflict with religious observance under Title VII.

**Current rule:** Under §2000e(j), the employer must reasonably accommodate religious practice unless it demonstrates inability without undue hardship. The governing Hardison threshold permits refusal of more than de minimis cost, including concrete lost efficiency, impaired training, safety burdens and protected seniority interests. Neutral scheduling and absence of hostility do not discharge this separate duty. If an effective reasonable accommodation resolves the conflict, the employer need not supply the employee’s preferred alternative or disprove every other possibility. If the conflict remains, hardship must address supported, materially distinct proposals and actual operations; a concession about one proposal does not dispose of materially different ones.

**Authority by component:**

- Beadle — both accommodation holdings, 1996-02-05; Stevens and all seven other Associates (eight); Stone-Zsela dissents.

**Present operation effective:** Beadle, February 5, 1996.

**Exceptions and limits:** Professional evidence and reasonable operational inferences may establish hardship without waiting for an accident or monetizing every burden. No independent negotiation duty arises when effective accommodation or adequate hardship already exists. General safety concerns create no occupational exemption, and this rule establishes no particular proposal’s feasibility or entitlement.

**Holdings navigation:** Beadle v. City of Tampa, judgment below 42 F.3d 633.

## Standards and Tests selection: Qualified-immunity legal appeals and factual sufficiency

### Qualified-immunity legal appeals and factual sufficiency

**Governing question:** The boundary of immediate review under §1291 when qualified immunity is denied.

**Current rule:** Immediate review may address the separable legal question whether conduct on the relevant assumed facts violates clearly established law, including the legality of a categorical direct-evidence condition. It may not determine only whether pretrial evidence creates a genuine dispute about the official’s participation, or choose between disputed motive, causation or factual accounts. A legal appeal uses the district court’s assumed facts; if not stated, the appellate court may identify likely assumptions favorable to the nonmovant without deciding truth or reweighing sufficiency. An abstract legal contention does not bring the factual dispute within immediate review. An earlier pleading-stage appeal does not by itself exhaust review of a later summary-judgment order: a separable legal immunity question must independently satisfy the collateral-order conditions. This permits neither repetition of an unchanged ruling nor relitigation of matters resolved by the first mandate. Having to defend a different surviving claim does not itself defeat otherwise authorized claim-specific review, but neither supplies missing authorization nor makes that other claim reviewable.

**Authority by component:**

- Factual sufficiency is not an immediate qualified-immunity appeal: Factual sufficiency is not an immediate qualified-immunity appeal, 1995-06-12. Justice Breyer's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; all nine adopt this bounded rule.
- Separable legal review of the categorical evidence rule: Separable legal review of the categorical evidence rule, 1995-06-12. Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer; all nine support this legal-review proposition.

- Successive-stage review and preserved mandate limits: Behrens — Holding 1, February 21, 1996; Ginsburg Part I for Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and herself (seven).
- Conditional assumed-fact rule: Behrens — Holding 2, February 21, 1996; unanimous Part II. Claim-specific effect of another surviving claim: Holding 3, Part III for all eight Associates; Stone-Zsela does not join. Stevens and Breyer join these conditional rules while opposing authorization of the successive appeal.

**Present operation effective:** Behrens, February 21, 1996; Johnson and Kimberlin retain their original component authority.

**Open question:** Genuinely necessary connected review and a categorical rule for every mixed order remain unresolved. Johnson and Kimberlin use their common entering baseline; neither is treated as earlier same-day law for the other.

**Holdings navigation:** Factual sufficiency is not an immediate qualified-immunity appeal; Separable legal review of the categorical evidence rule; Behrens v. Pelletier, 516 U.S. 299.

## Standards and Tests selection: State-created liberty in prison discipline

### State-created liberty in prison discipline

**Governing question:** When substantive prison law creates a protected liberty interest.

**Current rule:** A State creates protected prison liberty when substantive law meaningfully restricts officials’ authority to impose a significant deprivation. Mandatory words alone are insufficient: courts examine the genuine constraint in the whole disciplinary scheme and whether the restraint is meaningful rather than routine. This is a sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement rule; state codification is unnecessary for directly protected constitutional liberty.

**Authority by component:**

- Substantive state constraints and meaningful prison liberty: Substantive state constraints and meaningful prison liberty, 1995-06-19. Breyer’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt this sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement rule.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-06-19; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** Other direct constitutional, unexpected-restraint and sentence-duration protections are not displaced. A protected interest and required process need their own determinations. No new multifactor test, categorical rejection of punitive constraints or automatic liability follows.

**Holdings navigation:** Substantive state constraints and meaningful prison liberty.

## Standards and Tests selection: Chapter 7 strip-down of allowed real-property liens under §506(d)

### Chapter 7 strip-down of allowed real-property liens under §506(d)

**Governing question:** Whether §506(d) reduces an allowed, lien-backed real-property debt to its current judicially determined collateral value in the Chapter 7 setting decided in *Dewsnup*.

**Current rule:** When the claim is allowed under §502 and secured by the real-property lien, §506(d) does not strip that lien down merely because §506(a) values the collateral below the allowed debt. Section 506(a) continues to value secured and unsecured amounts for treatment of the claim; that valuation does not automatically avoid the corresponding lien under §506(d). The rule distinguishes personal discharge from lien survival and does not immunize liens from an independently applicable express avoidance provision.

**Authority by component:**

- Limited Chapter 7 construction and continued valuation function: Dewsnup v. Timm — Allowed real-property liens in the presented Chapter 7 setting, January 15, 1992; Blackmun's opinion joined by White, Stevens, O'Connor, and Kennedy.

**Present operation effective:** *Dewsnup*, January 15, 1992.

**Open question:** Other statutory settings, reorganization restrictions, express statutory exceptions, and estate-administration issues are not resolved by this construction. It does not adopt an abandonment rationale.

**Holdings navigation:** Dewsnup v. Timm, 502 U.S. 410 (1992) — Liens and claim valuation.

## Standards and Tests selection: Principal-residence mortgage rights in Chapter 13

### Principal-residence mortgage rights in Chapter 13

**Governing question:** What instructions govern principal-residence mortgage rights in Chapter 13?

**Current rule:** When an allowed claim secured only by the debtor's principal-residence real property retains a secured portion, §1322(b)(2) protects the holder's mortgage rights as a whole despite §506(a) valuation and bifurcation. The debtor cannot modify the deficiency portion by altering protected repayment or enforcement rights. Independent restrictions and §1322(b)(5) remain: cure within a reasonable time and maintenance during the case are permitted when the last claim payment falls after the final plan payment, not general rewriting. The stay remains distinct. Wholly unsecured liens and classification of ancillary deed provisions are undecided; Dewsnup's Chapter 7 rule remains separate.

**Authority by component:**

- Valuation does not remove the protected rights of this mortgage holder: Nobelman v. American Savings Bank — Valuation does not remove the protected rights of this mortgage holder, 1993-06-01. Thomas’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Present operation effective:** 1993-06-01; Nobelman v. American Savings Bank, for the components identified above.

**Holdings navigation:** Nobelman v. American Savings Bank, 508 U.S. 324 (1993).
