### Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, No. 90-918

Merits decision, February 26, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 11, 1991. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 911 F.2d 617. The district court dismissed the complaint on the categorical ground that Title IX did not permit damages; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The questions are whether compensatory damages are available in the recognized Title IX private action for intentional discrimination, and whether the Spending Clause notice principle bars that relief.

Franklin seeks reversal and ordinary litigation of her damages claim. She alleges repeated sexual harassment and abuse by teacher/coach Andrew Hill while she attended a federally funded high school, official awareness and failure to act, discouragement of her complaint and closure of the investigation following Hill’s conditional resignation. These are allegations, not judicial findings. She no longer attends the school. The lower court relied on its Title VI precedent Drayden and a Spending Clause limitation; Judge Johnson regarded Drayden as controlling without reaching the additional constitutional characterization. Respondents and the United States defend the damages bar. Backpay and prospective student relief would not redress the pleaded completed injury.

#### Judgment

The Eleventh Circuit is reversed, 9–0, and the case is remanded for further proceedings on the damages claim.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical bar to Title IX damages | Reversed and remanded; 9-0 | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | Compensatory relief may be sought for proved intentional discrimination; liability, attribution, causation and amount remain to be adjudicated. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Compensatory relief in the established Title IX action

**Controlling proposition:** When a cognizable federal statutory cause of action exists, courts may award appropriate relief absent clear congressional direction limiting it; recognizing that remedy does not create a new cause of action. In Title IX’s established private action, compensatory damages are available for proved intentional discrimination, subject to proof of recipient responsibility, causation and compensable loss.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy and Souter; seven Justices control the remedial rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Title IX does not confine an established private action to remedies incapable of redressing a completed personal injury. Cannon supplies the cause of action; Bell v. Hood and Davis v. Passman distinguish that authority to sue from the appropriate relief a court may grant. Borak confirms that statutory silence about remedy does not itself confine a recognized action to a declaration. Guardians and Darrone support compensatory relief for intentional violations of closely related discrimination statutes. Congress’s 1986 reference to remedies at law and in equity, followed by legislation preserving private enforcement, supplies no direction to bar damages. Recognizing compensation therefore implements the existing action rather than expanding judicial power to hear a new one. Franklin’s departure from school shows why backpay and prospective relief cannot compensate the pleaded past injury. The allegations still require adjudication; the Court awards nothing and decides neither a final recipient-attribution rule nor punitive or unintended-effects liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — applied as the established Title IX cause of action; not reopened.
- *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 — applied for appropriate remedies in an existing cognizable action, absent a congressional limit.
- *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 — applied to separate existence of an action from available relief.
- *J. I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426 — applied against limiting relief merely because the statute does not enumerate the remedy.
- *Guardians Association v. Civil Service Commission*, 463 U.S. 582 — its intentional-discrimination remedial support is applied without treating that decision as having a single majority rationale.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — analogous monetary relief supports the availability of compensation.

##### Notice does not bar damages for the pleaded intentional violation

**Controlling proposition:** The Spending Clause notice principle does not categorically bar compensatory relief for an intentional violation of Title IX’s express prohibition of sex discrimination within its established private action. Ambiguous funding conditions, unintentional effects and the separate requirements for attributing misconduct to a recipient are not resolved.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by the same seven Justices, rejects the distinct funding-notice ground offered to sustain dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted lack of notice cannot sustain this categorical damages bar. Pennhurst requires fair notice of conditions imposed on recipients of federal funds; it does not authorize intentional breach of a clear nondiscrimination condition while limiting the injured person to ineffective prospective relief. Title IX expressly forbids sex discrimination, and Cannon supplies the established enforcement action. The pleaded sexual harassment and abuse describe intentional discrimination of the kind addressed by Meritor Savings Bank’s explanation of sex-based harassment, without deciding here when a school is responsible for a teacher’s conduct. Darrone also defeats the premise that a spending-based statute necessarily excludes a monetary award for intentional violations. The objection cannot succeed merely by describing compensation as payment from a recipient’s treasury. This conclusion rests on the enacted condition and existing enforcement law, not a general power to redress every injury. Genuinely ambiguous conditions, proof of institutional responsibility and unintended discriminatory effects remain distinct questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1 — its funding-condition notice rule is preserved; it does not bar relief for this alleged intentional violation.
- *Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 — its identification of sex-based harassment informs the pleaded discrimination; no Title VII attribution formula is imported.
- *Consolidated Rail Corp. v. Darrone*, 465 U.S. 624 — applied against a categorical objection to monetary remedies under spending legislation.
- *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677 — the established action supplies the enforcement premise for the notice analysis.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 26, appropriate relief in a cognizable statutory action is not barred by remedial silence alone, and Title IX permits compensation for intentional discrimination. Before this decision, the cause existed but the presented damages bar was unresolved; afterward, that bar and the categorical Spending Clause objection are rejected. No new implied action, punitive remedy, liability for unintended effects, automatic institutional responsibility, or finding against the school is established.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Thomas, agrees that the damages claim may proceed because the 1986 enactment acknowledges legal as well as equitable remedies. They decline to presume every remedy merely from a cause of action that judges previously implied, and do not join the Court’s broader remedial reasoning.

Dates and procedural background: [503 U.S. 60 (1992), pp. 60–78](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-60.pdf).

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review ends with reversal and remand. The damages complaint returns for ordinary proceedings on liability, attribution, causation and compensable loss. No amount is awarded and no prospective school-supervision order is entered.

**End of entry: Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, merits decision, February 26, 1992.**

### Suter v. Artist M., No. 90-1488

Merits decision, March 25, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 1991. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, which decided *Artist M. v. Johnson*, 917 F.2d 980, on October 29, 1990. Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

Children in Cook County alleged that Illinois failed promptly to assign and replace the caseworkers through whom protective and reunification services were arranged. The Seventh Circuit affirmed denial of dismissal and a preliminary injunction requiring assignment and reassignment within three working days, together with weekly compliance reports. It relied on §1983 and an implied private action, principally discussing §671(a)(15) of the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act. The complaint also presented claims under §671(a)(9) and §671(a)(16). The officials seek reversal of private enforcement and the resulting injunction; the children seek preservation of prospective relief. The separately dismissed constitutional count was not appealed. The B.H. decree covers a different class and specified obligations and includes no admission.

The question is whether the children may enforce the asserted reasonable-efforts and reporting duties through §1983 or directly under the Act, with separate consideration of the case-plan and review claim.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses private enforcement of the reasonable-efforts and reporting claims, vacates the existing preliminary relief, and preserves the independent case-plan and review claim for consideration below.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The separate B.H. decree leaves this controversy live

**Controlling proposition:** The B.H. consent decree neither moots this action nor prevents the officials from contesting the asserted statutory right: its class omits children remaining at home under protective orders, its obligations do not subsume this injunction, and its defined undertaking contains no admission of the independent statutory claim. The Court therefore reaches the private-enforcement questions presented here.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine Justices adopt this threshold ruling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a decree in another action ends this controversy only if its actual operation removes the live dispute. The B.H. class excludes children who remain in their homes under protective orders, while this action includes them. Its limits on caseloads and other defined duties do not supply the three-working-day assignment and reassignment requirement at issue here. The officials' agreement also disclaims an admission and specifies the efforts it requires. Those terms establish that accepting the decree does not concede the independent statutory entitlement asserted here. Rufo's preservation of consent obligations beyond constitutional minima provides an analogy, not a holding on this statute or this decree. The children's reliance on shared defendants and overlapping objectives cannot overcome the differences in coverage and relief. The Court leaves the B.H. decree in place and decides neither its modification nor any asserted violation of it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, January 15, 1992 — its preservation of consent obligations beyond constitutional minima provides analogous support; its Rule 60(b)(5) standard is not applied to modify B.H., and its holding remains unchanged.

##### Section 671(a)(15) supplies no individual reasonable-efforts right enforceable under §1983

**Controlling proposition:** Section 671(a)(15), read in the Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act's plan and administrative-enforcement structure, does not confer on these beneficiaries an individual right enforceable through §1983 to compel the asserted reasonable efforts to prevent removal or reunify families. An approved plan's reasonable-efforts commitment, §671(a)(3)'s statewide-operation requirement, and the record's caseworker deficiencies do not supply that missing federal right.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this rule and reverse the §1983 ruling under §671(a)(15).

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a federal funding condition does not by itself identify an individual right enforceable through §1983. Thiboutot permits enforcement of statutory rights, while Wright and Wilder require examination of the particular entitlement Congress established. Pennhurst requires ascertainable obligations when States accept federal funds. Unlike the rent limitation in Wright and the reimbursement requirements in Wilder, this reasonable-efforts clause supplies no further measure of the services an individual child may demand. Illinois has an approved plan containing the commitment; the statewide-operation language does not convert that commitment into the asserted private entitlement. The serious absence of caseworkers and the feasibility of a three-day remedy cannot answer the prior question whether federal law creates that entitlement. Administrative funding review and the required removal findings give the clause operation without this private action. The Court does not hold that those mechanisms comprehensively foreclose otherwise existing §1983 rights, or that every requirement contained in a state plan is unenforceable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Maine v. Thiboutot, 448 U.S. 1 (1980) — preserves statutory-right enforcement under §1983; a violation of federal law still must invade an enforceable right.
- Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority, 479 U.S. 418 (1987) — distinguishes its defined rent and utility entitlement; its enforceability holding and separate foreclosure inquiry remain operative.
- Wilder v. Virginia Hospital Association, 496 U.S. 498 (1990) — distinguishes its substantive reimbursement obligation and supplied benchmarks; neither its Medicaid holding nor its rejection of automatic foreclosure by funding withdrawal is overruled.
- Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman, 451 U.S. 1 (1981) — applies its clear-notice principle to the asserted funding obligation; no categorical immunity for state-plan provisions is created.

##### Section 671(a)(15) supplies no separate implied action

**Controlling proposition:** The Act does not create an implied private cause of action to enforce §671(a)(15)'s reasonable-efforts clause because the claimants have not established congressional intent to create that remedy. This conclusion concerns the distinct action asserted directly under the Act and does not make failure to imply an action a bar to an otherwise available enacted §1983 remedy.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this independently necessary ground for rejecting the second enforcement route sustained below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a statute's beneficial purpose cannot substitute for congressional intent to authorize the private action asserted. Cort identifies the considerations bearing on an implied remedy, and Transamerica makes legislative intent the controlling inquiry. Children and families plainly benefit from the Act's objectives, but that fact does not resolve who may enforce every undertaking through a lawsuit. The reasonable-efforts clause directs the contents of the funding plan, leaves substantial choices to the State, and operates with administrative review and removal-related findings. Those features do not show an intention to create the additional private action sought here. The Court of Appeals' reliance on the desirability of effective services cannot supply the missing authorization. This inquiry remains distinct from determining whether an existing right may be enforced through §1983. Franklin likewise permits appropriate remedies only after a cognizable action exists; it cannot create this one. No other provision's implied remedy is resolved merely by this conclusion about reasonable efforts.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cort v. Ash, 422 U.S. 66 (1975) — applies its inquiry into an implied private remedy, with congressional intent decisive.
- Transamerica Mortgage Advisors, Inc. v. Lewis, 444 U.S. 11 (1979) — applies its requirement of congressional intent to create the particular remedy asserted; statutory purpose alone is insufficient.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February 26, 1992 — preserves the distinction between the existence of a cognizable action and appropriate relief within it; no Title IX holding is narrowed.

##### The asserted §671(a)(9) reporting claim is not enforceable through §1983

**Controlling proposition:** Section 671(a)(9)'s requirement that a state plan provide for reporting to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency when a state agency has reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse, or exploitation, and the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B, does not confer on these respondents a private federal right enforceable through §1983 on the claim presented. The reporting ground therefore cannot independently sustain the challenged caseworker injunction.

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-A, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this separate statutory holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the reporting provision does not supply these respondents with the private action missing from their reasonable-efforts claim. Section 671(a)(9) addresses a state agency's reason to believe a child's home or institution is unsuitable because of neglect, abuse, or exploitation, where the child's care is paid for in whole or in part under Title IV-E or IV-B. It specifies notice to the appropriate court or law-enforcement agency. That administrative reporting direction does not itself turn the plan feature into the individual federal entitlement asserted here. Wright and Wilder require identification of the right created by the particular statutory provision, not merely conduct a State has undertaken to include in its approved program. The lower court cannot sustain this injunction simply by grouping reporting with reasonable efforts. The Court rejects the asserted §1983 route while leaving the State's reporting undertaking and administrative enforcement intact. It does not decide the separately defined case-plan and review claim under §671(a)(16).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wright, 479 U.S. 418 — applies the need to identify a privately enforceable statutory right; its distinct rent holding remains in force.
- Wilder, 496 U.S. 498 — applies provision-specific examination of a binding individual entitlement; no general rule against enforcement of plan provisions replaces it.

##### The asserted §671(a)(9) claim supplies no separate implied action

**Controlling proposition:** Section 671(a)(9) does not create an implied private action for these respondents to obtain the challenged caseworker relief: its reporting requirement and the Act's enforcement structure do not establish congressional intent to authorize that action. Rejection of this distinct statutory vehicle does not decide private enforcement of §671(a)(16).

**Authority:** White's Opinion of the Court, Part IV-B, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt the ground rejecting direct implied enforcement of the reporting claim.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a duty to notify a designated public authority does not, without the required legislative intent, authorize the private suit asserted here. Cort and Transamerica require examination of the particular action Congress intended to permit. Section 671(a)(9) specifies reporting within the state-plan structure; it does not identify an additional private judicial vehicle for obtaining caseworker assignments. The children's interest in safe placements explains why the reporting obligation matters, but beneficial purpose and the usefulness of an injunction do not establish the missing congressional choice. The Act's administrative oversight remains available, and rejecting this asserted action neither excuses noncompliance with the plan nor decides that every more specifically defined obligation has the same enforcement consequences. Franklin's rule about appropriate relief in an existing action cannot bridge the absence of the asserted action itself. The Court therefore rejects this alternative route separately from its §1983 analysis and leaves the case-plan claim for its own consideration on remand.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cort, 422 U.S. 66, and Transamerica, 444 U.S. 11 — apply the congressional-intent requirement to the distinct implied reporting action.
- Franklin, February 26, 1992 — applies its action/remedy distinction without changing the established Title IX action or relief available within it.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The Court does not decide whether §671(a)(16), read with §675(1) and §675(5)(B), creates a privately enforceable right or implied action, whether the preserved allegations establish a violation, or what additional relief would be proper. Those matters return for provision-specific consideration. The Court does not find the reporting claim abandoned, revive the dismissed constitutional count, award damages, prescribe a nationwide three-day assignment deadline, modify the B.H. decree, or hold that administrative funding withdrawal necessarily forecloses an otherwise existing §1983 right. Safety, eligibility, actual statutory requirements and proper remedial scope remain separate questions wherever legally presented.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective March 25, the Court rejects §1983 and direct implied enforcement of the asserted §671(a)(15) and §671(a)(9) claims and removes their existing preliminary remedy. Section 671(a)(16) remains undecided for separate consideration below. The B.H. decree supplies no bar based on mootness or preclusion: it neither ends this live controversy nor prevents litigation of the asserted statutory entitlement.

The Court applies the distinctions among a statutory right, a cause of action, and the relief available in that action without overruling the precedents addressed above. It announces no general bar to enforcement of state-plan provisions, constitutional §1983 claims, or ordinary relief in existing actions. Administrative enforcement remains operative. The Court establishes no proof burden or substantive child-welfare standard for the unresolved claim.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Stevens and, on §671(a)(15) only, Stone-Zsela, would apply *Wilder*'s protection of a binding individual entitlement to the mandatory, individualized reasonable-efforts obligation. The absence of the essential caseworker permits a judicial inquiry without prescribing every welfare decision; administrative funding withdrawal does not establish incompatibility with §1983. The supported three-day remedy is confined to this record. Blackmun and Stevens also would sustain the reporting claim under §1983, while Stone-Zsela leaves its enforceability and merits for provision-specific consideration. All three leave a redundant implied action under §671(a)(15) undecided. Stone-Zsela's separate writing preserves that narrower position on §671(a)(9) and distinguishes the existing §671(a)(15) injunction from possible additional relief on remand. These positions do not control the rejected reasonable-efforts and reporting claims.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment is reversed in part and vacated in part, and the case is remanded to the Seventh Circuit for proceedings consistent with these directions. The existing three-working-day assignment and reassignment injunction and ancillary weekly monitoring order are vacated. The judgment is vacated insofar as it otherwise resolves the independent §671(a)(16) claim. Further proceedings below must separately consider that claim's enforceability, merits, and any additional lawful relief; no entitlement or violation is found, and the existing injunction is not transferred automatically to that claim. No §671(a)(9) claim remains available on the rejected private-enforcement theories. The constitutional count remains outside the case, and the B.H. decree is not modified. The Court orders no damages, nationwide assignment deadline, or retained Supreme Court supervision. This Supreme Court merits review ends with the remand.

### TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources, No. 92-479

Merits disposition, 1993-06-25

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-31; decided 1993-06-25. On writ of certiorari to Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia,187 W.Va.457,419 S.E.2d870 (1992).

**Question:** Whether a $10 million punitive award accompanying $19,000 compensatory damages for slander of title violates due process because of its amount or the procedures supporting it.

**Material record:** Viewed favorably to the verdict, TXO knowingly used a worthless quitclaim claim to attack valid development rights and pressure a renegotiation of royalties. The compensatory award represented expenses actually caused; the potential royalty loss was much larger but its proper valuation and relationship to the jury’s award were contested. The trial materials included TXO’s wealth, similar conduct, and arguments concerning its out-of-state corporate identity. The charge addressed punishment, deterrence, wealth, and “additional compensation.” The state appellate court upheld the award under its punitive-damages law and its interpretation of Haslip.

**Relief sought:** TXO seeks reversal of the punitive award or a new punitive determination and constitutionally adequate review. Alliance and the other prevailing parties seek affirmance.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 5–4 in the judgment; the $10 million punitive award and $19,000 compensatory award remain undisturbed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The particular judicial review is constitutionally sufficient

**Controlling proposition:** The jury process followed by the trial judge’s hearing and review and the state supreme court’s review of this punitive award satisfy procedural due process on this record. The trial judge’s failure to provide a separate written explanation does not by itself invalidate the award; this decision supplies neither a universal procedural checklist nor a substantive permissible ratio.

**Authority:** Stevens’s plurality procedural discussion, expressly agreeing on this proposition with Scalia’s concurrence joined by Thomas, this proposition; Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). Stevens, Blackmun and Kennedy adopt the procedural discussion; Scalia and Thomas expressly concur that the instructed jury and actual judicial reasonableness review provided here satisfy due process. This common case-specific proposition has five votes; no formal whole-opinion join or Marks substantive rule is inferred.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds, at the level shared by five Justices, that the process actually afforded this award is sufficient. Haslip identifies meaningful judicial review as a safeguard against arbitrary punitive awards without prescribing its Alabama procedures as the only constitutional form. Here the trial judge heard the parties’ postverdict arguments and expressed agreement with the appraisal of deliberate misconduct. The state supreme court examined the punitive challenge, the relevant precedents and the conduct, threatened harm and deterrence considerations. Its colorful terminology does not eliminate that review. The opposing view fairly questions the sparse explanation and the risk that wealth displaced wrongdoing, but the absence of a written trial ruling alone does not establish a constitutional denial of process. This shared procedural conclusion does not settle the separate disagreement about substantive excessiveness, adopt the plurality’s potential-harm estimates, approve every instruction as a reusable model, or determine that any later award with a similar ratio is lawful.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Haslip,499 U.S.1: actual procedural safeguards applied; no mandatory reproduction of every Alabama feature.
- Garnes v. Fleming Landfill,186 W.Va.656,413 S.E.2d897: state review framework examined as part of this record, not transformed into a federal checklist.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 25, the judgment sustains this award by five votes. Five expressly support the adequacy of the particular process, but no majority adopts potential-harm arithmetic, Kennedy’s entire substantive test, Scalia’s rejection of substantive review, a ratio or an instruction-waiver rule. Haslip’s existing governing force remains; no later punitive-damages framework enters.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, would sustain the amount under Haslip’s substantive reasonableness inquiry. They regard the small realized loss as an incomplete measure of a deliberate scheme aimed at valuable royalties; threatened harm, repeated misconduct and financial resources support punishment. They adopt no mathematical ceiling. Kennedy joins their procedural discussion but rejects reliance on reconstructed potential-harm calculations not established as the jury’s actual foundation. His separate ground is that the whole trial record supports rational deterrence and retribution for deliberate malicious conduct rather than demonstrated bias. Scalia, joined by Thomas, rejects an unenumerated substantive right to a correct amount and finds the traditional jury process and judicial review sufficient. Their ground is not a logical subset of Stevens’s substantive review or Kennedy’s motive-focused inquiry; no unified substantive Marks rule follows. Stone dissents: the charge’s additional-compensation language, wealth and outsider appeals require meaningful examination of objective support and constraints on the amount. He accepts supported potential harm and general verdicts, supplies no cap, and would require another punitive determination only if adequate correction cannot occur on the existing record. O’Connor, joined by White throughout, finds both the amount and safeguards deficient; she would order a new punitive determination for the prejudice risk or at least constitutionally adequate review. Souter joins her portions addressing actual jury guidance, prejudice, deficient review and relief, not her whole comparative-proportionality discussion. ### Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp., No. 92-1123

Dismissal as improvidently granted, 1993-11-30

On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit, 971 F.2d 728 (1992). Argued October 12, 1993; decided November 30, 1993. The stated question concerns routine vacatur of district-court judgments at the parties' request after settlement during appeal. The antecedent dispute is whether Izumi can secure review without a properly presented challenge to denial of intervention. Izumi participated in the earlier patent trial but not the retrial that produced judgments for Windmere. Philips and Windmere settled for $57 million and jointly sought vacatur; their settlement was not conditioned on vacatur. Izumi, an indemnitor seeking to preserve possible issue-preclusion benefits in related litigation, sought intervention to oppose the motion. The Federal Circuit denied intervention and vacated the judgments. Its settlement-vacatur practice was general, not exceptionless. Izumi discussed intervention in the petition's body and added an express intervention question at the merits stage.

The Court dismisses the writ as improvidently granted, 7–2. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg support the per curiam Opinion of the Court and dismissal. They adopt its party-review and prudential question-presentation grounds, without reaching the settlement-vacatur merits. Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Justice Blackmun; they would reach intervention, reverse its denial and the routine vacatur disposition, and remand for an equitable assessment. The Federal Circuit's orders remain undisturbed, without a merits affirmance or reversal.

Under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1), an unsuccessful applicant for intervention may seek review of the denial, but cannot obtain review of the parties' underlying judgment on the strength of an unreviewed intervention denial or a financial interest alone. Because Izumi's denial of intervention remains undisturbed, Izumi lacks the party status necessary to press the settlement-vacatur question in this petition; the Court does not decide whether intervention should have been allowed.

Per curiam opinion of the Court supported by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg, seven Justices; Blackmun and Stevens dissent. This stated threshold ground supports the dismissal and carries procedural authority, without a settlement-vacatur holding.

The Court holds that an interest in the outcome is distinct from the status needed to seek review of the judgment between the parties. Section 1254(1) authorizes a party to petition for review of a case in a court of appeals. Automobile Workers v. Scofield preserves the important route for an unsuccessful intervention applicant: that applicant may petition to review the denial itself. The route does not make the applicant a party before the denial is overturned. Izumi participated in the earlier patent litigation, but it did not participate as a party in the retrial producing the judgments now challenged. Its financial support, indemnity exposure, and desire to preserve possible issue-preclusion benefits do not themselves replace successful intervention. Whether those interests justified intervention is a separate question; the Court does not affirm the denial on its merits. Because that denial remains undisturbed, Izumi cannot obtain review of the settling parties' vacatur through the question it presented. Article III interest, statutory party status, and the proper presentation of a question must remain distinct.

*Automobile Workers v. Scofield*, 382 U.S. 205 (1965): applies its route for an applicant denied intervention to seek review of that denial; no automatic party status or right to review the underlying judgment follows from applying to intervene.

Rule 14.1(a) creates a strong prudential presumption against deciding a question not stated in the certiorari petition or fairly included in a stated question, subject to exceptional circumstances; a separate intervention denial is not fairly included merely because it must be overturned before the Court can reach a settlement-vacatur question. Neither discussion in the petition's body and later merits briefing nor the antecedent relationship establishes exceptional urgency or economy on this record, so the Court declines review of intervention and dismisses the writ as improvidently granted, leaving vacatur undecided.

Per curiam opinion of the Court supported by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg, seven Justices; Blackmun and Stevens dissent. The reasoned dismissal establishes this question-presentation ruling, not the merits of the judgment below.

The Court holds that a separate adverse order does not become a subsidiary question merely because overturning it would permit consideration of the stated question. Yee v. City of Escondido supplies the distinction between an argument supporting a preserved claim and a related but independent question outside the grant. Whether Izumi should be allowed to intervene depends on its litigation relationship and the circumstances of its application; whether settlement warrants vacatur concerns the treatment of judgments at the settling parties' request. Discussion of intervention in the petition's body and merits briefing supplied notice, but did not place that distinct question among those presented or fairly included. The strongest contrary argument is that intervention is indispensable and has been fully argued. That connection does not establish exceptional urgency or economy sufficient to overcome the presentation rule in this fact-dependent dispute. The rule is prudential, so the Court does not deny its power to make an exception; its obligation to notice jurisdictional defects is separate. Declining the intervention question leaves no route to the vacatur merits and requires dismissal of this writ.

*Yee v. City of Escondido*, 503 U.S. 519 (1992), question-presented portion: applies the distinction between related independent questions and subsidiary questions, with the narrow prudential exception retained; it does not convert the limit into a jurisdictional prohibition. *Automobile Workers v. Scofield*, 382 U.S. 205 (1965): its review route remains available, but does not dispense with presenting the intervention question.

Scofield's review route is applied without making unsuccessful intervention applicants automatic parties to the underlying case. Yee's distinction between related independent questions and subsidiary questions is applied, retaining its prudential exception. Munsingwear, Karcher, and Cardinal Chemical receive no new controlling treatment on settlement vacatur; their possible application appears only in the dissent. Leaving the judgment below undisturbed does not endorse its vacatur policy.

Effective November 30, 1993, the reasoned threshold opinion establishes the two procedural propositions stated above. The statutory party requirement and prudential question-presentation rule remain distinct; the latter does not eliminate the Court's obligation to notice jurisdictional defects. The dismissal establishes no rule approving or forbidding routine settlement vacatur, no intervention entitlement on these facts, and no collateral-estoppel result in other litigation. Section 2106's equitable authority and existing vacatur precedents are left without a new merits construction.

Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun. They would exercise the prudential exception to reach intervention, reverse the denial and the routine settlement-vacatur disposition, and remand for an actual equitable assessment. The intervention argument was disclosed in the petition and addressed adversarially; in their view, dismissal after plenary review gives the omitted question heading more weight than the notice and economy purposes of Rule 14.1(a) warrant. *Yee* preserves exceptional consideration, while *Scofield* supplies the route to review the denial. Izumi's indemnity exposure and the settling parties' newly adverse request support the limited right to intervene to oppose erasure. The fact that an applicant was not already a party does not itself answer whether it should be allowed to become one.

On vacatur, the dissent reads §2106 as requiring an actual equitable judgment that accounts for public and third-party interests. *Munsingwear* addresses loss of appellate review through mootness, while *Karcher* distinguishes voluntary abandonment; voluntary settlement alone does not automatically justify erasing a judgment. *Cardinal Chemical* supplies a limited analogy against an automatic practice disguised as discretion, rather than a decision about settlement itself. Judgments represent public work and can affect persons outside the bargain. The dissent would require those interests to be assessed before vacatur, while preserving lawful case-specific relief. It does not declare settlement vacatur categorically unavailable, give every nonparty an intervention right, or command permanent preservation of these judgments. Its proposed intervention and vacatur rules receive two votes and do not govern the Court's disposition.

**Source Notes:** 28 U.S.C. §§1254(1), 2106; Supreme Court Rule 14.1(a); Scofield, 382 U.S. 205; Yee, 503 U.S. 519. [U.S. Philips v. Windmere, 971 F.2d 728](https://static.case.law/f2d/971/cases/0728-01.json), records the denial of intervention and vacatur. The [official report, 510 U.S. 27](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510027/usrep510027.pdf), pages 28–34, reproduces the settlement, question-presentation history, statutory review provision, and applicable Rule. The complete petition is not reproduced in that report; the question and added question are supported by its stated excerpts.

The writ is dismissed as improvidently granted. The Federal Circuit's denial of intervention and its vacatur orders remain undisturbed, including the underlying direction to dismiss the settled case with prejudice. This Court issues no new remand, does not reinstate a district-court judgment, and does not decide collateral estoppel in the related litigation. Proceedings on this writ are concluded subject to ordinarily available post-decision procedures.

### Central Bank of Denver, N.A. v. First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A., No. 92-854

Merits decision, 1994-04-19

#### Chronology and Posture

511 U.S. 164 (1994). Argued November 30, 1993; decided April 19, 1994. On certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 969 F.2d 891 (1992), which reversed summary judgment for Central Bank and reinstated the purchasers’ secondary claim against the indenture trustee. Review concerns the existence of the private aiding-and-abetting action and its scienter and substantial-assistance requirements. The Court reaches those questions for affirmative assistance.

The claim concerns Central Bank’s agreement to postpone independent appraisal review until after a bond offering, following outside warnings and internal concerns about the collateral appraisal. Contrary explanations and evidence remain disputed. The lower court found no independent disclosure duty to these purchasers; the surviving bank theory rests on affirmative assistance, not the abandoned silence-only claim. Pring’s separate control-person and secondary claims are outside this review.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision. Nine participated; no recusal or nonparticipation is recorded.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Tenth Circuit’s reinstatement of the private aiding-and-abetting claim against Central Bank and remands, 5–4. Justice Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela also concurs separately. Justice Kennedy dissents, joined by O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pleaded private aiding-and-abetting claim against Central Bank | Affirmed and remanded, 5–4 | Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Affirm reversal of summary judgment and permit further proceedings under the confined affirmative-assistance rule; no liability or damages award. |

The four dissenters would reverse reinstatement and preserve summary judgment on the ground that no private secondary action exists. The Court requires proof of the underlying fraud, culpable substantial assistance, legally sufficient reliance, causation and recoverable loss. Purchasers need not recast the pleaded claim as an unpleaded primary-misrepresentation claim. No other defendant’s independent claim is adjudicated.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relation to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone, Blackmun, Souter, Ginsburg | Affirmance and remand | Entire opinion: received action, confined knowledge/severe-recklessness and affirmative-assistance rule, record application, proof requirements, limits and remedy. |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court’s opinion and judgment in full | Additional omission rules and detailed assistance, attribution and proof qualifications; these additional propositions do not control. |
| Dissent | Kennedy | O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Would reverse reinstatement | Statutory ground rejecting the secondary action; does not reach the contested scienter or assistance questions. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The received private action includes culpable substantial assistance

**Controlling proposition:** The established private action under §10(b) and Rule 10b-5 permits recovery from a person who knowingly or with severe recklessness substantially assists an underlying securities fraud through affirmative conduct, subject to proof of that fraud, the defendant’s qualifying participation, legally sufficient reliance, causation and recoverable loss. This is accessory responsibility within the received investor action, not a finding that the assistant made a primary misrepresentation or a rule imposing liability through professional status or control alone.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the existence of the confined action and its received-remedy rationale. No fractured-decision synthesis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the established investor remedy includes responsibility for culpable substantial assistance to a statutory fraud. Bankers Life recognizes the private action; Musick, Peeler permits bounded development of its incidents, although contribution assumes liability and does not itself establish accessory responsibility. The distinct justification here is the assistant’s own culpable participation in a proved securities fraud, understood against the substantial lower-court development of the received remedy. Curran gives that legal setting interpretive weight without turning congressional silence into an enactment. The reference to indirect conduct accommodates mediated participation but cannot alone establish unlimited liability. The contrary argument from statutory silence and express control-person liability has force. Control under §20(a) differs from culpable assistance; relabeling control cannot bypass the defense for a controlling person who acted in good faith and did not directly or indirectly induce the acts constituting the violation. Santa Fe’s requirement of manipulation or deception remains indispensable in the underlying violation. Central Bank therefore cannot defeat the pleaded claim solely because it is secondary. The Court neither creates a general professional-care action nor derives a civil action from criminal aiding-and-abetting law, and it reserves other statutory and enforcement settings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Superintendent of Insurance v. Bankers Life & Casualty Co., 404 U.S. 6 — applied as recognition of the received private action; it did not itself decide accessory liability.
- Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance of Wausau, 508 U.S. 286 — applied for bounded development of the received action and distinguished as to its actual contribution holding; shared liability must still be established before allocation.
- Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Curran, 456 U.S. 353 — used by analogy for the significance of an established judicial remedy; its different statutory setting does not establish congressional enactment of this action.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green, 430 U.S. 462 — applied: the underlying wrong must involve the required manipulation or deception, not unfairness or fiduciary misconduct alone.
- Pinter v. Dahl, 486 U.S. 622 — distinguished: its construction of express seller liability under §12 does not settle this received §10(b) action; its own statutory holding remains intact.
- Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185 — its reservation of aiding and abetting is resolved here for the confined private affirmative-assistance action; Hochfelder itself did not decide that question.
- Herman & MacLean v. Huddleston, 459 U.S. 375 — its reservation of aiding and abetting is resolved here for the confined private affirmative-assistance action; Huddleston itself did not decide that question.

##### Knowledge or severe recklessness in affirmative substantial assistance

**Controlling proposition:** For affirmative substantial assistance, the defendant must know or severely recklessly disregard both the fraudulent character of the activity and the assisting role of its conduct; severe recklessness means an extreme departure from ordinary care presenting a specific danger of deception known to the defendant or so obvious that the defendant must have appreciated it. No independent disclosure duty is required for that affirmative participation, but negligence, valuation concerns alone, professional status, ordinary services without qualifying culpability and assistance, or mere silence without a relevant duty do not suffice.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; all five adopt this confined mental-state and affirmative-assistance rule, not a universal recklessness rule for every securities claim.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court requires awareness of fraud and of the defendant’s substantial assisting role, not merely awareness that an investment might fail. Hochfelder excludes negligence but leaves recklessness open; the Court resolves that question only for the affirmative assistance presented here. The Tenth Circuit’s Pring analysis distinguishes a communicated agreement facilitating an offering from a passive failure to warn. Its demanding recklessness formulation preserves that distinction. Requiring a fiduciary duty even for affirmative assistance would immunize culpable participation simply because the participant had no separate duty to volunteer information. Conversely, calling an omission an affirmative decision cannot evade the limits of today’s holding. The inquiry concerns what the bank actually arranged and how it advanced the alleged fraud. Ordinary banking activity alone proves neither assistance nor culpability; an appraisal dispute alone proves neither deception nor awareness of it. Santa Fe continues to require an underlying deceptive or manipulative wrong. The Court adopts no general investigation duty and does not decide the full rules governing duty-based omissions or consciously intended assistance through duty-free inaction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185 — its exclusion of negligence remains controlling; its recklessness reservation is resolved only for this private affirmative-assistance setting.
- Santa Fe Industries, Inc. v. Green, 430 U.S. 462 — its deception-or-manipulation requirement prevents ordinary investment risk and professional error from becoming securities fraud.
- First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. v. Pring, 969 F.2d 891 — its confined affirmative-assistance and extreme-recklessness analysis is adopted for the bank claim; inconsistent knowledge-only or categorical duty requirements for such affirmative assistance cannot govern after this decision. Its separate rulings concerning Pring are outside this review.

##### Triable issues require further proceedings, not a liability award

**Controlling proposition:** Viewed favorably to the purchasers at summary judgment, the appraisal warnings, internal concerns and agreement postponing an already demanded independent review until after the offering permit a genuine dispute over severe recklessness and substantial affirmative assistance; the Tenth Circuit’s reinstatement of the pleaded bank claim is affirmed and the case remanded. The purchasers must still prove the underlying violation, the bank’s qualifying assistance and culpability, legally sufficient reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss under the ordinary civil burden; neither the default nor the bonds’ face value establishes fraud damages.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Souter and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the record application, preserved proof requirements and limited affirmance and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court permits adjudication of the secondary claim on its own elements. Pring identifies evidence that the bank first demanded independent review, then agreed to postpone it despite specific warnings, while the offering used the challenged collateral appraisal. A factfinder could find culpable assistance; the contrary explanations, certifications and collateral evidence remain available to the bank. Huddleston supplies the ordinary preponderance burden at trial, not a presumption of liability from the existence of a factual dispute. Basic preserves the need for legally sufficient reliance. Retaining accessory responsibility attributes the consequences of a proved primary fraud to its culpable substantial assistant; investors need not discover and personally rely on concealed assistance. They must nevertheless establish reliance on the underlying fraud under the governing rules, and no reliance presumption is established on this record. Causation and compensable loss remain independent requirements. The Court does not decide whether the bank was actually culpable or whether any particular loss resulted from fraud. Musick, Peeler allows contribution only after shared liability is proved, and supplies neither an automatic indemnity nor a substitute for these elements.

**Precedent treatment:**

- First Interstate Bank of Denver, N.A. v. Pring, 969 F.2d 891 — affirmed insofar as it reverses summary judgment on the purchasers’ affirmative-assistance claim against Central Bank; triable issues are not findings of fraud or damages.
- Herman & MacLean v. Huddleston, 459 U.S. 375 — the ordinary preponderance standard is preserved; neither institutional status nor information held by the defendant shifts that burden.
- Basic Inc. v. Levinson, 485 U.S. 224 — its reliance requirement is preserved. Today’s decision establishes no presumption for these municipal bonds and, as part of the retained accessory action, requires reliance on the underlying fraud rather than on the assistant’s concealed participation.
- Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance of Wausau, 508 U.S. 286 — its shared-liability prerequisite and contribution limits remain effective; this remand establishes no entitlement or allocation.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The Court resolves the private secondary-liability question reserved in Hochfelder and Huddleston and, for affirmative substantial assistance, the reserved recklessness question. It preserves Hochfelder’s exclusion of negligence, Huddleston’s ordinary civil burden, Santa Fe’s underlying deception requirement and Basic’s reliance requirement. Bankers Life supplies the received private action; Curran supports attention to its established development. Musick, Peeler’s contribution rule remains conditioned on shared underlying liability and does not itself establish this action. Pinter’s express §12 rule remains intact. No Supreme Court holding is overruled. Conflicting lower-court rules requiring actual knowledge or an independent disclosure duty for all affirmative assistance cannot displace today’s confined standard. Mertens’s remedy-category holding and reservation of the antecedent cause question remain unchanged and do not decide this securities action.

Effective April 19, 1994, the private §10(b)/Rule 10b-5 action includes knowing or severely reckless substantial affirmative assistance to an underlying securities fraud. Severe recklessness concerns a specific danger of deception and the defendant’s assisting role, not ordinary negligence or investment risk. A separate duty to disclose is unnecessary for affirmative assistance; merely renaming a failure to act does not make it affirmative. Ordinary services without the required culpability and assisting effect, and mere duty-free silence, remain insufficient.

Reliance on the underlying fraud, causation and recoverable loss remain necessary. Concealed assistance need not itself have been known to or relied upon by investors; no presumption of reliance is established for these bonds. The Court leaves open the full rules for duty-based omissions and consciously intended assistance through duty-free inaction; Stone’s additional rules on those questions are noncontrolling. It adopts no universal scienter standard for other securities settings, general investigation duty, automatic liability from default, primary claim against this bank or comprehensive SEC-enforcement rule. Independent statutory claims and defenses, final judgments and Musick, Peeler’s separate shared-liability requirements remain undisturbed.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs, joining the Opinion of the Court in full. His additional account of omissions and proof does not enlarge the Court’s affirmative-assistance holding. For reckless failure to act, he would require an independently established duty covering the omitted protective step, with its source, beneficiaries, trigger and scope identified. Neither the title indenture trustee nor the description gatekeeper creates a general investigation obligation. Without a relevant duty, he would permit liability for inaction only upon proof of conscious intent to assist a known fraud and substantial assistance in fact; knowledge plus a failure to volunteer a warning is insufficient. Woodward and Woods supply circuit distinctions between culpable assistance, ordinary commercial participation and omissions. These omission rules are his separate position; the Court leaves their full formulation open.

Stone requires the court to identify what the defendant supplied, arranged, authorized or otherwise did, and how it meaningfully advanced the particular fraud. Nature, amount, timing, duration, relationship and practical effect may inform that inquiry, but no factor list replaces proof of the actual contribution. Assistance need not be indispensable. A routine service knowingly used to facilitate a particular fraud may qualify; commercial novelty, fees and institutional size do not establish the elements. An internal decision to defer work remains an omission unless an additional arrangement actually coordinates or enables the fraud. A communicated postponement agreement can perform that affirmative function. If the evidence establishes only nonperformance, his separate omission rules would govern, without reviving an abandoned claim through a change of label.

He treats traditional accessory principles as an analogy, not an independent source of federal liability. Halberstam illustrates responsibility for knowing substantial assistance without a conspiratorial agreement, but is a common-law decision, not a §10(b) holding. In his formulation the plaintiff need not prove a separate judgment against the primary wrongdoer, a shared purpose to secure the fraud’s success or a personal share of its proceeds. Proof of the underlying violation remains indispensable. He accepts severe recklessness for affirmative assistance despite litigation costs because particularized proof of awareness and contribution separates serious participation from professional error.

Stone emphasizes three causal links: assistance to fraud, fraud to the investment decision, and actionable fraud to recoverable loss. Basic’s reliance requirement cannot be satisfied by automatically assuming an efficient market for municipal bonds; any presumption needs its own foundation and remains subject to rebuttal. Loss from concealed collateral inadequacy must be separated from loss caused independently by market conditions or unrelated business failure. Musick, Peeler permits allocation of established common liability, not duplicate recovery or automatic indemnity. Serious appraisal concerns without the required awareness of deception defeat culpability; loss arising independently of fraud defeats recovery for that loss.

The bank may offer a credible valuation basis, a justified timetable inconsistent with culpability or an absence of substantial assisting effect. A genuine independent assessment may bear on awareness; nominal reliance on an assessment known to be unreliable supplies no automatic defense. Knowledge must be attributed under applicable agency rules rather than assembled from innocent fragments held by unrelated employees. Further proceedings should identify which warnings reached responsible officials before closing, whether they indicated deception, what officials understood about the agreement’s assisting role, and whether it advanced the fraud and caused a compensable loss. Huddleston’s ordinary civil burden applies to individual and institutional investors alike. Circumstantial evidence may suffice; neither an admission nor a special burden for bank defendants is required, and the bank’s possession of information does not shift the purchasers’ burden. No unpleaded primary claim, ultimate liability, damages or general enforcement rule is decided by his concurrence.

Justice Kennedy dissents, joined by O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas. They would reverse reinstatement of the secondary claim because §10(b) prohibits the defendant’s own manipulative or deceptive conduct, rather than assistance that does not itself satisfy that statutory requirement. Hochfelder supplies attention to statutory language and the negligence limit; Santa Fe requires manipulation or deception; Pinter illustrates adherence to the persons and conduct specified in express securities remedies. In their view, the reference to indirect conduct cannot substitute for that predicate, and Congress’s express choices concerning control-person and other secondary responsibility weigh against a judicial addition.

The dissent distinguishes Musick, Peeler’s allocation among persons already liable from a rule making an additional participant liable in the first place. Longstanding circuit practice, investor protection and common-law analogies do not overcome that difference, and criminal aiding-and-abetting law supplies no general private civil action. The dissent would leave summary judgment for Central Bank intact on the pleaded theory without reaching recklessness or substantial assistance. It gives banks and other professionals no immunity when their own conduct satisfies an available primary claim and does not decide an unpleaded primary case or the full range of SEC enforcement authority.

#### Source Notes

The case returns through the Tenth Circuit for further proceedings on the reinstated private affirmative-assistance claim consistent with the Court’s standard. ### O’Melveny & Myers v. FDIC, No. 93-489

Merits decision, June 13, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

512 U.S. 79. Argued March 21, 1994; decided June 13, 1994. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 969 F.2d 744, which reversed an unexplained summary judgment for the law firm and used federal common law to reject defenses to the receiver's acquired California claims. The question is whether federal or California law governs imputation and related defenses, not whether malpractice or damages have been proved.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Ninth Circuit's federal-common-law judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded, 9–0.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Source of law governing imputation and related defenses to acquired California claims | Reverse and remand, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | No opposing Justice | Determine preserved California questions without a federal anti-imputation exception; no judgment on liability or damages. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and II | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Reverse and remand | State-law and independently sufficient pre-FIRREA ground; conditional FIRREA construction; temporal and merits reservations |
| Concurrence | Stevens | Blackmun, O'Connor, Souter | Joins the Court and judgment | Explains the distinct authority of state courts to develop state common law without dictating California's answer |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### These acquired California claims carry California imputation law

**Controlling proposition:** On the pre-FIRREA framework, California law governs imputation and related defenses to the institution's California negligence and fiduciary-duty claims acquired by the federal receiver: fund protection, greater recovery and general administrative uniformity do not establish the particular significant conflict with federal policy required for the proposed federal exception. The Ninth Circuit's federal-common-law ruling is reversed and the case remanded to determine the preserved state-law questions, without directing judgment for either party.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. All nine adopt the same pre-FIRREA ground, independently sufficient to reject the proposed rule without deciding FIRREA's temporal reach.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that acquiring a state-created claim does not ordinarily enlarge it by federal judicial decree. Erie denies general federal common law; Wallis requires a particular significant conflict before a special federal rule displaces state law. Boyle preserves exceptional displacement when its federal-interest and conflict requirements are met, rather than making federal involvement enough. Kimbell Foods shows that even rights generated by federal programs do not invariably require nationally uniform rules. Here the receiver sues on the institution's professional-negligence and fiduciary-duty rights. The strongest contrary argument is that imputation reduces recoveries for innocent creditors and the federal insurance fund after the dishonest officers lose control. That policy supplies no limiting principle identifying the required conflict: more recovery would benefit a federal claimant in virtually any lawsuit. United States v. Texas preserved an established federal debt-interest rule in a different setting. California's own imputation, adverse-interest and receiver doctrines must be considered without assuming their answer. The Court neither decides liability nor abolishes established categories of federal common law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Erie Railroad v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64: applies the absence of general federal common law to privately generated state causes; exceptional federal fields are not abolished.
- Wallis v. Pan American Petroleum Corp., 384 U.S. 63: applies its requirement of an identified significant conflict with federal policy; ordinary federal financial interests are insufficient here.
- Boyle v. United Technologies Corp., 487 U.S. 500: preserves federal-interest and conflict prerequisites for exceptional displacement; the necessary conflict is absent in these acquired claims.
- United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc., 440 U.S. 715: distinguishes federally generated program rights and preserves its rejection of automatic nationwide uniformity even there.
- United States v. Texas, April 5, 1993: distinguishes the established federal delay-compensation obligation on an assumed federal debt; it supplies no new receiver exemption from state tort defenses.
- FDIC v. Meyer, February 23, 1994: preserves the separation of sovereign consent, federal-party status and substantive cause of action; this action remains state created.

##### FIRREA, if applicable, supplies no additional anti-imputation rule

**Controlling proposition:** If FIRREA governs these claims, §1821(d)(2)(A)(i) transfers the institution's rights, titles, powers and privileges to the FDIC subject to the legal incidents of those rights and the federal exceptions Congress actually enacted; it does not authorize adding the proposed federal exemption from California imputation law. The Court leaves FIRREA's temporal reach unresolved because the independently sufficient pre-FIRREA analysis produces the same state-law remand.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. All nine adopt this conditional statutory construction and its temporal reservation. It is not a finding that FIRREA applies retrospectively.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that FIRREA does not authorize the additional rule the receiver requests. Its succession provision transfers the institution's rights; it does not recast every state claim as an enlarged federal one. Congress separately addressed limitations in §1821(d)(14), contract repudiation and its consequences in §1821(e)(1) and (3), officer and director liability in §1821(k), and certain agreements in §1821(d)(9). Those provisions address different subjects and cannot be combined into a residual power to disregard ordinary imputation. The FDIC argues that protecting the insurance fund warrants filling the statutory silence. In this setting silence leaves the acquired rights and enacted exceptions in place; it does not delegate creation of any additional advantage that might increase recovery. This conclusion does not determine the application of any listed exception to a properly presented different claim. Landgraf requires a separate temporal inquiry when application of later legislation matters. Because the pre-FIRREA ground already resolves the proposed federal rule, no such determination is necessary here. California's substantive answer and the firm's professional liability remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Erie Railroad v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64: preserves state substantive law where no enacted or otherwise valid federal displacement is established.
- Boyle v. United Technologies Corp., 487 U.S. 500: remains available within its actual exceptional-federal-law requirements; this construction does not occupy every federal common-law field.
- Landgraf v. USI Film Products, April 26, 1994: preserves its temporal-direction and new-consequences framework; FIRREA's temporal application is unnecessary to decide and is not presumed.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide California imputation, adverse-interest exceptions, estoppel, engagement scope, duty, breach, causation, recoverable client losses, assigned investor claims, a statute-of-frauds defense, or FIRREA's temporal reach. Reversal does not reinstate the unexplained district-court summary judgment. The complaint does not seek reimbursement of the investors' rescission payments.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

United States v. Texas retains its established federal debt-interest rule, and Boyle retains its exceptional displacement inquiry. Meyer does not supply a federal malpractice cause. Landgraf's temporal rule is left intact because the same outcome follows without resolving FIRREA's temporal application. No precedent is overruled.

Effective June 13, 1994, federal receivership, a desire for greater recovery and general uniformity do not create a federal exception to California imputation and related defenses in these acquired California claims. The independent pre-FIRREA rule and conditional FIRREA construction both require the same state-law inquiry. Enacted federal exceptions and established federal common-law fields keep their own requirements. California may supply receiver-protective rules; the Court decides neither their existence nor their application here. This is not a rule that every FDIC action necessarily uses state law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs, joined by Blackmun, O'Connor and Souter, while all four join the entire Court opinion. Federal judicial restraint does not require California's common law to remain fixed or dictate imputation against the receiver. Erie distinguishes a federal court's creation of a new national rule from legitimate state common-law development. State courts may adapt their own rules to the displacement of fraudulent insiders and protection of innocent creditors within their lawmaking authority; a federal court adjudicating these claims must ascertain California law rather than invent a federal substitute. The availability and content of any California receiver-protective rule remain unresolved. This explanation does not establish a new federal exception, direct California to excuse imputation, or find malpractice proved. Its state-law-development emphasis is a noncontrolling concurrence, not an alternative Court holding.

Full engagement proof and all underlying loss records were not independently recovered. The case returns for consideration of California imputation, estoppel, professional duty and other preserved state-law questions. The unexplained original summary judgment is not automatically reinstated. ### Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp., No. 93-1525

Merits decision, February 21, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 7, 1994; decided February 21, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, reviewing 12 F.3d 388 (1993), which reversed the district judgment, 811 F. Supp. 993, on the absence of governmental action and directed dismissal. The question is whether Amtrak is a governmental actor for First Amendment purposes despite its statutory disclaimer. Michael Lebron challenged Amtrak's refusal to display his political photomontage on the Spectacular billboard at Penn Station. The First Amendment claim remained before the lower courts although he expressly disavowed treating Amtrak itself as a governmental entity and relied instead on attributing the challenged conduct to government.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice is noted as not participating.

#### Judgment

The Second Circuit judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 8–1, for adjudication of the First Amendment claim treating Amtrak as governmental. The Court does not decide whether rejection of the advertisement violated the First Amendment or order its display.

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The Court may consider this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal

**Controlling proposition:** The Court elects to consider Amtrak's governmental-entity status despite Lebron's earlier express disavowal of that argument because it supplies a legal basis for the same preserved First Amendment claim, falls within the governmental-actor question presented, and can be resolved on the established institutional record without a new factual theory or additional findings. This bounded exercise of review creates no obligation to consider every abandoned argument and does not dispense with limits on distinct claims, questions, requested relief, factual stipulations, or missing material facts.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's reachability portion, joined by Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Seven Justices adopt the stated discretionary-review rationale; Chief Justice Stone-Zsela reaches the question but does not join this portion, and Justice O'Connor declines the argument as a prudential matter.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the earlier litigation choice deserves consideration but does not require refusal of this legal argument. Yee separates a new supporting argument from a distinct claim and independently enforces the question presented. Lebron's injury, constitutional claim, rejected advertisement, and requested relief remain the same. The question before the Court expressly concerns governmental status despite the statutory disclaimer, and the established corporate structure permits its resolution without new factual findings. Bray withheld a separate statutory claim outside the granted dispute and lacking a necessary finding; those defects are absent here. Deliberate disavowal is more substantial than mere omission, but the Court exercises its authority to decide this included legal basis on the present record. The appellate reliance on Amtrak's private status makes that premise relevant without establishing a rule that any passing discussion preserves every argument. No actual adversarial prejudice or broader concession is found, and the Court imposes no burden on the respondent to prove prejudice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519 (1992): applies its distinction between a supporting argument and a new claim, together with its independent question-presented limit; the present decision separately addresses deliberate disavowal in this setting.
- Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic, 506 U.S. 263 (1993): distinguishes the unadjudicated separate hindrance theory, outside the granted questions and lacking a necessary finding; its scope limits remain operative.
- Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Crenshaw, 486 U.S. 71 (1988): applies, through Yee, the allowance of supporting arguments for a preserved federal claim, without erasing this Court's discretion over deliberately excluded grounds.
- Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services, Inc., 500 U.S. 90 (1991): preserves the distinction between legal arguments and the claim to which they relate; no unrelated claim is introduced.
- United States v. Mezzanatto, decided January 18, 1995: distinguishes the actual, limited evidentiary agreement there from this decision whether to consider an included argument; its waiver rule does not make every litigation choice an irrevocable surrender of an entire constitutional claim.

##### Special federal creation and permanent majority appointment make Amtrak governmental

**Controlling proposition:** When the Federal Government creates a corporation by special law to advance governmental objectives and permanently retains authority to appoint a majority of its governing directors, the corporation is governmental for First Amendment purposes notwithstanding a statutory declaration that it is not a governmental agency. Amtrak satisfies those combined conditions; ordinary operating discretion in its corporate officers does not require a separate external federal command for each challenged act.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's constitutional-status portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight Justices directly support the same permanent-majority-appointment rule and its application; Justice O'Connor's conditional view of a question she declines to reach is not a join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress cannot settle constitutional responsibility simply by choosing a corporate label. Amtrak was specially created to pursue national transportation objectives, and the statutory arrangement permanently retains governmental authority over selection of its governing majority. The Secretary's position, the five other direct Presidential selections, and the preferred shareholder's governmental selections must not be mistaken for nine direct Presidential appointments. The different routes nevertheless establish the required enduring majority authority; the corporate president's selection within the institution does not defeat it. Congress's nonagency classification keeps its statutory operation, but cannot place an institution created and governed in this manner beyond the Constitution. San Francisco Arts & Athletics distinguishes a federally chartered and assisted private corporation; charter, subsidy, or regulation alone is not the ground here. Nor does delegation of everyday decisions to corporate officers make this institution private. An external federal order to reject Lebron's work is therefore unnecessary. The Court decides governmental status, leaving the advertising policy's constitutionality and the suitable remedy for later adjudication.

**Precedent treatment:**

- San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Inc. v. United States Olympic Committee, 483 U.S. 522 (1987): distinguishes federal charter and assistance to a private institution from special creation coupled with permanent governmental majority-appointment authority.
- Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co., 500 U.S. 614 (1991), and Georgia v. McCollum, 505 U.S. 42 (1992): preserve attention to actual governmental legal machinery in their jury-selection settings; neither makes billboard advertising an exclusively governmental function or supplies a general rule for all publicly benefited corporations.
- Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 419 U.S. 345 (1974), and Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991 (1982): distinguish attribution of a private entity's particular act from identifying an institution that is itself governmental under the combined rule.
- Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp., decided November 14, 1994: keeps the distinct inquiry into an entity's state sovereign immunity separate; absence of immunity does not imply constitutional privacy.
- American National Red Cross v. S. G., 505 U.S. 247 (1992): preserves its express charter-jurisdiction rule, which supplies no constitutional actor classification.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Federal charter, subsidy, regulation, governmental property, public benefit, a single appointment, temporary investment, or merely the current ownership of shares is not independently sufficient under this rule. Permanent authority to appoint the governing majority, governmental objectives, and special governmental creation must be present together. The Court decides no universal agency status, sovereign immunity, Treasury obligation, federal tort-liability rule, or charter jurisdiction. The 1994 recodification from former 45 U.S.C. §541 to 49 U.S.C. §24301(a) does not itself adjudicate constitutional status. The statutory board includes the Secretary of Transportation; Amtrak's president; three Presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed directors representing the statutory labor, state-executive, and business categories; two Presidential selections from commuter-authority nominees under the statute; and two preferred-shareholder selections. The governmental preferred shareholder supplies those last two selections on this record. Neither the entire board's consideration of this particular refusal nor a new direct federal order to reject it is found.

##### Governmental status requires a remand, not an immediate display order

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the Second Circuit's governmental-action ground restores the First Amendment claim for adjudication with Amtrak treated as governmental, but does not establish that this rejection was unconstitutional or that Lebron is entitled to mandatory display. The lower courts must address the unreviewed speech and forum questions, lawful relief, and the related disposition of the contract claim and TDI counterclaim under their own governing rules; the district display injunction is not automatically reinstated.

**Authority:** Justice Scalia's remand portion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Eight Justices adopt the same threshold reversal and limited remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that governmental responsibility identifies the institution's constitutional duties without deciding whether the challenged restriction violates them. The Court of Appeals stopped at attribution and did not adjudicate the First Amendment merits. The district court's contrary decision and mandatory-display order therefore cannot be restored merely by correcting that threshold. The character of the advertising forum, the grounds and operation of the speech restriction, and the conditions for equitable relief remain separate questions. Young preserves the distinction between the existence of substantial federal jurisdiction and the legal defeat of a federal claim; restoration of the claim accordingly requires reconsideration of associated state-law dispositions without prescribing their answers. Tome illustrates only the limited principle that correcting one ground does not automatically establish ultimate relief; its hearsay and criminal-error rules do not govern this civil remand. No damages amount, contract entitlement, forum classification, or validity of a substitute advertising policy is determined here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Young v. Northern Illinois Conference of United Methodist Church, decided January 17, 1995: applies the distinction between statutory jurisdiction and legal defeat on the federal merits without extending its ministerial-selection defense.
- Tome v. United States, decided January 10, 1995: uses only its separation of threshold legal error from automatic ultimate relief by analogy; no evidentiary or criminal harmless-error standard is transferred.

**Remedial limits:** The Court awards no display, damages, final contractual relief, or adjudication of TDI's requested declaration permitting termination. The Second Circuit's dismissal directions must be reconsidered consistently with the restored federal claim and ordinary law governing the separate state-law issues. The lower courts may not treat this actor-status ruling as a merits judgment approving the advertisement or invalidating every political-advertising policy.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Yee and Bray preserve the distinction between a legal supporting argument, a separate claim, and the actual question presented; the Court exercises bounded discretion over this deliberately disavowed argument. San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Jackson, and Blum retain their private-actor scope. Edmonson and McCollum concern governmental jury machinery. Hess addresses immunity and Red Cross charter jurisdiction, neither of which settles constitutional actor status. Young and Tome support only the stated separation of thresholds, merits, and relief. No earlier holding is overruled.

Effective February 21, 1995, special federal creation for governmental objectives combined with permanent federal authority to appoint a governing majority establishes First Amendment governmental status despite a statutory nonagency label. That rule governs Amtrak on this record without requiring an external governmental command for the particular refusal. The decision also permits consideration of this included legal argument despite its earlier disavowal on the stated claim, question, and record conditions, without a universal rule requiring review of abandoned arguments. The advertising merits and final remedies remain unresolved.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O'Connor would affirm. Lebron preserved his First Amendment claim, but deliberately excluded the theory that Amtrak itself was a governmental entity in his presentations below. The express governmental-actor question here permits consideration of that theory; it does not require the Court to disregard the litigant's earlier choice. Her objection is prudential. She does not claim that the Court lacks jurisdiction, that the entire constitutional claim was waived, or that entity status falls outside the question presented. Nor does she find an additional concession or actual reliance prejudice on a record that establishes neither.

She would decide the conduct-attribution theory on the private-entity premise on which it was litigated. Jackson and Blum require a supported governmental connection to a private actor's particular choice, while her Edmonson dissent distinguishes such choice from governmental assistance. The reported funding, property, stockholding, and board relationships do not establish an external governmental direction causing this refusal or an exclusively governmental advertising function. The political subject of the artwork does not itself supply the missing connection. On that limited path she would leave the appellate rejection of the federal claim and disposition of the unresolved state-law matters in place. She does not convert the failure of the federal theory into a universal jurisdictional dismissal rule.

O'Connor recognizes that the permanent public governance would support the Court's structural conclusion if that alternative were reached. That conditional agreement supplies no vote for a holding she declines to adjudicate and no judgment vote for reversal. Even an affirmative actor ruling, she explains, would not itself establish the right to the district court's mandatory-display injunction; forum, speech, and equitable questions would still require decision.

### BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, No. 94-896

Merits decision, May 20, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 11, 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Supreme Court of Alabama, 646 So. 2d 619. The questions are whether Alabama provided a meaningful remedy after identifying improper reliance on out-of-state transactions and whether the remitted $2 million punitive award is grossly excessive under the Fourteenth Amendment. The latter question includes, but is not confined to, the 500-to-1 ratio to compensation.

Gore paid $40,750.88 for a BMW sold as new. Acid-rain paint damage during transit had required $601 in refinishing, which was not specifically disclosed. BMW NA's policy withheld disclosure of repairs costing less than three percent of suggested retail price. A detailer discovered the refinishing after about nine months; no apparent finish problem during that use or physical injury was established. The signed damage/inspection acknowledgment did not defeat the adjudicated suppression claim. The jury awarded $4,000 for diminished value and $4 million in punitive damages. Alabama found sufficient clear-and-convincing evidence of the conscious or deliberate fraud required for punitive entitlement in this action.

The parties stipulated to 983 refinished vehicles; BMW estimated 11–14 Alabama transactions. Counsel proposed roughly 1,000 transactions multiplied by $4,000. Alabama found substantial jury reliance on foreign transactions but distinguished their permissible use to show pattern or intent from their use as a punitive multiplier. Because the record did not establish which foreign transactions were unlawful, the state court rejected that multiplier and expressly excluded foreign acts from its own amount analysis. The trial court had held an evidentiary postverdict hearing and found no bias, passion or prejudice. The Alabama Supreme Court applied its punitive-review criteria, compared awards and conditionally reduced punitive damages to $2 million.

The operative opinion is the August 19, 1994 substituted opinion, not the withdrawn October 1993 opinion. Gore accepted the remittitur on August 26. The September 9 certificate affirms a total $2,004,000 judgment, with interest and costs. The separate judgment against manufacturer BMW AG was reversed and rendered for that defendant. BMW NA's punitive challenge does not reopen that disposition or the $4,000 compensation.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision. No Justice is recused or otherwise nonparticipating.

#### Judgment

**Reversed insofar as the Alabama judgment sustains $2 million in punitive damages, and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, who would affirm the remitted judgment against the presented federal challenge.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Stevens delivers the Opinion of the Court. The substantive framework, invalidation of the remaining award and remand command five votes; Part II commands six.

| Opinion of the Court, Parts I, III-A–B and IV | Justice Stevens | Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | All of these Parts: gross excessiveness, legitimate punitive interests, fair notice, the three guideposts, the substantive invalidity of the remaining $2 million, and the limited remand. |

Justice Scalia dissents, joined by Justice Thomas. Justice Ginsburg separately dissents. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in Part II and otherwise dissents, including from the judgment.

The dissenters' agreement on affirmance does not merge their reasons. The Court's five direct joins require no *Marks* synthesis.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Grossly excessive punitive damages violate due process; fair notice includes severity

**Controlling proposition:** A punitive award grossly excessive in relation to the State's legitimate interests in punishment and deterrence violates due process. The inquiry begins with those interests. Fair notice encompasses both conduct subject to punishment and the possible severity of the penalty; wealth does not remove that constitutional constraint.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** States may punish fraud and deter its repetition through damages exceeding compensation. The different functions of compensation and punishment preclude a rule requiring the two to move in exact proportion. They do not authorize every amount. Haslip sustained punitive adjudication bounded by reasonable constraints and a relationship to legitimate punitive purposes; it did not approve every later Alabama award.

The strongest objection is that traditional procedures and actual judicial review should leave the selected amount to state judgment. Those safeguards are indispensable, but the conclusion that review occurred does not dispose of a separately presented claim of gross excessiveness. TXO sustained its particular process without supplying a controlling substantive formula. Oberg requires meaningful examination and lawful correction of the amount, while reserving the substantive question answered here.

Notice of liability and notice of severity perform related but distinct work. Alabama's established disclosure duty remains valid, and the adjudicated suppression is not made lawful by disagreement among States or a later statute. Nonetheless, the punishment must remain within the constitutional protection against grossly excessive deprivation. Corporate wealth can bear on deterrence without replacing the required relationship to the wrong. The Court adopts neither all criminal procedural protections for civil actions nor a right to mathematical advance calculation of every permissible award.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip's substantive and procedural protections are applied. TXO's record-specific procedural holding and Oberg's amount-review requirement remain intact; neither is recast as an earlier adoption of today's substantive framework.

##### II. Foreign evidence and punishment are distinct; Alabama's stated correction is accepted

**Controlling proposition:** Alabama may not punish conduct lawful where it occurred and having no impact on Alabama or its residents, or use punitive deterrence to impose its policy on other States. Whether a State may seek to change unlawful conduct elsewhere remains open. Relevant foreign transactions may illuminate pattern, intent and reprehensibility; their evidentiary use does not itself impose punishment for those transactions.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: six.

**Controlling explanation:** Alabama has a legitimate interest in protecting its purchasers from fraud. That interest does not give it authority to punish lawful transactions disconnected from the State simply because it would prefer a different disclosure policy elsewhere. Differences among state policies therefore matter to the permissible reach of deterrence. The holding is qualified by lawfulness and absence of Alabama impact; it supplies no universal immunity for conduct outside the State.

Evidence and punishment answer different questions. A recurring policy, including transactions elsewhere, can support knowledge, intent or the character of the Alabama wrong when governing law makes it relevant. The Court neither excludes all such evidence nor adjudicates the legality of every sale in the national sample. Nor does it decide whether another State could punish conduct unlawful within its own authority.

The original multiplier presented the identified problem. Alabama rejected it and expressly excluded foreign acts from its own amount determination. That statement is accepted; a separate state concurrence's description does not establish that the majority covertly retained the multiplier. The judgment under review is the accepted $2 million remittitur. Recognizing the correction neither reinstates the original verdict nor determines whether the remaining amount survives the independent substantive inquiry in Part III.

**Precedent treatment:** Shutts and Allstate v. Hague support attention to the State's connection with the transactions affected. Their choice-of-law principles are not transformed into an evidence exclusion or a numerical punitive ceiling.

##### III-A. Three qualified guideposts structure constitutional excessiveness review

**Controlling proposition:** Review considers the reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct; the relationship of punitive damages to actual or potential harm to the plaintiff; and civil or criminal sanctions authorized or imposed for comparable misconduct. These are operative guideposts, with reprehensibility especially important, but not an exhaustive mechanical checklist, fixed ratio or dollar ceiling.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III-A, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** Reprehensibility connects severity to the wrong being punished. The nature of the injury, deliberate or affirmative misconduct, repeated behavior and vulnerability can distinguish more blameworthy conduct from less aggravated wrongdoing. Economic injury is not categorically immune from substantial punishment; intentional misconduct or exploitation can justify a substantial award without physical injury.

The harm comparison likewise informs rather than mechanically decides proportionality. Actual compensation need not measure the harm deliberately threatened. Supported potential harm belongs in the inquiry, while speculative loss cannot be invented to justify a chosen number. Small compensation, egregious conduct, injury difficult to detect or harm difficult to value can support larger ratios. The Court announces no bright line beyond which every award fails.

Comparable authorized or imposed sanctions inform the seriousness with which the State treats the misconduct and the notice of possible severity. They do not automatically become exclusive civil-punishment caps. State discretion, deterrence and differences among remedies remain relevant, but cannot make every comparison immaterial.

These guideposts supply reasons for constitutional judgment rather than an unexplained impression that an award seems large. Haslip's refusal to establish a universal mathematical boundary is preserved. TXO's divided potential-harm reasoning cannot be treated as binding arithmetic, and disparity among jury outcomes alone establishes no bias. The inquiry requires judgment applied to lawful purposes and supported facts.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip's reasonableness inquiry is developed into the stated guideposts. TXO establishes no controlling substantive formula, and this decision adopts no fixed ratio.

##### III-B. The remitted $2 million is grossly excessive on this record

**Controlling proposition:** After accepting Alabama's removal of foreign transactions from its punitive calculation, the remaining $2 million is grossly excessive in light of the established wrong, supported harm and comparable sanctions. Neither adequate power to review nor actual reduction conclusively establishes a constitutionally permissible amount.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III-B, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court accepts the deliberate-suppression finding and entitlement to punitive damages. Recurrence and concealment support punishment beyond compensation. The injury nevertheless was economic, without an established health or safety danger, and the character of this nondisclosure does not equate it with the most aggravated fraud. A repair bill alone would understate Gore's loss; the jury's $4,000 diminution finding supplies the actual compensation.

No supported much larger injury to Gore supplies the missing scale. The estimated 11–14 Alabama transactions can illuminate pattern and culpability, but are not adjudicated individual claims or an automatic aggregate punishment base. Neither national loss nor a larger future injury is established in this record. The 500-to-1 disparity therefore raises a substantial concern on this record, though that number is not itself a universal rule.

Variable disclosure laws inform notice and blameworthiness without erasing Alabama's adjudicated duty. The identified $2,000 statutory sanction and the comparison awards provide no adequate notice or justification for this magnitude. The statutory figure is a comparator, not an exclusive cap, and BMW's proposed $56,000 calculation is not adopted.

The strongest argument for affirmance combines deliberate recurring conduct, deterrence and Alabama's actual hearing, review and halving. Those circumstances receive weight; they do not justify the extraordinary remaining amount under the guideposts. BMW's ability to pay cannot fill the gap. This is a substantive conclusion about the corrected award, not a finding that the reviewing judges were dishonest, that no review occurred or that the original multiplier remained secretly operative.

**Precedent treatment:** Haslip did not immunize every Alabama result. TXO's supported threatened harm is not presumed present here. Oberg's protection of amount review is preserved without treating its distinct procedural defect as established in this case.

##### IV. Alabama chooses the lawful means of redetermining punitive damages

**Controlling proposition:** Reverse only the judgment sustaining the $2 million punitive award and remand for a lawful punitive disposition. Alabama initially determines whether a new punitive trial or an independent appellate determination is appropriate. The Court selects no sum and does not require a new jury merely because the original verdict used an improper multiplier.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: five.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court has decided that the corrected amount is grossly excessive, not merely that Alabama omitted a hearing or failed to provide enough explanatory words. Its remedy therefore requires a disposition consistent with that substantive holding. Repeating the same $2 million on this record with fuller prose would not answer it.

The accepted remittitur establishes the operative judgment; it does not prevent further lawful correction after federal review. State procedures retain the first choice between a new punitive trial and an appropriate appellate redetermination. No constitutional maximum, mandatory reduction or universal rule governing every judicial alteration is selected.

The compensation and suppression liability supply no basis for reopening, and entitlement to a lawful punitive award survives. BMW AG's separate favorable disposition remains outside this challenge. Neither revival of the discarded $4 million nor judgment on other purchasers' claims follows. The limited remand respects Alabama's punitive authority while enforcing the constitutional constraint applied here.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

Haslip remains governing: States may employ punitive damages with reasonable constraints, actual review and a relationship to legitimate punishment and deterrence. Its rejection of a universal mathematical line survives. The present guideposts and application develop substantive excessiveness review without facially invalidating Alabama's punitive system.

TXO's five-Justice procedural sufficiency proposition remains intact. Its distinct substantive writings establish no common ratio or potential-harm formula. Oberg continues to require meaningful examination and lawful correction of punitive **amount**, or an adequate functional substitute; review of entitlement or legal error alone does not necessarily suffice. The Court does not overrule that safeguard or impose its Oregon-specific result on Alabama's different process.

Shutts and Allstate v. Hague support territorial limits within their stated roles; neither creates a general prohibition on relevant foreign evidence. No precedent is overruled. The three-Justice supplemental constraints explanation and the dissenters' competing federalism and source-of-right theories retain their separate authority.

Due process forbids grossly excessive punitive awards in relation to legitimate state punishment and deterrence. Fair notice includes possible severity. Reprehensibility, the relationship to actual or potential harm to the plaintiff, and comparable sanctions are controlling guideposts, with their stated qualifications. They are neither optional observations nor a fixed ratio, exclusive checklist or dollar cap.

The qualified territorial rule has six votes. It excludes punishment of conduct lawful elsewhere and having no Alabama impact, preserves relevant evidence, and reserves the question of efforts to change unlawful foreign conduct. Alabama's stated removal of foreign transactions is accepted. Five Justices nevertheless invalidate the surviving $2 million on substantive grounds and require a lawful punitive redetermination.

Meaningful amount review remains required. The existence of process does not immunize every result, and this result does not establish absence of process. Compensation, punitive entitlement, BMW AG's dismissal, the validity of every form of judicial alteration and questions of repeated punishment for the same conduct remain within the limits stated above. No new general corporate-rights catalogue is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Breyer, concurring, joined by O'Connor and Souter.** All three join the Court's opinion in full and supply an additional explanation. A procedurally proper award ordinarily deserves a strong presumption of validity. Here both the standards' failure significantly to constrain discretion as interpreted and applied and the gross disproportion to legitimate punitive objectives overcome that presumption. The existence of hearings, state criteria and actual appellate correction is acknowledged.

Clear-and-convincing proof of punitive entitlement does not distinguish every permissible amount. Harm, culpability and profit criteria must constrain the actual sum; wealth and litigation incentives do not supply a missing limit, and inapplicable mitigation factors do not do so. A supported economic theory can provide meaningful discipline, but a hypothetical model cannot replace the grounds and evidence before the state court. Historical comparisons illustrate the concern without creating a damages formula. The concurrence neither invalidates Alabama's system on its face nor requires written arithmetic, a compulsory economic model or an automatic new trial. Its conjunctive rationale is supported by three Justices, not an additional Court holding.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They would affirm. Their substantive/process distinction preserves traditional adjudication and meaningful judicial review enforcing state-law limits while rejecting an independent federal constitutional entitlement to a different punitive sum. They regard the guideposts as lacking a sufficient constitutional source and workable measure for federal supervision of amounts. Disagreement with a number does not prove the absence of a safeguard.

Alabama held a postverdict hearing, applied its review criteria, expressly removed the foreign multiplier and reduced the judgment. Assuming the identified error required correction, the correction occurred; they need not adopt the Court's territorial rule. Their position approves neither the original $4 million nor every possible remittitur. Both retain Oberg's amount-review protection; Thomas's agreement rests on his own TXO and Oberg positions, without adopting a separate opinion he did not join.

**Ginsburg, dissenting.** She would affirm from institutional restraint, federalism and respect for actual state correction. Alabama's courts addressed deliberate recurring fraud, considered their substantive criteria and comparison awards, and replaced the jury's national measure with a reduced judgment. The difficulty of supplying a workable federal scale counsels against displacing that state judgment here. Her concern is not answered simply by identifying a high ratio.

She does not join Scalia's categorical rejection of every substantive due-process protection or affirm by applying the Court's new guideposts. Her earlier reservation of a properly presented gross-excessiveness challenge remains meaningful. The present federal challenge does not justify additional intervention in light of the safeguards and correction actually supplied. State reform may address punitive practice, but proposed reforms are not treated as operative law, and a later disclosure amendment does not erase this adjudicated wrong. Her conclusion rests on the established review and correction.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part II and otherwise dissenting.** He would affirm the remitted $2 million punitive judgment. His answer to the first question is yes: Alabama supplied a constitutionally sufficient remedy for the identified use of foreign transactions through meaningful review and its established, accepted remittitur procedure. His answer to the second is no: constitutionally adequate adjudication and meaningful review do not leave an additional federal substantive power to invalidate an amount merely because its magnitude or ratio appears excessive.

BMW's corporate form does not remove its property and procedural protections, but those protections do not themselves establish a substantive entitlement to the sum a federal judge prefers. Each claimed constitutional consequence requires its own source. TXO established no controlling ratio or general substantive formula; Oberg requires meaningful examination and lawful correction of the amount without supplying a maximum, mandatory remittitur or federal recalculation formula. That amount-review function cannot be reduced to checking punitive entitlement alone.

He agrees that Alabama cannot punish beyond its lawful authority and that relevant foreign evidence differs from foreign conduct made an independent object of punishment. Knowledge, intent, deliberateness, notice or a common policy may make such evidence relevant. Alabama expressly corrected its identified error; another potentially lawful remedy does not automatically become constitutionally necessary.

Compensation measures loss, while punitive damages serve punishment and deterrence. A large ratio alone cannot determine whether the latter rests on a lawful basis; nor does a large compensatory award justify any punishment. Reprehensibility, ratios and comparable sanctions may inform state-law or otherwise established review without becoming the Court's new constitutional elements. Courts retain meaningful amount review and correction of unlawful punitive availability, instructions, proof, considerations, procedural unfairness and statutory violations. Those examples are not an exhaustive checklist.

His conclusion neither makes punitive damages unreviewable nor authorizes punishment outside Alabama's lawful authority. It preserves traditional remittitur, restores no part of the original $4 million, and reserves whether every form of judicial alteration is permissible. He joins Part II's narrow territorial and evidentiary propositions, but not the Court's independent substantive framework, application or remand.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Alabama must provide a lawful punitive disposition consistent with the substantive constitutional ruling. It determines in the first instance whether a new punitive trial or an independent appellate redetermination is appropriate. The Court sets no replacement sum, mandatory remittitur figure or constitutional ratio. Merely adding explanation for the same $2 million on the same record would not cure the holding that this amount is grossly excessive.

The $4,000 compensation, underlying suppression liability, entitlement to some lawful punitive award and BMW AG's separate dismissal remain undisturbed. The original $4 million is not restored. No nationwide recovery, adjudication of other purchasers' claims or ruling on multiple punitive awards for the same conduct is entered. Stone-Zsela's affirmative answer concerning Alabama's correction and his partial territorial join do not constitute a separate Court judgment affirming the punitive award.

The case returns to Alabama for a lawful punitive disposition, with the State initially selecting between a new punitive trial and an independent appellate redetermination. No replacement sum, automatic jury proceeding, special mandate date or retained Supreme Court merits proceeding is prescribed. The original $4 million remains displaced; compensation, underlying suppression liability, lawful punitive entitlement and BMW AG's separate dismissal are not reopened. Ordinary mandate procedures apply.
