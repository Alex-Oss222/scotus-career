### Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, No. 90-1424

Merits decision, June 12, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 3, 1991; decided June 12, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Eighth Circuit, [911 F.2d 117](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/911/911.F2d.117.89-5386.89-5192.html), which affirmed the District of Minnesota's summary judgment, declaratory and mandatory rulemaking relief, and dependent attorney-fee award after an earlier remand on standing.

The questions are whether the conservation organizations have Article III standing to challenge the geographic limit on consultation under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) and, if so, whether §7(a)(2) reaches agency actions abroad. The Secretary seeks reversal; respondents seek to preserve the declaration and revised-regulation order. The Court resolves standing and does not reach the statute's geographic scope.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Article III elements must be supported at the litigation stage

**Controlling proposition:** A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction must establish a concrete and particularized, actual or imminent injury, a causal connection fairly traceable to the defendant rather than independent third parties, and a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the injury. At summary judgment, the plaintiff must supply specific facts by affidavit or other evidence on the challenged elements; favorable reasonable inferences remain available, but allegations cannot substitute for the missing evidence.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela concurs separately in the judgment on the personal-injury ground and does not join this section. The general redressability element is controlling; its application to these absent agencies is not.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that standing is part of the plaintiff's case and must be supported as the litigation progresses. Warth v. Seldin and Allen v. Wright require a personal injury connected to the challenged conduct; Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization requires likely redress through the requested judgment. Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation supplies the distinction between allegations sufficient at pleading and evidence required at summary judgment. Respondents therefore cannot rely on the earlier decision allowing their allegations to survive dismissal. They must produce specific facts supporting their own threatened injury. Rule 56 preserves favorable reasonable inferences from evidence; it does not authorize a court to presume facts necessary to establish jurisdiction merely because the claim otherwise fails. Where independent third parties determine whether the plaintiff will be harmed or helped, respondents must support the necessary causal connection. The Court resolves this action for want of personal injury and does not adopt the separate opinion's application of redressability to the absent financing agencies.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): applied for the plaintiff's personal stake and burden to establish standing; its statutory-rights discussion remains subject to concrete injury.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied for the constitutional elements and the limits on generalized judicial supervision.
- Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization, 426 U.S. 26 (1976): applied for fair traceability and likely redress; no new majority application concerning these absent agencies follows.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied for the evidentiary distinction between pleading and summary judgment; its separate APA programmatic-review holding is not enlarged.
- Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): applied to require evidence of challenged essential elements while preserving genuine material disputes and reasonable inferences.

##### 2. Indefinite return intentions and general species interests do not establish threatened personal injury here

**Controlling proposition:** Aesthetic, recreational and professional interests in observing wildlife can support standing, but these plaintiffs must show threatened interference with their own concrete use. Prior visits and an indefinite intention to return, without concrete plans or another supported basis for imminent renewed exposure, do not establish standing for these prospective claims; an ecosystem, animal or professional connection that supplies only general interest in distant species does not cure that defect.

**Authority:** Part III-A of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela's separate concurrence supplies a sixth vote for this record-specific personal-injury conclusion without joining the Court's writing. No purchased-ticket or particular-document prerequisite is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a recognized interest must be threatened in the plaintiff's own life. Sierra Club v. Morton recognizes aesthetic and recreational injury while requiring a member of the plaintiff organization to be among those harmed. The affidavits identify actual visits to the Egyptian and Sri Lankan areas and an interest in observing their wildlife. They provide no sufficiently definite return undertaking or continuing activity showing imminent exposure to the threatened loss. Los Angeles v. Lyons and Whitmore v. Arkansas require more than past exposure followed by a conjectural future injury. The Court accepts reasonable inferences from the supplied facts, but an unspecified intention cannot itself establish when personal use will recur. The proposed ecosystem and professional theories fail for the same evidentiary reason: they broaden the class of interested persons without showing a concrete effect on these members. National Wildlife Federation requires an affected personal use, not a general environmental concern. The Court does not decide a case involving actual use of an affected ecosystem or demonstrated injury transmitted across geographic distance.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): applied; aesthetic injury is cognizable, but an organization's special interest cannot replace injury to a member.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333 (1977): applied to require an injured member for this associational claim; no other associational requirement is changed.
- Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95 (1983): applied for the distinction between past exposure and a present or imminent injury supporting prospective relief.
- Whitmore v. Arkansas, 495 U.S. 149 (1990): applied for actual or imminent rather than conjectural injury.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990): applied to specific affected use; no categorical bar to geographically transmitted injury is established.
- Japan Whaling Association v. American Cetacean Society, 478 U.S. 221 (1986): distinguished because its whale-watching and studying members alleged an adverse effect on their own activities; concrete professional or ecological injury remains possible.

##### 3. Citizen-suit authorization does not dispense with a threatened concrete interest

**Controlling proposition:** ESA §11(g)'s authorization for any person to sue does not give Article III standing to a plaintiff asserting only the public's common interest in lawful administration. A procedural right may protect a plaintiff's threatened concrete interest without requiring proof that the procedure will certainly change the ultimate agency decision, but the procedural label cannot replace that personal interest; these respondents have not shown the required connection.

**Authority:** Part IV of Scalia's Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls with five votes. Stone-Zsela does not join this section or its broader congressional-power analysis; he separately concludes only that the claimed consultation deprivation fails to connect to sufficiently imminent interference with these members' concrete use.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that statutory permission to bring suit and constitutional injury remain distinct. Fairchild v. Hughes and the generalized-grievance decisions refuse federal jurisdiction based solely on every citizen's interest in lawful government. Section 11(g) authorizes enforcement but does not itself demonstrate that every violation injures every person. Respondents cannot establish the missing personal connection by treating consultation as an abstract entitlement available to the public at large. This conclusion does not exclude procedural injuries as a class. A person whose concrete interest a required procedure protects need not prove with certainty that performing the procedure will change the final agency choice. Sierra Club preserves the requirement of personal injury while Warth permits Congress to recognize legally protected injuries. Congress may recognize concrete factual injuries previously inadequate in law; the Court does not settle the outer reach of that power. Here, however, the citizen-suit provision does not identify a concrete injury to these members independently of the unsupported future use already examined. The Court leaves the statute's geographic reach undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126 (1922): applied to reject a general right to compel lawful administration as a sufficient personal injury.
- Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- Ex parte Levitt, 302 U.S. 633 (1937): applied for the distinction between direct injury and an undifferentiated public grievance.
- United States v. Richardson, 418 U.S. 166 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War, 418 U.S. 208 (1974): applied to generally shared governmental grievances without distinctive concrete harm.
- Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church and State, Inc., 454 U.S. 464 (1982): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984): applied; a claimed right to lawful government conduct alone does not satisfy Article III.
- Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490 (1975): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Sierra Club v. Morton, 405 U.S. 727 (1972): preserved for Congress's power to recognize injuries and broaden protected interests while retaining the plaintiff's own concrete injury.
- Trafficante v. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co., 409 U.S. 205 (1972): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.
- Hardin v. Kentucky Utilities Co., 390 U.S. 1 (1968): distinguished as statutory recognition of concrete personal interests, not authorization of a wholly abstract grievance.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 12, 1992, respondents' standing fails for insufficient threatened personal injury. Ordinary evidentiary burdens apply at each litigation stage. The ESA citizen-suit provision does not turn an undifferentiated demand for consultation into concrete injury. Procedural protections tied to a personal interest remain cognizable without certainty that the final agency choice will change. The Court applies and clarifies existing Article III doctrine on this foreign-project record and imposes no documentary prerequisite.

The Court applies Sierra Club's personal-injury requirement, National Wildlife Federation's evidentiary requirements, and the generalized-grievance authorities described above. No earlier precedent is overruled. The Court does not decide whether the Secretary's regulation binds absent agencies, whether particular funding changes would alter the projects, or whether §7(a)(2) reaches actions abroad. Each Court holding has five supporters independently of the judgment-only agreements. Part III-B's redressability application has three votes and Stevens's statutory ground has one; neither controls or changes precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia's Part III-B, joined by White and Thomas, would independently reject redressability because the action agencies are absent and their response, and the foreign projects' response, to a revised rule is not adequately established. Three Justices adopt that application; it does not control.

Kennedy, joined by Souter, reserves that redressability question. Their concurrence leaves room for factually demonstrated ecosystem or professional injuries and for Congress to define injuries and causal chains, while requiring Congress at least to identify the injury and relate it to the persons entitled to sue.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment because the past visits and indefinite return intentions do not show imminent renewed personal use. A definite undertaking or an established return practice could supply the necessary evidence; purchased tickets or a particular travel document are unnecessary. He reserves disputed redressability, the outer constitutional scope of congressionally created procedural rights, and ESA geographic coverage. Actual use of an affected ecosystem presents a materially different record; multiple contributors alone would not defeat such a claim.

Stevens concurs in reversal while finding standing. In his view, the personal aesthetic and professional interest suffers when the threatened species and habitat are destroyed, and consultation can provide meaningful relief. He reads §7(a)(2)'s domestic consultation and exemption structure, alongside the Act's express international provisions, to exclude foreign actions. That construction receives no majority and is not the Court's law.

Blackmun, joined by O'Connor, dissents from ending the action on summary judgment. The prior visits, professional interests and intended return permit favorable inferences about future exposure, and the agencies' involvement and potential project influence create a factual issue about redress. Their procedural-injury discussion concerns Congress's capacity to protect concrete interests through required processes; it does not supply a controlling exception to Article III.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court reverses and remands. Supreme Court review is complete. The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for vacatur of the merits declaration, mandatory rulemaking relief and dependent fee award, and dismissal for want of Article III jurisdiction. No substitute fee amount or independent fee-entitlement rule is adopted.

The Court requires no cancellation of a foreign project, new regulation, consultation, factfinding remand or particular later filing. The jurisdictional dismissal does not resolve the statute's geographic reach or preclude a later action supported by sufficient standing facts.

**End of entry: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, merits decision, June 12, 1992.**

### Young v. Northern Illinois Conference of United Methodist Church, No. 93-1917

Merits decision, January 17, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 29, 1994; decided January 17, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, reviewing 21 F.3d 184 (1994), lower-court No. 93-2157. The questions are whether the Religion Clauses bar a probationary minister's Title VII race, sex, and retaliation claims challenging advancement and termination, and whether that protection is jurisdictional or substantive. Young sought reinstatement, reconsideration for full clergy membership, back pay and benefits, compensatory and punitive damages, fees, and costs after the March 4, 1992 decision. No discrimination is found.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice is noted as not participating.

#### Judgment

The jurisdictional judgment is vacated and the case remanded for dismissal of the stated Title VII ministerial-selection claims on the merits. All nine agree on the defense and jurisdiction; eight join that mandate, while Chief Justice Stone-Zsela would also affirm the dismissal on its corrected merits basis.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jurisdictional characterization | Vacated, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No Justice | Statutory jurisdiction remains; the defense is substantive. |
| Title VII race, sex, and retaliation claims challenging ministerial advancement and retention | Dismissal on the merits required, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No Justice | Enter substantive dismissal; no employment or monetary relief. |
| Form of mandate | Vacate the jurisdictional judgment and remand for substantive dismissal, eight Justices; the Chief would vacate its jurisdictional characterization, affirm dismissal on the merits, and remand | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Stone-Zsela differs only in the formal affirmance of dismissal | The governing mandate requires entry of a merits dismissal of these claims; the Chief expressly calls for dismissal with prejudice. |

| Concurrence in part and in the substantive disposition | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Would vacate the jurisdictional characterization, affirm dismissal on the merits, and remand | Preserves his distinct formal disposition and additional limits on ministerial autonomy. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Ministerial selection claims encounter a constitutional defense

**Controlling proposition:** The Religion Clauses bar the application of Title VII to Young's race, sex, and retaliation claims challenging her advancement and retention as a probationary minister, because deciding those claims or imposing the requested relief would subject the church's choice of its religious leadership to civil review. The defense covers reinstatement, compelled reconsideration, and financial liability resting on that same selection decision; it does not exempt every religious employee or independently actionable secular conduct from civil law.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion, this proposition, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine adopt this claim-specific rule; their formal descriptions of the judgment are distinguished in the Judgment table.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these claims would place a civil court in control of a ministerial selection. Kedroff protects church government and the selection of ecclesiastical authorities from state control. Milivojevich forbids civil reconsideration of ecclesiastical decisions through an inquiry into whether the church followed its own internal rules. Young seeks review of denied advancement and termination of her ministerial service, including alleged departures from church procedure. Reinstatement or compelled reconsideration would directly control that office; damages for the same decision do not make the underlying adjudication independent. Race and sex discrimination and retaliation are serious civil wrongs, but calling their asserted motives secular does not remove this inquiry's control of religious leadership. Section 702's religious-preference permission is not enlarged to include those categories. Jones v. Wolf permits neutral adjudication of genuine property obligations; this record establishes no independently pleaded secular claim of that kind. The Court does not find discrimination, religious motivation, or procedural compliance, and leaves disputed ministerial status and unrelated civil obligations for appropriate cases.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Kedroff v. St. Nicholas Cathedral, 344 U.S. 94 (1952): applies its protection of church government and selection of ecclesiastical authorities to this ministerial-employment claim.
- Serbian Eastern Orthodox Diocese v. Milivojevich, 426 U.S. 696 (1976): applies its bar on reviewing internal ecclesiastical decisions for arbitrariness or compliance with church procedures; its unresolved fraud-or-collusion possibility remains unresolved.
- Jones v. Wolf, 443 U.S. 595 (1979): distinguishes neutral property adjudication from review of ministerial selection and preserves the former within its own limits.
- Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872 (1990): distinguishes regulation of ordinary conduct by neutral general law from civil control of the choice of religious leaders; its rule is not replaced.

##### The defense defeats the claims on their merits, not statutory jurisdiction

**Controlling proposition:** A federal court with jurisdiction under Title VII §706(f)(3) and 28 U.S.C. §1331 over a substantial Title VII claim exercises that jurisdiction when it decides that the Religion Clauses defeat the claim; it must enter a substantive dismissal rather than characterize this defense as a withdrawal of subject-matter jurisdiction. Young's pleaded claims are substantial federal claims, and the remand is for entry of a merits dismissal of the stated ministerial advancement-and-retention claims, not a discrimination trial or an award of relief.

**Authority:** Justice Souter's opinion, this proposition, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine adopt the jurisdiction-and-merits distinction; eight vacate the jurisdictional judgment as a whole, while the Chief would vacate the jurisdictional characterization and affirm the dismissal on the merits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the power to decide a federal claim does not depend on the claimant ultimately overcoming a constitutional defense. Bell v. Hood separates a substantial federal question from the legal sufficiency of the asserted right to relief. Congress supplied jurisdiction over Title VII claims, and Young's discrimination allegations invoke that statutory subject. The church's successful defense therefore supplies the reason for a merits judgment, not a reason to deny that the court possessed adjudicatory authority. Ecclesiastical autonomy limits the permissible application of civil law; deciding that limit is itself an exercise of jurisdiction. Bell's exceptions for immaterial claims made solely to obtain jurisdiction and wholly insubstantial and frivolous claims are not presented here. The Court does not decide their treatment. The correction changes the legal basis and preclusive character of the dismissal without requiring a trial about church motives. No reinstatement, promotion, back pay, or damages follows from recognition that the federal court had power to decide the defense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678 (1946): applies the distinction between a substantial federal claim's jurisdictional basis and its legal defeat; the immaterial-jurisdiction-device and wholly insubstantial-or-frivolous exceptions are not reached.
- Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375 (1994): preserves the need for a statutory or otherwise lawful jurisdictional basis; Title VII and §1331 supply that basis here, unlike the separate enforcement claim in Kokkonen.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The constitutional defense rests on Young's undisputed reported ministerial role and her selection-dependent claims, not a comprehensive definition of minister. No fraud or collusion is alleged; the Court neither adopts nor rejects an exception for it. The relevant church decision is that of the highest body to which this dispute was actually carried, without a new exhaustion requirement reaching the denomination's ultimate hierarchy. RFRA's invocation, preservation, and application to this suit remain undecided. A genuinely severable salary, property, tort, contract, or other claim remains outside this adjudication only if independently pleaded and resolvable without deciding doctrine, ministerial fitness, or the selection of religious leaders; no such claim is found to exist here.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Kedroff and Milivojevich supply the claim-specific selection defense. Jones preserves neutral independent property adjudication. Bell supplies statutory jurisdiction despite a defeating defense. Smith, Lukumi, Kiryas Joel, Lamb's Chapel, and Clearwater remain within their different subjects; no RFRA application or new §702 race/sex exemption is adopted. Kokkonen's requirement of a jurisdictional source is satisfied, not displaced.

Effective January 17, 1995, these selection-dependent Title VII claims encounter a substantive Religion Clause defense, and substantial federal jurisdiction survives to decide that defense. The ruling creates no general immunity from civil law for religious institutions, does not enlarge Title VII §702, and leaves RFRA applicability and independently pleaded secular claims outside its holding.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the Court's ministerial-selection defense and jurisdictional distinction. He would vacate the jurisdictional characterization, affirm dismissal of the ministerial-employment claims on the merits, and direct dismissal with prejudice insofar as those claims challenge ordination, ministerial promotion, discipline, assignment, or continued ministerial employment. The Court's mandate also requires substantive dismissal, but he would expressly affirm that disposition while correcting its basis.

In his view ministerial autonomy follows genuine function rather than title: who preaches, leads worship, teaches doctrine, and represents a faith belongs to the religious body. Even substantial evidence that sex entered a selection decision cannot authorize judicial review of whether doctrinal or ministerial reasons were valid or pretextual. That limit does not excuse discrimination in ordinary secular employment and supplies no general church immunity. An independently pleaded and genuinely severable salary, property, tort, contract, or other claim may proceed only if adjudication avoids religious doctrine, ministerial fitness, and the selection of religious leaders; he identifies no such claim in Young's complaint as reported. Public money, tax benefits, licensing, or other governmental contact alone does not waive ministerial autonomy. Government may impose lawful, clearly stated conditions on secular uses of public funds and recover diverted funds, but cannot purchase control of clergy selection. Those additional public-funding observations are his own; the Court does not decide a funding controversy in this case.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Seventh Circuit's jurisdictional judgment is vacated and the case remanded for entry of a substantive dismissal of the stated ministerial advancement-and-retention claims. No new discrimination trial, ordination, reinstatement, promotion, reconsideration, money, or costs is ordered. The Chief would additionally affirm dismissal on the merits and expressly direct dismissal with prejudice. Review here is complete; entry of the corrected judgment remains below.

### Beadle v. City of Tampa, Eleventh Circuit No. 93-3271

Merits decision, February 5, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On merits review of the Eleventh Circuit's judgment, 42 F.3d 633. Beadle's Sabbath observance conflicted with the police field-training schedule. The question is whether the City's neutral rotation and asserted training burdens satisfied Title VII's distinct religious-accommodation duty.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela.

The lower courts must apply the proper accommodation and hardship inquiry. No particular schedule, placement, reinstatement, damages award, or new trial is ordered. The separate release determination remains undisturbed.

#### Opinions and Holdings

Stevens writes for the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stone-Zsela dissents. The eight-Justice Court opinion controls both holdings and their remedy limits below.

##### Neutral scheduling does not alone discharge the accommodation duty

**Operative rule:** Under 42 U.S.C. §2000e(j), an employer must reasonably accommodate religious observance or practice unless it demonstrates inability to do so without undue hardship on its business. Under Hardison's governing rule, the employer need not incur more than a minimal cost, including concrete lost efficiency, deficient training, or consequent safety burdens; once an effective reasonable accommodation resolves the conflict, Ansonia does not require the employee's preferred alternative or proof against every alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** Title VII's accommodation duty does work separate from its prohibition on intentional discrimination. A neutral rule and absence of hostility do not themselves answer whether a religious conflict can reasonably be accommodated. Here the ordinary rotating schedule and one isolated Saturday leave did not resolve the continuing conflict.

Trans World Airlines v. Hardison permits an employer to refuse accommodation imposing more than the governing modest cost, including interference with protected seniority rights and concrete operational burdens. It does not exempt police work from the statute. Ansonia Board of Education v. Philbrook ends the alternative inquiry when the employer has supplied a reasonable accommodation. It does not turn an unchanged, still-conflicting schedule into one.

The City may rely on professional evidence and reasonable operational inferences about training and safety. It need not await an accident or reduce every burden to dollars. The Court imposes no independent negotiation duty when an effective accommodation or sufficient hardship defense exists, and imports neither a compelling-interest test nor a later hardship standard.

##### A concession about one trainer does not resolve materially different proposals

**Operative rule:** When the work-religion conflict remains unresolved, a hardship justification must address the supported, materially distinct accommodations and actual operations at issue. A concession that using one trainer would impair training does not establish that every proposed arrangement preserving multiple trainers would cause undue hardship; the accommodation judgment must be reconsidered where that mismatch underlies the decision.

**Controlling explanation:** The trial court relied on the neutral system and general police responsibilities while finding no demonstrated deprivation of another trainee's scheduling benefit. The appellate court relied on Beadle's concession that assignment to one trainer throughout the program would reduce educational variety. The record also presents multiple qualified trainers while retaining Dawson's squad, qualified trainers on other Sabbath-compatible nontraining squads, and a four-to-eight-week extension to address rotation deficits. The concession does not decide those different proposals.

Hardison allows actual training deterioration and operational cost to establish hardship. The City may prove those consequences here. But Ansonia's protection against employee selection among effective accommodations does not remove the unresolved statutory inquiry. The Court finds an error in that inquiry, not that any proposal must succeed.

Later practice does not prove an arrangement was available during Beadle's training. Extensions given to other recruits are relevant without establishing that this extension would be costless. Section 11.3 makes classification seniority a factor when skill, performance, and ability are equal and the supervisor finds the task unaffected, but removes seniority from consideration when training affects assignments. That training exception and the absence of a required-swap finding prevent assuming a seniority conflict, without eliminating legitimate coworker interests. A possible downtown posting is not a guaranteed vacancy. The existing record may support a properly reasoned decision; further proceedings are required only as lawfully necessary.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison, 432 U.S. 63:** Preserved and applied; concrete noncash operational burdens and protected seniority interests may establish hardship under its more-than-minimal-cost rule.
- **Ansonia Board of Education v. Philbrook, 479 U.S. 60:** Preserved; a sufficient reasonable accommodation ends the alternative inquiry, but the continuing conflict here has not been resolved by one isolated leave day or the ordinary schedule.

#### Law After Decision

Police departments remain subject to Title VII's actual accommodation duty and governing hardship defense. Neutrality and general safety concerns are insufficient substitutes for the required application. This decision establishes neither the feasibility of Beadle's proposals nor a right to an exemption regardless of cost, training, seniority, or safety.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** He would affirm on the established training and assignment burdens while leaving the rejected release defense untouched. He agrees that neutral scheduling alone is insufficient and that Hardison requires accommodation unless the employer establishes hardship. In his view, the trial findings concern actual training exposure, work conditions, assignments, and the commitment to a later posting. Those operational burdens satisfy Hardison's existing threshold without a categorical public-safety exception.

He would consider an available arrangement that genuinely preserves the training objectives, but finds none established as one the City was legally required to adopt on this record. His position permits neither gratuitous refusal of workable alternatives nor automatic compliance through neutrality, and sets no universal staffing ratio or religious priority. His disagreement is with the majority's assessment of the existing hardship showing and resulting remand.

### United Food & Commercial Workers Union Local 751 v. Brown Group, Inc., No. 95-340

Merits decision, May 13, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 20, 1996, in October Term 1995. The Court reviews under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) the Eighth Circuit's judgment, 50 F.3d 1426, affirming a standing dismissal without prejudice.

Brown Shoe's January 17, 1992 letter to an International union employee announced closure of its Dixon, Missouri plant and displacement of 277 workers beginning March 20. Local 751 alleges that notice should have gone to the Local and that layoffs began immediately. It sued June 16 as the employees' certified exclusive representative for WARN back pay, benefits and fees. Its representative status is undisputed. The complaint's material allegations are accepted as true and construed favorably at this dismissal stage; no statutory violation, employee eligibility or amount has been tried.

The court below found the first two Hunt conditions satisfied but held that individual participation needed for damages made the representative action constitutionally impermissible. It separately rejected a direct-union-injury theory and upheld denial of postjudgment amendment. The petition presents representative standing, including Hunt's third condition; it does not present the amendment ruling. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded on representational standing, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I and II, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both controlling propositions and their explanations. No separate opinion is filed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. WARN authorizes the exclusive representative to enforce employees' monetary claims

**Controlling proposition:** Section 2104(a)(5), read with WARN's representative definition and employee-liability provisions, authorizes the exclusive representative to pursue the represented employees' back-pay and benefit liability. The representative enforces the employees' entitlement without becoming the beneficiary of their recovery. The clause supplies enforcement authority as well as venue.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2101(a)(4) defines the representative as an exclusive employee representative under the specified labor statutes. Section 2102 gives that representative a notice function. Section 2104(a)(1) makes a violating employer liable to aggrieved employees suffering employment loss; paragraph (5) expressly includes a representative among persons who may enforce that liability for others. Reading these provisions together gives effect to both Congress's chosen enforcer and its chosen beneficiary.

Respondent argues that the requirement of being aggrieved restricts every listed plaintiff to someone personally entitled to payment, leaving the representative reference as obsolete language in a venue provision. But that reading deprives the expressly retained representative language of its natural function. A representative's enforcement of an employee's liability is not an award of damages to the union. The deletion of an earlier proposed union-specific remedy does not erase the enacted authority to enforce the workers' recovery.

The statute therefore authorizes the action actually pleaded, rather than a general implied cause of action. Its reference to employee liability does not permit the union to collect a local government's distinct penalty. Nor does naming a representative conclusively resolve Article III or the lawful handling of individual claims. Part II addresses the asserted constitutional obstacle while retaining the statute's limitations on who may recover and what may be awarded.

##### II. Congress has displaced Hunt's prudential individual-participation condition here without displacing Article III

**Controlling proposition:** Hunt's third requirement—that neither the claim nor the requested relief require individual-member participation—is prudential. Congress may displace it and has done so through WARN's express representative authorization. The represented employees must still have concrete injury fairly traceable to the challenged conduct and likely redressable by the requested relief, supported as the litigation stage requires. Individual proof is not made unnecessary; its potential necessity no longer categorically bars this authorized action.

**Authority:** Part II of the same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Hunt ordinarily requires members with standing, interests germane to the organization's purpose, and a claim and remedy not requiring individual participation. The first condition secures an actual controversy through injured represented persons. The third addresses the practical suitability of representative litigation, including questions arising when individual entitlements must be determined. Those concerns do not become Article III requirements merely because the requested relief is monetary.

Warth's damages discussion and Brock's reservation of later individual determinations provide the strongest contrary argument: individualized recovery can raise serious proof, adequacy and binding-effect problems. But neither decision considered Congress's express authorization here. Representative standing already separates the named organization from the persons whose concrete interests are at stake. Congress can remove the prudential participation restriction without authorizing adjudication of an abstract grievance or dispensing with independent constitutional protections.

On the accepted allegations and undisputed representative status, affected workers suffered the employment losses and deficient notice for which the statute provides their monetary remedy; germaneness is also satisfied. These pleading conclusions establish no trial-level violation or amount. Different dates, wages and medical expenses may require individual evidence. That evidence may be handled under applicable law without treating its necessity alone as a constitutional bar.

Germaneness for standing does not automatically establish adequate representation for binding absent persons. Adequacy is not disputed on this record, and the Court orders no new adequacy proceeding, class certification, joinder arrangement or jury procedure. Applicable protections and defenses remain. The independent organizational-injury theory is unnecessary to this sufficient representative ground.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

*Warth*, *Hunt* and *Brock* retain their representative-standing principles and original limits. Earlier approval of common legal challenges did not decide representative recovery of individual damages under this express congressional authorization. *Hunt's* third condition is prudential and displaced here; its first condition and *Lujan's* concrete injury, causation, redressability and stage-appropriate proof requirements remain unchanged.

*Northeastern Florida* remains effective on associational standing and pleading-stage support; it did not establish that every *Hunt* condition is constitutional or decide this congressional-displacement question. *Rowland's* statutory treatment of association applications is not an Article III holding. *Varity* and *Meghrig* preserve the distinction between statutory enforcement authority and additional remedies; their different statutory relief is not imported into WARN. *Matsushita's* adequate-representation protections govern lawful binding and supply no categorical bar to commencing this authorized suit. No precedent is overruled.

WARN's exclusive representative may enforce employees' monetary claims despite the need for individual participation. Congress has displaced Hunt's third condition for that action, not the constitutional core. Without clear congressional authorization, the third condition remains operative. The Court leaves open whether a sufficiently simplified damages claim could satisfy it without abrogation. It also leaves open whether Hunt's second, germaneness condition is prudential and subject to congressional displacement; germaneness is satisfied here without deciding that classification.

The authorization enlarges no WARN remedy. Section 2104(a)(1) retains back pay for each violation day at not less than the higher of the employee's prior three-year average regular rate and final regular rate, plus covered employee-plan benefits, including medical expenses that would have been covered absent the employment loss. Liability lasts no more than 60 days and no more than half the employee's employment days. An aggrieved employee must have failed to receive timely statutory notice directly or through the representative.

Paragraph (2) requires reductions for wages paid for the violation period, voluntary unconditional payments not required by legal obligation, and employer payments to third parties or trustees on the employee's behalf attributable to that period. Defined-benefit liability may also be reduced by service credit for all plan purposes during that period. Paragraph (4) permits discretionary reduction when the employer proves good faith and reasonable grounds for believing its conduct lawful. Prevailing-party fees remain discretionary under paragraph (6).

The separate local-government penalty remains at most $500 per violation day and does not apply if the employer pays each aggrieved employee the amount owed within three weeks after ordering the shutdown or layoff. The union receives no right to that penalty. Section 2104(b) makes the statutory remedies exclusive and withholds authority under WARN to enjoin a plant closing or mass layoff. None of those amounts, predicates, reductions or exceptions is adjudicated here.

Actual individual proof, legally applicable joinder or certification, representation, duplicate recovery, binding effect and any properly presented jury question remain governed by their own law. No assignment, payroll-only proof rule or universal need for employee testimony is established. The remand prescribes no additional mandatory procedural framework.
