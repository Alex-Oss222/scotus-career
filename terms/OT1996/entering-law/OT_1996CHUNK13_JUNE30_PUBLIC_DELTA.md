# 1997-06-26 — Raines v. Byrd; No. 96-1671.

Authority: ../records/Raines_v_Byrd_merits_1997-06-26.md

## Event

Raines v. Byrd, No. 96-1671. Argued May 27, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal under 2 U.S.C. §692(b) from the judgment of a single District Judge for the District of Columbia, reported at 956 F. Supp. 25. The questions are whether six Members of Congress have Article III standing and, only if they do, whether the Line Item Veto Act violates Article I's lawmaking requirements or nondelegation principles.

The Act was approved April 9, 1996, effective January 1, 1997; the Members sued January 2. The District Court found standing and ripeness and declared the Act unconstitutional on April 10; appeal followed April 11. Four Senators and two Representatives had voted against the Act. Their votes were counted, no cancellation had injured them personally, and neither House authorized them to represent Congress. The congressional amici defended the Act's validity without taking a standing position. Senator Hatfield subsequently retired; five plaintiffs remained Members.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court vacates the District Court's judgment and remands with instructions to dismiss the complaint for lack of Article III jurisdiction.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of Article III standing, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer.

Stevens would affirm the constitutional declaration. Breyer would recognize jurisdiction and proceed to the merits, which he reserves. Souter's judgment support does not join the principal injury analysis.

## Holdings

### I. These individual legislators lack a concrete personal injury

**Holding and operative rule:** Individual Members of Congress whose votes were counted and given full effect lack Article III standing to challenge the Line Item Veto Act on the asserted abstract dilution of Congress's institutional power and alteration of future votes. Express statutory permission to sue removes the applicable prudential obstacle but cannot supply the missing concrete personal injury.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg. These six Justices adopt this rule, its explanation, limits and remedy. Souter supplies a seventh judgment vote on a separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Lujan requires a plaintiff's own concrete injury, with causation and redressability; legislative permission cannot replace that constitutional stake. These Members cast fully effective votes against the Act and lost. They retain office and their voting entitlement, apart from Hatfield's subsequent retirement, and identify no cancellation injuring them personally. Powell concerned exclusion from a seat, an individual deprivation absent here. Coleman concerned complete nullification of votes sufficient to determine a legislative outcome; it does not turn every change in the practical force of future votes into that injury.

Akins establishes that numerical sharing does not make a concrete deprivation abstract. Its voters were denied particular information useful to their own electoral participation. These Members instead assert an institutional allocation of power. Their strongest argument is that cancellation authority presently deprives them of voting on the ultimate legal text. But no vote of theirs was overridden or denied legal effect; the asserted change belongs to the legislative institution, which has not authorized their representation. Available legislative remedies and a later concrete cancellation controversy reinforce this bounded conclusion. They create no legislative-exhaustion requirement and do not guarantee any future plaintiff standing. The Court decides no universal rule against official injury or legislator litigation.

### II. The jurisdictional defect requires vacatur and dismissal

**Holding and operative rule:** Because these plaintiffs lack Article III standing, the District Court's constitutional declaration must be vacated and the complaint dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The Court does not decide the Act's constitutionality, nondelegation, Presentment or the independent ripeness question.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice Court opinion controls the jurisdictional disposition and its limits; Souter separately agrees with vacatur and dismissal.

**Controlling explanation:** Steel Co. requires resolution of disputed Article III power before disputed constitutional merits in this setting. Congress's special direct-review and expedition provisions supply a route for review, not jurisdiction over an uninjured plaintiff's constitutional complaint. Vacating the declaration removes a merits judgment the District Court lacked power to enter; it does not sustain the Act on its merits. Personal exclusion, lost salary, complete nullification of decisive votes, an authorized institutional suit meeting Article III, and an actual cancellation causing concrete injury remain distinct questions. The Court neither adjudicates any such future controversy nor adds an independent holding on traceability.

## Precedent Treatment

- Lujan supplies the concrete-personal-injury requirement and the distinction between statutory permission and constitutional injury; its rule is applied without enlarging its noncontrolling redressability analysis.
- Akins preserves standing for concrete injury shared by many; its informational deprivation is distinguished from the institutional dilution alleged here.
- Powell remains authority for personal exclusion from office, a circumstance not presented.
- Coleman remains authority for complete nullification of decisive votes; it is distinguished from votes counted and lost.
- Steel Co. supplies the requirement to decide disputed Article III power before the disputed merits in this setting.
- Bender and Allen support the distinction between an individual's legally cognizable stake and unauthorized institutional litigation and the separation-of-powers limits on jurisdiction. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

These individual legislators cannot invoke Article III on this institutional-dilution claim. Congress's authorization and expedition provisions do not change that conclusion. The Act's constitutional validity remains undecided, and concrete personal or properly authorized institutional injuries remain subject to their own jurisdictional analysis.

## Separate Writings

**Souter, concurring in the judgment, joined by Ginsburg.** Official injury is not categorically excluded by Article III: Coleman and authorized institutional representation prevent that shortcut. Whether this injury is personal rather than official, or concrete rather than abstract, is fairly debatable. Souter instead gives decisive weight to Allen's separation-of-powers foundation and Valley Forge's restraint against unnecessary constitutional adjudication. The Members seek judicial intervention in an unauthorized internal and interbranch contest, while a beneficiary deprived of an actual spending or tax benefit may present a conventional controversy satisfying injury, causation and redressability. These conditions make the present claim constitutionally insufficient. This is neither prudential abstention, an absolute political-question bar, nor a universal requirement that another plaintiff exist.

**Stevens, dissenting.** A Member's opportunity to vote on the law's ultimate text is an individual constitutional function. The Act's present alteration of that function is concrete without waiting for a particular cancellation; Coleman supports protection of official voting power, and Bender's prohibition on unauthorized representation does not defeat a Member's own injury. Stevens finds the claim ripe and would affirm the declaration on Article I grounds. The Act permits the President, after a bill becomes law, to cancel in whole a defined discretionary dollar amount, new-direct-spending item or limited tax benefit under its conditions. Chadha requires bicameralism and presentment for repeal. Giving cancellation a different name or intelligible standards does not turn removal of enacted legal force into execution; Field's contingent execution and ordinary impoundment do not answer the distinct spending-obligation and tax effects. His ground is allocation of lawmaking authority, not a finding that the delegation's standards are insufficiently intelligible. He adds no private presidential-reservation theory and decides no later cancellation remedy.

The cancellation permission requires determinations that the action reduces the deficit, does not impair essential Government functions and does not harm the national interest; the special message must be sent within five calendar days excluding Sundays. Cancellation takes effect upon both Houses' receipt; enacted disapproval nullifies it and restores the provision from its original effective date. A disapproval bill cannot itself be canceled. Disapproval requires legislation, and the special procedures do not abolish ordinary later legislation. The Act has no force or effect on or after January 1, 2005. None of these limitations, in Stevens's view, changes the Article I character of a repeal after enactment.

**Breyer, dissenting.** The systematic impairment of the Members' existing constitutional work is sufficiently concrete. Coleman does not limit official injury to one decisive voting event, and express congressional authorization removes a prudential barrier while reducing separation-of-powers concerns; it does not enlarge Article III. The prospect of a later private suit cannot erase present injury if that injury exists. Breyer finds standing and ripeness and would proceed to the merits. He expressly reserves the Act's constitutionality and does not join Stevens's merits conclusion or vote to sustain the Act.

## Procedure After Action

The constitutional declaration is vacated. The case returns to the District Court with instructions to dismiss the complaint for lack of jurisdiction. No merits remand, injunction, cancellation adjudication or new deadline is ordered.

# 1997-06-26 — Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union; No. 96-511.

Authority: ../records/Reno_v_ACLU_merits_1997-06-26.md

## Event

Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511. Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under §561 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The question is whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad and insufficiently tailored to protecting minors.

The Act was enacted February 8, 1996. The district court's preliminary-injunction order was dated June 11 and entered June 12, 1996; its June 28 clarification preserved June 12 as the appeal-clock entry date. The plaintiffs demonstrated present speech restrictions and practical compliance burdens. The record describes diverse lawful Internet expression and age-verification costs and barriers, especially for noncommercial and small speakers.

Section 223(a)(1)(B) reaches knowing creation or solicitation and initiation of transmission by a telecommunications device of an obscene or indecent communication, knowing its recipient is under eighteen, regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The enacted definition of telecommunications device excludes an interactive computer service. Section (d)(1)(A) separately reaches knowing use of an interactive computer service to send to specified persons under eighteen; (d)(1)(B) reaches display available to a person under eighteen. Their content category covers contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive under contemporary community standards. The corresponding facility offenses require knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for the prohibited activity. Violations carry fines, imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Section (e)(1) protects mere access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the provider's control, including incidental functions that do not create content. Section (e)(2) denies that defense to a conspirator with an entity actively creating or knowingly distributing prohibited communications, or one knowingly advertising them; the conspirator need not personally create content. Section (e)(3) separately denies it for access to an offending network the provider owns or controls. Employer liability under (e)(4) requires within-scope employee or agent conduct plus knowing authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5) provides alternative defenses: good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures under the circumstances to restrict minors' access, or restricted access requiring a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number. Under (e)(6), the FCC may describe suitable measures; it may not approve, sanction, compel or enforce their use or endorse particular products. Their use is evidence of good faith. Interactive services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers.

Section (f)(1) protects lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions against suit. Section (f)(2) bars inconsistent state or local liability for the identified commercial, library and higher-education activities, while allowing complementary regulation confined to intrastate services without inconsistent interstate duties and preserving regulation of conduct outside this section. Section (g) preserves other federal laws. Section (h)'s device definition adds no broadcast-licensee or cable-operator obligations under other indecency provisions; its access-software definition covers content-neutral filtering, selection and transmission tools that do not create or provide the communication's content. These distinct savings provisions supply no absent narrow content definition.

## Holdings

**Authority for both holdings:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. These eight Justices directly adopt the following propositions, explanations, limits and remedy. O'Connor's agreement with part of the judgment does not join this opinion.

### I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** Sable recognizes the compelling interest in protecting children but forbids reducing adults to children's speech when less restrictive means are available. Ginsberg sustained a confined commercial restriction on material obscene for minors, including a value limitation absent here. Pacifica's scarcity, intrusiveness and broadcast setting do not describe this medium; ACT/Pacifica requires independent protection of adult access even there. Turner concerns content-neutral carriage, not this direct selection of prohibited content. Denver Area's compulsory-blocking holding supplies the practical adult-access analogy, without extending its fractured permission judgments into a universal rule.

The criminal categories reach serious discussion of health, sexuality, art, politics and personal experience. Uncertainty about content and community standards chills speech; scienter cannot tell a speaker which protected discussion is criminal. Knowledge of a minor or reference to specified recipients does not prevent the prohibition from reaching conversations also intended for adults. Other communication channels do not cure exclusion from this one.

The defenses do not remove those burdens. Many small and noncommercial speakers lack feasible age verification; costs, loss of anonymity and exclusion of lawful adults persist. Proposed tagging does not establish the effective protection the defense requires on the present record. User-controlled filtering and precise regulation of unprotected material offer materially less restrictive approaches. The Government has not justified this broad criminal suppression in light of those alternatives. The Court does not find every age screen invalid or all future controls ineffective. Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no independent Fifth Amendment holding is needed.

### II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions

**Holding and operative rule:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Controlling explanation:** The word choice permits removal of the indecency branch without disabling the distinct obscenity rule. The proposed further construction would instead change the offenses' substantive coverage. Knowledge and audience limits do not provide the missing narrow content definition, and a serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be supplied by judicial legislation. The statutory defenses preserve their own terms but do not establish workable protection for the record's diverse speakers. The challenge's varied activities and substantial protected reach make a selective judicial reconstruction inappropriate. O'Connor would retain knowing transmissions solely to minors; the Court does not adopt her conclusion that minors' protected-speech overbreadth is insubstantial or her proposed application severance.

The ruling does not hold that Congress can never regulate protected Internet indecency, decide the proposed blanket ban on such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, or resolve the separate question of extraterritorial efficacy and scope. It does not require one constitutional classification for every Internet service. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, true-threat, fraud, unlawful-solicitation and direct-harmful-contact provisions remain governed by their own predicates; they do not save these overbroad restrictions. A future precisely drawn rule using effective, nonexclusionary age assurance remains open.

# 1997-06-26 — Vacco v. Quill; No. 95-1858; October Term 1996, chunk 13.

Authority: ../records/Vacco_v_Quill_merits_1997-06-26.md

## Event

Vacco v. Quill, No. 95-1858. Argued January 8, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. Its April 2, 1996 judgment, 80 F.3d 716, held New York's distinction unconstitutional as applied to competent terminally ill persons in the circumstances presented. The question is whether allowing refusal of unwanted treatment while prohibiting intentional assistance in suicide denies equal protection. New York seeks reversal; the physician respondents seek affirmance. The three initially named patients died before the District Court's decision; the operative appellants below were three physicians. The related due-process case, Washington v. Glucksberg, is separately decided today.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the judgment below and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Second Circuit's equal-protection invalidation is reversed. On remand the courts must reject the presented challenge to New York's treatment-refusal/intentional-assistance distinction. Independent treatment-refusal, lawful pain-care and good-faith palliation protections are undisturbed.

## Holdings

##### New York may distinguish treatment refusal from intentional suicide assistance

**Holding and operative rule:** A State does not deny equal protection by permitting a competent person to refuse unwanted medical treatment while prohibiting intentional assistance in suicide when the distinction reasonably rests on bodily consent, legal causation, intent and professional responsibility and serves legitimate protective interests. New York's distinction satisfies ordinary rational-basis review on the physicians' claim for competent, terminally ill patients; neither the shared eventual consequence of death nor the different practical options of patients dependent on life support requires the State to permit lethal assistance.

**Authority:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. Those five directly support the rule, explanation and limits; the unanimous judgment does not make the four judgment concurrences full rationale joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The law draws no suspect classification, and the presented claim identifies no established fundamental entitlement to the intentional assistance sought. Cruzan grounds treatment refusal in bodily integrity and informed consent; its assumed competent-refusal premise does not establish a right to another person's lethal assistance. Casey preserves a defined reproductive choice and its complete regulation standard, not an unrestricted right to every medical choice.

Equal wording alone does not answer the physicians' objection: patients who depend on life support have a practical option others lack. Heller requires a relationship to that actual disadvantage, and Romer rejects an unexplained disability without demanding a separate hostility finding. Here the patient's refusal ends an unwanted intervention and ordinarily lets the underlying condition take its course. Providing lethal means intentionally to cause death involves a different purpose, causal role and professional responsibility. Good-faith palliation can foresee a shorter life without adopting death as its purpose. The State may preserve bodily consent while protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable patients against error, pressure and abuse, and maintaining medicine's responsibility to care.

Beach permits reasonable generalizations without formal findings or empirical certainty. The distinction need not describe every patient's or physician's intention in every instance: a patient's wish to die, or the label attached to an intervention, does not alone settle purpose or causation. Those possible overlaps do not make the general protective line irrational. The availability of narrower regulation does not require its adoption under ordinary review. Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid bare harm and irrational prejudice; the supported protective connection here supplies a legitimate explanation for the actual difference in treatment.

**Operative limits:** The Court does not authorize forced treatment, displace competent refusal or valid advance directives, permit discriminatory denial of lawful pain care, or punish good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen. A materially different individual claim, including discriminatory administration, remains open on its own facts and legal grounds. No individual entitlement to a lethal prescription or new hearing is awarded. Cruzan's assumption concerning artificial nutrition and hydration is not enlarged into a comprehensive holding on every refusal decision.

## Precedent Treatment

- Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health: applies its bodily-integrity and informed-consent foundation to explain the refusal/assistance distinction. Its assumption of a competent person's constitutional refusal right and its approval of Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes retain their actual scope.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey: preserves protected reproductive choice, individualized viability, timely necessary life-and-health care and the complete six-step standard. That established liberty supplies no general right to intentional death assistance; no component is displaced.
- FCC v. # 1997-06-26 — Washington v. Glucksberg; No. 96-110; October Term 1996, chunk 13.

## Event

## Holdings

##### I. The asserted physician-assistance right is not fundamental

**Holding and operative rule:** The Due Process Clause does not establish a fundamental right for a competent, terminally ill adult to obtain a willing physician's intentional assistance in suicide through a lethal prescription. The asserted class-wide entitlement therefore receives no heightened protection; a materially different, more particularized claim is not foreclosed merely by this disposition.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas. The same five Justices directly support both holdings and their limits. The other four Justices concur in the judgment on the separate grounds identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses the claim actually made: a competent person approaching death seeks willing medical assistance to cause death, without demanding compulsory service or public financing. Its terminal-illness and competence limits matter. Neither an unrestricted wish to die nor a generic appeal to autonomy adequately describes it.

The text and structure of the Due Process Clause, the relevant precedents and the enduring legal treatment of assistance in suicide do not establish this entitlement as a fundamental liberty. The tradition of prohibiting such assistance bears on this claimed right; the Court does not adopt an exclusive rule requiring the most specifically imaginable historical analogue for every constitutional liberty. Cruzan explains refusal of unwanted treatment through bodily integrity, battery and informed consent. It assumed a competent person's right to refuse lifesaving nutrition and hydration and upheld Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. Ending an unwanted intervention does not itself establish permission for another person intentionally to supply lethal means.

Casey retains the protected reproductive decision, individualized viability, timely necessary life-and-health care and its complete six-step regulation standard. Mazurek requires application of that entire standard, including its purpose and residual-interference protections. Those established holdings are not reduced to a generic substantial-obstacle rule or displaced by history alone. They concern a defined reproductive liberty, however, and do not recognize every important medical decision as fundamental. The involvement of a willing professional is not itself dispositive: protected reproductive care also involves assistance. What remains unestablished here is constitutional protection for this distinct intentional lethal act. The refusal and reproductive precedents therefore do not require the asserted extension.

**Limits:** The Court does not decide forced treatment, interference with competent refusal or valid directives, denial of lawful pain care, punishment of good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen, or discriminatory administration. The reserved claim must be materially different from the class entitlement rejected here; the reservation neither recognizes that entitlement nor creates a new pending action.

##### II. Washington's prohibition rationally serves its protective interests

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the asserted assistance entitlement is not fundamental, Washington's prohibition is valid against this substantive-due-process challenge if it is rationally related to legitimate state interests. It meets that standard by protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable people against error and pressure, maintaining medical responsibility and containing the risks of a legalized lethal practice; the Ninth Circuit's class-wide invalidation cannot stand.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies direct majority support. This rationality determination completes the rejection of the claim; the absence of a fundamental right alone is not treated as sufficient to dispose of every constitutional objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The State may protect human life without grading the value of particular lives. Terminal illness does not eliminate the risks of mistaken diagnosis, depression, coercion, family pressure, unequal care or indifference toward vulnerable persons. A physician's involvement does not guarantee a reliable determination that a request is informed, responsible and voluntary. Washington may also preserve the professional duty to care and guard against expansion from the proposed class into voluntary or involuntary euthanasia.

The physicians answer that competent adults near death can choose deliberately and that safeguards could confine the practice. The Court does not find that any named person lacked capacity or that any physician acted improperly. The issue is whether the State can reasonably address risks attending authorization of the practice as a class. Difficulty assessing voluntariness, predicting terminal decline and enforcing the proposed boundary supplies the required connection. Rational review does not require certainty that every risk will occur, a finding that every request is involuntary, or proof that prohibition is the best available policy. Nor must the State adopt a less restrictive scheme solely because one can be proposed.

The legal availability of treatment refusal and good-faith pain relief preserves their distinct functions; the challenged prohibition does not require unwanted intervention or forbid palliation because a shorter life is foreseeable. That permission is not a guarantee that every patient's suffering is relieved. States remain free to authorize and regulate assisted death within applicable constitutional limits. This judgment sustains Washington's chosen protective response to the claim before the Court without imposing a national legislative policy.

**Remedy and scope:** The Ninth Circuit must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted substantive-due-process right to physician-assisted suicide. The remand does not authorize lethal prescriptions, command a new patient-specific hearing or adjudicate a materially different claim merely because it could arise.

# 1997-06-27 — Foreman v. Dallas County; Nos. 96-987 and 96-1389.

## Event

Foreman v. Dallas County, Nos. 96-987 and 96-1389. Summary disposition on June 27, 1997, without oral argument. Direct appeals under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from a three-judge District Court for the Northern District of Texas. No. 96-987 concerns refusal of injunctive relief; No. 96-1389 concerns the subsequent Rule 12(b)(6) final dismissal. Dallas County changed its partisan appointment formulas in 1983 and later years, most recently in 1996. Precinct election judges supervise election-day voting. The state recodification and thirty-page change summary submitted in 1985 identified appointment beginning dates and duration but not the County's particular formulas. ## Holdings

### I. The final judgment supersedes the earlier injunction appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** A qualifying interlocutory refusal of an injunction is appealable under 28 U.S.C. §1253, but the subsequent final dismissal absorbs the same prospective-relief dispute presented here. No. 96-987 is dismissed on that ground; the final judgment remains reviewable in No. 96-1389.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela concurs only in the judgment on No. 96-987 and does not join this rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1253 expressly reaches qualifying interlocutory injunction orders. Nonfinality alone therefore cannot defeat this route. Shaffer v. Carter supplies the merger principle when the later final decree embraces the same injunction controversy. The subsequent dismissal places the prospective-relief dispute before the Court through the final-judgment appeal, and no distinct surviving interlocutory issue is established. This ruling neither treats the earlier request as a temporary restraining order nor imposes a universal finality requirement under §1253.

### II. County discretion does not exempt a changed election-administration practice

**Holding and operative rule:** A covered county's change in the formula for selecting precinct officials who supervise election-day voting is subject to §5 when it differs from the operative coverage-date practice. Authority to exercise discretion under a precleared state statute does not exempt the new local method from preclearance.

**Authority:** Parts II–IV of the per curiam opinion are joined by all nine participating Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Presley requires a direct connection to voting, rather than the mere redistribution of governing authority. Election judges supervise polling; the formula selecting them concerns administration of voting itself. That connection distinguishes the County's methods from an internal allocation of road-government powers. Allen and Hampton County cover practical electoral changes, formal or informal. Monterey and Young establish that administration and discretionary implementation do not escape §5 through an enabling enactment. The contrary theory would make unexamined local choices precleared merely because state law permits them. The Court rejects that exemption without finding that the challenged method actually differs from the November 1, 1972 practice.

### III. The 1985 submission did not preclear undisclosed county formulas

**Holding and operative rule:** Preclearance of the state recodification and its identified changes to appointment timing and duration did not approve the County's partisan-selection formulas, which neither the code nor the explanatory submission identified. A distinct local method is approved only if the submission fairly identifies that method for review; a separate sufficiently specific submission or approval, if established, remains relevant.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition for Parts II–IV controls this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** McCain and Clark require identification of the actual submitted change. Monterey and Young apply that principle to administration under related legislation. The 1985 materials described when election-judge appointments would begin and how long they would last; they did not describe the County's evolving partisan formulas. Receipt of the complete code does not oblige the Attorney General to infer and approve every possible exercise of local discretion. This conclusion neither rescinds approval of the identified recodification nor prevents a jurisdiction from submitting a sufficiently described local implementation with state legislation.

### IV. The absent 1972 baseline requires remand and conditionally governs nonuse

**Holding and operative rule:** The final dismissal must be vacated because the erroneous discretion and submission grounds do not resolve whether the County changed its November 1, 1972 appointment practice. On remand the District Court must identify that practice, compare each challenged later method and determine actual submission status; it must enjoin prospective use of any legally relevant covered change that lacks preclearance.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition for Parts II–IV controls this holding and remedy.

## Event

Pounders v. Watson, No. 96-1383. Certiorari and summary disposition on June 27, 1997, without oral argument, reviewing the Ninth Circuit's December 13, 1996 judgment reported at 102 F.3d 433. ### I. The appellate court unduly restricted the direct-contempt power

**Holding and operative rule:** The narrow power to adjudicate direct contempt summarily is not confined to noisy or physical obstruction, a pattern of violations pervading the trial, or a separate finding that misconduct would recur without immediate punishment. The Ninth Circuit's excessive reliance on the limited duration of Watson's questioning and absence of demonstrated future repetition cannot sustain its summary-process ruling on this record; the Court does not finally resolve her unaddressed notice and willfulness claims.

**Controlling explanation:** Oliver retains the exceptional power for misconduct openly before the judge, with its essential elements actually observed, disturbing the court's business and requiring vindication of judicial authority. Mere observation, irritation or a short sanction is insufficient. Wilson establishes that intentional conduct may obstruct an ongoing trial without noise or violence. Sacher permits deferral of punishment to protect trial continuity and representation; delay alone does not defeat qualifying summary action. McConnell involved announced questioning that was not carried out after a recess, unlike the renewed punishment question here after an immediate admonition.

The trial judge found deliberate defiance and serious jury prejudice. Those are the trial court's findings, not an independently established account of the jurors' minds. At the scope of the process question decided below, the renewed question and the threat to the jury's lawful consideration support correction of the appellate restriction. Protecting that decision process is part of trial administration. The strongest contrary argument is that instructions and ordinary later proceedings could address completed misconduct. But continuation after the contempt finding does not establish that the action already taken was unnecessary; neither Wilson nor Sacher makes a lengthy pattern or immediate imprisonment essential. The Court need not attribute a formally announced categorical rule to the Ninth Circuit to reject its reasoning here. It does not approve summary punishment for every evidentiary violation, eliminate Oliver's limits, or comprehensively redefine the exception.

### II. Former §2254(d) governs the remaining claims without converting constitutional legality into fact

**Holding and operative rule:** Former 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) governs this noncapital habeas application filed before AEDPA. Its conditional protection of qualifying state historical findings does not conclusively determine the constitutional legality of summary procedure, and the Ninth Circuit's express nondecision of notice and willfulness does not amount to accepting those findings.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh supplies the application-date rule for chapter 153. Sumner preserves the former statute's factual presumption, while Thompson distinguishes historical circumstances from an independently reviewed ultimate constitutional question in its setting. Neither decides every contempt issue by label.

The presumption concerns a competent state court's factual determination after a merits hearing involving the applicant and the State or its officer or agent, evidenced by findings, opinion or other reliable adequate writing. The exceptions concern unresolved factual merits; inadequate factfinding procedure; inadequately developed material facts; lack of jurisdiction over subject matter or applicant; unconstitutional failure to appoint counsel for an indigent applicant; absence of a full, fair and adequate hearing; other denial of due process; or a pertinent state record, considered as a whole, failing fairly to support the finding. At a federal evidentiary hearing, after due proof of the state determination, the applicant must establish error by convincing evidence unless one of the first seven circumstances is established, otherwise appears or is admitted, or the federal court finds the eighth.

The trial judge's written findings therefore retain whatever protection the statute gives them. Watson's disagreement does not automatically establish an exception or require a hearing. Conversely, the appellate court left her knowledge of a sufficiently definite order and deliberate disobedience undecided. Those claims cannot disappear through characterization of its silence as assent. A defense depending on facts beyond the judge's observation requires its applicable due-process assessment if that predicate is established. The Court decides no such predicate and makes no finding of judicial bias.

### III. No additional nonretroactivity ground is introduced

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court declines to introduce a Teague defense that is not established as preserved and fairly included in this review record. That choice is not a finding of categorical waiver, a determination of finality or novelty, or a change to either contemporary Teague exception.

**Controlling explanation:** Schiro treats nonretroactivity as a nonjurisdictional defense and permits discretionary refusal of a late presentation; it does not command refusal in every case. Caspari concerns a consistently preserved subsidiary defense limiting the constitutional rule and writ under review. That premise is not established here. Preservation and fair inclusion have not been established; no ruling treats silence as waiver or determines finality. The Court resolves the presented summary-process ground without creating a new collateral bar or adjudicating an unestablished finality date.

### IV. Reversal returns the claims the court of appeals did not decide

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's summary-process ruling and dependent instruction to grant habeas relief are reversed. The case is remanded for consideration of the notice and willfulness claims expressly left unresolved and lawful further disposition under the governing former habeas statute.

**Controlling explanation:** Removing the appellate court's sufficient ground for the writ does not resolve every other claim. Its own opinion reserved notice and deliberate disobedience, and this Court's confined review supplies no substituted merits decision on them. The mandate therefore neither finally validates all aspects of the contempt judgment nor directs final denial of habeas relief. Watson has served the two-day sentence; the mandate orders no repeated confinement, new contempt prosecution, automatic disciplinary result, new evidentiary hearing or particular custody deadline. The reported possible professional consequences preserve a live controversy without establishing a final disciplinary disposition.

## Holdings

##### The officers may challenge the compulsory duties imposed on their offices

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal command requiring these plaintiff officers to perform identified work through their offices presents a concrete injury redressable by relief against that command. Their official status does not bar review, but it supplies no standing to obtain an advisory decision about independent obligations imposed only on absent dealers or purchasers.

**Authority:** The jurisdictional section of Souter's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Raines distinguishes concrete injury from unauthorized legislators' abstract institutional dilution after fully effective votes. These officers instead challenge duties assigned directly to their offices. The Government's disavowal of criminal prosecution does not remove the asserted compulsory civil work. The Court reaches that controversy without inventing an actual prosecution or deciding the independent obligations of persons who are not parties. The grant does not reopen the separate lower-court vagueness and Thirteenth Amendment dispositions.

##### Part I — Appropriate temporary executive records assistance is not categorically forbidden

**Holding and operative rule:** In implementing an otherwise valid direct regulation of private transactions, Congress is not categorically barred from requiring appropriate, temporary records-based assistance within a state executive officer's proper function under an affirmative statutory direction, while federal law supplies the substantive transaction rule and federal authorities retain responsibility for coercive enforcement. That source-and-function rule defeats the asserted categorical incapacity to require the confined assistance presented here, but does not decide whether the actual compulsory arrangement is permissible in light of the officers' unresolved fiscal and unauthorized-expenditure objections.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those five directly adopt this limited rule; their agreement does not extend to unconditional validity of the officer obligations, a right to fair compensation, or the ultimate disposition. The four Justices who reject Part I contribute no vote to it through their agreement with the remand.

**Controlling explanation:** New York forbids compelling a State to legislate for private actors or assume their property and liabilities. It did not decide every directive to an executive officer. Here Congress directly regulates commercial handgun transfers, supplies the transaction rule and expressly directs interim assistance. The executive task concerns receiving prescribed information, consulting available records and making the eligibility assessment those records support; it leaves state policymaking and general coercive enforcement with their respective governments. Garcia and Baker preserve federal regulation affecting state activity, and FERC and Testa show that federal and state responsibilities need not always be performed through wholly separate institutions. Their distinct settings do not themselves decide this case.

The structural objection correctly identifies real work and use of state resources. Part I neither treats that work as free nor makes convenience, low cost or temporary duration a constitutional power. It instead distinguishes the source, function and responsibility assigned. Voting Rights Coalition rests on separate election powers and supplies no general commerce exception. Lopez limits commerce power and forbids inserting a missing statutory element; this transaction regime has its own commercial basis, and the Necessary and Proper Clause implements that established power.

The reasonable-effort term, available-record language, interim context and absence of a command to arrest or prevent a transfer support the confined question decided. The word including does not mean only; the Court makes no categorical textual excision of every conceivable nonrecord act and supplies no independent investigative power. It decides no compulsory field-investigation, permanent state enforcement-organization or general policy-administration command. Nor does the officer's oath independently create a task absent enacted direction. The remaining fiscal objection concerns whether this actual arrangement may finally be sustained, and is genuinely left open.

**Statutory scope and conditions:** Section 922(s)(2) directs reasonable effort within five business days after the specified dealer notice, including research in available state and local systems and the designated national system. It does not require an arrest, prosecution, prevention of a transfer or adverse dealer notification. Section (6)(B) concerns a transmitted purchaser statement: unless the officer determines the transaction unlawful, the statement, derived-information records and notice-created records must be destroyed within twenty business days after the purchaser made the statement; disclosure is limited to a person needing the information to carry out the subsection, and use is limited to that purpose. The same unlawful-transaction exception qualifies those information restrictions. Section (6)(C) separately requires written reasons only after an ineligibility determination and the individual's request, within twenty business days after receipt of that request. No transaction-specific trigger is found satisfied or unsatisfied here.

**Limits:** The holding preserves New York's prohibition, the distinct sources for state-court and election duties, valid preemption, generally applicable regulation, voluntary cooperation and lawful spending arrangements. It establishes no general cheap-command exception, permanent compulsory records power, state-law veto of valid federal law or constitutional payment entitlement. No categorical holding validates or invalidates every ancillary information duty or independent dealer obligation.

No substantive entitlement to compensation, compensation cause of action, measure of fair value, state-law liability or taking is decided.

# 1997-06-30 — In re Cohen; Third Circuit No. 96-5155; no separately supplied Supreme Court docket.

## Event

In re Cohen. Argued and decided June 30, 1997. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to review the Third Circuit's February 6, 1997 judgment, 106 F.3d 52, lower appellate No. 96-5155. The questions are whether §523(a)(2)(A) limits nondischargeability to the money acquired by fraud or includes the full legally established liability, and whether the existing treble damages, fees and costs fall within that liability.

Cohen co-owned a rent-controlled Hoboken apartment building. The administrator ordered $31,382.50 in refunds; the owners neither paid nor perfected an administrative appeal and sought Chapter 7 relief. The tenants' adversary proceeding produced federal fraud findings and a $94,147.50 treble judgment, with attorney's fees and costs already awarded. The courts below sustained the fraud determination and discharge treatment. This review concerns that existing liability, not a new damages assessment.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. No Justice is excluded.

## Public Action

The Court unanimously affirms the Third Circuit's judgment sustaining nondischargeability of the existing qualifying fraud liability, including the treble judgment and attributable fees and costs.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The existing $94,147.50 treble judgment and the existing attributable attorney's-fee and cost award remain nondischargeable. No Justice opposes the judgment. No new award, recalculation or additional hearing is ordered.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Thomas delivers the opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Each joins every controlling proposition and the affirmance. No separate writing issues.

## Holdings

### I. Federal fraud remains a necessary predicate

**Holding and operative rule:** On the actual-fraud theory presented under 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A), a qualifying acquisition requires actual and justifiable reliance proved by a preponderance of the evidence. The sustained federal fraud findings permit decision of this debt's extent; a State consumer-fraud label or treble award alone would not establish that predicate.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common nine-Justice coalition controls all four holdings and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 523(a)(2) addresses debts for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by its specified conduct. Subparagraph (A) covers false pretenses, false representation or actual fraud, but excludes statements respecting the debtor's or an insider's financial condition. Subparagraph (B) separately requires a materially false writing respecting that financial condition, reasonable reliance, and the debtor's causing its making or publication with intent to deceive. This is an actual-fraud acquisition of rents, not a financial-statement case or a decision of every representation theory.

Field v. Mans supplies actual and individualized justifiable reliance; Grogan supplies the preponderance burden. The recipient's knowledge, capacities and circumstances matter. There is no universal duty to investigate the ordinance. A recipient cannot rely on falsity personally known or obvious to that recipient; an actually encountered warning may require inquiry under that recipient-specific standard. Objective imprudence may bear on whether reliance actually occurred, without becoming a prudent-person test. Limited tenant knowledge of the ordinance therefore does not itself defeat reliance. The bankruptcy findings, sustained below, include Cohen's selective knowledge of favorable provisions and deliberate avoidance of rent limits. The Court makes no new findings and identifies no supported material federal-fraud error requiring remand. A material preserved defect would receive its ordinary disposition under Field; the possibility of additional facts supplies no basis for an exploratory proceeding.

### II. The exception covers valid liability for the fraudulent acquisition

**Holding and operative rule:** Once money or property is obtained through the fraud covered by §523(a)(2)(A), the nondischargeable debt includes the legally enforceable liability arising from that acquisition, not merely the value the debtor received. A qualifying acquisition and a legal connection between it and the liability remain necessary; lawful receipts and unrelated debts are outside the rule.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part II in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The Code defines debt as liability on a claim. The acquisition language identifies which receipts give rise to the excepted liability; it does not redefine that liability as restitution alone. The overcharged rents supply the qualifying acquisition here, while lawfully received rents do not. State law establishes enforceable liability, and federal law independently determines its discharge treatment.

Cohen's strongest reading accepts that the modifier attaches to the acquired money or property but argues that unreceived enhancements cannot represent what was obtained. That reading gives the acquisition words a role but too little work to debt. Liability can arise from the qualifying acquisition without every remedial consequence being another acquisition. Sections 523(a)(4) and (6) address distinct wrongs; their different language does not impose a restitution cap on (a)(2)(A) after its acquisition condition is met. Their independent elements are not decided. Neither the placement of the acquisition phrase nor a general fresh-start policy replaces the Code's liability definition. Text and structure suffice; legislative history is not essential to the result.

Field expressly left debt extent and the obtained-by relationship open. Noland's adherence to statutory categories supports disciplined construction, but its priority and equitable-subordination holding does not resolve this discharge question. The Court creates no State cause of action or federal damages remedy and does not except every obligation of a dishonest debtor.

### III. The existing treble judgment is nondischargeable even assuming a wholly punitive enhancement

**Holding and operative rule:** The existing $94,147.50 treble judgment for $31,382.50 in fraudulently acquired rent is nondischargeable in full. Even if the enhancement is assumed wholly punitive, that character does not remove valid liability for this qualifying acquisition from §523(a)(2)(A).

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part III in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The enhancement is an enforceable consequence of the same fraudulent receipts. A court cannot add a general equitable exception for punitive consequences to the category Congress enacted. Noland reinforces respect for statutory categories, without making every penalty nondischargeable. An unrelated penalty, or an obligation outside this qualifying acquisition, remains outside this holding. The Court does not decide that New Jersey's enhancement is wholly punitive: the lower court assumed that characterization, while Cox v. Sears recognized a partly compensatory purpose. The federal result is the same under the assumption, so settling that State-law question is unnecessary. The $94,147.50 figure is the existing total, not a new addition to the $31,382.50 acquisition.

### IV. Existing attributable fees and costs share the debt's treatment; no new award is made

**Holding and operative rule:** Valid State-law attorney's fees and costs imposed because of, and forming part of liability for, this qualifying fraudulent acquisition are also nondischargeable. The Court affirms the existing connected fee-and-cost award with the treble judgment, without adding an amount, awarding new relief or directing further factfinding.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part IV and the judgment in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The same liability construction governs the existing fees and costs attributable to establishing or recovering this debt. A valid underlying entitlement and the fraud connection are necessary; federal discharge law supplies neither a new fee entitlement nor coverage for unrelated claims. No separate fee-and-cost amount is specified here, and none is calculated. The decision awards no Supreme Court fees, new appellate fees, future costs, interest or enlarged enhancement. Ordinary enforcement remains in the proper proceedings. The Court sets no distribution priority, collection terms or payment schedule, assesses no liability against another person and orders no recalculation or additional hearing.

## Precedent Treatment

- Field v. Mans preserves actual and justifiable reliance for the actual-fraud theory and its individualized limits. Its express reservation of debt extent is resolved here for this qualifying acquisition; its distinct financial-condition rules remain intact.
- Grogan v. Garner supplies the preponderance burden for the federal discharge exception.
- United States v. Noland reinforces adherence to the Code's enacted categories. Its priority and equitable-subordination rule is not extended into a direct holding on fraud discharge or every punitive debt.
- Cox v. Sears identifies a partly compensatory purpose in New Jersey's treble remedy; the Court leaves the exact State-law characterization undecided because even the wholly punitive assumption does not change the result. No earlier decision is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Once satisfied, §523(a)(2)(A) reaches valid liability for that acquisition, including its enforceable enhancement and attributable fees and costs. It is not an exception for every debt associated with dishonesty or every penalty. State law supplies the existing liability; the Code determines its discharge status.

## Procedure After Action

## Event

United States v. Mussari; United States v. Schroeder, consolidated review of Ninth Circuit Nos. 95-10479 and 95-10513; district cases CR-95-00009-PGR and CR-95-00010-1-PGR. Argued May 27, 1997; decided June 30, 1997. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to review the Ninth Circuit's September 5, 1996 judgment, 95 F.3d 787, reversing each separate indictment dismissal and remanding both prosecutions. The questions concern Congress's commerce power over the interstate support-payment obligation, the Tenth Amendment and domestic-relations objections, and the temporal limit necessary to lawful further proceedings.

Both indictments were returned January 11, 1995. Mussari's concerns support for two Arizona children and alleges nonpayment beginning November 1, 1988; Schroeder's concerns four Arizona children and alleges nonpayment beginning February 1, 1992. Both allege different-state residence and obligations exceeding $5,000 and unpaid longer than one year. Those are allegations, not findings of the offense. Each defendant seeks restoration of his dismissal; the United States seeks to preserve both trial remands.

## Public Action

## Judgment & Remedy

**Supporting both components:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No Justice opposes either disposition.

## Opinion Topology

Justice O'Connor delivers the opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Each joins every controlling proposition and both disposition components. No separate writing issues.

## Holdings

### I. Congress may protect the legally fixed interstate payment obligation

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may punish willful post-enactment failure to pay a legally fixed past-due child-support obligation when the obligor and child reside in different States, because §228 directly protects that cross-border economic payment obligation. The different-state relation is essential to this holding; the Court sustains neither regulation of every family debt nor federal creation or revision of the underlying support duty.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common nine-Justice coalition controls every holding and the affirmance in both prosecutions.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 228(a) requires willful failure to pay a past-due support obligation for a child residing in another State. Section 228(d)(1) requires an amount fixed by a court order or State-law administrative process for support of a child, or a child and custodial parent, unpaid longer than one year or exceeding $5,000. Those thresholds are alternatives; the indictments happen to allege both. Neither the amount nor duration substitutes for the interstate relation.

The object regulated is the required cross-border payment itself. Different residence does constitutional work because the existing obligation calls for satisfaction between the obligor and children in another State. That is a legal characterization of the charged class, not a finding of actual transfer. A completed payment need not precede an offense addressing its willful withholding. The State-law origin and absence of a negotiated bargain do not erase the obligation's economic payment character.

The strongest objection treats the default as a local family failure with a geographical label. Lopez rejects deriving federal power from remote chains through poverty, welfare expense, crime or productivity. The Court relies on none. Harris and Bishop/Stokes protect the covered vehicle as an interstate instrumentality against direct forcible taking and require proof of the enacted vehicle element; Hale addresses possession integral to direct market regulation. They require attention to the actual object and relation, not an automatic commerce label for every debt or object with interstate history. The present due cross-border payment supplies that relation independently.

Lopez also forbids rewriting a statute to supply a missing element. No flight, travel, voluntary State crossing, bank wire, particular instrument or motive to evade is added here. The Court decides no statute lacking the different-state condition and no unrelated obligation bearing only an incidental geographical feature. It makes no finding of deliberate evasion or ability to pay.

### II. Direct regulation of these private obligors neither commandeers a State nor issues a domestic-relations decree

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 228's direct regulation of the private obligor does not compel State administration, and the limited domestic-relations exception does not bar these federal prosecutions. The existing operative support order supplies a predicate; federal courts may apply the offense's elements and lawful defenses but may not create or modify that obligation, custody, parentage or domestic status.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part II in full.

**Controlling explanation:** New York v. United States distinguishes valid direct private regulation from compelled State responsibility. Section 228 commands no State legislature, court or executive officer to legislate, enforce the debt, investigate for federal officials, alter an order or assume the obligor's liability. Printz's qualified executive-assistance rule concerns a different means of implementation. This prosecution requires no approval of compulsory State assistance and presents no analogous fiscal question. The absence of commandeering alone supplies no commerce power; Part I independently supplies it.

Ankenbrandt v. Richards confines the statutory diversity exception to issuance of divorce, alimony and child-custody decrees, rather than imposing a general Article III exclusion for all matters involving families. This federal criminal proceeding issues no such decree. The State-law origin of the debt therefore neither defeats the established federal power nor transfers family-law authority to the federal tribunal. The Court does not recalculate support, determine a modification or prescribe State enforcement machinery. A cognizable challenge to whether the predicate order exists and remains operative is not foreclosed. Hypothetical interference with a different State proceeding is not decided.

### III. Both prosecutions remain confined to post-enactment failures and proof of every element

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal conviction and punishment must rest on willful failures after §228's October 25, 1992 enactment, with every applicable element proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Earlier arrears may establish lawful background or past-due status, but a completed pre-enactment failure cannot itself constitute the federal offense.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part III in full.

**Controlling explanation:** Mussari's alleged period starts November 1, 1988, and Schroeder's February 1, 1992. Neither indictment's earlier starting date enlarges the enacted offense. The Ex Post Facto Clause independently prohibits punishment for conduct completed before enactment. Landgraf and Rivers reinforce attention to the legal consequences attached to completed conduct; their distinct civil approaches do not replace that criminal prohibition or require agreement on every earlier temporal rationale.

The constitutional ruling establishes neither defendant's willfulness, capacity to pay, qualifying fixed amount, required unpaid duration, different-state residence for the necessary period, or any sentencing fact. Ability-related defenses recognized by law and other lawful defenses remain available. Each existing prosecution returns for ordinary proceedings within its charges and applicable statutory, temporal and procedural limits. No new allegation, amended indictment, offense period, conviction or special trial instruction beyond this legal boundary is directed. The Court adopts no comprehensive continuing-offense theory to eliminate a separate proof or notice question.

### IV. Each appellate trial remand stands; any restitution remains a separate conditional issue

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment reversing each district-court indictment dismissal and remanding each prosecution is affirmed, subject to the post-enactment limit and ordinary proof requirements. Section 228(c) requires restitution under §3663 upon conviction equal to the past-due obligation existing at sentencing; this pretrial decision fixes no amount and does not decide the precise lawful treatment of older arrears in such an order.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part IV and both disposition components in full.

**Controlling explanation:** Sustaining the presented constitutional basis for prosecution does not decide guilt or sentencing. The statutory first-offense exposure is a fine, imprisonment up to six months, or both; a subsequent offense permits a fine, imprisonment up to two years, or both. No prior offense or sentence is found here. Restitution's separate command is conditional on conviction. The rule against punishing a completed pre-enactment omission does not automatically exclude every older arrear from a later restitution calculation; nor does the sentencing-balance language automatically approve inclusion without the separate statutory and constitutional inquiry. Both questions remain for any proceeding in which they actually arise. Neither dismissal is restored. The Court orders no detention, conviction, restitution award, compensation proceeding, State-order revision or compulsory State administrative act.

## Precedent Treatment
