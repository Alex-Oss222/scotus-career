### Pinder v. Johnson

Merits decision, May 21, 1996

October Term 1995. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Fourth Circuit in No. 93-2125, reviewing its May 30, 1995 en banc judgment, 54 F.3d 1169. The question is Officer Johnson's individual qualified immunity on the substantive-due-process claim arising from his alleged conduct and assurances before Pittman's attack.

On March 10, 1989, Johnson responded after Pittman broke into Pinder's home, assaulted her, threw objects and threatened her and her children. Johnson was told of prior threats and an earlier attempt to burn the house. After arresting Pittman, Johnson assured Pinder that Pittman would remain detained overnight. Pinder alleges that this induced her to return to work instead of staying with or arranging protection for her children. Johnson charged trespass and malicious destruction of property under $300. Commissioner Ames released Pittman that evening; Pittman returned, set the house on fire and killed Pinder's three children. No warning of release is alleged.

The district court denied dismissal and immunity. A divided panel affirmed, but its opinion was vacated on rehearing. The en banc court granted immunity, 9–4 in judgment. Its principal opinion classified the account as nonprotection and found inadequate notice while recognizing a different affirmative-danger paradigm; the supporting judges did not all adopt the same reasoning. The competing view treated the assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection as affirmative causation. Review concerns those legal characterizations on proper favorable assumptions, not a finding that Pinder proved the alleged causal chain.

All nine Justices participated in the decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

**Opposing:** Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would vacate the reviewed immunity disposition and remand on a bounded affirmative-danger theory.

Johnson receives judgment on the individual damages claim within this immunity issue. The Court does not decide that the assumed affirmative conduct was constitutional or that custody is indispensable to every possible affirmative-deprivation claim.

#### Controlling holdings

##### I. Review reaches the legal immunity issue on favorable assumptions

**Holding and authority.** Part I, joined by all nine Justices, holds that §1254(1) review and the underlying §1291 legal-immunity appeal permit determination of the separable legal question on the properly assumed favorable account. They do not permit deciding factual sufficiency, selecting disputed intent or causation, or adjudicating the separate excluded claims.

**Controlling explanation.** *Mitchell* permits appeal of the separable legal immunity issue. *Johnson v. Jones* and *Kimberlin* distinguish that question from whether the plaintiff can prove the facts. *Doe* and *Behrens* preserve the same boundary: an official may obtain the legal consequences of the favorable account, but cannot obtain appellate findings that the alleged warnings, contribution or causal effect did not occur.

The district court expressly used Rule 12(b)(6); the appellate opinions describe summary judgment. Neither description authorizes an adverse factual determination here. The asserted assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection can be assumed for legal review without deciding credibility, knowing falsity or whether that withdrawal actually caused the deaths. No second pretrial immunity appeal is established, and no ruling on successive-appeal conditions is necessary.

Official-capacity or municipal claims, equal protection, §1985 and state tort counts do not enter this appeal merely because they concern the same events. *Behrens* preserves their distinct posture. No new procedural-due-process claim is supplied. The legal question can therefore be decided without resolving factual disputes or extending review to every claim.

##### II. Private nonprotection, affirmative deprivation and historical notice remain distinct

**Holding and authority.** Part II of the same unanimous opinion holds that knowledge of danger, promises of protection and custody of Pittman create no custodial special relationship with Pinder or her children. The alleged affirmative loss of otherwise available private protection must be considered separately from a failure to supply public protection. Individual immunity requires particularized objective notice from all relevant preconduct law, without an identical-case requirement. Neither judicial disagreement nor the official's subjective belief in legality independently decides that inquiry.

**Controlling explanation.** *DeShaney* generally rejects a due-process duty to protect against private violence. Its custodial rule arises from state restraint of the injured person's ability to act, not knowledge, assistance or a promise. It also emphasized that the State had neither created the danger nor increased Joshua's vulnerability; it did not announce a complete test for every affirmative-danger claim. Johnson's alleged alteration of available private protection therefore cannot be discarded simply by identifying Pittman as the assailant.

That distinction does not establish liability. Section 1983 supplies a remedy for an attributable federal deprivation, not a general constitutional duty to perform state protective functions. *Daniels* excludes merely negligent injury. Culpability must concern the deprivation, not merely the intentional utterance of an assurance; its reservation concerning recklessness and gross negligence does not establish that any nonnegligent reassurance is unconstitutional.

*Anderson* requires unlawfulness sufficiently apparent in pre-existing law at the appropriate level of specificity. The exact act need not already have been held unlawful. *Elder* requires the entire relevant preconduct landscape, including adverse decisions and their limits. A subjective element of the underlying claim differs from the official's belief about legality; *Kimberlin* permits competent circumstantial proof without inventing a substantive mental-state requirement.

March 10, 1989 fixes notice. *Collins*, *Doe*, *Hodge*, *Love* and *Sheets* inform present analysis but cannot supply notice retroactively. *Love* permits a dispositive notice inquiry without mandating either merits-first or immunity-first sequencing. These common principles do not decide whether the affirmative-danger theory succeeds on its constitutional merits or whether its particular wrong was sufficiently established then.

##### III. Contemporaneous law did not sufficiently establish this particular obligation

**Holding and authority.** Part III, adopted by the five Justices joining that part, holds that Johnson has individual qualified immunity on the reviewed claim. Even accepting the alleged assurance-induced withdrawal of private protection for legal review, pre-March 10, 1989 authorities did not make sufficiently apparent that this noncoercive undertaking, ensuing release and uncorrected exposure constituted his own constitutional deprivation. The Court reserves the distinct affirmative-danger merits.

**Controlling explanation.** The alleged loss of private protection is consequential and more specific than a general failure to prevent crime. The question remains whether existing law sufficiently identified the combination of assurances, charging decisions, third-party release and reliance as this officer's constitutional wrong. *DeShaney* had rejected knowledge-and-promise protective duties sixteen days earlier, leaving affirmative increases in danger incompletely defined.

*Bowers* distinguished an active state tortfeasor but rejected liability for releasing a dangerous person. *Archie* discussed increased risk and interference with private aid, yet found neither culpable deprivation nor state creation of danger or hindrance of aid. Its discussion of intentional deprivation and extreme indifference to known mortal risk cannot be detached from those limits. *Jensen*'s distinct danger discussion remains relevant, but its broader knowledge-and-promise relationship ground cannot survive *DeShaney*. The adverse outcomes do not erase these cases' reasoning; neither does general recognition of affirmative injury settle this noncoercive reliance mechanism.

The contrary argument identifies the qualifying wrong as culpable displacement of otherwise available protection against a known immediate mortal danger. Even on that favorable legal account, the extension from earlier affirmative-injury and private-aid principles to this undertaking was not sufficiently apparent. The uncertainty concerns constitutional attribution, not a requirement that an earlier case reproduce every fact or award a plaintiff relief. Judicial disagreement is not itself the test.

As *Hodge* and *Doe*'s Caplinger holding illustrate in their own settings, an individual notice judgment can leave constitutional sufficiency unresolved. Those later decisions provide no retrospective notice here. The conclusion rests neither on finding negligence nor on rejecting alleged reliance. Greater charges are not established to have ensured detention, and unlawful preventive confinement is not the prescribed solution. No freestanding warning entitlement, endorsement of affirmative injury, general custody prerequisite or new actual-reliance immunity defense is adopted.

#### Precedent treatment

- *Mitchell v. Forsyth*, 472 U.S. 511, supplies the separable legal-immunity collateral-order channel. It does not bring factual sufficiency or all related claims into review.
- *Johnson v. Jones* and *Kimberlin v. Quinlan*, June 12, 1995, supply the legal/factual appellate boundary. *Kimberlin* preserves circumstantial proof subject to actual constitutional elements and ordinary burdens.
- *DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189, limits private-protection and custodial duties while preserving the distinction concerning state-created or increased vulnerability. Its foster-care, culpability, individual-immunity and municipal-responsibility reservations remain unresolved.
- *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, excludes mere negligence. Its reservation concerning recklessness and gross negligence remains open.
- *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, requires particularized apparent unlawfulness without an identical prior case.
- *Elder v. Holloway*, February 23, 1994, requires independent review of all relevant preconduct authority on the preserved question. It supplies no retrospective notice or mandatory merits-first sequence.
- *Collins v. Harker Heights*, February 26, 1992, preserves the distinction between a federal deprivation and state duties or municipal attribution. Its reservation of a different affirmative-danger claim remains.
- *Doe v. Taylor Independent School District*, October 16, 1995, supplies the review boundary and preserves its distinct personal-causation and Caplinger notice holdings. Official-authority abuse is not treated as the same as this private attack.
- *Hodge v. Jones*, October 23, 1995, supplies the method of individualized notice adjudication with constitutional merits reserved. Its confidential-record claim does not become a danger-creation rule.
- *Love v. Pepersack*, December 11, 1995, preserves lawful sequencing and the distinction between state illegality and a federal wrong. No mandatory order of decision or general merits test is inferred.
- *Behrens v. Pelletier*, February 21, 1996, supplies favorable legal-review assumptions and separate-claim limits. No second-appeal premise is supplied.
- *Sheets v. Salt Lake County*, April 29, 1996, retains its confined affirmative-disclosure rule. It neither defines this danger theory nor supplies March 1989 notice.

*Bowers v. DeVito*, *Archie v. City of Racine* and *Jensen v. Conrad* are considered as preconduct circuit authority with their adverse outcomes and limited affirmative-danger discussions intact. They are neither treated as Supreme Court holdings nor overruled. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled; *Albright*'s separate positions do not become a general bodily-integrity bar or state-tort cure.

#### Law after the decision

Effective May 21, 1996, Johnson is individually immune on the reviewed due-process claim because the particular constitutional attribution was insufficiently established on March 10, 1989. The ruling applies existing objective notice law. It creates no identical-facts prerequisite and adopts no defense requiring actual reliance on authoritative law.

The outer substantive boundary of affirmative state-created danger remains unresolved. A private assailant does not categorically exclude an attributable affirmative official deprivation, but neither promises, serious injury nor intentionally speaking establishes one. The decision announces no minimum culpability standard for every lesser account, general warning duty, mandatory detention rule or expanded remedial entitlement. Constitutional merits, personal causation, historical notice and appellate authority retain their separate functions.

#### Dissent

Justice Ginsburg's dissent would vacate the reviewed immunity disposition and remand. Injured-party custody is not indispensable where an official's own sufficiently culpable conduct actually removes otherwise available protection and thereby exposes the victims to a known immediate mortal danger. The dissent concludes now that pre-March 10, 1989 law sufficiently identified the qualifying form of this alleged deprivation to defeat immunity disposing of the entire theory at this stage.

*Bowers*'s active-tortfeasor distinction, *Archie*'s private-aid reasoning and *Jensen*'s distinct danger discussion, read with *DeShaney*'s no-increased-vulnerability limit, supply that particular principle. Their adverse outcomes and culpability restrictions remain material. Purposefully giving reassurance alone is insufficient: culpability must attach to the deprivation, and actual causal displacement must be shown. The dissent neither restores a knowledge-and-promise special relationship nor infers notice from the deaths' severity. It disagrees with the en banc application to this account, not with an invented universal custody rule.

The favorable pleading possibility includes intentional or conscience-shocking deprivation without establishing that mental state. The dissent selects no minimum standard for every lesser account, finds no knowing falsity or favoritism, and does not assume greater charges would have prevented release. A later factual finding cannot create historical legal notice. Materially different or less culpable conduct retains its own constitutional and immunity analysis.

The requested remand would proceed through the Court of Appeals under ordinary preservation, pleading and proof rules, addressing culpability, actual causal predicates and genuinely available defenses only as needed. It would not unconditionally restore the entire district order or vacated panel opinion, mandate discovery, a hearing or trial, award damages, or adjudicate excluded counts. This bounded merits-and-notice position is noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, remedy, and stage

The Fourth Circuit's judgment granting Johnson individual qualified immunity on the reviewed substantive-due-process claim is affirmed. The receiving courts must give effect to his judgment within that issue. No new merits or damages proceeding on the reviewed claim, liability finding or compensation award is ordered.

Other defendants and legally distinct preserved claims retain their proper disposition. Official-capacity or municipal liability, equal protection, §1985, Maryland wrongful-death and survival counts remain outside this disposition. No unpresented procedural claim is added. Supreme Court merits review is complete, subject to ordinary mandate procedures; no formal mandate date or additional Supreme Court event is prescribed.

### California Department of Corrections v. Morales, No. 93-1462

Merits decision, April 25, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 9, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing Morales v. California Department of Corrections, 16 F.3d 1001 (February 9, 1994), which reversed denial of his ex post facto habeas claim and required offense-date parole-consideration frequency. The question is whether the 1981 California provision permitting a reasoned postponement of annual reconsideration for at most three years may apply to his 1980 offense. The State seeks reversal; Morales seeks preservation of that consideration-only relief.

At the time of the offense, reconsideration after denial was annual. The 1981 California Penal Code §3041.5(b)(2) amendment retains that default, but permits the next hearing no later than three years after denial if the prisoner has been convicted, in the same or different proceedings, of more than one offense involving the taking of a life, and the Board finds that parole cannot reasonably be expected at a hearing during the intervening years and states its bases. Postponement is permitted, not mandatory. In re Jackson requires reasons separately directed to postponement and permits supporting facts to overlap the reasons for present unsuitability; it establishes review of that initial choice, not a generally available later reopening procedure. Morales's initial hearing occurred in 1989, and his earliest eligibility was August 2, 1990. The Board denied parole and separately deferred reconsideration for three years. Its interval findings addressed the grave offense, prior murder, criminal history, and need for longer observation, evaluation, or treatment. They were predictions, not findings that earlier release was legally impossible.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit’s judgment on Morales’s ex post facto hearing-frequency claim, 5–4. Relief requiring consideration at offense-date annual frequency remains in force. The Board retains discretion to deny parole under lawful suitability standards.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Retrospective application of the 1981 hearing-deferral provision to Morales | Affirm the Ninth Circuit’s ex post facto hearing-frequency judgment, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Breyer | Preserve the Ninth Circuit’s remand for offense-date annual consideration; no release order. |

The previously rejected false-information and sentence claims remain undisturbed and outside this review; they receive no new merits vote. The four dissenters would reverse the hearing-frequency judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with rejecting that claim. None would grant parole.

Each part has five votes. |

| Dissenting opinion | Kennedy | Scalia, Thomas, Breyer throughout | Would reverse the hearing-frequency judgment | The confined individualized-prediction distinction from Cavanaugh; no reliance on unproved reopening or a guaranteed release lag. |

An Opinion of the Court controls the two propositions below. The concurrence’s broader substantive formulation is not adopted by the Court. No fractured-decision or Marks inquiry is needed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The initial prediction does not preserve the meaningful later opportunity lost here

**Controlling proposition:** Applying the 1981 California hearing-deferral provision to Morales’s 1980 offense violates the Ex Post Facto Clause because the actual three-year deferral withdraws offense-date mandatory annual consideration during a period when parole is legally available, without an established ordinary substitute for the meaningful intervening opportunities. The separately reasoned prediction of continued unsuitability and review of that initial prediction do not preserve consideration of later rehabilitation, changed circumstances, or a materially mistaken forecast; relief restores consideration, not release.

**Authority:** Part II of O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg; five Justices adopt the same rule, application and relief.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prediction about a future hearing does not supply the meaningful occasion removed on this record. Cavanaugh protects a mandatory avenue to earlier release without proof that parole would have followed, while leaving individualized postponements open. California provides genuine safeguards: annual review remains the default, only prisoners with multiple life-taking convictions qualify, postponement is permissive, reasons must address the intervening years, and the interval cannot exceed three years. Jackson permits review of that initial choice and factual overlap between its reasons and the suitability reasons. Those protections constrain the forecast; they do not hear later developments. Morales became eligible during the postponed interval, and the flexible matrix does not foreclose earlier release as a matter of law. Weaver protects meaningful opportunities to shorten custody; Miller rejects unchanged outer limits as an answer to binding adverse operation. Collins confines the inquiry to punishment. The Court rejects both a categorical procedural exemption and the Ninth Circuit’s broader suggestion that every reduction in frequency suffices. Materially different individualized arrangements remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993: applies its opportunity rule to this reserved individualized setting; its original holding and rejection of proof of the omitted hearing’s outcome remain in force.
- Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981): applies protection of a legally available avenue to reduced custody and the distinction between guaranteed benefits and discretionary alternatives; it supplies no right to parole.
- Miller v. Florida, 482 U.S. 423 (1987): applies scrutiny of binding adverse operation despite unchanged outer limits and a possibly identical result.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990): preserves the punishment boundary; no free-standing rule against every retrospective procedural disadvantage is adopted.
- In re Jackson, 39 Cal.3d 464 (1985): accepts its separate-reasons construction, permitted factual overlap and initial review; its state-court federal characterization does not displace Cavanaugh or establish ordinary later reopening.

##### The claimant proves meaningful loss; the State substantiates a substitute it invokes

**Controlling proposition:** A prisoner challenging retrospective withdrawal of a parole-consideration opportunity must show the earlier legal entitlement, its retrospective actual withdrawal, and a concrete connection to a legally available avenue to earlier release; inconvenience alone is insufficient, and proof that the omitted hearing would actually have produced parole is unnecessary. After that showing, the State must substantiate any replacement mechanism it invokes by identifying its legal basis and establishing its practical availability and timely capacity to provide the relevant consideration; the prisoner retains the ultimate burden of establishing an unconstitutional increase in punishment.

**Authority:** Part III of O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg; five Justices expressly adopt this bounded evidentiary direction and its application. Recognition of a source limitation in the dissent is not an additional join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court requires proof of the meaningful loss before examining an asserted substitute. Morales establishes an annual statutory entitlement at the offense date, its removal by the later deferral, and its connection to discretionary release after eligibility. Cavanaugh does not require him to prove the result of a hearing withheld by law. The State may substantiate its proposed replacement, but must identify a legal basis, a usable means of presenting relevant developments, a responsible decision process, and timely capacity for consideration while earlier release remains possible. The reply’s assertion about reviewing inmate communications and Jackson’s hypothetical extraordinary advancement establish no such ordinary process here. An initial appeal addresses the earlier prediction, not later suitability. Weaver explains why discretionary opportunities cannot be assumed to compensate for guaranteed benefits. Requiring support for the State’s own answer neither transfers ultimate constitutional proof nor demands proof that parole is impossible. The Court prescribes no universal filing procedure or response deadline and does not decide whether a demonstrated different mechanism would suffice.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cavanaugh v. Roller, November 30, 1993: preserves meaningful opportunity loss and the absence of an actual-release prerequisite; this decision expressly clarifies substantiation of an asserted replacement.
- Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981): applies its refusal to assume discretionary alternatives compensate for guaranteed benefits; it did not itself prescribe this parole evidentiary direction.
- In re Jackson, 39 Cal.3d 464 (1985): confines review and conceivable advancement to what the opinion establishes; initial review does not become later reconsideration.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The holding concerns Morales’s demonstrated meaningful loss under the challenged 1981 provision. It does not invalidate every individualized postponement, require an interim hearing in every possible individualized scheme, or constitutionalize every scheduling detail. Maximum deferral is permission, not a statutory command or a proved near-uniform cohort practice. The Court neither requires wholly different facts for the two decisions nor treats the initial forecast as unreviewable.

No universal probability threshold, general presumption transferring constitutional proof to the State, numerical response deadline, mandatory filing procedure, suitability finding, release date, damages award, or right to favorable parole is adopted. Adequacy of a demonstrated materially different arrangement, later amendments and their transition or mootness effects are not decided. The false-information and sentence claims are not reopened. Unresolved credit and matrix calculations establish neither release entitlement nor legal impossibility of earlier release.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Cavanaugh remains controlling and its opportunity rule extends to the meaningful withdrawal established here, notwithstanding the genuine individual-prediction constraints. Its reservation is resolved for this record, not erased for every individualized arrangement. Weaver’s protection of legally available opportunities and Miller’s focus on binding adverse operation remain intact; Collins excludes a general procedural-disadvantage rule. Jackson’s separate-reasons construction and initial review are recognized, but its federal characterization cannot override this Court’s intervening law. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

Effective April 25, 1995, California may not impose the challenged three-year interval on Morales on this record. His offense-date annual-consideration relief remains operative, with lawful suitability standards and release discretion preserved. Cavanaugh is unchanged within its original scope; this decision adds its application to the reserved individualized setting established here and the express claimant/substitute substantiation direction. It rejects an automatic rule against every frequency reduction and adopts neither the concurrence’s complete enforceable-substitute formulation for other systems nor the dissent’s view that the prediction suffices here.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela fully joins the Court’s opinion and the opportunity-protecting position joined by Justice Stevens. He adds that when a State removes a legally guaranteed meaningful avenue to earlier release, a replacement must preserve the opportunity in substance. It must be legally available, practically accessible, timely enough to permit consideration before the postponed hearing while earlier release remains legally possible, and enforceable as a usable protection. Discretionary authority to advance a hearing does not, by itself, establish an adequate substitute. Weaver’s treatment of discretionary credits supports distinguishing an enforceable opportunity from officials’ abstract power to act. Lindsey and Miller explain why a possibly identical punishment under former law does not defeat the claim; Cavanaugh supplies the direct opportunity rule.

The prisoner must identify the former entitlement, its retrospective withdrawal and its concrete link to possible earlier release. The State must then substantiate any replacement it relies on, including legal basis, practical availability and timely capacity; theoretical authority requires evidence where operation matters. Uncertainty about that answer cannot justify denying otherwise established relief. Ultimate constitutional proof remains with the prisoner. He accepts the annual default, narrow class, permissive three-year maximum, separate findings, lawful factual overlap and initial review, but finds they do not preserve later rehabilitation, changed circumstances or correction of a materially mistaken forecast. A different arrangement may suffice if it actually preserves the opportunity through an adequate enforceable substitute; no hypothetical system, universal filing procedure or numerical deadline is approved. His full joinder remains intact; the broader formulation has no additional joins and is noncontrolling.

Justice Kennedy dissents, joined throughout by Justices Scalia, Thomas, and Breyer. They would reverse the hearing-frequency judgment and remand consistently with rejecting the claim. They accept Cavanaugh’s binding opportunity protection and absence of an actual-parole proof requirement, but give weight to its individualized-postponement reservation. California retains annual consideration unless a hearing produces a separate, reviewable prediction directed to the intervening years. Permission is restricted to multiple life-taking convictions, requires stated bases, permits a shorter interval and is capped at three years. Jackson requires separately directed reasons while allowing underlying facts to overlap suitability reasons. Morales’s continued-risk findings supply an additional legal decision absent from automatic deletion of alternate hearings.

They acknowledge that forecasts can become outdated and initial review is not later reconsideration. Their sufficient distinction relies on neither unproved ordinary reopening, a binding nineteen-year minimum, aggregate probabilities nor legal impossibility of earlier release. Collins’s punishment boundary and Cavanaugh’s reserved category support their conclusion; a procedural label alone would not suffice. They accept substantiation of an asserted substitute without making equivalent interim review indispensable to every justified delay. Kennedy accepts a bounded method of testing such assertions without joining the Court’s application; Scalia, Thomas and Breyer do not join an independently reusable enumerated allocation rule. Defective, perfunctory, categorical or materially different punitive implementation remains open. They grant no parole and leave rejected claims and later enactments untouched. Their distinction has four votes and does not control.
