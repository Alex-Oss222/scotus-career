# Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Association; No. 96-713; OT1996, chunk 3, matter 31.

Authority: [Public decision](../records/Bibles_v_Oregon_Natural_Desert_Association_summary_review_1997-02-18.md).

## Event

Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Association, No. 96-713; October Term 1996. Reasoned summary merits disposition, February 18, 1997. On petition for a writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 83 F.3d 1168. The question is whether obtaining the Bureau of Land Management's newsletter mailing list to send recipients competing information supplies a cognizable public interest under FOIA Exemption 6. Organizational names and addresses have already been released; private individuals' information remains in dispute. The District of Oregon ordered disclosure, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed. No oral-argument date is reported.

## Participation

All nine sitting Justices participated in the petition's consideration and the February 18 decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No Justice is reported as not participating. Participation does not disclose a petition or merits roll call.

## Public Action

The petition for a writ of certiorari is granted. By an unsigned opinion the Court vacates the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remands for reconsideration under the correct Exemption 6 public-interest standard. This is a reasoned summary merits decision. No aggregate vote or majority lineup is announced. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's limited separate position is stated below.

## Judgment & Remedy

The Ninth Circuit's judgment is vacated, and the case is remanded. The advocacy-access rationale may not enter the balance, but any properly supported disclosure value concerning the Bureau's own conduct and the actual privacy interests remain for assessment. The Court orders neither release nor final withholding and issues no stay. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the legal correction and remand but would reverse rather than vacate. No additional tally or lineup is disclosed.

## Holdings

#### I. Advocacy access is not the relevant disclosure interest

**Holding and operative rule:** In the ordinary third-party inquiry under FOIA Exemption 6, obtaining an agency mailing list to send recipients competing information is not itself a public interest that weighs in favor of disclosure. The relevant interest is the demonstrated contribution the requested information makes to public understanding of the agency's own conduct, including any genuine information that its selection of recipients reveals.

**Authority:** The Court's unsigned opinion adopts this rule and the explanation below. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the rule and explanation; his disagreement concerns the formal disposition stated separately. The institutional opinion supplies controlling authority without a published roll call.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, which limits the ordinary third-party public-interest inquiry to understanding government activity, and Department of Defense v. FLRA, which retains that inquiry when applying Exemption 6. A useful private project does not become information about government merely because a government list would help accomplish it. The Ninth Circuit recognized requester neutrality but then treated access to the Bureau's audience for competing communications as a substantial public benefit. That reasoning makes the asserted future use do work the statute assigns to the information disclosed.

The association's stronger argument is that the recipients' identities may reveal whom the Bureau chooses to inform. That is a different claim. Its value depends on what the list actually adds to public knowledge of the Bureau; neither its official source nor the desire to correct the Bureau's message establishes that value automatically. The Court therefore rejects the outreach rationale while leaving a supported agency-transparency showing available on remand. The Court does not reopen Reporters Committee's retained statutory rule or declare every possible use of a mailing list irrelevant.

**Precedent treatment:** Reporters Committee is applied at its ordinary third-party scope, without substituting its Exemption 7(C) threshold. FLRA is applied for requester neutrality and incremental agency transparency, without transferring its separate Privacy Act prohibition to this case.

#### II. Correcting the public-interest premise does not complete the privacy balance

**Holding and operative rule:** On remand the government must establish that disclosure would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy under Exemption 6, balancing the actual privacy interests against any demonstrated incremental agency-transparency value. Subscription to the official newsletter alone does not establish consent to unrestricted dissemination; neither final withholding nor final disclosure follows from rejecting the outreach interest, and Exemption 6 permits withholding when satisfied rather than commanding secrecy.

**Authority:** The Court's unsigned opinion adopts this remand rule and explanation. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the rule and explanation and agrees that the lower court must conduct the actual balance, while preferring reversal to vacatur of its judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Department of State v. Ray, as preserved in FLRA, requires attention to concrete privacy and the actual addition to public understanding. Receiving official mail does not by itself surrender control over dissemination of a name and address to all requesters. Nor can the intended recipient's assumed willingness to receive this association's material establish willingness to receive contacts from everyone who could obtain the same information. These points preserve a real inquiry; they do not fix the weight of the privacy interest here.

The government retains its statutory burden. Exemption 6 covers personnel, medical and similar files and requires a clearly unwarranted invasion; the different law-enforcement standard in Exemption 7(C) does not govern. FLRA's conclusion that its particular home-address requests failed to require disclosure was record-specific. Its resulting Privacy Act prohibition arose from a separate statute and invoked exception, neither of which is adjudicated here. Only private individuals' information remains disputed because the organizational information has been released. The lower courts must reconsider that live dispute without the unlawful outreach interest. The Court neither creates a categorical mailing-list exemption nor orders release or nondisclosure.

**Precedent treatment:** Ray remains a concrete-balance authority, and FLRA remains a record-specific application rather than categorical home-address secrecy. No earlier Supreme Court decision is overruled.

## Precedent Treatment

- Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, 489 U.S. 749: applied to the ordinary third-party public-interest inquiry and requester neutrality; its separate Exemption 7(C) language is not imported into Exemption 6.
- Department of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Authority, February 23, 1994: applied for incremental government transparency and real dissemination privacy. Its retained precedent-based explanation, record-specific balance and distinct Privacy Act prohibition remain separate; no general mailing-list bar results.
- Department of State v. Ray, 502 U.S. 164: its concrete privacy and incremental-transparency inquiry is preserved. A prior disclosure balance does not decide the present balance automatically.

## Law After Decision

Exemption 6 continues to require the government's showing of a clearly unwarranted invasion, measured against what disclosure actually reveals about agency conduct. The decision applies that law to reject advocacy access as a disclosure interest while preserving a genuine recipient-selection transparency claim and a concrete privacy balance. It creates neither categorical mailing-list secrecy nor a right to obtain a list merely to communicate with its members. No Privacy Act prohibition is decided.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the Court's controlling explanation and the requirement of a new Exemption 6 balance, but dissents from vacatur to the limited extent that he would reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand. He applies FLRA and Reporters Committee unchanged: access to private recipients for competing advocacy is not itself information about the Bureau. His position leaves the weight of privacy and any genuine recipient-selection evidence for the lower court and does not direct final nondisclosure. His writing gives no distinct legal ground for choosing reversal rather than vacatur.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit for proceedings consistent with the Court's legal correction. Neither a final disclosure result nor a new deadline is prescribed. The District Court's earlier stay was pending the Ninth Circuit appeal; it does not establish a Supreme Court stay, and this disposition issues or extends none.

# California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Construction, N.A., Inc.; No. 95-789; OT1996, chunk 3, matter 32.

Authority: [Public decision](../records/California_Division_of_Labor_Standards_Enforcement_v_Dillingham_merits_1997-02-18.md).

## Event

California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Construction, N.A., Inc., No. 95-789; October Term 1996. Argued November 5, 1996; decided February 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 57 F.3d 712. The question is whether ERISA §514(a) preempts the application of California's public-works wage rule permitting lower apprentice wages only for apprentices enrolled in state-approved programs. The District Court granted summary judgment to the state defendants; the Ninth Circuit reversed on ERISA grounds, leaving independent NLRA and contingent damages issues unresolved.

## Participation

All nine sitting Justices participated at argument and decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No nonparticipation is established.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's ERISA-preemption judgment and remands. Justice Souter delivers the opinion of the Court.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The ERISA ground for preventing this prevailing-wage application is removed. The mandate does not direct final judgment on every claim: the independent NLRA theory, its contingent §1983 damages consequence, genuinely remaining fee issues, nonpreemption defenses and wage calculation retain their proper lower-court postures. The particular §1988 fee denial based on an asserted ERISA right not pursued under §1983 is not automatically reversed, and the previously unpreserved contractual-estoppel ground is not revived.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Souter writes the opinion of the Court, Parts I-III, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The complete opinion uses the concrete plan-command and economic-effects inquiry stated in Holdings. No separate opinion is filed.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, Parts I-III, is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Each proposition below has nine direct rationale joins; no narrower-opinion calculation is needed.

#### I. The wage criterion does not refer to ERISA plans

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the challenged public-works wage application as a reference to ERISA plans: the lower apprentice rate turns on state approval of an apprenticeship program, not on its ERISA status or the benefit content of a covered plan. The Court accepts this program's covered status for purposes of deciding preemption without holding that every apprenticeship or training arrangement is an ERISA plan.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers distinguishes state laws whose legal operation identifies covered plans or uses their benefits as a benchmark from laws operating independently of that status. Board of Trade supplies the contrasting case: its replacement-coverage obligation used a covered plan's benefit content as the mandatory measure. California's criterion concerns approval of apprenticeship programs as such, and programs subject to that criterion need not all be ERISA plans. Its operation therefore does not depend on the existence of a covered plan.

The contrary argument begins with this program's covered status and the fact that approval conditions affect its operation. But a law does not acquire an ERISA-specific reference merely because a covered plan encounters it. That would replace the statutory inquiry with the presence of an ERISA litigant. The particular program may be assumed covered without making that assumption the legal measure of the State's wage rule. The Court does not decide every requirement in California's apprenticeship system or the classification of other training arrangements.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers is applied for independently operating state cost rules. Board of Trade is distinguished because its mandatory covered-plan benchmark is absent; its holding remains intact.

#### II. The wage choice does not establish a preempted plan command

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, §514(a) does not preempt California's requirement that contractors pay prevailing journeyman wages when they do not use apprentices from an approved program. The approval-linked wage incentive neither dictates covered-plan benefits, funding, structure, administration or remedies nor is shown to have economic effects so acute that the retained wage choice becomes an effective plan command; a direct command or demonstrated coercive application remains outside this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers requires examination of a law's actual operation. Its hospital-payment holdings preserve ordinary economic influence while reserving effects that operate as substantive coverage commands or effectively restrict choice. A contractor here may retain an unapproved program and pay the prevailing journeyman wage. The State's incentive favors approved training, but no supported finding shows that the higher wage eliminates that alternative or requires a change in a covered plan's internal terms. Later approval of the program was not retroactive and does not erase the dispute over the earlier wages.

Dillingham's strongest contention is that the wage differential pressures programs to conform to substantive state training standards. Influence is real, but it does not by itself establish the compulsion Travelers requires. Treating every substantial cost consequence as preemption would discard that decision's distinction. Traditional state responsibility for wages and apprenticeship informs the statutory setting; it does not immunize every public-works measure or establish a percentage safe harbor. The Court applies the concrete command-and-effects inquiry, not a formula under which any verbal relationship to a covered plan suffices. It neither creates a competing ERISA enforcement remedy nor sustains regulation of every plan merely because the State acts through a contractor.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers's indirect-effects rule and acute-effects reservation are applied without change. Board of Trade remains controlling when plan benefits supply the mandatory legal benchmark, which this wage application does not use.

#### III. Reversal of the ERISA ground leaves independent issues for remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's ERISA-preemption judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with the absence of §514(a) preemption of this wage application. The mandate removes that federal obstacle to enforcement without directing final judgment on the independently unresolved NLRA theory, contingent §1983 damages, any properly remaining fee issue, nonpreemption defenses or the underlying wage calculation.

**Controlling explanation:** The District Court granted judgment to the state defendants on the ERISA and NLRA theories. The Ninth Circuit reversed on ERISA, rejected the Fitzgerald Act saving theory, and did not decide the independent NLRA issue or its contingent damages consequence. Removing the ERISA ground therefore does not supply a decision on those unreviewed questions. The separate rejection of the particular §1988 fee request based on an asserted ERISA right not sought to be enforced under §1983 is not automatically reversed, and the State's contractual-estoppel contention, declined below because it was first raised on appeal, is not revived.

The contrary remedial path would reinstate final judgment on every claim merely because the sole appellate preemption ground fails. That would decide more than the granted ERISA question and eliminate issues the Court of Appeals has not reached. Conversely, remand does not authorize a new challenge to the settled absence of §514(a) preemption on these facts. Because that provision does not reach this application, the Court need not decide §514(d) or whether the Fitzgerald Act would save an otherwise preempted rule. Nor does the State obtain blanket immunity merely from public ownership of the project.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers governs the ERISA ground at its actual scope. Neither Boston Harbor nor Chamber of Commerce v. Reich supplies an automatic public-contract immunity or decides the independent NLRA and damages issues left for remand.

Its acute-effects reservation and unresolved direct-self-funded questions remain intact; no numerical threshold or general-applicability immunity is added.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguished because California's wage criterion does not use covered-plan benefit content as a mandatory measure. The covered-plan benchmark holding remains binding.

The Court does not resolve the independent NLRA claim by applying a procurement label.

## Law After Decision

California may apply the reviewed wage consequence without the ERISA bar imposed below, subject to independently unresolved issues and ordinary enforcement requirements. State approval of apprenticeship programs as such is not a reference to ERISA status, and this record's wage incentive is not an actual or effective plan command. Direct plan regulation, an ERISA-specific legal benchmark, competing enforcement remedies and demonstrated acute compulsion remain outside the holding. No categorical immunity for wage laws, apprenticeship laws, public purchasing or indirect economic effects is adopted.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed. The Court's opinion does not revive an unlimited reading under which every relationship to a covered plan establishes preemption.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit for the independent issues it did not resolve and proceedings consistent with the ERISA holding. No final wage amount, damages award, fee entitlement or new deadline is ordered. The Court need not reach §514(d) or the Fitzgerald Act saving theory because §514(a) does not preempt this application. The program's later approval remains nonretroactive.

# Federal Election Commission v. Legi-Tech, Inc.; D.C. Circuit Nos. 94-5379 and 95-5085, 75 F.3d 704; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied.

## Event

Federal Election Commission v. Legi-Tech, Inc.; D.C. Circuit Nos. 94-5379 and 95-5085, 75 F.3d 704. Merits decision, February 18, 1997. On review of the D.C. Circuit's February 16, 1996 judgment reversing dismissal without prejudice of the Commission's enforcement action. The district court's dismissal remains reversed, and the enforcement action may continue under the lawfully constituted Commission's authorization. Specifically supported statutory-prerequisite, timing, evidentiary and remaining merits defenses remain available. ## Holdings

### Actual lawful reconsideration permits this enforcement action to continue

**Holding and operative rule:** After unconstitutional congressional participation has ended, a lawfully constituted Commission with available authority may independently adopt the necessary factual and legal determinations and authorize continued enforcement on an existing investigative and conciliation record. That cure is sufficient here because the lawful Commission actually reconsidered the matter, found probable cause and authorized continuation; it cannot replace an unperformed mandatory statutory opportunity, cure a lack of capacity, or evade an expired statutory deadline.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Thomas's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court accepts the original structural defect and gives the timely challenge effect. A prediction that the same commissioners inevitably would reach the same result would not supply lawful authority. The reported new recommendations, deliberation, probable-cause finding and authorization establish an actual lawful enforcement decision instead. The Commission may use existing evidence and adopt the completed attempted conciliation without replaying each step merely because the earlier body was defective. No distinct mandatory opportunity is shown to have been omitted here.

Ryder requires a real replacement for the lawful decision-making function denied; higher legal review there could not replace lost fact, credibility and sentence review. Here the properly constituted body itself performs the executive probable-cause and prosecution function, while the court retains the liability decision. FEC v. NRA prevents ratification beyond an expired period for authorized Supreme Court review; it establishes no categorical bar to a presently authorized enforcement decision. No such expired period is established in this case. Neither lack of personal bias nor administrative convenience is an independent basis for cure, and judicial examination of whether lawful reconsideration actually occurred remains available.

### Retroactive governing law and valid curative relief remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** A governing structural rule applies to this still-open case, but its equal application does not require dismissal after an independently valid lawful decision supplies the authority needed to proceed. Reliance on the former arrangement alone would not justify withholding the rule or preserving defective authority.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Thomas's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Harper requires the same governing rule on open direct review; Reynoldsville Casket prevents a remedy label from concealing selective prospectivity. The original Commission is therefore not treated as constitutionally valid. The action can continue because the subsequent lawful Commission itself made the required enforcement decision, an independent curative ground rather than reliance protection. The district court erred in treating the earlier remedy as mechanically mandatory despite this different remedial fact. Whether Legi-Tech violated the commercial-use restriction, and whether any particular remaining statutory or evidentiary defense succeeds, remain for the enforcement proceeding.

## Precedent Treatment

- Buckley v. Valeo supplies the structural principle and the distinction between defect and remedy; its treatment of past acts supplies no universal de facto defense against a timely challenge.
- Ryder v. United States is applied to require genuine lawful performance of the relevant function and distinguished because the lawful Commission supplied that executive function here; its fresh-adjudication remedy remains controlling in its own setting.
- FEC v. NRA Political Victory Fund is distinguished by available authority and timing. Its Supreme Court representation and expired-petition-period holdings remain intact; it decided no congressional-membership or structural-ratification merits.
- Harper v. Virginia Department of Taxation is applied to require equal governing-law treatment while keeping valid remedies distinct.
- Reynoldsville Casket Co. v. Hyde is applied to reject reliance-only relief and permit an independently valid curative basis.

## Law After Decision

A timely structural objection remains consequential, but an actual authorized decision by the lawful executive body can permit enforcement to continue without automatic repetition of every completed preliminary step. Statutory prerequisites, genuine conciliation where required, available authority and deadlines retain independent force. No generic ratification power, constitutional harmlessness test based on bias, or de facto validation is adopted. The underlying restriction and its exception for using a political committee's name and address to solicit that committee are not adjudicated.

## Separate Writings

No separate opinion is filed.

## Procedure After Action

The enforcement suit proceeds to ordinary judicial resolution under the valid Commission authorization. The Court orders no additional investigation or production, awards no penalty and directs no final injunction. Specifically supported remaining defenses may be considered in those proceedings.

# General Motors Corp. v. Tracy; No. 95-1232.

Authority: [Public decision](../records/General_Motors_v_Tracy_merits_1997-02-18.md).

## Event

General Motors Corp. v. Tracy, No. 95-1232. Argued October 7, 1996; decided February 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Ohio, reviewing 73 Ohio St. 3d 29, 652 N.E.2d 188 (1995), which sustained the use-tax assessment. Questions: purchaser standing, the Commerce Clause comparison between regulated local distribution companies and independent gas marketers, and the separate Equal Protection Clause challenge. The purchases occurred October 1, 1986 through June 30, 1990. ## Holdings

#### I. The assessed purchaser may bring this challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** A purchaser directly liable for a tax has standing to challenge an exemption that allegedly burdens its interstate purchases. The adverse assessment supplies the purchaser's own economic injury, caused by the challenged tax rule and redressable through lawful relief from that assessment, whether or not payment has been established.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** General Motors contests a charge imposed on its own purchases. Its injury does not become another seller's claim merely because the tax also affects competing suppliers. Bacchus recognizes a taxed purchaser's ability to challenge a discriminatory exemption; Fulton likewise examines the taxpayer's own liability although the distinction concerns another market participant. Relief from an unlawful assessment would remove the asserted burden. Ohio's seller-only objection therefore fails. Standing establishes access to review, not the truth of the claimed comparison between the sellers. The Court does not find an assessment amount or prior payment and orders no refund on the strength of standing alone.

**Precedent treatment:** Bacchus Imports, Ltd. v. Dias supplies purchaser standing; Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner confirms that the claimant may contest its own tax burden without being the disfavored seller. Their merits and remedial boundaries remain.

#### II. This record does not establish the asserted discrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio's exemption for sales by regulated local distribution companies does not violate the Commerce Clause on this record, because their compulsory rate, reliability and service obligations materially distinguish their regulated supply business from independent marketers' commodity sales. Commodity overlap alone does not establish equivalence of those offerings where the record does not establish a distinct industrial-sales category separated from the obligations relied upon; the holding confers no general immunity on utilities or all of their competitive transactions.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Stone-Zsela and Stevens do not join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court must examine the practical competitive relationship, rather than accept a utility label or the fact that local marketers also pay the tax. West Lynn requires examination of an integrated fiscal preference; Carbone requires attention to the actual service market. Here the favored sellers must maintain regulated supply and meet reliability and human-needs obligations rather than select only profitable customers. Those duties materially affect their offering. General Motors bought virtually all its gas from interstate marketers and often obtained local delivery, but the record gives little detail about the industrial market and does not establish the asserted unregulated negotiated LDC sales during the assessment period. The common gas commodity does not by itself remove the established service differences.

The objection that duties in a captive market cannot justify preference in a separate competitive market would require considering such a supported separation. This record does not establish it. The Court neither finds that every industrial sale funds a particular public duty nor presumes that any alternative would endanger service. The 1996 enactment cannot prove the character of 1986–1990 purchases. Oregon Waste and Fulton continue to require demanding justification and actual tax equivalence once discrimination is established; no compensatory defense is adopted here. A materially different proved sales category remains for its own case. No independent incidental-burden balancing claim or congressional-authorization theory is decided.

**Precedent treatment:** West Lynn's integrated-operation rule and Carbone's practical service-market inquiry apply without categorical utility immunity. Oregon Waste's discrimination-and-justification rule and Fulton's cumulative compensatory conditions remain unchanged; the Court does not reach justification after rejecting the asserted comparison.

#### III. The economic classification has a rational basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio may distinguish regulated local distribution companies from independent natural-gas marketers under ordinary economic equal-protection review because the companies' actual rate and service duties rationally relate to the tax classification. Competitive overlap does not eliminate that legitimate distinction, and this rational-basis holding does not reduce the independent Commerce Clause requirements.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Stone-Zsela and Stevens reserve this question on their proposed Commerce Clause dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** Beach permits an ordinary economic classification when a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis supports it, without requiring perfect fit or formal legislative findings. Ohio's rate, reliability and service obligations supply such a basis here. An industrial buyer's opportunity to purchase from either class does not make it irrational to distinguish the regulated service business from independent marketers. The Court does not equate rationality with proof that utility taxes exactly offset the challenged charge. Nor does the Court attribute this analysis to the Ohio court, which treated equal protection as submerged in its Commerce Clause discussion. The ordinary economic challenge fails on this separate ground; independent constitutional prohibitions retain their force.

**Precedent treatment:** FCC v. Beach Communications supplies the ordinary economic-classification rule and its limits; it does not displace Commerce Clause scrutiny.

## Precedent Treatment

## Holdings

#### I. A spouse's contingent death-benefit claim does not trigger predeath forfeiture

**Holding and operative rule:** An injured worker's spouse is not a person entitled to LHWCA death-benefit compensation before the covered death and qualifying survivorship that create that statutory right. A spouse's predeath third-party release therefore does not trigger §33(g)'s employer-approval forfeiture of the later death-benefit claim merely because the release covers a possible future wrongful-death action.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Cowart makes an existing substantive right, rather than payment or a formal award, the entitlement threshold. Mrs. Yates had no vested §9 death-benefit right when the predeath releases were signed. Her husband was alive, and the required covered death and her qualifying survivorship might never occur. Her consortium claim and possible wrongful-death claim are not the distinct federal death-benefit entitlement; her husband's disability entitlement does not become hers. The later death and causation stipulation cannot retroactively supply those missing prerequisites.

Ingalls argues that approval protects its exposure and that the words concerning compensation to which a person would be entitled include potential beneficiaries. That comparison measures the compensation of a person already entitled when settling; it does not erase the threshold identifying that person. The employer's protective policy cannot add a forfeiture the enacted condition does not reach. Cowart's current approval, filing and notification requirements remain distinct: covered settlements below entitlement require prescribed prior written employer-and-carrier approval and thirty-day filing, while judgments and settlements at least equal to the compensation entitlement require notification rather than that prior approval. The Court does not hold that every third-party recovery requires approval or apply later procedural amendments automatically to releases ending in January 1984. It decides no vested disability claim, postdeath settlement, benefit amount, §33(f) offset allocation, contract credit or deference question.

**Precedent treatment:** Estate of Cowart v. Nicklos Drilling Co. is applied at its existing-right threshold; its payment-independent entitlement rule and separate approval/notification branches remain in force.

#### II. The Director may respond in this private-party review

**Holding and operative rule:** The Director of OWCP, as the Secretary of Labor's designated representative, is a proper respondent under the then-effective Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 15(a) in an employer-initiated appeal from a Benefits Review Board order while the private parties maintain a live controversy. This respondent role does not authorize an independent Director appeal based only on a general administrative interest or decide whether the Director could alone continue the controversy after all private adversity ends.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 15(a) requires the agency to be named respondent and includes an agency, board, commission or officer within that term. The Department administers the Act while the Board performs its independent adjudicative work. That internal division does not require the adjudicator itself to advocate or exclude the Department's designated litigating representative from the existing review. The 1996 designation regulation identifies the Director as the Secretary's proper party in §21(c) proceedings. Section 921a provides Secretary-appointed representation in the stated court proceedings but expressly excepts this Court. Those representation provisions identify who acts for the Department; Rule 15 supplies the respondent-status ground.

The Board-only objection confuses the allocation of decisional responsibility within the Department with its representation in appellate review. The Director need not have authored or defend the Board's order to represent the Department's view. Newport News denied independent §921(c) review based on general administrative interest and reserved respondent status. Ingalls initiated this review; it and Mrs. Yates supply the live controversy. The holding neither manufactures aggrievement by regulation nor extends jurisdiction through party nomenclature. It grants no relief outside the live appeal, resolves no special-fund case and adopts no merits-deference rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Director, OWCP v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co. retains its independent-review limit; the respondent question it reserved is now resolved only for this live private-party review.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Thomas.** Both join Part I's refusal to forfeit the death-benefit claim based on predeath releases. The Department's common administrative umbrella does not turn the Director into the Board or make its order his. A command to name the agency whose order is reviewed does not independently confer substantive party capacity on every officer designated to represent the Department. # Robinson v. Shell Oil Co.; No. 95-1376.

Authority: [Public decision](../records/Robinson_v_Shell_Oil_merits_1997-02-18.md).

## Event

Robinson v. Shell Oil Co., No. 95-1376. Argued November 6, 1996; decided February 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, reviewing its en banc judgment, 70 F.3d 325 (1995), No. 93-1562. The question is whether employees in Title VII §704(a) includes former employees alleging retaliatory postemployment conduct by a former employer. Shell discharged Robinson in 1991; while his EEOC racial-discharge charge was pending, a prospective employer sought a reference. His complaint alleges false information and negative references in retaliation. The claim was dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6) solely because he was no longer Shell's employee. The vacated panel disposition is not the judgment under review.

## Participation

## Public Action

The Fourth Circuit's categorical former-employee coverage judgment is reversed and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Robinson's retaliation claim is restored for ordinary proceedings on the unresolved elements, genuinely remaining defenses and relief. The Court does not find that Shell gave false information, acted with retaliatory motive, caused denial of a job or owes damages or equitable relief. ## Opinion Topology

Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. There are no separate writings.

## Holdings

#### Former employees fall within this retaliation protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Title VII §704(a)'s protection of employees includes former employees alleging postemployment retaliation by their former employer because they engaged in Title VII-protected activity. Former-employee status therefore cannot alone support dismissal of Robinson's claim; inclusion within the protected category does not establish retaliatory conduct, motive, causation, actionable harm or a remedy.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Title VII's definition identifies an employment relationship but does not supply one temporal answer for every statutory use. Section 704(a) contains no current-employment qualifier. Its protection for making a charge or participating in enforcement must be read with the discharge and remedial provisions: discharge complaints ordinarily continue after employment ends, and §706(g)'s reinstatement of employees confirms that the noun can include people no longer employed. References and blacklisting can punish that continued resort to enforcement. This enacted context resolves the temporal meaning; desirable policy does not override an unambiguous exclusion.

The express applicants category does not require the opposite result. Applicants need never have had an employment relationship, whereas former employees did; the separate category does not transform the latter into strangers. Nor does a uniform statutory definition require one time of reference. Walters counts existing relationships on each specified working day under §701(b); separated workers remain excluded from subsequent daily counts. Section 704(a) contains no corresponding daily-count requirement. Darden's worker-status inquiry and Town & Country's compatible-service analysis likewise require attention to each statute's own work, rather than a universal temporal definition.

The complaint alleges a false, unfavorable reference after Robinson's EEOC charge. Those allegations have not been adjudicated. The Court reverses the categorical coverage dismissal and remands for unresolved conduct, prohibited reason, causation, legally required harm, defenses and relief. It defines no general actionable-harm standard, new employment-nexus element, universal postemployment tort or deference rule. The separate discriminatory-discharge merits are not reopened.

**Precedent treatment:** Walters's genuine-relationship daily count is preserved at its §701(b) scope; Darden and Town & Country supply attention to legal relationship and statutory context without deciding the temporal reach of §704(a).

## Precedent Treatment

- Walters v. Metropolitan Educational Enterprises, Inc.: distinguished by the §701(b) daily time units; its exclusion of separated workers from later daily employer-size counts remains intact and its reservation of retaliation liability is respected.
- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden: preserves its ERISA common-law agency status inquiry; no universal rule terminating statutory protections at separation is inferred.
- NLRB v. Town & Country Electric, Inc.: its attention to actual compatible employment and each statute's exclusions supports context-sensitive interpretation; its NLRA holding does not itself decide Title VII temporal coverage.

## Law After Decision

Former employees are within §704(a)'s retaliation protection at the scope stated. Employment separation does not itself defeat that coverage; it still ends later daily relationship counts under Walters. Coverage remains distinct from proving a prohibited retaliatory act and obtaining relief. No general standard of actionable postemployment harm, causation rule, liability finding or new remedy is adopted.

## Event

Uhlrig v. Harder; Tenth Circuit No. 94-3190, 64 F.3d 567. Argued November 5, 1996; decided February 18, 1997. On review of the final judgment affirming summary judgment for five state administrators on the estate's individual-capacity §1983 substantive-due-process claim. The questions concern affirmative creation or intensification of danger, constitutional culpability and whether conduct-time law defeats qualified immunity. Official-capacity, survivors' personal and state-tort claims remain outside this review.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the reviewed judgment.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The eight Associate Justices rest solely on individual qualified immunity. The Chief Justice agrees with the judgment on constitutional merits and does not reach immunity. | Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |

## Holdings

### Conduct-time law did not sufficiently identify these administrators' acts as their own constitutional deprivation

**Holding and operative rule:** Individual qualified immunity applies unless all relevant law existing when each official acted made the particular conduct's federal unlawfulness apparent to a reasonable official; no identical prior case is required. Even accepting the estate's supported favorable knowledge and causal account, law at these administrators' 1991 acts, no later than February 23, 1992, did not sufficiently establish that the challenged hospital restructuring, followed by clinical placement and the reported warnings and training, constituted each administrator's own substantive-due-process deprivation.

**Authority:** Justice Breyer's opinion, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight direct votes. The Chief Justice's judgment concurrence adds no vote to this rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that immunity resolves this individual damages claim without deciding the reserved constitutional merits. Harlow supplies objective protection for conduct not violating clearly established rights; Anderson requires a particularized inquiry without a factual twin. Elder requires all relevant contemporaneous law, and Pinder separates nonprotection from an affirmative deprivation without making its earlier notice result automatic here.

The Court assumes the supported account that officials knowingly closed the protective unit despite grave risk to a defined staff group. That assumption does not merge Harder, Vega, Thompson, Whiteman and Lee or attribute a clinical placement decision to any of them. Cornelius supplies the strongest contrary analogy: affirmative workplace exposure to dangerous prisoners through placement, supervision and assurances. Nishiyama's provision of apparent police authority, Wood's creation of roadside vulnerability and Ross's obstruction of rescue likewise concern affirmative conduct. Together with Bowers, Archie and Archuleta, they do not sufficiently settle the constitutional attribution of each administrator's different acts through this clinical and institutional chain.

Warnings and professional procedures are features of that legal comparison, not proof of reasonable care, lack of awareness, no causation or constitutional waiver. Neither lower-court disagreement nor absence of identical facts independently establishes immunity. Collins, decided after Uhlrig's death, and later Elder and Pinder govern present analysis but cannot provide retrospective notice. The Court leaves constitutional culpability, the affirmative-danger merits and the Tenth Circuit's five-part test undecided.

## Precedent Treatment

- Harlow v. Fitzgerald supplies objective qualified immunity and remains intact.
- Anderson v. Creighton supplies particularized apparent unlawfulness without requiring an identical prior case.
- Elder v. Holloway is applied to require examination of all relevant conduct-time authority while preserving the favorable factual account and ordinary claim/evidence limits; it supplies no mandatory merits-first sequence.
- Pinder v. Johnson is applied to distinguish an affirmative deprivation from omitted protection and to keep notice separate from merits. Its March 1989 result does not resolve this later conduct automatically, and its substantive reservation remains open.
- Collins v. City of Harker Heights retains its no-general-workplace-duty rule and its separate, bounded arbitrariness holding. It does not reject every affirmative-danger claim or supply notice before its February 26, 1992 decision.
- DeShaney v. Winnebago County and Daniels v. Williams preserve the distinctions between a general protective duty, an affirmative deprivation and negligence; the Court does not convert them into a complete constitutional answer here.
- Bowers, Nishiyama, Archie, Wood, Cornelius, Ross and Archuleta are considered as conduct-time circuit authority at their actual scopes, without converting their formulations into Supreme Court holdings.

## Law After Decision

The decision establishes only the particularized immunity result for the five administrators on this reviewed claim. It supplies no general affirmative-danger test, categorical public-employment bar, mandatory deceit or assurance requirement, no-awareness finding or constitutional approval of budget choices. The distinction between a general workplace-safety duty and an attributable affirmative deprivation remains. A different official act, record or conduct-time legal landscape requires its own analysis.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment. He would reach the constitutional merits under Collins's workplace-duty boundary and a proposed rule for the reserved affirmative-danger claim. He would require an official's affirmative creation or material increase of a specific foreseeable danger, or knowing obstruction of available protection, with conscious disregard of a known serious risk when time and practical opportunity permit a response, and a causal connection to the injury. Assurances, reliance and imminence may bear on that inquiry but are not universally necessary elements; he would add no undefined independent conscience-shocking requirement after those conditions are proved.

On this record, closing the unit and delegating placement were affirmative policy acts, but the warnings, file access and clinical process do not demonstrate the personal culpable contribution his rule requires. No named defendant is shown personally to have selected Waddell's destination, given Uhlrig a concrete assurance inducing abandonment of available protection, concealed a known immediate threat or personally overridden clinical safeguards to direct her into the encounter. He does not find that all officials lacked awareness or that the decision was sound policy. The gravity of the harm and possible negligence cannot alone make senior administrators constitutional insurers.

He would preserve liability questions involving concealment of an imminent threat, an affirmative direction into a uniquely dangerous encounter after a safety assurance, or a personal override of safeguards with deliberate indifference. Independently pleaded federal rights and genuinely different compulsory or intentionally harmful conduct remain outside his no-general-duty reasoning. Because his merits inquiry finds no constitutional violation, he does not decide qualified immunity. His proposed framework and application are noncontrolling.

# United States v. Brockamp and United States v. Scott; No. 95-1225, one petition embracing Ninth Circuit judgments reported at 67 F.3d 260 and 70 F.3d 120.

## Event

United States v. Brockamp and United States v. Scott, No. 95-1225. Argued December 3, 1996; decided February 18, 1997. One petition brings up the Ninth Circuit judgments of October 5, 1995, reported at 67 F.3d 260 and 70 F.3d 120. |---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and II and Part III's Scott disposition | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Filing, independent amount rule and Scott reversal/remand |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III's deposit-remand explanation | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Preserves Brockamp's deposit alternative; Stone-Zsela agrees with this component's disposition without joining its reasons |

## Holdings

### The enacted filing scheme rebuts nonstatutory equitable suspension

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 6511's integrated filing, allowance and amount scheme does not permit the requested nonstatutory equitable extension for mental incapacity or extraordinary inability to file. Assuming Irwin's rebuttable presumption and the taxpayers' private-collector analogy apply, the scheme rebuts that presumption; incapacity and absence of prejudice supply no additional period.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices; direct unanimous authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress separately prescribed when a claim may be filed, when a refund may be allowed and which payments may be returned. For return taxes, §6511(a) uses three years from actual return filing or two years from payment, whichever expires later, and two years from payment if no return was filed. Stamp taxes separately use three years from payment. Section (b)(1) repeats the prohibition on late allowance absent a timely claim, while (b)(2) adds independent monetary limits. This coordinated and repeated structure, reinforced by Congress's particular statutory adjustments, defeats the requested general equitable insertion.

Irwin allows rebuttal from the enacted scheme; an express anti-equity sentence is unnecessary. The Court assumes that refund actions' private-collector history favors the taxpayers. Their strongest claim—actual inability followed by filing and no unfair prejudice—differs from ordinary attorney neglect, but cannot alter this scheme. Suspending both filing and the monetary interval, and adding the taxpayers' proposed matching assessment extension, would change distinct statutory operations. The scale of tax administration corroborates the reading but is not its independent ground. Specific exceptions alone likewise do not carry the decision.

Dalm preserves the connected statutory conditions without itself answering this disability question; Henderson requires this particular text-and-function inquiry. The Court neither finds Scott capable nor decides Brockamp's disputed dementia facts. It does not classify every tax deadline as jurisdictional, adjudicate a separate constitutional disability claim, or decide unpresented fraud, affirmative governmental inducement or distinct recoupment questions.

### The payment lookback independently bars these tax-payment recoveries

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming an otherwise timely return-based claim under §6511(a), §6511(b)(2)'s separately prescribed payment window is not enlarged by the requested mental-incapacity or extraordinary-inability tolling. These tax payments fall outside the applicable ordinary windows, including any supported return-filing extension; this amount limit independently defeats the ordinary tax-refund recoveries without deciding whether McGill's disputed remittance was instead a deposit.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices; an independently sufficient unanimous ground.

**Controlling explanation:** A timely claim does not necessarily entitle the claimant to recover older payments. Under (b)(2)(A), a claim within the three-year filing route reaches payments in the immediately preceding three years plus any extension for filing the return; the stamp-tax proviso uses three years. Subparagraph (B) limits other claims to payments within two years. Subparagraph (C) separately limits an allowance without a claim as though the claim were filed on the allowance date. A return-filing extension is not an assessment-extension agreement under §6511(c).

Lundy supplies the distinction between selecting a claim route and measuring recoverable payments, although its Tax Court hypothetical differs from these actual claims. The Court need not reject a favorable late-return filing assumption to enforce the independent cap. The taxpayers argue that equitable filing relief must carry the amount interval with it to preserve an effective remedy. That reasoning presupposes the very equitable power the integrated text withholds; filing relief cannot itself move the dates of refundable payments. Williams preserves express refund consent with its actual conditions, and O'Gilvie's receipt rule creates no extension.

No qualifying assessment agreement, special-period tax item or mitigation circumstance is established. Their statutory conditions remain intact, but the Court announces no new operative construction of an unused exception. The result rejects the asserted equitable ground without treating McGill's deposit characterization as already decided.

### Brockamp's separate deposit contention remains for the appellate court

**Holding and operative rule:** Reversal of Brockamp's tolling ground does not dispose of her preserved contention that the remittance was a deposit rather than a tax payment, which the appellate majority did not reach. The remand leaves that contention and its lawful consequences for ordinary appellate consideration, without ordering repayment or unconditional restoration of the district judgment.

**Authority:** Part III's deposit-remand explanation in Justice Breyer's opinion is joined by all seven other Associate Justices; the Chief Justice agrees with that remedial component without joining its reasons.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory ruling governs tax-payment refunds. The district court rejected the distinct classification contention, but the court of appeals left it unresolved after accepting tolling, and the Government acknowledges its preservation. Removing the tolling premise does not decide that independent appellate alternative. The Court neither treats suspense accounting or lack of assessment as conclusive proof of a deposit nor treats the extension form as conclusively disproving one. Those merits and the consequences of any lawful classification remain open. Brockamp's return reporting $427 liability is not a finding that all $7,000 must be refunded.

## Precedent Treatment

- Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs retains its rebuttable equitable-tolling presumption. The Court assumes its application and finds statutory rebuttal here; it does not treat established incapacity as ordinary attorney neglect.
- United States v. Dalm retains its connected waiver, administrative-claim and timing conditions and rejection of recoupment as independent refund jurisdiction. It is not redescribed as having decided disability tolling under Irwin.
- Commissioner v. Lundy supplies the distinct filing-route and payment-window functions; its Tax Court hypothetical and separate statutory routes remain confined to their terms.
- United States v. Williams preserves the express refund remedy with its administrative and timing requirements; its payer-eligibility holding supplies no equitable extension.
- Henderson v. United States supplies provision-specific attention to text, function and authorized enlargement; its service holding supplies no general power to extend tax limitations.
- O'Gilvie v. United States retains its payment-receipt trigger and expressly supplies no tolling rule.

## Law After Decision

The applicable §6511 filing and amount provisions independently reject the asserted nonstatutory incapacity and extraordinary-inability extension. Courts must distinguish actual return filing from a return's due date and filing timeliness from the separate amount recoverable. Express statutory adjustments retain their own conditions; none is established here. The decision supplies no universal jurisdictional classification, no later disability amendment and no adjudication of a separate fraud, inducement, recoupment or constitutional claim.
