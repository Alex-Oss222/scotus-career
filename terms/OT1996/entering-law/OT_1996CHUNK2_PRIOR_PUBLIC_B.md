### Heller v. Doe, No. 92-351

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-22; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Sixth Circuit, Doe v. Cowherd, 965 F.2d 109 (1992).

**Question:** Whether Kentucky’s different burdens of proof and family-participation rules for involuntary commitment of persons with intellectual disability and persons with mental illness violate equal protection, and whether family party status violates procedural due process.

**Material record:** Kentucky required proof of dangerousness, likely benefit from treatment, and a least restrictive placement predicate in the relevant commitment proceedings. For the category described in the then-operative statute as “mental retardation,” proof was clear and convincing and guardians or immediate family could participate as parties. For mental illness, proof was beyond a reasonable doubt and that party status was not supplied. The class challenged these differences. The medical and developmental distinctions offered as rationales concern typical onset, diagnostic history, prediction, and the information held by families; they are not individual findings that any class member is dangerous or incompetent.

**Relief sought:** Kentucky seeks reversal of the challenged federal rulings. The committed class seeks affirmance. Other district-court grounds outside the questions under review remain separate.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. Unequal proof burdens invalidated, 5–4; categorical invalidation of family-party participation reversed, 6–3.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The lower overall proof protection lacks a rational relation to the disadvantage imposed

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky’s clear-and-convincing burden for involuntary commitment of adults with intellectual disability, compared with proof beyond a reasonable doubt for mental illness, violates equal protection on the preserved rational-basis challenge: the offered diagnostic, prediction and treatment differences do not rationally justify assigning this class the greater risk of mistaken confinement. Addington’s federal minimum remains unchanged; the remedy must address lawful equalization and severability rather than impose a national reasonable-doubt standard or order every class member released.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory distinction must relate to the particular procedural disadvantage. Cleburne permits relevant distinctions between conditions but does not allow a label to substitute for that relationship. A longer history may make diagnosis or some predictions easier. Commitment, however, also requires dangerousness, likely treatment benefit and a least restrictive placement; the interests at stake remain physical liberty and protection from erroneous confinement. Addington explains the allocation of that error risk and supplies a constitutional minimum, not immunity from an equality challenge when a State provides greater protection to another class. The asserted difference in the intrusiveness of treatment likewise does not adequately explain the lower overall safeguard against confinement. Beach does not demand formal findings or perfect empirical fit, and the Court imposes neither requirement. It rejects the proffered relationship, not legislative generalization as such. The appropriate limited remedy must respect severability and the State’s lawful choices, while every continuing confinement still needs an independent lawful basis.

**Precedent treatment:**

- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: rational relationship to the actual disadvantage required; no new scrutiny tier adopted.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S.418: the clear-and-convincing minimum survives; unequal safeguards remain separately reviewable.
- FCC v. Beach Communications, June 1, 1993: no formal-findings or empirical-certainty requirement imposed; its specific economic justification does not decide this classification.
- Foucha, May 18, 1992: current lawful confinement predicates preserved; noncontrolling equal-protection reasoning is not promoted to law.

##### Family-party participation has a distinct rational basis

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky may distinguish the two commitment proceedings by allowing guardians and immediate family members to participate as parties for adults with a lifelong developmental disability. Continuing family knowledge rationally supports that participation; the availability of testimony or a less extensive form of participation does not by itself defeat ordinary rational-basis review.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that gathering useful information is different from assigning a person less protection against erroneous confinement. A developmental condition and sustained family involvement plausibly give relatives and guardians knowledge of abilities, daily functioning and available care that assists the proceeding. Parham recognizes the value of family information without giving a relative authority to decide whether an adult should be confined. Cleburne requires a rational relationship, and that informational relationship supports the participation distinction here. Kentucky need not show that every family has useful knowledge or that no less extensive procedure could work. Nor does the existence of mental illness within some families eliminate the permissible general distinction. The Court does not presume that family members always share the adult’s interests. Their preferences or convenience establish none of the statutory grounds for confinement, and the independent adjudicator must evaluate their evidence under the lawful burden. This holding sustains party participation as a category, not every application of it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cleburne, 473 U.S.432: relevant differences may support a particular procedural distinction.
- Parham v. J.R., 442 U.S.584: family information may be useful; adult confinement is not delegated to relatives.
- Schweiker v. Wilson, 450 U.S.221: ordinary review does not require the least restrictive legislative alternative.

##### Party participation does not itself deprive the adult of fair adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** Allowing relatives or guardians to participate as parties does not categorically violate procedural due process where the adult has counsel and an effective opportunity to contest adverse evidence before an independent decisionmaker. Relatives may neither supply a substitute for the statutory commitment grounds nor control the decision or suppress the adult’s position.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged party status must be examined within the actual procedural safeguards. Mathews requires consideration of the private interest, the risk of error and value of safeguards, and the government’s interests. The liberty interest is substantial. Kentucky nevertheless provides counsel, professional examination, an opportunity to testify and present evidence, cross-examination and independent adjudication. Relevant family information can improve accuracy when tested through those procedures. The possibility of adverse family interests requires such testing; it does not establish that adding a party inherently makes an accurate decision impossible. Parham does not permit the State to call an adult’s involuntary placement voluntary merely because a relative requested it. The adjudicator must find the statutory grounds under the lawful burden, regardless of the family’s preference. Proceedings that defeat effective contest or independent decisionmaking remain challengeable. The Court reverses the categorical due-process invalidation without determining that every class member’s hearing was fair.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S.319: private interest, error risk and safeguards, and government interests applied separately from equality review.
- Parham, 442 U.S.584: informational participation distinguished from final decisional control.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, the proof distinction is invalid under rational-basis review, with severability and lawful equalization left for remand. The family distinction and party status survive the categorical challenges subject to independent adjudication and effective contest. Addington, the substantive confinement predicates and the unresolved heightened-scrutiny question remain distinct. Kentucky’s repealed voluntary-admission rule and other unreviewed grounds are not decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, would sustain the proof distinction. They regard easier diagnosis, a longer behavioral history supporting prediction, and generally less intrusive treatment as independently sufficient rational grounds. Addington’s minimum is met, and ordinary review permits imperfect generalizations. Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would also invalidate family-party status: useful information can be obtained through witnesses, while party status adds an adversary able to call and cross-examine witnesses and appeal a refusal to confine. They find no rational connection between that additional burden and the different condition and therefore do not reach due process. Blackmun separately preserves his view that disability classifications and substantial restraints on liberty warrant heightened review, while accepting that the proof distinction fails the ordinary standard used here.

### United States v. Dunnigan, No. 91-1300

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

Argued December 2, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the Fourth Circuit, 944 F.2d 178 (August 30, 1991, amended September 12, 1991). The court below affirmed the cocaine-conspiracy conviction but vacated the sentence because it considered a mandatory Guidelines increase for a defendant’s trial perjury unconstitutional. The United States seeks reversal of that sentencing ruling. The questions are whether U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 constitutionally applies to the defendant’s own trial perjury, what findings it requires, and whether the findings here suffice.

The Court reverses the sentencing judgment, 9–0, and remands for proceedings consistent with the opinion. Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision. All nine reject a categorical constitutional bar to the perjury adjustment and agree that the district court’s findings encompass the required elements here. Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas. No separate writing is published.

A sentence increase under U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 for independently established trial perjury—willfully false testimony under oath or affirmation about a material matter—does not violate the defendant’s right to testify. Once the court properly determines that the defendant committed such perjury, the applicable Guideline requires its two-level obstruction adjustment; neither exercising the trial right nor a guilty verdict alone supplies that predicate.

Kennedy’s unanimous opinion directly controls this rule, its explanation, and its application to this record.

The Court holds that the right to testify does not protect deliberate material falsehood under oath. Nix v. Whiteside supplies that boundary: a defendant’s opportunity to present a defense does not include a right to perjury. United States v. Grayson permits sentencing courts to consider a defendant’s willful false testimony while warning against a reflex increase whenever a testifying defendant is convicted. Section 3C1.1 respects that distinction when the court independently finds perjury. The Fourth Circuit’s reliance on the Guidelines’ reduced emphasis on rehabilitation does not justify a categorical exemption, because intentional corruption of the trial process also bears on retribution and incapacitation. Nor does the mandatory consequence erase the factual prerequisites that make the increase lawful. A defendant may testify inaccurately through confusion or faulty memory, or truthfully advance a defense the jury rejects, without committing perjury. The Court rejects the constitutional prohibition here while preserving those limits; it does not require a separate perjury prosecution or decide a different sentencing framework.

Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S. 157 (1986), applied to the right’s boundary: the right to present testimony includes no entitlement to perjury; the decision does not authorize assuming falsehood from an unsuccessful defense.

United States v. Grayson, 438 U.S. 41 (1978), applied: actual willful trial falsehood may inform sentencing; its rehabilitation discussion is not an exclusive justification and its warning against reflex increases remains operative.

When a defendant objects to a § 3C1.1 increase based on trial testimony, the court must review the evidence and independently find willfully false sworn testimony concerning a material matter, excluding confusion, mistake and faulty memory. Separate clear findings for each element are preferable, but a single determination encompassing all the factual predicates is sufficient; the district court’s findings here meet that standard.

The same nine Justices join Kennedy’s perjury-definition and findings holding, including the sufficiency of the existing finding on this record; that holding controls directly.

The Court requires a finding of perjury, not merely a finding that the defendant lost at trial. Section 1621 supplies the established elements of sworn, willfully false testimony about a material matter. Grayson’s caution against reflex sentencing requires the judge to examine the evidence rather than treat the guilty verdict as the missing proof of those elements. Separate findings on falsity, materiality and intent are preferable because they make the basis for review clear. A combined finding is nevertheless sufficient when it actually encompasses each predicate. Here the district court reviewed the evidence, identified Dunnigan’s denials of participation, and found untruthful testimony on material matters designed substantially to affect the outcome. That determination encompasses intentional material falsehood; its reference to the verdict does not reduce it to the verdict alone. Respondent’s contrary reading overlooks the judge’s own assessment and the purpose attributed to the testimony. The Court requires no special verbal formula and leaves confusion, mistake and faulty memory outside perjury.

United States v. Grayson, 438 U.S. 41 (1978), applied with its safeguard intact: a sentencing response requires an actual assessment of deliberate false testimony, not an automatic consequence of conviction.

Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S. 157 (1986), left intact in its factual boundary: absence of a protected right to perjury does not establish that disputed testimony was perjurious.

Williams v. United States, decided March 9, 1992, is distinguished by the question presented and left undisturbed: its rules for departures based on invalid factors do not create a categorical constitutional bar to this adjustment or dispense with independent perjury findings. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. Summary contempt concerns a different power to punish immediate obstruction; its requirements do not add a separate actual-obstruction element to trial perjury under this Guideline. The Court selects no new general standard of proof and announces no general rule governing the binding force of all Guidelines commentary.

Effective February 23, 1993, the constitutional and findings holdings govern § 3C1.1 adjustments for trial perjury. They apply Grayson’s allowance for considering actual perjury and Nix’s boundary on the right to testify to the mandatory Guidelines, subject to independent findings of every perjury element. The Court does not order automatic punishment of every convicted defendant who testified. It requires no separate perjury prosecution and does not address sentencing or jury-finding questions beyond the perjury adjustment presented.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns through the Fourth Circuit for sentencing proceedings consistent with the rejection of its categorical bar and the sufficiency of the existing findings. The two-level adjustment is required when the properly found perjury predicate is satisfied. The Court neither requires a new evidentiary hearing nor requires adequate combined findings to be repeated in separate sentences, and it does not itself select a new sentence. The conviction remains affirmed; collateral indictment, evidentiary, and disclosure questions are not reopened.

### FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., No. 92-603

Merits decision, 1993-06-01

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-29; decided 1993-06-01. Review of the District of Columbia Circuit, 965 F.2d 1103 (1992).

Question: Whether the Cable Act’s exemption for facilities serving multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, without using a public right-of-way, survives the reviewed equal-protection challenge.

Satellite master antenna television operators challenged the distinction between systems serving commonly controlled buildings and systems serving separately owned buildings. After an earlier remand for an explanation, the court of appeals found the distinction unsupported and required extension of the exemption. The 1992 Cable Act renumbered the relevant definition from §522(6) to §522(7); that citation change did not enact the court of appeals’ constitutional conclusion. The reviewed exemption applies only to facilities serving subscribers in one or more multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, unless the facilities use a public right-of-way. Separate First Amendment scrutiny was not adjudicated below on the new statutory footing.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0 in the judgment.

| Equal protection of the ownership-based exemption | Reverse: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no dissent | The ownership distinction survives ordinary rational-basis review; further proceedings may address the separate questions not decided here. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Reenactment does not moot the live constitutional controversy

**Controlling proposition:** Congress’s reenactment of the cable definition without changing the challenged distinction does not adopt a lower court’s constitutional invalidation or eliminate the live controversy defended by the FCC. The Court therefore reaches the reviewed equal-protection question.

**Authority:** Thomas’s threshold opinion; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the parties continue to dispute the validity of an operative statutory distinction. Congress’s failure to alter that distinction while revising other portions of the cable legislation does not turn the court of appeals’ judgment into an act of Congress. A legislative adoption inference sometimes used to understand statutory language does not establish that Congress enacted a judicial conclusion about constitutional validity. The FCC continues to defend the distinction, and reversal would alter the parties’ rights under the exemption. The amendment therefore leaves a concrete controversy rather than an advisory question. This conclusion decides only the proposed ground for mootness. It does not treat congressional silence as approval of every agency interpretation, decide the new legislation’s constitutionality, or resolve the separate speech objections. Those objections must be addressed through the questions and record actually presented for their consideration.

**Precedent treatment:**

- The lower court’s constitutional judgment is reviewed, not treated as legislation adopted by congressional silence; no earlier controlling decision is overruled.

##### Common-owner bargaining and regulatory costs sustain the economic line

**Controlling proposition:** For an economic classification involving neither a suspect class nor a fundamental right under the question reviewed, a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis suffices; the challenger must negate such a basis, and formal legislative findings are unnecessary. Congress could rationally distinguish these commonly owned, controlled or managed dwellings from separately owned properties because bargaining arrangements and the relationship between regulatory cost and consumer protection differ; Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid irrational or impermissibly motivated classifications.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Parts I and II-A; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress could make this ownership distinction without proving that it was the only satisfactory line. Fritz permits economic classifications supported by a conceivable legitimate relationship, and the Nordlinger decision confirms that imperfect fit does not itself defeat rationality. A common owner can bargain for service on behalf of the property and can choose among suppliers before committing the premises. Congress could also conclude that regulating small systems imposes costs disproportionate to the consumer protection obtained. Separate ownership changes those bargaining conditions and permits expansion without the same common control. The absence of a fixed subscriber cap and the existence of atypically large exempt facilities do not negate the general relationship. Neither legislative silence about an actual motive nor the absence of empirical findings shifts the challenger’s burden. Moreno and Cleburne still exclude a forbidden purpose or irrational prejudice; no such defect or settled contrary fact defeats this relationship here. Separate speech scrutiny remains undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Railroad Retirement Board v. Fritz, 449 U.S.166: conceivable legitimate grounds and imperfect economic fit applied.
- Sullivan v. Stroop, 496 U.S.478: ordinary review of social and economic classifications applied.
- Nordlinger v. Hahn, June 18, 1992: the rational-basis holding applied without converting its particular tax grounds into universal immunity from review.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S.528: illegitimate-purpose limit preserved.
- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: irrational-prejudice limit preserved.

##### Competitive entry independently supports the ownership distinction

**Controlling proposition:** Congress could independently distinguish separately owned buildings because an incumbent provider can add nearby buildings at a lower marginal cost than a new entrant can incur to install its own reception facilities, creating a plausible first-mover advantage. This conceivable competition concern is sufficient under ordinary rational-basis review without proof that every regulated system possesses monopoly power.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Part II-B; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the ownership line has a sufficient competition rationale independent of the regulatory-cost explanation. A provider already operating a reception facility may connect additional separately owned buildings more cheaply than a new competitor can construct the facilities necessary to serve them. Congress could regard that advantage as a reason to retain franchising oversight. In a commonly owned development, by contrast, a single owner can negotiate for the whole property before selecting a provider. Fritz permits Congress to address such differences through an administrable category even when particular systems do not display the predicted advantage. The argument that all systems avoid public rights-of-way identifies a common characteristic but does not eliminate this different competitive characteristic. The holding does not find actual monopoly power, decide an antitrust claim or require a regulator to prove market dominance in every application. Nor does it decide whether a different standard applies to a separately presented burden on protected speech.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fritz, 449 U.S.166: a second conceivable legitimate economic basis independently sustains the line.
- Nordlinger, June 18, 1992: multiple sufficient rational grounds and tolerance for imperfect fit applied within ordinary review.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: ordinary rational-basis review and Nordlinger governed economic classifications, while the cable ownership line was contested. After this decision: the line is sustained by eight Justices on bargaining/regulatory cost and seven on the independently sufficient competition rationale. Seven supply the threshold mootness rationale. No First Amendment holding or heightened-scrutiny resolution enters; no precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, concurring in the judgment, accepts a concrete distinction between an owner’s private use of property and reaching beyond that property to participate in a regulated market. He doubts that hypothesized system size or competitive advantage, detached from the actual statutory distinction, provides a satisfactory account. Fritz’s presumption of legitimate, impartial legislative action does not in his view make any imagined relationship sufficient regardless of context. His narrower explanation supports reversal but is noncontrolling.

[Official report and underlying record description, 508 U.S.307](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508307/usrep508307.pdf); [Judgment below, 965 F.2d 1103](https://static.case.law/f2d/965/html/1103-01.html) supplies the actual invalidation and prior-remand posture. The official report at 309–315 identifies the Cable Act renumbering, continuing controversy and reserved First Amendment question.

Record and statutory descriptions are supported by these sources. The public opinion summaries are paraphrases, not verbatim quotations. The 1992 renumbering did not change the challenged ownership distinction. Unrelated provisions of that legislation and separate speech claims are outside this holding. No special mandate date or subsequent lower-court outcome is reported.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment invalidating the ownership distinction is reversed and the case remanded. No exemption is judicially extended to all private-property systems. The Court leaves separate speech claims, any properly presented heightened-scrutiny issue and challenges to particular applications for appropriate proceedings; it does not decide those claims or authorize public-right-of-way use under the exemption.

Supreme Court review is complete. The District of Columbia Circuit receives the case for proceedings consistent with the sustained economic distinction and the reserved questions. No special mandate date is supplied.

### Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc., No. 92-1168

Merits decision, 1993-11-09

On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 976 F.2d 733 (1992) (table), which affirmed a Middle District of Tennessee judgment for Forklift after a bench trial. Argued October 13, 1993; decided November 9, 1993. The question is whether sex-based harassment must cause serious psychological injury to create a hostile or abusive environment under Title VII. Harris seeks reconsideration under the correct standard; Forklift seeks affirmance on the findings already made. Harris managed Forklift from 1985 to 1987. President Charles Hardy repeatedly directed sex-based insults and sexual innuendo at her, promised to stop after her complaint, and resumed; she left in October 1987. The trial court treated serious psychological effects and injury as important prerequisites in a close case. No quid pro quo claim is presented.

The Court reverses the Sixth Circuit's judgment and remands, 9–0. Justice O'Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg. All nine support the complete rule, its application and limits, and reversal with remand; no Justice opposes the judgment. Justices Scalia and Ginsburg each file a concurrence joined by no other Justice, while also joining the Court's opinion in full.

Sex-based harassment violates Title VII when, considered in all the circumstances, it is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter employment conditions and create an environment a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive and that the employee actually perceives as abusive; serious psychological injury, treatment, diagnosis, and lost productivity are not prerequisites. Relevant circumstances include frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation rather than a merely offensive utterance, and unreasonable interference with work, but no listed consideration is indispensable; psychological effects may inform subjective abuse, while offensive conduct that does not meet the objective and subjective requirements remains insufficient.

O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg; O'Connor and those eight Justices supply nine votes for the complete rule and reversal with remand. 

The Court holds that Title VII protects the conditions in which an employee works, without requiring the employee first to suffer a psychological injury. Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson supplies the requirement of discriminatory conduct sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter those conditions. Its examples of extreme mistreatment illustrate the rule; they do not establish a minimum psychiatric consequence. The objective requirement excludes conduct that merely offends, while actual perception of abuse connects the claim to the employee's own working environment. Context determines whether those requirements are met, rather than any mandatory diagnosis or measurable decline in productivity. Forklift accepts that serious psychological harm is unnecessary but contends that the findings independently support judgment. They do not permit that conclusion here: the trial court repeatedly relied on psychological well-being and injury when resolving what it described as a close case. The president's repetition of degrading conduct after Harris complained must be assessed under the proper standard. Reversal therefore requires reconsideration, not an appellate finding of liability. The Court does not decide constructive discharge, damages, a new sex-specific reasonable-person test, or the proposed EEOC regulations.

*Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 (1986): applies and clarifies its severe-or-pervasive alteration-of-conditions rule; its extreme examples do not impose a psychological-injury element. Its employer-responsibility questions are not enlarged here.

Merits decision, June 27, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 25, 1994; decided June 27, 1994. Reported at 512 U.S. 594 (1994). On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, whose judgment at 981 F.2d 1262 (1992) affirmed without opinion.

The question is whether Rule 804(b)(3) permits admission of an accomplice's generally self-inculpatory confession, including accusations not themselves against that declarant's penal interest. Williamson also preserved a separate confrontation objection. He seeks relief from his drug-related convictions; the United States seeks to sustain them under the exception and any properly available harmless-error ground.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Each material assertion must be against the declarant's interest in context

**Controlling proposition:** When an unavailable declarant's statement is offered under Rule 804(b)(3) as a statement against penal interest, each offered material assertion must, in the circumstances when made, so tend to expose that declarant to criminal liability that a reasonable person in that position would not have made it unless believing it true; a generally self-inculpatory account does not carry an independently neutral, noninculpatory or self-serving assertion into the exception. This is contextual assessment, not grammatical atomization: an apparently neutral detail or identification of another participant can qualify if its actual effect sufficiently incriminates the declarant, while neither joint inculpation nor custody alone establishes admissibility or exclusion.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg. These seven participating Justices adopt the same rule, contextual qualification and explanation. Justices Kennedy and Thomas favor a different construction; their judgment votes add no support to this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exception follows the assertion's actual adverse effect, not the label attached to a larger confession. The reasonable-person condition supplies the reliability premise: genuine exposure to liability may make a statement credible, but a speaker can admit one fact and falsely assign greater responsibility to someone else. Salerno requires satisfaction of the selected exception's actual conditions; it does not decide the disputed unit here. Lee warns that accomplice accusations can be selectively unreliable, without establishing a categorical ban on every such statement. The broader reading has support in ordinary narrative usage and the Advisory Committee's discussion of related statements. But the note also asks whether the statement is actually against interest and warns about custodial efforts to obtain favor. Considering context answers the objection that separation destroys meaning: the surrounding account may explain an assertion's incriminating force without becoming admissible in its entirety. Harris's changing accounts, admitted transportation, cooperation setting and allocation of roles require that distinction. The Court decides the governing rule, not a common classification of every Harris assertion, and leaves the separate constitutional claim unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, decided June 19, 1992: applies its requirement that the selected hearsay exception's actual conditions be satisfied; its former-testimony and similar-motive holdings remain unchanged and do not themselves resolve Rule 804(b)(3)'s unit.
- Lee v. Illinois, 476 U.S. 530 (1986): draws on its warning about selective unreliability in an accomplice's accusation; does not turn its confrontation analysis into automatic evidentiary exclusion or decide the constitutional claim here.

##### Vacatur preserves the distinction between admissibility error and relief

**Controlling proposition:** The Eleventh Circuit's judgment is vacated and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the assertion-specific Rule construction, without a controlling determination that all of Harris's account qualifies or all of it fails; any properly available harmless-error argument must be considered under the standard applicable to the error actually found. For preserved nonconstitutional evidentiary error, Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos place the applicable harmlessness burden on the Government and require the reviewing court, considering the whole record, to have fair assurance that the error did not substantially sway the judgment, with grave doubt preventing a harmlessness finding; Chapman instead requires harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt only if constitutional trial error is actually reached and found, a new trial follows if error requiring relief is established, and neither acquittal nor automatic retrial follows from rejecting whole-account admission.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg. Seven Justices adopt these shared judgment-consequence limits while retaining the application disagreements described below. All nine Justices vote for vacatur and remand, but Kennedy and Thomas concur in that judgment under their different Rule construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proper evidentiary inquiry and the effect of an error are separate questions. The reported district ruling and unexplained appellate affirmance do not establish the required assertion-specific assessment. Kotteakos supplies the inquiry for preserved nonconstitutional error: the reviewing court must examine the whole record and the error's effect on the verdict, rather than ask only whether sufficient other evidence supports guilt. Chapman is reserved for an actually established constitutional trial error; no such violation is decided today. The Court orders further proceedings, not a present harmlessness finding. Whether a particular Government argument remains properly available depends on the proceedings, and the decision recognizes no unreported forfeiture or new admissibility ground.

**Precedent treatment:**

Oklahoma permits consideration of other criminal conduct when fixing punishment for the offense before the sentencing court; McMillan distinguishes lawful sentencing considerations from a separate offense, subject to independent safeguards. The statutory range was five to forty years, the calculated Guidelines range 292 to 365 months, and the actual substantial-assistance sentence 144 months. Those distinct quantities show why the sentencing label alone is not the answer: real aggravation occurred, but it calibrated one legally authorized marijuana punishment rather than imposed a separate cocaine penalty. Felix supplies the distinction between evidentiary use and prior prosecution, applied here by analogy rather than equating all sentencing with trial evidence. Dixon retained Grady's essential-conduct protection; the cocaine offenses were not previously prosecuted, and their conduct did not constitute the later marijuana attempt. Kurth Ranch involved a distinct punitive exaction and remains intact. The Court decides no guilt or future sentence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576 — applied to consideration of other conduct when fixing lawful offense-of-conviction punishment.
- McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 — applied within statutory authority; independent due-process and jury limits remain.
- United States v. Felix — applied by analogy for the difference between use of conduct and prior prosecution; no same-evidence bar is created.
- United States v. Dixon and Grady v. Corbin — expressly preserved; retained essential-conduct protection has no prior-cocaine-prosecution predicate here.
- Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch — distinguished as a distinct punitive exaction under its combined-operation analysis, not overruled.
- United States v. R.L.C. — preserved: statutory sentencing authority is not replaced by the Guidelines range.
- United States v. Harris — confined to authorized cumulative punishment in one prosecution; it does not cure a valid successive-prosecution bar.

##### Future sentence coordination remains conditional and separate

**Controlling proposition:** The possibility of an overlapping later sentence does not itself bar this prosecution, and any sentence following conviction must satisfy the then-applicable coordination provisions and independent constitutional limits. Under received § 5G1.3, subsection (a) first requires the instant sentence to run consecutively to the undischarged term if the instant offense occurred during service of that term, including work release, furlough or escape, or after sentencing for that term but before service began; only if (a) does not apply does subsection (b) require concurrency when the earlier offenses were fully taken into account in the instant offense level; in all remaining cases, policy statement (c) requires consecutiveness to the extent necessary for reasonable incremental punishment, with the commentary's approximation task, not an automatic total or repeated departure.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, conditional coordination discussion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six support this statutory and remedial proposition. Stevens still dissents from allowing the prosecution, and Kennedy does not join the detailed coordination discussion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that permission to prosecute and calculation of a later sentence are separate. Section 1B1.3 supplies relevant-conduct calculation; § 5G1.3 addresses an actual undischarged term in priority order. Subsection (a) requires consecutiveness for an offense committed during service, including work release, furlough or escape, or after sentencing but before service began. Witte's cocaine offenses preceded the earlier imprisonment and sentencing, so (a) does not apply here. Only if (a) is inapplicable does (b) require concurrency for earlier offenses fully included in the instant offense level. In remaining cases, (c) and its commentary seek reasonable incremental punishment, not an identical aggregate term in every case. Witte has no new cocaine conviction or sentence before the Court. Illustrative lower-court arithmetic supplies no second sentence, credit award or 144-month lifetime cap. - Harris — distinguished from successive prosecution; its one-prosecution authorization rule remains unchanged.
- Felix, Dixon and Grady — preserved; statutory sentence coordination cannot cure a valid prior-prosecution bar.

Grady remains governing, as Dixon held. Felix's evidence-use and distinct-offense rules remain; they are not enlarged into a complete sentencing constitutional framework. Kurth Ranch remains a narrow distinct-exaction holding. Harris remains limited to cumulative authorization in one prosecution. No alternative rule that Congress may authorize a new prosecution after valid prior punishment is adopted.

The indictment may proceed because the marijuana-attempt sentence was not a prior cocaine prosecution or separate punishment. Cocaine conduct really affected that calculation. No 292-month later sentence, thirty months actually served, 262-month remaining term or 144-month total cap is found. ### Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston, No. 94-749

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

Argued April 25, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, which sustained application of the public-accommodations law requiring the private organizers to include GLIB. A continuing injunction against the traditional parade and the organizers’ desire to resume it keep the controversy live despite a differently organized 1995 event.

The Court reverses and remands, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support the judgment requiring dissolution of the compelled-inclusion injunction. Justice Souter delivers the single Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. No separate writing is published.

Applying a public-accommodations law to compel private parade organizers to include an identified contingent conveying a message they do not choose violates the First Amendment. A parade remains expression despite broad admissions, multiple themes and the absence of a single precise message; the rule does not exempt ordinary nonexpressive goods or services from nondiscrimination law.

All nine Justices adopt this protection for private expression in Justice Souter’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that the right to speak includes control over the composition of a private expressive presentation. Barnette prohibits compulsory affirmation, and Tornillo protects editorial choice against compelled inclusion. Turner preserves the importance of a speaker’s selection within a particular medium; its separate cable context is not mechanically imported here. A parade presents expression through the groups and messages its organizers choose, even if those selections are permissive or varied. GLIB’s bannered unit has its own protected message, but that protection creates no entitlement to insert it into another private speaker’s presentation. Use of public streets under a neutral permit does not convert this parade into a governmental forum. Roberts and PruneYard concern access without the same compelled alteration of expression. Independent review of intertwined expression characterizations, with credibility deference, rejects the conclusion that absence of a single theme eliminates protection. The Court invalidates only this compelled expressive inclusion, without finding every customer identity a message or requiring proof that spectators endorse each contingent.

West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette supplies protection against compelled expression.

Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo supplies protection for editorial selection.

The Court applies the medium-specific editorial protection recognized in Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC without importing its unrelated cable results.

Roberts v. United States Jaycees and PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins are distinguished because they concern access that does not materially alter a private expressive presentation.

Bose Corp. v. Consumers Union and Fiske v. Kansas supply independent review of characterizations intertwined with the First Amendment issue, while retaining deference to credibility determinations.

The respondents’ state-action/public-forum theory against the City was dismissed and not cross-appealed; it is not revived here. The importance of message misattribution in another setting remains open. No general antidiscrimination exemption, facial invalidation or judgment about the correct political message is announced.

Ordinary nondiscrimination applications remain distinct and enforceable.

Private parade composition receives protection against compelled insertion of an identified expressive unit. Other expressive-association disputes require a comparably material expressive burden; ordinary customer identity alone is not a message.

**Source notes:** Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, 418 Mass. 238, 636 N.E.2d 1293; respondent’s predecision mootness suggestion and petitioner’s response, Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-749. The continuing injunction supplies the live controversy; no later Court action or fee amount is determined.

The Supreme Judicial Court judgment is reversed and the case remanded. The injunction compelling GLIB’s inclusion shall be dissolved. The public-accommodations law remains enforceable where its application does not compel private expression. No fee amount, fine or sanction is decided.

### United States v. Gaudin, No. 94-514

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

Argued April 17, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed the reviewed convictions after the district judge decided materiality for the jury. The question concerns the Fifth and Sixth Amendment allocation of that element in the §1001 prosecution.

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support affirmance of the reversal of the reviewed false-statement convictions. Justice Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. Justice Thomas also concurs, joined by Justices O’Connor and Breyer.

When materiality is an element of the charged criminal false-statement offense, the Fifth and Sixth Amendments require the jury to determine it beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge states statutory meaning and the governing legal standard; labeling its application a mixed question does not remove an element from the jury.

All nine Justices adopt this allocation of the offense element to the jury in Justice Scalia’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that the jury must decide every element necessary to establish criminal guilt, including application of an instructed legal standard to the charged facts. Winship supplies proof beyond a reasonable doubt; the constitutional jury guarantee places that determination with the jury. Materiality asks whether a statement has a natural tendency or capacity to influence the relevant governmental decision. That application does not become an abstract legal question simply because legal instruction is needed. Historical practice and functional convenience cannot reserve an offense element to the judge. Sinclair’s contrary criminal pertinency allocation cannot stand to that extent. Kungys concerns a civil denaturalization proceeding and a different appellate allocation, not this criminal jury guarantee. Here the Government concedes materiality is an element and did not challenge the lower court’s error classifications. Those premises preserve this reversal without deciding whether every section 1001 clause includes materiality or every withheld element is structural error. Statutory interpretation and threshold evidentiary sufficiency remain judicial functions.

In re Winship supplies the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt for facts establishing guilt.

Sinclair v. United States is overruled to the extent it assigns a criminal pertinency element to the judge; its unrelated propositions remain in force.

Kungys v. United States is distinguished because it concerns civil denaturalization and the allocation of responsibility on appeal.

Sullivan v. Louisiana retains its constitutional jury and burden-of-proof holding; this decision does not classify every error involving an omitted element.

Hubbard v. United States retains its rule about the entities covered by §1001; it supplies no authority to remove elements of the charged HUD offense from a jury.

The decision does not determine abstract statutory meaning by jury, classify every omitted-element error, adjudicate the exact trial-preservation history, or disturb a separately treated equity-skimming conviction or calculate its sentence.

The treatment accompanying the holding controls; only the incompatible criminal-element allocation of Sinclair is displaced.

A jury must apply the legal materiality standard beyond a reasonable doubt where materiality is an element. No universal materiality requirement for every §1001 clause or universal structural-error rule is created.

Thomas, joined by O’Connor and Breyer, concurs while joining the Court in full. The Government’s concession establishes materiality as an element for this case; the opinion need not construe every §1001 clause. The unchallenged lower error classifications do not settle harmless error, plain error or structural error for all later cases. These reservations qualify the questions decided, not the jury-element holding.

**Source notes:** Ninth Circuit en banc, 28 F.3d 943; §1001; petition in the Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-514. That collection item lacks separately listed merits and appendix components. The lower opinion and the conceded-element premises support the bounded question decided; the decision makes no exact finding about trial preservation.

The Ninth Circuit is affirmed and the reviewed false-statement convictions remain vacated. The Government may retry under instructions submitting every element, including materiality, to the jury. This is not an acquittal or a disposition of the separately treated equity-skimming conviction. This merits review is complete.

### Romer v. Evans, No. 94-1039

Merits decision, May 20, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 10, 1995, in October Term 1995; reported at 517 U.S. 620. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Colorado Supreme Court, which affirmed a permanent injunction against Amendment 2 on October 11, 1994, 882 P.2d 1335, Nos. 94SA48/94SA128.

Colorado voters approved Amendment 2 on November 3, 1992, by 813,966 to 710,151; it was certified December 16. The suit began November 12. The amendment added article II, §30 to the Colorado Constitution:

> No Protected Status Based on Homosexual, Lesbian, or Bisexual Orientation. Neither the State of Colorado, through any of its branches or departments, nor any of its agencies, political subdivisions, municipalities or school districts, shall enact, adopt or enforce any statute, regulation, ordinance or policy whereby homosexual, lesbian or bisexual orientation, conduct, practices or relationships shall constitute or otherwise be the basis of or entitle any person or class of persons to have or claim any minority status quota preferences, protected status or claim of discrimination. This Section of the Constitution shall be in all respects self-executing.

The authoritative state construction establishes withdrawal of existing orientation-based nondiscrimination protection and foreclosure of similar or greater protection through ordinary state and local legislative, executive and judicial channels unless the state constitution changes. The prohibition reaches the State itself, its agencies and schools, as well as local regulation in public and private settings. It is not confined to quotas, one ordinance or local preemption leaving the state legislature free to act. The Court assumes, without deciding, that generally applicable criminal, tort, contract and public-service protections remain available.

Evans I, 854 P.2d 1270 (1993), applied political-process strict scrutiny at the preliminary stage. After trial, the courts sustained a permanent injunction on that ground. The trial court rejected suspect or quasi-suspect status; plaintiffs did not appeal that ruling and do not concede its correctness. Respondents also pressed ordinary rational-basis invalidity below and here. The trial court and state majority did not decide that alternative because they applied strict scrutiny.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 7–2.** Amendment 2 violates the Equal Protection Clause under ordinary rational-basis review.

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Justices Scalia and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. All seven directly adopt the same rational-basis ground, its complete explanation and qualifications, and the full injunction. Justice Scalia dissents, joined throughout by Justice Thomas.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Amendment 2's class-specific disability fails ordinary rational-basis review

**Controlling proposition:** The Fourteenth Amendment forbids this combined withdrawal of existing nondiscrimination protection and constitutional foreclosure of comparable protection through ordinary government for the identified class. The amendment's established operation bears no rational relationship to the asserted legitimate interests. This conclusion does not depend on suspect status, political-process strict scrutiny, disappearance of generally applicable protection or a finding about every voter's motive.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court, joined by the seven Justices identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** Ordinary rationality permits reasonable predictions, imperfect categories and conceivable legitimate grounds without formal findings or empirical certainty. It does not require the least restrictive means. But the justification must relate to the actual disadvantage. Heller's proof holding, Moreno and Cleburne preserve that inquiry; Heller's family-participation holding shows why a narrower alternative alone is insufficient.

Uniformity explains a common rule, not why this class alone becomes constitutionally ineligible for protection from state as well as local authorities. Regulatory restraint can explain rejecting particular private obligations; it does not explain prohibiting ordinary public policies and protection across nonintimate relationships. Scarcity permits priorities, but does not explain a blanket prohibition regardless of the authority's resources or the protection's nature. Denver and Wisconsin findings of no added cost or impaired enforcement reinforce that conclusion without requiring empirical refutation of every possible saving.

Religious and intimate-association burdens are real concerns. Hurley likewise protects actual private expression, not a general right to discriminate in ordinary services. Exemptions illustrate the particular settings these interests concern; their availability alone is not decisive, and no municipality's exemption is finally approved. Those concerns do not rationally explain the amendment's prohibition across public and impersonal commercial settings.

Parents remain free to teach their moral convictions; no demonstrated connection makes nondiscrimination protection prevent that teaching, and government need not endorse every private belief. Stopping one side's political objectives or avoiding its participation because the issue is divisive cannot independently justify this disability. Bowers's specified-conduct holding does not explain barring protection by orientation throughout ordinary public and commercial life. Morality was preserved, but this conclusion neither invalidates every conduct-based moral judgment nor overrules Bowers.

The amendment's structure and established operation, rather than a free-standing hostility finding, expose the missing connection. Its class-wide disadvantage therefore falls within Moreno/Cleburne's prohibition. The Court imposes no general duty to enact nondiscrimination legislation.

##### II. The preserved ordinary-review ground supports the complete existing injunction

**Controlling proposition:** The Court may affirm on the fully presented rational-basis alternative without adopting the lower court's strict-scrutiny rationale. The established constitutional defect extends throughout the inseparable amendment, warranting preservation of the complete injunction without a new merits remand or a federal rewriting of state law.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's Opinion of the Court; the same seven Justices join in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The amendment, authoritative construction, asserted interests, relevant findings and adversarial ordinary-review arguments permit legal resolution now. Failure of strict scrutiny below does not itself establish failure of rational-basis review; Part I independently decides that question. Nor does the Court rely on an unconditional State concession that it could not satisfy strict scrutiny.

The State sought severance of orientation from conduct, practices and relationships. Colorado's court applied autonomy and enacting intent, concluding that those terms identify the same class and are inseparable. Deleting one description leaves the same constitutional disability. Because the defect found here permeates that common operation, no independent lawful remainder is established. The injunction corresponds to the violation rather than disabling a separately lawful structure. No new federal severability rule or universal facial-challenge formula is announced.

States retain substantial authority over governmental organization and substantive policy. That authority does not immunize a state constitutional provision from the Fourteenth Amendment. The continued ability to seek a constitutional amendment does not cure the particular unequal barrier to ordinary protection. Yet a policy defeat, referendum, repeal or constitutional reservation is not unconstitutional merely because it affects an identifiable group.

The Court neither reopens the unappealed classification issue nor adopts a fundamental right of every group to every lawmaking level. Particular ordinances, religious exemptions and future expression conflicts remain for their own cases. Reported separate First Amendment claims are neither decided nor presumed abandoned; their existence requires no new hearing or remand in this disposition.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

- FCC v. Beach Communications, 508 U.S. 307: preserves conceivable legitimate grounds, justified economic generalizations and the absence of a formal-findings requirement, together with its Moreno/Cleburne limit. It supplies no categorical immunity for every express group classification.
- Heller v. Doe, June 24, 1993: applies Souter's controlling invalidation of the unequal confinement-proof burden because the proposed differences did not justify the actual disadvantage. Kennedy's separate controlling family-participation holding preserves a relevant informational generalization with counsel, effective contest and independent adjudication; a narrower alternative alone does not defeat rationality. No new scrutiny tier follows from either holding.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S. 528, and Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432: apply the limits on bare harm and irrational prejudice while preserving relevant distinctions. Neither requires a finding about each elector's private motives.
- Hurley, June 19, 1995: preserves the private parade's expressive composition and ordinary nondiscrimination applications. Customer identity is not automatically a message, and ordinary service is not categorically expressive association. Swanner and American Life League retain their application-specific religious-liberty inquiries and actual RFRA burdens; no automatic exemption or transfer of those burdens results here.
- Hunter v. Erickson, 393 U.S. 385, and Washington v. Seattle School District No. 1, 458 U.S. 457: retain their racial political-restructuring settings. James v. Valtierra, 402 U.S. 137, Gordon v. Lance, 403 U.S. 1, and Crawford v. Board of Education, 458 U.S. 527, preclude treating every referendum, neutral voting rule or policy revision as unconstitutional exclusion. The Court does not adopt a new fundamental-right extension of those cases.
- Bowers v. Hardwick, 478 U.S. 186: remains in force on its specified-conduct question. The Court rejects the proposed justification for this wider eligibility-for-protection disability without deciding an unpresented overruling or declaring every conduct-based moral judgment illegitimate.
- Adarand, June 12, 1995: adopts a common substantial-justification standard for governmental racial classifications and retains the distinction between meaningful opportunity and entitlement to the requested benefit. It creates no sexual-orientation tier. Klinger preserves comparison directed to the actual challenged opportunity and distinguishes express classifications from purposeful discrimination in genuinely neutral decisions; Armstrong's federal-charging discovery threshold does not govern this express amendment.
- Dalton, March 18, 1996: preserves relief commensurate with an established violation and independent lawful operation. No general facial-review formula displaces the present inseparability analysis. Miller's abortion-specific method and 44 Liquormart's commercial-speech advancement and fit requirements supply neither a scrutiny tier nor an empirical burden here.

No precedent is overruled. The Court does not adopt Scott's separate lower-court Privileges or Immunities theory or the dissent's conditional Salerno argument as an additional holding.

Effective May 20, 1996, Amendment 2 cannot be enforced. Its established combination of withdrawing existing protection and making the identified class ineligible for comparable protection through ordinary government fails rational-basis review. The full injunction remains because that defect extends throughout the inseparable measure.

Ordinary policy choices and reasonable generalizations remain permissible. The decision creates no suspect or quasi-suspect classification, universal political-access right, evidentiary checklist or entitlement to any particular ordinance. It assumes no loss of all generally applicable legal protection and decides no broader general-law effect. Specific religious, intimate-association and expressive claims retain their own governing rules. Bowers remains in force, and no nationwide nondiscrimination code, quota requirement or damages remedy is established.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** The two Justices would reverse the reviewed equal-protection judgment and remove that foundation for the injunction, with remand bounded by any independently preserved unresolved claims. They reject an extension of Hunter and Seattle into a fundamental entitlement of every group to pursue legislation at every governmental level, and they would sustain the amendment under ordinary rationality.

They accept its full restriction on state as well as local institutions, including public and nonintimate settings. In their view, regulatory restraint, religious and associational choice, finite enforcement resources and statewide uniformity rationally support withholding the disputed antidiscrimination protection. Reasonable predictions and imperfect coverage suffice; narrower exemptions are not constitutionally mandatory. The breadth implements the substantive policy rather than defeating its connection to those purposes. Constitutional reservation does not immunize the policy from federal review, but neither makes an otherwise rational policy unconstitutional.

Bowers is a sufficient additional premise in their reasoning: constitutional power to criminalize the specified conduct supports the lesser choice of withholding the disputed protection. Conduct-based moral judgment is distinct from hatred of persons, and orientation may serve as a proxy in this noncriminal policy. They do not claim Bowers literally adjudicated Amendment 2 or that every moral dislike is legitimate. Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid bare harm; the dissent finds legitimate policy grounds here. Heller's controlling proof holding is distinguished as addressing an inadequately justified confinement risk, not displaced by its opposing opinion.

Their conditional facial argument also respects the state construction. If orientation, conduct, practices and relationships identify the same class, the Bowers inference, on their view, reaches the common operation. If a distinct orientation-only application is instead posited, constitutionally permissible conduct applications would defeat wholesale invalidation under their Salerno argument. That contention depends on their rejection of the Court's defect throughout the actual class-wide operation; it does not independently answer that defect. They order no severance, find no conduct by every class member and rely on no unverified admission by a respondent.

Scalia's commercial-speech position supplies no general prohibition of conduct-based moral legislation. Thomas's rejection of suppressing independently protected truthful speech, and his separate rule against keeping lawful purchasers ignorant to manipulate their choices, remain confined to that speech setting; he does not extend them into a universal bar on the different Bowers inference. Neither Justice treats actual religious conflicts as automatic exemptions for every ordinance. Their proposed remand would not adjudicate surviving First Amendment claims, invent a new hearing or authorize enforcement despite any independent lawful restraint. Their grounds and disposition are noncontrolling.

#### Source Notes

The amendment's text, authoritative minimum construction, trial findings and severability ruling appear in the Colorado proceedings, including Evans I, 854 P.2d 1270, and the reviewed opinion, 882 P.2d 1335. The parties' submissions establish their competing arguments; advocacy does not itself establish a finding or concession. The reproduced state opinion lacks printed pages B15 and B19. No absent filing, litigant default or additional fact is inferred from that omission; the supported text, construction and findings suffice for the questions decided.

Morality was preserved below; child welfare was not reasserted on the merits appeal. The trial findings concerning Denver and Wisconsin do not erase the State's competing claims about limited Colorado resources, unmet enforcement targets and future demands. Those predictions remain distinct from historical findings and from the proof required by strict scrutiny. The Court decides no individual ordinance's exemption, broader general-law effect, unappealed suspect-status question or reported separate First Amendment claim. No unconditional concession of strict-scrutiny failure or universal voter-motive finding is assumed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Colorado Supreme Court's judgment is affirmed, and the permanent injunction against enforcement of Amendment 2 remains effective in full. The constitutional defect extends throughout the amendment's common class-specific operation; the authoritative state severability determination supplies no independent remainder.

The Court's merits review is complete. No new merits hearing, rational-basis hearing, severance proceeding, factual inquiry, damages award, quota entitlement or enactment of a particular nondiscrimination ordinance is ordered. Any independently preserved unresolved claim remains subject to its existing procedural requirements. The Court decides no separate First Amendment claim and expresses no view on whether further proceedings are needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies the existing demanding intermediate scrutiny, not strict scrutiny or a least-restrictive-means test. Craig v. Boren supplies the important-objective and substantial-relationship requirements; Mississippi University for Women v. Hogan places the burden on the State and requires justification for the discriminatory allocation itself. Hogan's exceedingly persuasive justification describes that burden. J.E.B. rejects sex as a conclusive proxy for individual capacity, while Kelley permits genuinely justified sex-conscious educational choices. Klinger requires comparison of the particular benefit and gives neither immunity nor automatic liability merely because whole institutions differ.

VMI's integrated adversative method has educational value. The accepted findings and predictions that coeducation would change privacy arrangements, physical training and interpersonal methods are not discarded. Their constitutional sufficiency remains a legal question. Preserving an effective program and supplying institutional diversity do not, on this record, adequately explain why its distinctive public opportunity must be reserved to men. Some women can meet the existing physical standards and seek the experience; preferences attributed to most women cannot settle those individuals' entitlement to consideration. The presence of an exceptional individual does not by itself invalidate every sex classification, but these accepted general benefits do not establish sufficient fit for this complete exclusion.

A genuine present educational objective need not have been articulated in 1839 or set out in a contemporaneous written declaration. Litigation timing alone does not establish pretext, and this holding finds neither deceit nor hostility. The failure is the insufficiency of the justification for the actual allocation, not the absence of all pedagogical benefit. Real physical differences and appropriate accommodations remain relevant. The Court does not decide that every single-sex institution or program is unconstitutional.

##### Part II — Adequacy of the proposed alternative

**Holding and operative rule:** A separate program offered to cure an unconstitutional exclusion must actually remedy the denied educational opportunity, taking account of its material tangible and intangible benefits; a shared general objective or equal annual public subsidy does not alone establish adequacy. VWIL's approved design does not cure this exclusion even assuming full performance of its funding and alumni commitments, so the approval judgment in No. 94-1941 must be reversed.

**Controlling explanation:** Sweatt v. Painter supplies the insight that educational opportunity includes the training, resources, standing and connections an institution actually provides. Its racial setting does not establish a universal prohibition on separate education by sex. Klinger directs the inquiry to the benefit denied; neither each isolated disparity nor every institutional difference is an independent constitutional wrong.

VWIL offers real education: leadership courses, seminars, service, an externship, physical education and four years of ROTC at Mary Baldwin. Its cooperative method is a legitimate educational choice. The promised annual State payment equals VMI's per-student appropriation, ordinary student cost is no greater, and a separate endowment supports the program. The VMI Alumni Association promises recruitment assistance and the same access to its placement network, externships and advancement opportunities. The Court credits those promises fully and assumes faithful performance.

That assumption does not supply VMI's integrated military and adversative experience, its degree, its institutional history and standing, or its total resources. VWIL lacks VMI's on-campus engineering option; the alternative requires three years at Mary Baldwin and two years at Washington University, with private tuition and no proposed public discount for the final two years. The cumulative differences in training, accessible curriculum, facilities, resources and credential leave the particular opportunity materially unequal. Network access is not absent, but equal access to a network does not erase the other differences or assure identical outcomes.

Missouri v. Jenkins distinguishes installing and funding a program from effectively repairing an established injury. Monitoring faithful implementation cannot make this substantively inadequate design sufficient. The Court finds no breach of a promise or unreported implementation failure. It prescribes no endowment ratio, identical campus, expenditure minimum or guarantee of equal careers, and it does not question Mary Baldwin's independent educational value.

##### Part III — Effective relief and lawful State choice

**Holding and operative rule:** Virginia must promptly eliminate the established denial through admission on lawful terms or an actually adequate lawful alternative, with responsibility for lawful remedial design remaining initially with the State and implementation supervised below. If Virginia continues the same publicly supported VMI opportunity without establishing an adequate lawful alternative, qualified women must receive consideration without the categorical sex bar; the existence of VWIL or promises of future improvement cannot justify indefinite continuation of the injury.

**Controlling explanation:** The initial liability remand gave Virginia a choice of lawful responses, not advance approval of any separate program it might propose. Reversal of the present plan does not forfeit the State's responsibility to design an effective cure. Missouri v. Jenkins requires relief connected to the proved injury and distinguishes effective repair from general educational improvement. Lewis v. Casey likewise preserves government choice among effective means and permits necessary implementation without enlarging relief beyond the established wrong; its prison-conditions statutory restrictions supply no additional test here.

Appropriate privacy, physical-training and operational accommodations may preserve legitimate educational needs while providing effective opportunity. The Constitution does not require abandonment of genuine qualifications or admission of a named applicant regardless of them. Nor does this judgment order closure, identical public programs, a quota, equal career outcomes or a particular physical standard. An actual lawful change in State involvement remains different from retaining the same public institution under another label; no proposed withdrawal arrangement is preapproved. The lower courts must secure effective correction and assess any genuinely material remedial proposal consistently with this decision, without a newly required hearing, special master or additional procedural framework.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide the general validity of private single-sex education, every parallel undergraduate arrangement, every sex-conscious opportunity measure, or any independent Title IX claim. Ordinary respect for underlying factual findings remains intact. No damages, individual admission award, exact admissions date, prescribed training adjustment or finding about unreported 1995–1996 implementation is entered.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Craig v. Boren, 429 U.S. 190: applied for important-objective and substantial-relationship scrutiny; the scrutiny tier is unchanged.
- Mississippi University for Women v. Hogan, 458 U.S. 718: applied for the State's demanding burden and genuine justification for unequal allocation. Its nursing-school facts do not decide this integrated military program automatically, and its reservation of broader separate-education questions survives. A statutory admissions exemption supplies no constitutional permission.
- J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 U.S. 127: its prohibition on using sex as a conclusive individual-capacity proxy informs this application. Its peremptory-strike procedure is not made the proof framework for college admissions.
- Kelley v. Board of Trustees of the University of Illinois, decided May 22, 1995: its genuine-objective and substantial-fit rule remains governing; its permitted sex-conscious restructuring does not create an admissions exemption or require a compensatory objective for every lawful distinction.
- Klinger v. Department of Corrections, decided December 4, 1995: applied to the particular public opportunity. Material institutional differences remain relevant, but whole-institution difference supplies neither categorical immunity nor automatic liability.
- Sweatt v. Painter, 339 U.S. 629: its consideration of tangible and intangible educational opportunity informs remedial adequacy; its racial setting is not extended into a categorical ban on all single-sex education.
- Missouri v. Jenkins, decided June 12, 1995: applied for effective, violation-linked repair rather than mere program installation or generalized educational improvement; its distinct desegregation obligations are unchanged.
- Lewis v. Casey, decided June 24, 1996: its requirement of effective relief tied to the proved injury and genuine government choice of means informs the remand. Its prisoner-claim and prison-conditions statutory rules are not transferred to this admissions dispute.
No earlier holding is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 26, 1996, the settled intermediate-scrutiny standard governs this public admissions classification, with a controlling application invalidating VMI's complete exclusion of women. An alternative selected to repair that exclusion must provide an effective cure of the material opportunity denied; this VWIL design is inadequate even on full performance of every stated commitment. The decision adds these admissions and remedial applications without raising the scrutiny tier, requiring institutional identity, prohibiting all single-sex education, or displacing supported sex-conscious measures. State design discretion survives within the requirement of prompt and effective correction. The dissent supplies no rule of decision.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, dissenting; no joins.** Scalia applies the existing important-objective and substantial-relationship standard but reaches the opposite conclusion on the accepted record. In his view, preserving VMI's integrated method and a real option within a diverse educational system is an important actual objective. The findings about the value of single-sex education and the changes coeducation would cause establish substantial fit. Intermediate scrutiny permits an imperfect fit; some women's ability to meet physical standards does not alone defeat it. He distinguishes Hogan because its permission for men to audit classes undermined the claimed need for exclusion, whereas VMI's residential and educational methods operate together. His position does not treat tradition as immunity, replace heightened scrutiny with rational-basis review, or exempt unlike institutions from comparison.

His primary disposition would reverse liability in No. 94-2107 and, consequently, vacate the compulsory remedy in No. 94-1941 insofar as it rests on that rejected liability, remanding to remove those dependent obligations. That would not prohibit Virginia from voluntarily continuing VWIL.

Separately, assuming liability remains established, he would affirm the existing VWIL approval with its monitoring requirements. He considers the subsidized leadership education, four-year ROTC, endowed support and fully credited alumni assistance meaningfully comparable despite different methods, engineering costs, credentials, resources and prestige. In his view, rejecting the whole alternative gives those differences more constitutional weight than intermediate scrutiny permits. His conditional approval requires faithful funding and alumni performance, the District Court's retained jurisdiction and six-month reports, and the appellate directions for qualified leadership, recruitment and promotion, near-term funding and continuing professional review; promises are not findings of completed performance. This conditional ground neither accepts liability nor replaces his primary cross-docket disposition.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment at 976 F.2d 890 in No. 94-2107 is affirmed. The judgment at 44 F.3d 1229 in No. 94-1941 is reversed, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with Parts I–III. Liability remains established; the existing plan's approval cannot authorize continued unequal exclusion. The lower courts must secure a prompt effective cure while permitting Virginia to design lawful implementation and consider any genuinely material adequate alternative. No fixed admissions date, immediate individual admission, special mandate date or retained Supreme Court proceeding is ordered.
