### Espinosa v. Florida, No. 91-7390

Limited grant and summary merits decision, June 29, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

On petition for certiorari to the Supreme Court of Florida, 589 So.2d 887 (1991), which affirmed convictions and a death sentence. There was no oral argument. The Court takes the preserved question whether a vague heinous, atrocious or cruel aggravator can infect a sentence through a jury recommendation entitled to great weight despite the judge's direct use of valid aggravators. Although the petition seeks relief from conviction and sentence on additional grounds, this grant and contemporaneous summary decision form a single action limited to the sentencing issue. All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

#### Public Action and Judgment

The Court grants leave to proceed without prepayment of fees and certiorari limited to the preserved aggravating-instruction question, reverses the rejection of the sentencing claim, and remands.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preserved death-sentencing claim | Summary reversal and remand, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | White opposes summary disposition and would grant review and set argument; Scalia dissents and would deny review | Lawful reweighing or Chapman harmless-error review must address the recommendation's influence; a new sentencing proceeding is required if no lawful cure is available. |

White supplies no present merits vote. The guilt judgment remains outside this disposition, and no automatic life sentence follows.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A legally influential recommendation cannot rest on an inadequately narrowed aggravator

**Controlling proposition:** When a weighing State requires its sentencing judge to give great weight to a jury's recommendation, an inadequately narrowed aggravating instruction to that jury can infect the final sentence even if the judge directly finds only valid aggravators. The especially wicked, evil, atrocious or cruel instruction given here lacks the guidance the Eighth Amendment requires; the judge's separate findings do not by themselves cure the recommendation's legal influence.

**Authority:** The per curiam Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, seven Justices, for this rule and its application. White's preference for argument is not a present merits vote. Scalia rejects the weighing premise in dissent. The express seven-Justice majority supplies the controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that constitutional guidance must follow the actual allocation of sentencing responsibility. Godfrey, Maynard and Shell require an aggravating category to distinguish the cases to which it applies; the ordinary words given this jury supply no adequate boundary. Stringer and Sochor distinguish eligibility supported by valid factors from the later weighing that selects death. Florida law makes the jury's general recommendation consequential by requiring the judge to give it great weight, whether it recommends life or death. Mills does not permit an assumption that jurors ignored an authorized invalid category, and Walton's presumption that a judge follows the law means the recommendation received the weight state law required. The judge thus cannot be treated as wholly independent of the defective recommendation. Baldwin permits a different allocation of sentencing roles; it does not eliminate an actual duty of deference. The Court does not find that particular jurors selected particular aggravators. It decides the legal effect of the unguided recommendation and preserves the State's ability to structure a constitutionally guided sentencing process.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420 (1980), Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356 (1988), and Shell v. Mississippi, 498 U.S. 1 (1990): apply their requirement of meaningful guidance for a capital aggravating category; the instruction here is insufficiently narrowed.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992: applies the distinction between valid eligibility and constitutionally defective weighing; surviving valid aggravators alone do not remove the error.
- Sochor v. Florida, June 8, 1992: applies its weighing-error rule while distinguishing its independent state preservation bar to a different jury claim and its separate factual-invalidity issue.
- Mills v. Maryland, 486 U.S. 367 (1988): applies the refusal to presume that jurors disregard an instruction's constitutionally defective route; the Court attributes no factor-specific verdict to the jury.
- Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 (1990): preserves genuine judicial sentencing and the presumption that judges apply legal narrowing; distinguishes a system in which a separate unguided recommendation carries required weight.
- Baldwin v. Alabama, 472 U.S. 372 (1985), and Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447 (1984): preserve States' latitude in allocating sentencing responsibility; that latitude does not validate influential unguided weighing.
- Tedder v. State, 322 So.2d 908 (Fla. 1975), Smith v. State, 515 So.2d 182 (Fla. 1987), and Grossman v. State, 525 So.2d 833 (Fla. 1988): supply the state-law requirement to give great weight to jury recommendations of life or death.
- Smalley v. State, 546 So.2d 720 (Fla. 1989): rejects its premise that the jury's advisory designation alone makes this vague instruction constitutionally immaterial.

##### Correction must address the recommendation's influence on the death sentence

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the rejection of this sentencing claim requires further proceedings that remove the influence of the invalid jury recommendation through lawful reweighing or harmless-error review satisfying Chapman beyond a reasonable doubt. If no lawful cure is available, a new sentencing proceeding is required; valid remaining aggravators alone do not establish a cure, and neither an automatic life sentence nor disturbance of the guilt judgment follows.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join the per curiam opinion's remedy instruction. They expressly apply the existing Stringer, Sochor, Clemons and Chapman correction requirements to the two-stage defect found here. No agreement is attributed to White on this unargued merits question or to Scalia on the need for correction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction must reach the error actually found. Clemons permits an authorized reviewing court to reweigh valid aggravation against mitigation or determine that an invalid factor was harmless; it does not treat a surviving majority of factors as an answer. Chapman supplies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt harmlessness standard. Stringer and Sochor require an actual lawful cure of invalid weighing, and Parker requires attention to the sentencing record that genuinely existed. Here the jury's general recommendation carried legally required weight into the judge's decision. The judge's four valid aggravators and two mitigators therefore do not establish that this indirect error had no effect. Further review must account for that channel of influence, rather than repeat the premise that the judge alone imposed sentence. The Court does not undertake its own balancing, choose a sentence, or decide that Florida law authorizes every proposed appellate cure. It leaves those proceedings to the state court under the stated constitutional limits. The conviction and unrelated petition questions are outside this disposition.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 (1990): applies lawful appellate reweighing or harmless-error correction; merely counting valid aggravators does not cure the error.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applies harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt; no lesser standard or burden on the defendant is created.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992, and Sochor v. Florida, June 8, 1992: apply actual cure requirements to the recommendation's transmitted influence; no unreasoned harmlessness label suffices.
- Parker v. Dugger, 498 U.S. 308 (1991): applies review grounded in the real sentencing record and mitigation, not an assumed alternative record.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

No automatic reduction to life, retrial of guilt, adjudication of unrelated aggravators, factor-by-factor jury finding, or collateral-review gateway rule follows. The petition's severance and mitigation/time/funding/proportionality questions are not granted or adjudicated in this limited summary action; this action does not separately deny them. No prior Supreme Court decision is overruled.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 29, 1992, a jury recommendation that receives great legal weight is part of the constitutionally relevant weighing process. Its unguided aggravating category can contaminate the final sentence through that required influence even when the judge directly uses valid factors. The Court applies the anti-vagueness and weighing rules described above, distinguishes genuine independent judicial sentencing, and requires correction of this indirect influence. Stringer and Sochor supply the actual-cure requirement; Chapman supplies review for harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. Lawful reweighing or that review must remove the defect, with a new proceeding required if no lawful cure exists. States retain latitude in assigning sentencing roles. The decision creates no automatic life sentence, collateral-review rule or presumption of factor-specific jury findings, and overrules no Supreme Court precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White would obtain oral argument before deciding the merits. His procedural dissent expresses no merits position on the preserved claim. Scalia would deny review because the valid aggravators already satisfy the constitutionally required narrowing of the death-eligible class. Following his positions in Walton and Sochor, which reject a separate constitutional requirement for the later weighing stage, he rejects the premise that the Eighth Amendment requires removing an invalid factor from that stage. His one-Justice view does not control.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The limited petition and summary merits action end in remand to the Supreme Court of Florida. That court must supply lawful reweighing or harmless-error review under Chapman's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard addressing the recommendation's influence. If no lawful cure is available, a new sentencing proceeding is required. The convictions and unrelated petition questions receive no new Supreme Court merits adjudication. This disposition determines no later sentence or state finding of harmlessness.

### Stringer v. Black, No. 90-6616

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

Argued December 9, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 909 F.2d 111](https://static.case.law/f2d/909/html/0111-01.html).

Question presented: Whether Teague bars consideration of the vague-aggravator claim because Maynard or the requested application to Mississippi’s weighing system announced a new rule after Stringer’s sentence became final.

Stringer’s death sentence became final on February 19, 1985. The jury found three aggravating circumstances, including an undefined especially heinous, atrocious or cruel factor, in Mississippi’s system requiring aggravators to be weighed against mitigation. The state appellate court upheld the sentence. The federal district court denied habeas relief both on procedural-default and merits grounds. Following an earlier remand for consideration in light of Clemons, the Fifth Circuit again denied relief on Teague new-rule grounds without deciding the procedural bar. Stringer seeks reversal of that retroactivity ruling. The State invokes finality and the asserted distinction between weighing and nonweighing systems. Whether adequate state correction actually occurred and whether procedural default prevents relief remain for further proceedings. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 222](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-222.pdf).

The Court reverses the Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remands, 6–3. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor and Kennedy support reversal; Justices Scalia, Souter and Thomas dissent. Justice Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor. That opinion controls both the determination that the claim invokes an existing rule and the requirement of genuine reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review in this weighing system.

For a sentence final on February 19, 1985, Godfrey already prohibited the unguided heinousness aggravation later addressed in Maynard, and applying that protection to Mississippi’s weighing system does not create a new rule. Teague therefore does not bar consideration of this claim on the ground that Maynard or the corrective principles discussed in Clemons postdated finality.

Justice Kennedy’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor, controls this determination.

The Court holds that Teague protects reasonable reliance on existing law, not a refusal to recognize what that law already required. Butler requires attention both to the rule announced by a later decision and to whether its proposed application extends the precedent in a new way. Godfrey had already rejected an aggravating phrase that gave the sentencer no meaningful guidance. Maynard applied that same prohibition to closely equivalent language. Mississippi’s formal weighing of aggravation against mitigation makes the defect consequential to sentence selection; it does not exempt the system from the preexisting constitutional protection. Zant expressly distinguished its nonweighing arrangement and supplies no affirmative permission to weigh an invalid factor. The State’s finality argument therefore does not make this particular application novel. Clemons identifies lawful methods of correcting the error rather than first creating the right to challenge it. The Court does not hold that every later capital decision applies retroactively, and it decides no Teague exception or unresolved procedural default.

The Court applies the existing rule of Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288: the claim seeks an old rule at the relevant finality date. The Court applies Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407: both novelty of the later rule and novelty of its application must be considered. The Court applies Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420: meaningful guidance was already required for the heinousness aggravator. Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356, applies Godfrey’s existing rule to equivalent language. The Court applies Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738, for its permissible methods of correction; Clemons did not create a new protection in this setting.

When a capital sentencer weighs a constitutionally invalid aggravating factor against mitigation, remaining valid aggravators do not by themselves cure the error. The state judicial system must supply genuine reweighing without the invalid factor, where authorized, or constitutional harmless-error review establishing that the invalid factor did not affect the sentence; the adequacy of any cure here remains for further proceedings.

Justice Kennedy’s six-Justice opinion also controls the weighing and correction rule. Its application and any eventual conditional remedy remain reserved.

The Court holds that an invalid factor placed on the statutory scale cannot be ignored simply by counting the valid factors that remain. Zant sustained a system in which the invalid factor did not do the formal weighing work present here. Barclay also involved actual review of the sentencing choice and a different kind of invalidity; it does not permit automatic affirmance of this constitutional weighing error. Clemons recognizes that a properly authorized state appellate court can itself weigh valid aggravation against mitigation, or can determine under constitutional harmless-error principles that the error did not affect the sentence. Chapman places the harmlessness burden on the State beyond a reasonable doubt. Bare proportionality review or an assertion that other aggravators exist does not establish either correction. The Court leaves the actual state review and the unresolved procedural-default defense to the lower courts. If habeas relief becomes warranted after those matters are resolved, a conditional remedy may permit lawful state correction; death eligibility is not automatically negated.

The Court distinguishes Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862: its nonweighing holding does not automatically cure a weighing-system error. The Court distinguishes Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939: its actual review and state-law invalidity do not justify uncorrected constitutional aggravation. The Court applies Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738: genuine appellate reweighing or constitutional harmlessness can correct a weighing error. The Court applies Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, to the harmless-error route: the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court distinguishes Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231: duplication in narrowing does not answer vague-factor weighting and cannot supply a prefinality reliance premise.

Effective March 9, the Court applies Teague to this specific Mississippi claim and makes the weighing/nonweighing remedial distinction controlling at the stated scope. No universal retroactivity exception or automatic cure from surviving aggravators is added. Genuine reweighing and constitutional harmlessness remain different routes. Actual cure, procedural default and any eventual conditional-writ terms are unresolved.

Souter, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents because Zant reserved the weighing question and Barclay did not clearly require the precise constitutional correction now demanded. In his view, reasonable jurists at finality could distinguish constitutionally protected conduct from a vague aggravator and regard the Mississippi application as unsettled. The majority holds that the statutory weighing role strengthens the old guidance requirement rather than making it new.

Supreme Court review ends with remand to the Fifth Circuit. The preserved procedural-default defense remains undecided; the lower courts must consider it and any adequate state correction. If relief is otherwise warranted, a conditional writ may permit resentencing or authorized appellate cure. No unconditional writ, immediate release, automatic life sentence or finding that a particular state cure failed is made.

**End of entry: Stringer v. Black, Merits decision, March 9, 1992.**

### United States v. Olano, No. 91-1306

Merits decision, 1993-04-26

#### Chronology and Posture

United States v. Olano; No. 91-1306; October Term 1992. Argued: 1992-12-09; decided 1993-04-26. Source locator: 507 U.S. 725 (1993). Ninth Circuit, 934 F.2d 1425 (1991). On writ of certiorari to that court.

Question: Whether the unobjected-to presence of instructed alternate jurors during deliberations permits reversal under Rule 52(b), and how error, plainness, substantial rights and remedial discretion differ.

Two alternates remained during deliberations after a three-month joint trial; the court instructed them not to participate. One later left at his request. Counsel did not make a timely objection. The Government concedes error and plainness on the assumption that Rule 24(c) was not waivable; the Court does not decide that assumption. No participation or chilling effect was shown, and respondents did not request a hearing. The Ninth Circuit separately reversed convictions for insufficiency, then vacated the remaining convictions under a per se alternate-presence rule and did not reach other appellate issues.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1992-12-09) and decision (1993-04-26). No recusal or other nonparticipation is established.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 6–3, as to the new-trial ruling based on alternate-juror presence.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Forfeiture, waiver and the limits on plain-error authority

**Controlling proposition:** Rule 52(b) permits correction of a forfeited legal error only if it is plain and affects substantial rights; valid intentional relinquishment of a known right extinguishes error, while mere failure timely to assert a right does not. Plain means clear or obvious under current law, and the defendant ordinarily must show prejudice affecting the outcome; errors first clarified on appeal, special outcome-independent categories and justified prejudice presumptions are reserved.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that failure to object changes the conditions of appellate relief without making a violated rule disappear. Johnson v. Zerbst supplies the distinction between intentional surrender and omission; whether a right can be waived and what participation or procedures waiver requires depend on the particular right. Young reads Rule 52(b) as a limited power, not two disjunctive routes based on either plainness or substantial rights. Its prejudice inquiry ordinarily examines the record, as harmless-error review does, but places persuasion on the defendant who forfeited the objection. This does not decide that every error demands a specific outcome showing. Fulminante preserves the distinction between trial errors and defects affecting the tribunal’s basic structure. Here the Government’s concessions permit the Court to assume error and plainness without resolving whether Rule 24(c) can be waived. They do not concede prejudice. The preserved constitutional error in Brecht arose under a different review posture and does not supply the burden here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458: applies intentional relinquishment; right-specific waiver requirements remain.
- United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1: applies limited plain-error authority and ordinary prejudice requirement.
- Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279: preserves structural/trial-error distinction without deciding every Rule 52(b) category.
- Brecht, April 21, 1993: distinguished; preserved Doyle habeas review does not control forfeited Rule 24(c) error.

##### Correction remains discretionary

**Controlling proposition:** Even when plain forfeited error affects substantial rights, correction under Rule 52(b) is permitted, not automatic, and should occur only when the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Actual innocence is not a necessary condition, but satisfaction of substantial rights alone does not satisfy this additional inquiry.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the word “may” leaves a separate remedial judgment to the reviewing court. Atkinson supplies its governing measure: the effect on the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Young carries that standard into Rule 52(b). Treating every prejudicial plain error as requiring correction would erase the discretion the Rule deliberately retains. The contrary argument that relief must always depend on actual innocence also fails. Innocence can make correction compelling, but serious injury to the administration of justice can exist independently of guilt. The collateral gateway addressed in Sawyer concerns a different procedural obstacle and does not rewrite this appellate rule. These distinctions prevent either an automatic reversal rule or an innocence-only restriction. Because respondents have not satisfied the preceding substantial-rights requirement, the Court does not decide whether this particular intrusion, had it been prejudicial, would warrant the exercise of remedial discretion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157: applies the independent fairness, integrity and public-reputation standard.
- United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1: applies discretionary, circumscribed correction.
- Sawyer, June 22, 1992: distinguished; capital actual-innocence gateway is not a universal limit on appellate correction.

##### Instructed alternates do not establish prejudice by presence alone

**Controlling proposition:** For these alternates, instructed not to participate, mere presence during deliberations neither establishes prejudice nor warrants a presumption of prejudice under Rule 52(b). Without a specific showing of participation or a chilling effect, respondents have not shown an effect on substantial rights; the per se new-trial ruling must be reversed.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deliberative privacy protects against improper influence, and the inquiry must attend to that purpose. Parker and Remmer examine intrusions for prejudice; Smith v. Phillips rejects a new trial merely because a juror encountered a potentially compromising situation. Those authorities do not make this Rule 24(c) violation harmless by definition, but they defeat treating every intrusion as automatically reversible. The alternates heard the same evidence, took the ordinary oath, and received an express direction not to participate. Richardson supports the ordinary assumption that jurors follow instructions. Respondents showed neither actual participation, including nonverbal influence, nor restraint of the regular jurors’ discussion, and the circumstances do not justify presuming either. The Court does not decide the Government’s burden had an objection been preserved. It also reserves what Rule 606(b) would permit at a hearing and whether an appellate court may order such a hearing on plain-error review; none was requested here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Parker v. Gladden, 385 U.S. 363, and Remmer v. United States, 347 U.S. 227: apply the focus on prejudicial influence, preserving appropriate presumptions in other circumstances.
- Smith v. Phillips, 455 U.S. 209: applies rejection of automatic retrial for every compromising contact.
- Richardson v. Marsh, 481 U.S. 200: applies the ordinary instruction-following presumption, not an irrebuttable cure.
- Zafiro, January 25, 1993: consistent record-specific instruction analysis; no blanket rule that instructions cure intrusions.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before: Rule 52(b) and Atkinson/Young supplied discretionary correction without this complete articulation. After: four separate requirements govern forfeited-error review; ordinary defendant prejudice burden and the no-presumed-prejudice result for instructed alternates apply. Valid waiver, new clarity on appeal, structural categories and Rule 606(b)/hearing authority remain reserved. Brecht’s Chapman rule for preserved Doyle habeas error and Zafiro’s record-specific instruction analysis remain unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy joins the Court. He emphasizes that the defendant’s burden is the usual framework, not necessarily the formulation for an error noticed by the court on its own initiative. Rule 24(c) remains independently obligatory; trial judges should not put counsel in the position of resisting a proposed departure from the prescribed jury structure. A preserved objection would place the burden of showing harmlessness on the Government, which could face substantial difficulty. None of those observations permits withholding an objection and demanding automatic reversal later.

Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, would affirm. Drawing on Vasquez v. Hillery and Gray v. Mississippi, he regards the privacy of deliberations as protecting the tribunal’s integrity independently of measurable verdict prejudice. He nonetheless distinguishes satisfaction of substantial rights from discretionary relief. A reasonable judge could decline reversal after considering the nature of the violation, counsel’s conduct and likely effect; his conclusion is that the Ninth Circuit did not abuse its broad supervisory discretion by granting relief here.

### Gilmore v. Taylor, No. 91-1738

Merits decision, 1993-06-07

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-02; decided 1993-06-07. Review of the Seventh Circuit, 954 F.2d 441 (1992), reversing denial of federal habeas relief and directing a conditional writ.

Question: Whether Teague permits collateral relief under Falconer’s rule for the interaction of Illinois murder and voluntary-manslaughter instructions.

Taylor received a murder sentence of thirty-five years after a trial at which he relied on provocation and related mitigation. The charge permitted a murder verdict upon the stated elements without requiring jurors first to resolve the separate manslaughter instructions. Illinois discretionary review ended June 4, 1987, and Taylor did not seek certiorari. The lower court noted that choosing that date or the later expiration of the certiorari period made no difference because Reddick and Falconer came after both. The Court likewise has no need to decide that date question. The Seventh Circuit treated Falconer as an old rule and ordered a writ unless Illinois elected to retry Taylor within 120 days. Reddick held as a matter of state law that the prosecution must disprove the mitigating mental state beyond a reasonable doubt. Falconer instead addresses the federal sequencing defect that could let jurors convict of murder without considering the lesser-offense path. The State concedes error under Falconer but preserves its Teague defense. The separate home-invasion sentence is not reviewed.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed, 7–2.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Falconer announces a new rule for this collateral proceeding

**Controlling proposition:** Falconer’s specific requirement governing the interaction of these murder and voluntary-manslaughter instructions was not dictated by precedent at Taylor’s finality and is therefore a new rule under Teague. The State’s concession of error under Falconer does not waive its preserved retroactivity objection, and this conclusion does not approve the charge on direct review or exclude all defense-instruction errors from due process.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a general protection against an unfair charge did not dictate Falconer’s particular result at finality. Cupp requires consideration of the whole instruction and trial, but does not settle every relationship between an offense instruction and a state affirmative defense. Patterson and Martin show that the constitutional burden on offense elements does not automatically govern every fact relevant to an affirmative defense. The strongest contrary argument is that these instructions permitted jurors to convict without considering the only meaningful route to manslaughter. That concern may support a constitutional rule; it does not establish that existing precedent compelled this specific rule. The Estelle decision preserves reasonable-likelihood review without supplying a finality-era Falconer holding. Graham’s capital-mitigation result rested on its own earlier capital authorities and does not make this noncapital sequencing rule old. Connecticut v. Johnson’s plurality concerns presumptions affecting elemental proof, not this defense sequence. Boyde’s later capital-instruction analysis does not establish that Falconer was dictated at 1987 finality. Judicial disagreement alone is not the test. The State preserved Teague despite conceding Falconer error, and the Court resolves only that threshold.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S.288: finality-era dictated-by-precedent inquiry applied.
- Cupp v. Naughten, 414 U.S.141: whole-charge fairness preserved but not treated as dictating this specific rule.
- Connecticut v. Johnson, 460 U.S.73: plurality discussion of elemental presumptions distinguished; it is not treated as a controlling holding compelling Falconer.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S.370: later capital-instruction reasonable-likelihood analysis distinguished; its date does not itself supply a 1987 finality-era source.
- Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S.197, and Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S.228: offense-element burdens distinguished from affirmative-defense allocations.
- Estelle v. McGuire, December 4, 1991: the whole-charge reasonable-likelihood holding preserved; it neither decides Falconer nor supplies a source existing at 1987 finality.
- Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993: the capital-mitigation holding distinguished on its earlier capital authorities.

##### Neither then-existing Teague exception permits this new instructional rule

**Controlling proposition:** The Falconer rule regulates trial procedure rather than removing conduct or a class of persons from the State’s substantive power to punish, and it is not a watershed procedural rule essential to the fundamental accuracy and fairness of the criminal process. Neither exception recognized by Teague at this event’s date permits its retroactive use to obtain the reviewed habeas relief.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). This proposition has direct majority support.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the character of the new rule prevents reliance on either Teague exception. The rule changes how the jury considers a defense within the trial; it does not make Taylor’s conduct constitutionally immune from punishment or exclude a class of persons from the penalty at issue. The substantive exception therefore does not apply. The watershed exception remains part of the governing law, but it is reserved for exceptional procedural changes fundamental to the accuracy and fairness of the criminal process. The possibility of a serious instructional error is not itself enough to place every new instruction rule within that category. The dissent emphasizes that jurors may have missed the only basis for a lesser offense. The majority does not dismiss that concern; it concludes that this specific sequencing rule does not reconstruct the process at the exceptional level Teague requires. No harmless-error ruling is necessary after the retroactivity bar, and both exceptions remain available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague, 489 U.S.288: both the substantive and watershed exceptions applied in their event-date form.
- Sawyer v. Smith, 497 U.S.227: the demanding watershed category applied; importance of a procedural protection alone is insufficient.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993: the preserved-Doyle Chapman holding remains distinct; harmlessness is not reached here.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana, June 1, 1993: the absence of a valid reasonable-doubt verdict is a different instructional defect and does not bypass Teague in this case.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before this decision: Teague’s exceptions remained available, Estelle supplied whole-charge review and Graham required effective capital mitigation on its specific earlier authorities. After this decision: seven Justices bar this Falconer claim as new and outside both exceptions. Neither the charge’s ultimate constitutional validity on direct review nor harmlessness is decided; the dissent’s watershed application is noncontrolling. No categorical due-process immunity for affirmative-defense instructions is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, joins the Court and emphasizes that Eddings and related capital-sentencing cases do not automatically dictate the sequencing of murder and affirmative-defense instructions in a noncapital trial. Patterson and Martin allow distinctions that the proposed analogy would obscure. Their concurrence adds no holding that every defense-instruction error is constitutionally irrelevant. Blackmun, joined by Stevens, dissents. Cupp’s protection against a fundamentally unfair charge, in their view, reaches instructions that permit jurors to return the greater verdict without evaluating the only basis for a lesser offense. The accused’s admission to the killing cannot fairly be separated from his offered explanation by a misleading route through the charge. They also regard the protection as within Teague’s watershed exception even if the rule is new, because preventing that bypass bears directly on reliable adjudication of the grade of guilt. They would preserve the Seventh Circuit’s conditional writ requiring Illinois to elect retrial within 120 days; that remedy is not the Court’s mandate.

### Caspari v. Bohlen, No. 92-1500

Merits decision, February 23, 1994

#### Chronology and Posture

510 U.S. 383. Argued December 6, 1993. Certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 979 F.2d 109, which reversed the denial of habeas relief and directed a conditional writ. The stated question concerned double jeopardy in repeated noncapital sentencing; Teague was argued in the petition and consistently asserted in the litigation. The Court determines whether that defense is fairly included and bars this writ.

All nine participated at argument and decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

#### Judgment

The Eighth Circuit judgment granting habeas relief is reversed, 8–1.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justice | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reach the Teague defense as fairly included | Consider, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Stevens | No exceptional-circumstances bypass |
| New-rule bar and resulting habeas judgment | Reverse, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Stevens | The conditional writ cannot rest on this claim |

The Court leaves the constitutional merits unresolved. Stevens would decline Teague and affirm the conditional writ on the merits; alternatively he would treat the confined application as old law. The formal disposition is reversal. Ordinary lower proceedings must implement that bar, without a new proof hearing, a replacement sentence or disturbance of other properly preserved claims not resolved here.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Reverse | All three holdings; ultimate constitutional merits reserved |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Affirm conditional relief | Rule 14 objection, trial-like adjudication and alternative old-rule analysis |

Blackmun joins the Court's entire threshold opinion without deciding against the constitutional merits of a future eligible claim. No separate opinion supplies a controlling alternative merits rule.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The preserved nonretroactivity defense is fairly included here

**Controlling proposition:** In this habeas case, Missouri's consistently asserted Teague defense is fairly included as a subsidiary limit on applying the very constitutional rule and granting the writ challenged by the petition, although the stated question names the double-jeopardy issue rather than Teague. The Court therefore considers that nonjurisdictional defense; preservation, disclosure in the petition's body or logical priority alone does not make every independent omitted question reviewable.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight Justices. Stevens disagrees with fair inclusion and would decline the defense under Rule 14.1(a).

**Controlling explanation:** The Court separates preservation from the question actually accepted for review. Missouri asserted nonretroactivity throughout the relevant proceedings and described it in the petition, so the late-defense concern addressed in Schiro is absent. That observation alone does not settle Rule 14. Yee and Izumi require distinguishing an argument within a presented question from a related independent question. Here Teague limits whether the particular constitutional rule supporting this same habeas judgment may be applied to the final conviction. It is not a request to reverse an additional party-status order or decide another constitutional claim. The Court treats that confined remedial limit as subsidiary and therefore needs no exceptional-circumstances bypass. Stevens correctly identifies different legal inquiries into constitutional merits and retroactivity, but their difference does not make this limit on the same claimed writ an independent question. The holding neither makes nonretroactivity jurisdictional nor turns a State's preserved defenses into a general exemption from the Court's question-presentation rules.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido: applied to distinguish subsidiary legal arguments from distinct questions; preservation and fair inclusion remain separate requirements.
- Izumi Seimitsu Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha v. U.S. Philips Corp.: preserved; an omitted independent question does not become reviewable merely through notice, logical priority or ordinary economy. This confined same-rule habeas limitation is distinguished from its separate intervention determination.
- Schiro v. Farley: distinguished on presentation; the defense there first appeared at merits briefing after earlier omissions. Its discretionary treatment of a nonjurisdictional defense remains intact.

##### The requested extension was a new rule at finality

**Controlling proposition:** Bohlen's conviction and sentence became final on January 2, 1986, and the rule forbidding renewed proof of persistent-offender status in this noncapital proceeding was not dictated by the precedent then in force. Federal habeas relief therefore depends on a recognized Teague exception; the Court does not decide whether the proposed double-jeopardy protection should govern a case eligible for a decision on the constitutional merits.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight Justices adopt the finality-specific new-rule determination, not a prospective rule permitting every noncapital sentencing rehearing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines what the earlier precedents required, rather than demanding identical facts or counting disagreements. Graham preserves relief when an established constitutional duty already dictates its application; Gilmore confirms that a materially different noncapital extension can remain new. Bullington and Rumsey protected acquittal-like determinations of entitlement to death. Bohlen's status hearing had substantial trial protections, including pleaded facts, proof beyond reasonable doubt and adversarial presentation. Its consequence, however, was transfer of sentencing authority and removal of the jury's ordinary constraint within an unchanged class-A range. DiFrancesco had rejected automatic treatment of ordinary sentencing as an acquittal. Those decisions left a reasonable jurist room to distinguish this noncapital status determination. Total failure of the State's proof makes the finality argument substantial, but does not establish that the extension was already compelled in 1986. Prefinality disagreement corroborates that openness only after this comparison. Later explanations guide today's method without becoming part of the prefinality landscape. The Court leaves the ultimate trial-like merits question unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane: applied to the date at which the state judgment became final and the distinction between dictated applications and new rules.
- Graham v. Collins: applied in its controlling form; a dictated application does not become new merely because the facts differ or courts disagree.
- Gilmore v. Taylor: applied to preserve the possibility of a genuinely new noncapital extension after substantive comparison.
- Bullington v. Missouri and Arizona v. Rumsey: preserved for acquittal-like capital adjudications; their extension to this noncapital status proceeding was not dictated at finality, and their ultimate reach is not decided.
- United States v. DiFrancesco: applied as prefinality support for distinguishing sentencing determinations from offense acquittals.
- Griffith v. Kentucky: used for the finality convention; direct review was exhausted and the certiorari period expired on January 2, with January 1 a legal holiday.
- United States v. Dixon: its retention of Grady remains in force but does not answer whether this sentencing-status proceeding has the necessary jeopardy character.

##### Neither Teague exception permits this writ

**Controlling proposition:** The proposed rule falls within neither the exception for rules excluding conduct or a class of persons or punishments from the State's substantive power, nor the exception for watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. Its protection against renewed litigation of offender status does not remove the robberies or these authorized sentences from lawful punishment and does not supply a watershed accuracy procedure; the habeas grant on this new rule must therefore be reversed.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; eight Justices apply both exceptions as they exist at this decision's date. Neither exception is eliminated or prospectively narrowed out of existence.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses each exception rather than treating collateral review as the end of analysis. Penry recognizes that some rules remove conduct, persons or punishments from substantive state authority. The asserted bar concerns another opportunity to establish prior convictions in choosing the sentencer; it does not make robbery unpunishable or place these authorized terms beyond the State's power. Teague and Sawyer also preserve a narrow category of fundamental procedures whose absence seriously threatens accuracy and fairness. A finality protection may be important without serving that distinct watershed function. Preventing renewed proof after evidentiary failure protects against repeated adjudication; it does not create a new bedrock safeguard for reliable determination of the underlying criminal charge. Bullington's acquittal protection remains fully effective in its established setting, but its importance does not excuse the required exception inquiry for this new extension. The Court reverses the writ on the presented claim without authorizing another proof proceeding, disturbing unrelated preserved claims or prescribing a replacement sentence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane: applies both contemporary exceptions after finding a new rule; neither is abolished.
- Penry v. Lynaugh: applies the substantive exception's protection against punishment beyond the State's constitutional power; this claim does not satisfy it.
- Sawyer v. Smith: applies the distinction between an important procedural protection and the exceptional accuracy-and-fairness requirement.
- Bullington v. Missouri: preserved; refusing retroactive extension does not overrule its established capital-acquittal protection.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No capital-acquittal precedent is overruled. The presentation rule is applied through fair inclusion, not an exception based on notice alone. Graham's dictated-application principle and Gilmore's new-rule analysis remain coordinated; Dixon's retained Grady rule is not displaced. Both recognized Teague exceptions remain available in appropriate cases.

This preserved, fairly included habeas limitation bars relief based on a noncapital offender-status extension not dictated at January 2, 1986 finality and satisfying neither then-recognized exception. It does not establish that double jeopardy never applies in noncapital sentencing, make every preserved defense fairly included, permit every repeated proof attempt, or abolish a Teague exception. Ordinary sentence enhancements are not assumed to increase every applicable maximum: these class-A ranges were unchanged. The Court leaves constitutional merits and other unresolved claims open.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens dissents, joined by no other Justice. He treats Teague's historical-dictation and exception inquiries as independent questions omitted from the petition's stated question. Under Yee and Izumi, prior assertion, briefing and an antecedent relationship do not by themselves supply fair inclusion or exceptional circumstances. Defining the question solely by the identity of the writ would allow almost any distinct defense into the case. His own preference for greater flexibility in Izumi does not relieve the Court of applying its adopted presentation rule evenhandedly.

On the presented merits, Stevens would affirm the conditional writ. Missouri required formal allegations, proof beyond reasonable doubt, findings and adversarial protections before persistent-offender status could remove the jury's ordinary sentencing constraint. The State offered no qualifying proof, even after the appellate invitation to supplement it. Bullington, Rumsey and the insufficiency-finality principle drawn from Burks support treating that failed adjudication as final. The unchanged class-A punishment range does not erase the concrete consequence of changing who controls the sentence. The first judge did not expressly acquit Bohlen of status; the appellate determination of evidentiary insufficiency supplies the asserted equivalent. Trial-error reversal would present a different issue.

Alternatively, Stevens regards this confined application as dictated by the existing trial-like-acquittal principle, rather than new merely because the sentence is noncapital. That is not a second route under a Teague exception: if classified as new, he identifies neither a substantive-punishment exclusion nor a watershed accuracy rule. His proposed disposition would preserve conditional relief against the invalid renewed status adjudication, while leaving lawful sentencing without retrying the failed allegation possible. It would not acquit the robberies, impose a replacement term, invent a new release deadline or create a freestanding constitutional right to jury sentencing. These views are noncontrolling.

The proceedings and statutory safeguards are supported by [official United States Reports, 510 U.S. 383](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510383/usrep510383.pdf) and the [Eighth Circuit judgment, 979 F.2d 109](https://static.case.law/f2d/979/html/0109-01.html). Persistent-offender status required two or more felonies committed at different times, proved beyond reasonable doubt. For these class-A robberies it changed sentencing authority, not the ten-to-thirty-year-or-life range; both sentencings produced three consecutive fifteen-year terms. Finality was January 2, 1986, after the certiorari period and holiday adjustment. The earlier opinion's shorthand date and generalized enhancement language do not alter those particulars. The reports support the petition-presentation account; no independently recovered complete petition or unreported finding is claimed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit's conditional-habeas direction on this claim is reversed. Proceedings implementing the mandate must respect the nonretroactivity bar and the reserved merits. The Court directs no new evidentiary hearing, State proof opportunity, release deadline or replacement sentence and leaves the robbery convictions otherwise undisturbed.

### Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis, No. 94-325

Merits decision, June 14, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 21, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, Latsis v. Chandris, Inc., 20 F.3d 45 (Mar. 24, 1994), which ordered retrial after a status-only verdict and June 17, 1993 final judgment. The questions concern the employment connection needed to assess the drydock instruction and whether all Galileo drydock time could categorically be excluded. The preserved specific objection suffices; broader Rule 51 objections are not automatically reopened.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Second Circuit judgment requiring a new trial is affirmed, 9–0.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judgment requiring new trial on seaman status | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | No opposing Justice | New trial under lawful status instructions; liability and damages remain conditional |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg; Stone-Zsela in the general connection and preserved drydock discussions | Affirm retrial | Five support full framework including nonabsolute temporal guide; Stone joins general actual-assignment connection and preserved drydock holding only |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Thomas, Breyer | Affirm retrial | Actual-crew/high-seas-voyage approach; refuses full duration framework |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Contribution and substantial employment-related vessel connection

**Controlling proposition:** Jones Act seaman status requires work contributing to a vessel's function or mission and an employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation or identifiable fleet that is substantial in both duration and nature. The actual assignment governs; a job label or isolated moment aboard is not conclusive, and a genuine change of essential assignment is assessed on the new position rather than mechanically diluted by earlier shoreside service.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined at this level by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six support the general connection rule. Stevens, Thomas and Breyer concur only in the judgment on their different actual-crew approach.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that contribution and substantial employment connection are distinct requirements. Wilander removes any requirement that a seaman perform navigational duties, while retaining the line between a vessel's workforce and land-based employees incidentally aboard. Gizoni rejects occupational labels and voluntary compensation as conclusive substitutes for that employment inquiry. Nature and duration together identify whether the relationship ordinarily exposes the worker to the vessel's work as part of its company. The lower court's duration-or-nature formula does not preserve both dimensions. The parties stipulated that Latsis contributed to the mission, but disputed the nature and extent of his shipboard assignment. Neither that stipulation nor his presence on the injury voyage settles the remaining connection. A genuinely new essential assignment is evaluated on its own activities; prior shoreside years cannot mechanically defeat a newly established shipboard role. Conversely, later employment cannot retroactively create a relationship absent when injury occurred. The Court leaves actual status to proper factual evaluation and decides neither negligence nor damages.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander, 498 U.S. 337 — applied and clarified: mission contribution is necessary but does not eliminate substantial employment connection.
- Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni — applied to reject categorical occupational exclusions and preserve genuine connection disputes; conclusive-record summary judgment remains possible.

##### A nonabsolute temporal guide for ordinary assignments

**Controlling proposition:** For an ordinary employment arrangement, about 30 percent of service in connection with a vessel in navigation supplies a rule of thumb for substantial duration, not an inflexible cutoff or jurisdictional condition. Justified departures and genuinely changed essential assignments remain possible; courts may resolve status as a matter of law only when the properly assessed facts permit one reasonable conclusion.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, this temporal-guide discussion, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; five directly support it. Stone-Zsela does not join this discussion. No judgment-concurrence vote is aggregated into its authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a temporal guide may make the duration inquiry usable without replacing the actual employment relationship. Wilander supplies the statutory distinction between seamen and land-based workers; it does not provide a numerical answer to every arrangement. Gizoni preserves factfinding when work and connection remain genuinely disputed, and leaves room for judgment on a conclusive record. The approximate proportion reflects the practical difference between substantial vessel service and incidental presence in an ordinary assignment. Its purpose fails if the number becomes an automatic bar regardless of a worker's newly established essential duties or other justified circumstances. Nature remains an independent dimension, so passing the guide does not prove seaman status and falling below it is not invariably dispositive. The competing estimates of Latsis's vessel time are not reconciled by selecting one as fact. The Court accordingly preserves ordinary judicial definition and genuine jury application, rather than converting a useful guide into an immutable threshold or deciding this worker's ultimate status.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wilander — clarified through a practical nonabsolute guide to duration; mission contribution remains separately necessary.
- Gizoni — preserved: disputed status ordinarily requires factual resolution, while conclusive-record judgment remains open.

##### The preserved categorical drydock exclusion requires a new trial

**Controlling proposition:** Temporary repair does not invariably withdraw a vessel from navigation, although major reconstruction can do so on a sufficient record; withdrawn-vessel time is not automatically qualifying navigation service but may illuminate the actual employment relationship. Here the record did not justify a single categorical navigation conclusion and the instruction excluded the drydock period for every purpose, prejudicing the preserved status issue and requiring a new trial without deciding ultimate status or negligence.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined on this preserved instructional holding by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six support this rationale. The three judgment concurrences supply no full-framework join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the preserved drydock objection independently supports retrial. Senko and Butler reject treating ordinary immobility or repair as a mechanical withdrawal from navigation; they do not establish that a major conversion is temporary repair. Galileo underwent substantial reconstruction, including removed bottom plates and propellers, engine work and passenger-space rebuilding. A properly developed conclusive record could therefore support withdrawal as a matter of law. This record supplied no adequate findings or stipulations making the categorical instruction unavoidable. The instruction also barred consideration of the period for every purpose, confusing qualifying navigation time with evidence of the nature and continuity of an assignment. Later work may support or undermine an inference about the earlier relationship without creating retroactive status. Because the verdict addressed status alone, that broad exclusion materially affected the contested issue. Rules 51 and 61 require a preserved, prejudicial error; the specific drydock objection supplies it. The Court need not excuse every unpreserved objection to the broader charge or ratify that charge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp., 352 U.S. 370 — applied against mechanical repair/immobility exclusions; no finding that every reconstructed vessel remains in navigation follows.
- Butler v. Whiteman, 356 U.S. 271 — applied to genuine navigation/status fact disputes, not to eliminate conclusive-record rulings.
- Wilander and Gizoni — applied to distinguish contribution, connection and factual status from the occurrence of an injury aboard.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Wilander's mission-and-connection distinction and Gizoni's employment/factfinding rule remain. The Court clarifies both duration and nature; the five-Justice temporal guide is nonabsolute. Senko and Butler do not prevent withdrawal rulings on a properly conclusive major-reconstruction record.

Lower courts apply contribution plus substantial connection in both nature and duration, using the qualified temporal guide at its five-Justice scope. Neither contribution alone, a job label, every voyage, every drydock period nor a numerical threshold conclusively supplies status. A genuine essential reassignment is assessed on its own work. Later drydock work may be evidence of the employment relation, but cannot retroactively create status. Actual navigation, status, negligence, causation and damages are unresolved. The broader unpreserved charge is not ratified.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Thomas and Breyer, would identify a worker serving as part of the ship's company on the actual high-seas voyage as a seaman during that voyage. They rely on statutory crew membership and maritime work rather than the Court's broader temporal framework, and therefore concur in retrial while refusing its duration test. They decide no negligence or damages. Stone-Zsela's partial join supports the general substantial nature-and-duration connection and the preserved instructional correction; it does not adopt the numerical guide or ratify unpreserved parts of the broader charge. No separate opinion is filed by him.

#### Source Notes

The Second Circuit majority and Kearse dissent, petition appendix A1–A33, and status-only verdict and final judgment A34–A36 appear in the [Archive docket collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0554); petitioner’s brief pp. 20–31 and respondent’s brief pp. 2–13 supply the competing positions. The substantial six-month conversion and competing work estimates remain distinct from findings about navigation or status. Mission contribution was stipulated; the connection was not. The review questions are stated without a quotation from the grant order, and the decision does not resolve every unpreserved objection.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The district court conducts a new trial under lawful employment-connection instructions, without excluding all drydock work for every purpose on this record. The factfinder determines genuinely disputed navigation and assignment facts; negligence and damages are reached only if status and the other statutory elements are established. No seaman finding, injury liability or damages award is entered. The proceedings on the merits in this Court are complete; trial proceedings resume.

### United States v. Gaudin, No. 94-514

Merits decision, June 19, 1995

Argued April 17, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which reversed the reviewed convictions after the district judge decided materiality for the jury. The question concerns the Fifth and Sixth Amendment allocation of that element in the §1001 prosecution.

The Court affirms the Ninth Circuit, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer support affirmance of the reversal of the reviewed false-statement convictions. Justice Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the other eight Justices. Justice Thomas also concurs, joined by Justices O’Connor and Breyer.

When materiality is an element of the charged criminal false-statement offense, the Fifth and Sixth Amendments require the jury to determine it beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge states statutory meaning and the governing legal standard; labeling its application a mixed question does not remove an element from the jury.

All nine Justices adopt this allocation of the offense element to the jury in Justice Scalia’s Opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that the jury must decide every element necessary to establish criminal guilt, including application of an instructed legal standard to the charged facts. Winship supplies proof beyond a reasonable doubt; the constitutional jury guarantee places that determination with the jury. Materiality asks whether a statement has a natural tendency or capacity to influence the relevant governmental decision. That application does not become an abstract legal question simply because legal instruction is needed. Historical practice and functional convenience cannot reserve an offense element to the judge. Sinclair’s contrary criminal pertinency allocation cannot stand to that extent. Kungys concerns a civil denaturalization proceeding and a different appellate allocation, not this criminal jury guarantee. Here the Government concedes materiality is an element and did not challenge the lower court’s error classifications. Those premises preserve this reversal without deciding whether every section 1001 clause includes materiality or every withheld element is structural error. Statutory interpretation and threshold evidentiary sufficiency remain judicial functions.

In re Winship supplies the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt for facts establishing guilt.

Sinclair v. United States is overruled to the extent it assigns a criminal pertinency element to the judge; its unrelated propositions remain in force.

Kungys v. United States is distinguished because it concerns civil denaturalization and the allocation of responsibility on appeal.

Sullivan v. Louisiana retains its constitutional jury and burden-of-proof holding; this decision does not classify every error involving an omitted element.

Hubbard v. United States retains its rule about the entities covered by §1001; it supplies no authority to remove elements of the charged HUD offense from a jury.

The decision does not determine abstract statutory meaning by jury, classify every omitted-element error, adjudicate the exact trial-preservation history, or disturb a separately treated equity-skimming conviction or calculate its sentence.

The treatment accompanying the holding controls; only the incompatible criminal-element allocation of Sinclair is displaced.

A jury must apply the legal materiality standard beyond a reasonable doubt where materiality is an element. No universal materiality requirement for every §1001 clause or universal structural-error rule is created.

Thomas, joined by O’Connor and Breyer, concurs while joining the Court in full. The Government’s concession establishes materiality as an element for this case; the opinion need not construe every §1001 clause. The unchallenged lower error classifications do not settle harmless error, plain error or structural error for all later cases. These reservations qualify the questions decided, not the jury-element holding.

**Source notes:** Ninth Circuit en banc, 28 F.3d 943; §1001; petition in the Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-514. That collection item lacks separately listed merits and appendix components. The lower opinion and the conceded-element premises support the bounded question decided; the decision makes no exact finding about trial preservation.

The Ninth Circuit is affirmed and the reviewed false-statement convictions remain vacated. The Government may retry under instructions submitting every element, including materiality, to the jury. This is not an acquittal or a disposition of the separately treated equity-skimming conviction. This merits review is complete.

**End of entry: United States v. Gaudin, merits decision, June 19, 1995.**

### Gray v. Netherland, No. 95-6510

Merits decision, June 20, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

**518 U.S. 152.** Argued April 15, 1996. On certiorari to the Fourth Circuit's June 28, 1995 judgment in Gray v. Thompson, 58 F.3d 59, Nos. 94-4009 and 94-4011, reversing a federal grant of sentencing relief and directing dismissal.

The questions concern the capital defendant's opportunity to meet the Sorrell-murder evidence, whether the asserted protection was established when direct review ended on October 5, 1987, and the separate presentation and procedural treatment of meaningful opportunity, affirmative misrepresentation, and Brady suppression. The independent vileness-instruction question is outside this review.

The District Court's May 26, 1993 partial amendment reopened the identified Sorrell due-process claim and left the other dismissals, including the independent Brady ground, unchanged. After an April 21-22, 1994 evidentiary hearing, its August 25 decision found that the prosecutor's statements-only assurance and materially different last-minute presentation impaired preparation and created a substantial probability of a different sentencing result. The Fourth Circuit treated the notice claim as a new rule. Hall concurred in its judgment but withheld assent to that rationale, relying instead on a factual-development bar. The defendant seeks relief from the death sentence; the guilt and firearm convictions are not before the Court for reversal.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Holdings and Controlling Reasons

##### I. The independent Brady dismissal remains separate

**Operative rule:** Reopening the identified due-process challenge to the Sorrell evidence did not reopen the independently dismissed Brady ground when the amendment expressly preserved the remaining dismissals. The independent dismissal remains undisturbed in this review and is excluded from a general availability or merits remand; evidence relevant to the properly available due-process injury may still be considered for that distinct purpose.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees only in the judgment on this component. The eight direct joins supply the controlling explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The amendment identified the claim it reopened and preserved the other dismissals. Federal Ground A's suppression allegations and Gray's continued-withholding argument do not undo that procedural distinction. Kyles describes the collective lawful uses of favorable evidence on an available Brady claim; it does not itself reopen a dismissal. The Court therefore grants no independent suppression relief and makes no new suppression or materiality finding.

The opposite conclusion, that every use of related evidence is barred, would also disregard the amendment. Picard and Anderson require the substance of the constitutional claim, while Vasquez distinguishes supplementation from fundamental alteration. Evidence bearing on the inability to investigate and answer the penalty case may establish the injury or effect of the live due-process claim without reviving the independent suppression ground. Shared evidence alone neither merges the claims nor establishes their separate procedural predicates.

The initial order's nonpresentation and state-habeas factual-availability determinations were reported as uncontested in this Court. That description does not resolve the parties' dispute over counsel's knowledge at the 1985 trial. The Court does not adopt an additional appellate-abandonment finding or decide every asserted predicate of a Virginia adequate-and-independent-ground defense. Those additional propositions are unnecessary to the limited disposition of this independent ground.

##### II. A meaningful opportunity requires more than eventual disclosure

**Operative rule:** The established right to deny or explain material information used to select a capital sentence is denied when the State's supported assurances and materially different last-minute presentation deprive the defendant of a real opportunity to investigate and answer that case. Applied to this supported loss of the protected function, rather than to a fixed advance-disclosure interval, the claim invokes law dictated before Gray's October 5, 1987 finality and is not barred as a new rule under Teague.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, and Breyer: six direct joins. O'Connor's join does not extend to the final merits application or remedy in Parts III-IV.

**Controlling explanation:** Gardner protects an opportunity to meet the information used to impose death. Its protection would be empty if disclosure immediately before use always sufficed, regardless of an established inability to investigate, prepare objections, or answer the proof. Weatherford denies a general constitutional discovery entitlement; it does not establish that State-created impairment of this protected opportunity is permissible whenever the evidence eventually appears in open court.

The Commonwealth emphasizes that Gardner involved undisclosed information and that Gray could cross-examine and testify. Those facts matter, but the issue is whether those formal opportunities could perform their adversarial function after the supported assurance and last-minute change. Graham requires that functional comparison without dispensing with Teague's dictated-at-finality requirement. Caspari likewise demands the actual rule and finality date, not a general appeal to fairness. Gardner supplies the pre-1987 protection; the later cases do not insert new law into that date.

The Court adopts no universal notice period, automatic exclusion remedy, or right to more preparation merely because counsel encounters surprise. It requires a supported deprivation of the existing opportunity. No Teague exception is invoked, and neither contemporary exception is abolished. A separately reformulated affirmative-deception claim still requires its own proper presentation and legal predicates; this old-rule holding does not automatically decide every such claim.

##### III. This presented injury and its effect are established

**Operative rule:** Gray's reopened due-process claim fairly encompasses the preparation injury adjudicated here, and the accepted existing findings establish denial of the protected opportunity and a prejudicial effect on selection of death. Neither the lack of a formal continuance motion nor the surviving vileness finding defeats relief on this record; the constitutional entitlement to sentencing relief does not depend on prevailing on a newly recast deception claim or the dismissed Brady ground.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer: five direct joins. The holding determines this claim's availability, violation, and effect without declaring all related theories exhausted or all due-process errors structural.

**Controlling explanation:** Claim IX and the state appeal presented delayed disclosure, impaired preparation, and fundamental unfairness; federal Ground A carried that complaint forward. The amendment expressly reopened that consistently presented due-process claim. Picard and Anderson require its substance, not identical wording; under Vasquez, the evidence accepted here develops the same preparation injury rather than fundamentally altering it. Claim IX's previously-adjudicated treatment is not converted into another claim's omission-based default. Keeney retains Townsend's hearing requirement when a properly presented claim turns on material facts inadequately developed in state proceedings, unless the prisoner deliberately bypassed the available state process. Through Fay, that qualification concerns the prisoner's knowing, considered choice to forgo the state opportunity; ordinary counsel negligence, or counsel's choice without the prisoner's participation, does not automatically establish bypass. Deliberate bypass permits refusal of the otherwise required hearing rather than compelling denial, and does not replace Coleman's distinct claim-default rule. Hall's negligence-based substitution therefore cannot sustain dismissal on this existing hearing record.

The District Court found an unequivocal statements-only assurance, reliance, and concrete impairment of investigation, impeachment, objections, and response when photographs and police and medical-examiner evidence were introduced. The preparation objection and renewed exclusion request were real, although no formal continuance motion was made. That omission does not establish a feasible cure or an adequate procedural bar here. The Court accepts the findings of the protected injury and a substantial probability of a different sentence.

Other violent-crime evidence and lawful eligibility do not resolve the effect of an effectively unanswered graphic presentation on the choice of death. Tuggle distinguishes eligibility from that choice. The existing findings and whole penalty record establish substantial injurious influence and also preclude a conclusion beyond reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute. Brecht and O'Neal retain their category-specific holdings; the Court need not choose a general standard for this different category because relief follows under either competing approach. This conclusion neither identifies another perpetrator nor prohibits all uncharged-crime evidence.

##### IV. Conditional sentencing relief requires lawful prospective implementation

**Operative rule:** The reopened claim's established constitutional violation and prejudicial effect require reversal of the adverse appellate disposition and restoration and implementation of conditional relief from the death sentence. Before an operative writ is entered, any genuinely applicable, unadjudicated statutory requirement material to that relief must be resolved; once met or held inapplicable, the Commonwealth must provide a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding within a reasonable prospective District Court period or replace death with a lawful noncapital disposition.

**Authority:** Souter's Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer: five direct joins to this mandate and all its qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The defect concerns the penalty proceeding. A new constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding, or lawful noncapital disposition, corrects that defect without retrying guilt or disturbing independent custody. Graham and Simmons preserve the distinction between sentencing relief and the unaffected conviction. The existing constitutional violation and effect are decided, rather than returned for unrestricted reconsideration.

The 1996 habeas amendments nevertheless cannot be bypassed by a constitutional conclusion if a statutory restriction actually governs. An applicable amended section 2254(d) restriction on a claim adjudicated on the merits in state court requires its own temporal and predicate analysis. An applicable state-fact presumption or chapter 154 requirement likewise retains its force. Section 107(c)'s pending-case clause neither establishes Virginia's qualifying counsel system nor settles chapter 153's transition. No express exhaustion waiver, statutory exception, or satisfaction of an unadjudicated condition is presumed. Landgraf requires attention to the provision's enacted direction and actual operation, rather than resolution by the bare fact that litigation is pending.

This is a reservation for an actual applicable legal requirement, not a direction to reopen settled issues or search for hypothetical impediments. The federal evidentiary hearing occurred in 1994; no new hearing is authorized, and no exemption from section 2254(e)(2) is assumed. The receiving court must settle prospective implementation terms without treating the old 180-day calendar as presently running, automatically restored, or expired. The mandate fixes no immediate hearing date and orders no acquittal, guilt retrial, particular noncapital sentence, or release from independent lawful custody.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349:** Applied to the meaningful opportunity to deny or explain material capital-sentencing information; eventual public disclosure is not necessarily effective response.
- **Weatherford v. Bursey, 429 U.S. 545:** Distinguished from the supported denial of an existing opportunity; its rejection of a general discovery entitlement remains.
- **Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288:** Applied using October 5, 1987 finality and dictated law. Both contemporary exceptions remain: rules placing conduct, classes of persons, or punishments outside the State's substantive power, and watershed procedures essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. Neither exception is needed for the Court's old-rule holding.
- **Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993:** Applied for a functional comparison with the prefinality protection and for a conditional sentencing remedy that leaves guilt intact.
- **Caspari v. Bohlen, February 23, 1994:** Applied for finality-specific identification of the actual rule; no broad fairness exception replaces that inquiry.
- **Picard v. Connor, 404 U.S. 270:** Applied to the substance of the federal claim presented to the State.
- **Anderson v. Harless, 459 U.S. 4:** Applied to distinguish a presented federal claim from facts alone or a merely similar complaint.
- **Vasquez v. Hillery, 474 U.S. 254:** Applied to supplementation that does not fundamentally alter a presented claim; its deliberate-withholding reservation remains.
- **Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, May 4, 1992:** Its deliberate-bypass qualification continues to govern the identified factual-development objection; ordinary negligence is insufficient, while claim defaults and distinct factual-presumption rules remain separate.
- **Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293:** Its fifth mandatory-hearing circumstance and deliberate-bypass qualification remain operative through Keeney for inadequate factual development of a properly presented claim.
- **Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391:** Its knowing, considered-choice definition remains operative through Townsend for this hearing question; its displaced claim-default rule is not revived.
- **Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722:** Preserved for demonstrated independent and adequate state claim defaults; no new exception or across-the-board default finding is announced.
- **Kyles v. Whitley, April 19, 1995:** Its collective lawful-use materiality rule remains confined to available Brady claims and does not itself reopen the independent dismissal.
- **Tuggle v. Netherland, October 30, 1995:** Applied to distinguish remaining capital eligibility from the effect of an independent injury on actual sentence selection; its assumed-violation posture is not substituted for the findings accepted here.
- **Brecht v. Abrahamson, April 21, 1993:** Its preserved-Doyle Chapman holding remains; it does not already establish a universal collateral standard for this claim.
- **O'Neal v. McAninch, February 21, 1995:** Its preserved personal-intent category and separate violation/remedy requirements remain; the proposed general extension is not adopted here.
- **Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18:** Its beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard supplies one competing test that the established injury fails; no category-wide extension is necessary.
- **Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750:** Its whole-record substantial-influence approach supplies the other competing test under which this injury warrants relief; no general replacement of Chapman is adopted.
- **Simmons v. South Carolina, June 17, 1994:** Its opportunity-to-answer and sentence-only remedy distinctions remain; its specific future-danger/parole trigger and direct-review rule are not converted into a universal notice or collateral-error rule.
- **Landgraf v. USI Film Products, April 26, 1994:** Its provision-specific temporal inquiry is preserved for genuinely material statutory questions, without a general ruling on AEDPA's reach here.

#### Law After Decision

The existing capital opportunity-to-answer protection reaches a supported assurance and last-minute evidentiary change that actually prevent meaningful preparation and response; Teague does not bar this dictated application. The rule creates no fixed discovery deadline or automatic relief for surprise. This presented claim's violation and prejudicial effect warrant conditional sentencing relief on the existing record, subject to genuinely applicable unadjudicated statutory requirements before a writ operates.

The independently dismissed Brady ground remains outside the remand, while related evidence may support the distinct available due-process injury. Claim presentation, independent defaults, factual-development limits, and statutory restrictions retain their separate predicates. No universal collateral harmlessness standard, structural-error category, broad uncharged-evidence exclusion, new hearing entitlement, or AEDPA temporal exemption is established. The separate writings' broader reliability objection and alternative remand positions are not controlling law.

#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring alone.** Stevens joins all four parts of the Court's opinion. He would also find this presentation fundamentally unreliable even if adequately announced: weakly supported allegations of separate murders, a graphic account, and insufficiently tested linkage supplied the jury an aggravating case whose reliability counsel could not meaningfully test. Gardner's concern with a trustworthy capital determination supports his additional objection; his Lankford approach likewise attends to the defense's real ability to answer the case. His conclusion concerns this record, not a categorical prohibition of every uncharged act, and it does not identify Timothy Sorrell as the perpetrator. He accepts the established prejudicial effect under demanding whole-record substantial-influence review, without requiring a universal Chapman rule. No other Justice joins his independently sufficient reliability ground.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** O'Connor joins Parts I-II. Graham's functional treatment of an established capital protection, Gardner's opportunity to answer, and Caspari's finality-specific discipline support treating the supported meaningful-opportunity claim as an old-rule application rather than an automatic discovery timetable. Her Lankford and Simmons positions recognize the legal significance of misleading assurances. But correcting that legal premise does not, in her view, settle the present claim boundaries, findings, violation, and error effect sufficiently to require a writ. She would vacate and remand for lawful review of those actual questions, applying Keeney's factual-development rule and the appropriate independent claim requirements. Like her reserved remedial position in Simmons, that disposition leaves sentencing relief dependent on the proper application below. She preserves the separate Brady dismissal, rejects an exploratory search for hypothetical injury, and orders neither a new federal hearing nor a present penalty proceeding.

**Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Part I but reject the ordinary-notice old-rule holding and the present sentencing relief. Gardner concerned information the defendant could not see; Weatherford supplies no general constitutional entitlement to earlier evidentiary disclosure. Under Teague and Caspari, those precedents did not dictate a preparation-time entitlement for evidence disclosed before use merely because additional time would help. Neither contemporary Teague exception permits relief on that newly formulated notice rule. Graham's distinct effective-mitigation holding does not eliminate the finality inquiry.

Affirmative misrepresentation is a distinct allegation requiring an actual assurance, reasonable reliance, a supported deprivation, and proper claim presentation. They would vacate the global dismissal only for that bounded inquiry, including whether the Commonwealth preserved a procedural-default defense; an unpreserved defense is not automatically revived. They decide neither that formulation's novelty nor its constitutional merits, effect, or eventual remedy. Keeney governs a properly available factual-development question, without curing an independent omitted claim. Their remand preserves the independent Brady dismissal, authorizes no new hearing or open-ended investigation, and supplies no current writ. Scalia's Lankford insistence on supported reliance and Kennedy's Lankford and Simmons distinctions between notice and misleading assurance remain consistent with that confined route.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fourth Circuit to implement the conditional sentencing mandate. The reopened claim's constitutional violation and prejudicial effect are settled. Before an operative writ, the receiving court resolves any genuinely applicable, unadjudicated statutory requirement material to relief, including an actually applicable section 2254(d) restriction, state-fact presumption, or chapter 154 condition, without presuming temporal reach, Virginia qualification, waiver, or an exception. Once those requirements are satisfied or inapplicable, the District Court fixes a reasonable prospective period for a constitutionally adequate new penalty proceeding or lawful noncapital disposition.

The independent Sorrell Brady dismissal and distinct Smallwood, investigator-assistance, guilt-sufficiency, and ungranted vileness-instruction rulings are not reopened. No new federal evidentiary hearing, Supreme Court stay, execution authorization, supplemental-briefing schedule, or immediate release from independent lawful custody is ordered. The existing 1994 hearing and a new hearing remain distinct, and the old 180-day decree is not automatically restored or adjudged expired.
