### Department of State v. Legal Assistance for Vietnamese Asylum Seekers, Inc., No. 95-1521

Post-grant vacatur and remand, October 21, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 15, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. The decisions under review, 45 F.3d 469 and 74 F.3d 1308, concern the Department's refusal to process immigrant-visa applications in Hong Kong for Vietnamese applicants screened out of refugee status. The question is whether the policy violates 8 U.S.C. §1152(a)(1), and how the intervening enactment of §633 of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA) affects the statutory premise, live claims and available relief. The Government seeks vacatur or reversal; respondents seek affirmance or dismissal of the writ as improvidently granted. This is an argued post-grant disposition, not an asylum-eligibility proceeding.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The D.C. Circuit judgment is vacated and the case returned for prompt consideration of §633's meaning, temporal reach, effect on the challenged processing policy, surviving claims and lawful relief. The receiving courts may reach the same result. No forfeited defense is revived and no independently closed issue is reopened. Additional factfinding is required only for identified material questions that the existing record cannot resolve.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Stevens delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Its common ground concerns an actual intervening enactment and the specific equities of this remand; it adopts no new rule for changed Executive advocacy.

#### Holdings

##### Reconsideration after the intervening visa-processing enactment

**Rule:** Section 633 directly addresses the processing-location premise of the judgment below and creates a reasonable probability that reconsideration will alter an outcome-material statutory premise or continuing relief. Independent consideration of the equities warrants vacatur and a confined remand, without deciding the old-law merits or the new provision's final construction and temporal reach.

**Authority:** Stevens's unanimous opinion controls. The Court applies the reasonable-probability standard; a reasonable possibility alone is not its rule.

The Court holds that the new enactment warrants reconsideration because it addresses the very activity regulated by the judgment. The D.C. Circuit applied the nationality and residence nondiscrimination provision to the place of immigrant-visa processing. Congress has now added a clause concerning the Secretary's authority over processing procedures and locations. This is an enacted legal development, not simply a Government request for a different outcome.

Lawrence v. Chater supplies the separate requirements of outcome-material probability and independent equitable judgment. Both are met. Review remains timely; the amendment followed the appellate decisions; and the parties have submitted their opposing interpretations. The applicants have already endured substantial delay. Their asserted danger of forcible repatriation, allegedly following the end of a Hong Kong undertaking upon this Court's disposition, deserves concrete weight. But neither dismissal of this writ nor remand establishes continuing foreign protection, and leaving the judgment intact would not settle the amendment's effect on actual processing relief. A prompt, bounded remand permits the new clause, the asserted matured claims and the independent claims to be considered together.

Landgraf and Rivers preserve the distinction between new consequences for completed conduct and regulation of future activity. The Court does not decide that an injunction is necessarily prospective in every relevant sense, that corrective legislation necessarily reaches past conduct, or that the pending case automatically receives the new provision's asserted effect. The lower courts must resolve those questions under governing law. Congress's enactment alone neither moots the independent APA and constitutional claims nor establishes that any applicant must lose.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Lawrence v. Chater:** Applies its reasonable-probability and independent-equities requirements; the receiving court retains responsibility for the development's actual effect. Application to this intervening statute does not enlarge Lawrence's agency-development holding.
- **Landgraf v. USI Film Products:** Preserves provision-specific temporal analysis and rejects disposition based merely on a pending-case or injunction label.
- **Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc.:** Preserves the rule that a corrective legislative purpose does not itself establish retrospective reach. The remand decides no conflict between the previously published rationales in Landgraf and Rivers.

#### Law After the Decision

The decision applies the existing reconsideration rule to §633 and leaves its substantive construction open. The amendment is effective legislation; that fact does not decide its application to completed conduct or reparative relief. The APA and constitutional theories remain live for lawful consideration. No asylum status, visa eligibility, entitlement to Hong Kong processing, damages, enforceable foreign undertaking, repatriation event or specific interim deadline is adjudicated. Courts may consider prompt interim relief within their authority; none is promised or ordered by this disposition.

#### Source Notes

The statutory premise and procedural history appear in the D.C. Circuit decisions, including [45 F.3d 469](https://openjurist.org/45/f3d/469). [IIRIRA §633, Pub. L. 104-208, Division C, 110 Stat. 3009-701](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ208/html/PLAW-104publ208.htm), was enacted September 30, 1996 and carries no separate delayed effective date or express pending-case instruction. The parties' [supplemental briefs in No. 95-1521](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0706), documents 09 and 10, establish their arguments, the surviving APA and constitutional theories, and respondents' asserted repatriation risk. The asserted foreign undertaking and October 11 declaration are not independently established findings, and no operative expiration or extension is adjudicated.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Supreme Court review concludes with a limited remand to the D.C. Circuit. The next act is consideration of §633 and any surviving grounds and relief under the governing legal standards. No asylum proceeding is opened and no foreign protection is extended.

**End of entry: Department of State v. Legal Assistance for Vietnamese Asylum Seekers, Inc., Post-grant vacatur and remand, October 21, 1996.**

### California v. Roy, No. 95-2025

Summary merits disposition, November 4, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On petition for a writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, reviewing its en banc habeas judgment in Roy v. Gomez, 81 F.3d 863. No oral argument is held in this Court. The questions concern whether omission of an aider and abettor's purpose to facilitate robbery is structural or trial error, the harmlessness burden and permissible method on federal habeas, and the antecedent objection that the named petitioners were not parties below. California seeks vacatur or reversal; Roy seeks rejection of review or retention of the habeas-relief judgment.

The trial charge required knowledge of the perpetrator's unlawful purpose and affirmative assistance, excluding mere presence, but omitted Roy's purpose to facilitate the robbery. The jury returned Mannix robbery and first-degree felony-murder convictions and a knife-possession finding without personal knife use. It also convicted Roy of Clark second-degree murder with personal knife use and acquitted him of Clark robbery. The state court reversed robbery-murder and multiple-murder special circumstances and their sentence while otherwise affirming. This federal challenge concerns only the two Mannix convictions. The state and district courts found harmlessness through their functional-equivalence assessments; the en banc Ninth Circuit expressly reached the contrary Chapman conclusion before its separate collateral-standard analysis.

#### Judgment

The petition for a writ of certiorari is granted.

**Vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg, who would affirm the habeas-relief judgment on their respective grounds.

The remand concerns only the Mannix robbery and first-degree felony-murder convictions. The Ninth Circuit must conduct the qualified whole-record inquiry, with District Court proceedings if otherwise proper. It may reach the same result. If an available violation is not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, conditional relief must permit a reasonable retrial period for those convictions; proper harmlessness permits denial. The Court makes no present harmlessness finding, directs no immediate release, fixes no retrial deadline, grants no new stay, restores no special-circumstance punishment and does not dispose of the separate Clark conviction.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Rejects the party-status objection and reaches the questions | Existing official appellate party and the confined succession/identification rationale |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Common classification and governing burden | Trial-error reviewability and Chapman for this preserved personal-intent category |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Souter | O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas, Breyer | Vacatur and remand | Qualified actual-verdict method, application limits, preservation and remand directions |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | — | Supports vacatur and remand; joins Part II only | His whole-record Chapman and conditional-relief ground; no join in Parts I or III |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Stevens | — | Would affirm; joins Parts I and II | Record-specific failure of the State's Chapman showing without an exclusive-findings rule |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Scalia | Ginsburg | Would affirm; both join Parts I and II | Actual-findings constraint for this omitted-purpose error and failure to establish its required connection |

Stone-Zsela agrees with rejection of the party-status objection without adopting its rationale. Five Justices directly join Part III; the judgment's sixth vote does not enlarge that opinion coalition.

#### Holdings

##### I. The existing official appellate party may seek review

**Rule:** Section 1254(1) requires a petitioner who was a party in the court of appeals; on this record, that court's repeated identification of the Attorney General as an official-capacity respondent and the timely petition expressly naming the incumbent in that capacity satisfy the requirement. The Court accepts the existing official party's identity despite the contrary earlier custodial pleadings; it neither adds a party nor holds that a caption, counsel's participation or a State's interest invariably establishes party status.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion controls with the eight-Justice coalition identified above. Stone-Zsela agrees only with rejecting the objection and reaching the case, without joining this rationale.

The Court holds that the official litigant identified by the court of appeals may seek review through the incumbent Attorney General. Scofield requires a party below; Perini keeps that statutory requirement distinct from injury. California's interest in its conviction alone would not suffice.

The amended habeas petition names Gomez and Borg, the answer identifies them as proper respondents, and the subsequent order substitutes Merkle for Borg. Those documents establish custodial participation and replacement. They do not supply an appellate ruling removing the Attorney General's office. The June 1995 panel opinion, September rehearing order and April 1996 en banc opinion repeatedly name Van de Kamp as a respondent. The Court accepts that appellate identification on these competing documents. Lungren's timely, expressly official petition identifies the incumbent of the same office.

FRAP 43(c)(2) and FRCP 25(d)(2) permit designation of an existing official-capacity party by title, subject to the court's authority to require a name. Their corresponding automatic-substitution provisions operate when an existing official-capacity party leaves office during the respective appellate proceeding or action. They disregard an immaterial misnomer, and omission of a formal substitution order does not defeat substitution. These rules continue an existing party; they do not create one. The Court does not assume that the change of Attorney General occurred during this appeal or this Court's proceeding. The appellate identification is the decisive predicate, not an imagined intervention or succession order.

Smith v. Barry supports reading a timely document for the litigant it actually identifies, while retaining mandatory party information. No custodian is added to the petition under Rule 12.4, and Rule 35.3 does not establish original party status below. Whether the People separately qualify is unnecessary to decide. The Attorney General's appearance as counsel does not independently establish party status.

##### II. This personal-intent omission permits harmless-error review under Chapman

**Rule:** The preserved omission of Roy's purpose to facilitate robbery is a constitutional trial error subject to harmlessness review, not an error requiring automatic reversal merely because an element was omitted. For this personal-intent violation properly available on federal habeas, the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt, on the whole record, that the error did not contribute to the verdict; neither collateral posture nor absence of accompanying prosecutorial argument substitutes a weaker burden.

**Authority:** Part II controls unanimously. Agreement on this category and burden does not establish agreement on the method or result of harmlessness review.

The Court holds that the jury's responsibility to determine the defendant's required intent remains protected, while the effect of this instructional omission may be examined. Gaudin requires jury determination of an offense element but does not classify every failure to submit an element as structural. Sullivan addresses a different defect: an unconstitutional reasonable-doubt standard governing all findings leaves no valid reasonable-doubt verdict to examine. The omission here does not destroy that standard throughout the charge.

O'Neal requires the State's Chapman showing for its preserved personal-intent category. That case involved combined instructions and prosecutorial argument permitting conviction without the defendant's own required intent. This charge permits the same constitutional failure by omitting the purpose to facilitate robbery. An additional prosecutorial statement is not the basis of the protection and supplies no reason to withhold it here. Knowledge of the perpetrator's unlawful purpose and affirmative assistance were required, so mere presence was insufficient; neither knowledge nor assistance is legally identical to the omitted purpose.

The State retains the burden. Roy need not prove innocence, and the reviewing court may not supply the missing intent itself. This decision does not adopt Chapman for every constitutional habeas claim, reopen a resolved exhaustion issue, or decide the temporal operation of AEDPA.

##### III. Necessary equivalence is sufficient, but not the exclusive permissible inquiry

**Rule:** Under Chapman, the State may establish that this omission did not contribute to the actual verdict through supported whole-record inferences as well as findings necessarily embracing the omitted purpose; it may not substitute evidence sufficient for a hypothetical properly instructed conviction, equate knowledge plus assistance with purpose, or infer purpose from general mental capacity. Under Yates, the court must examine whether the instructions led the jury to consider the evidence supporting the proposed inference; if the omission kept the relevant personal-purpose question from the jury so that the inference cannot reliably address its actual verdict, strong evidence alone does not carry the State's beyond-reasonable-doubt burden.

**Authority:** Part III controls through the five direct joins identified above. Stone-Zsela supports remand on his separate whole-record Chapman ground; his sixth judgment vote does not enlarge that coalition. Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg dissent from remand. This holding rests on the Court's opinion, without combining separate rationales.

The Court holds that the inquiry concerns what the error did to the verdict actually returned. Yates requires examination of the evidence the instructions put before the jury and distinguishes actual contribution from proof sufficient to convict. Necessary equivalence between a finding and the omitted purpose is one sufficient route. It is not invariably the only way to determine the effect of this knowledge-and-assistance charge.

The Ninth Circuit expressly found the omission not harmless under Chapman before applying a separate collateral-review analysis. Remand is warranted because its necessary-findings restriction foreclosed the qualified actual-effect inquiry, not because Chapman was never considered. The charge submitted knowledge, affirmative aid and related mental-state questions. That permits examination of their connection to the actual verdict, without assuming the jury considered the omitted purpose. The receiving court must address the possibility that the omission itself prevented the relevant consideration; inability to establish a reliable connection defeats the State's showing.

The record requires more than an overwhelming-evidence label. Possession of property, wet clothing, assistance testimony and reported statements must be assessed with the verdicts and the conflicts in those accounts. The clothing allocated to McHargue in the lower opinions cannot be treated as undisputed property in Roy's backpack. Hudspeth's impeachment and rebuttal and Hall's reported statements and retraction matter together; no verdict establishes wholesale acceptance or rejection of every witness. The possible inference that Roy helped a companion without intending to facilitate robbery is an inference to assess, not a newly found event. The State's and district court's functional-equivalence conclusions are legal assessments, not express jury findings. These matters remain for the proper inquiry, and the Ninth Circuit may again conclude that the State fails its burden.

##### IV. The limited remand preserves the record, argument and remedial boundaries

**Rule:** The Ninth Circuit judgment is vacated for a qualified Chapman review of the preserved personal-intent claim affecting the Mannix robbery and first-degree felony-murder convictions, with District Court proceedings if otherwise proper; no present harmlessness finding follows. If an available violation is not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, conditional relief must allow the State a reasonable period to retry those convictions; proper harmlessness permits denial, and neither this remand nor the special-circumstance argument restores reversed punishment, affects the Clark conviction or orders immediate release.

**Authority:** Part III's remand instructions control through the same five direct joins. Stone-Zsela separately agrees with the Chapman and reasonable-retrial remedy, supplying the sixth judgment vote without joining Part III.

The Court holds that renewed review must apply the proper method to the supported record and respect genuine preservation limits. O'Neal distinguishes a corrected harmlessness inquiry from an automatic writ and requires a reasonable retrial opportunity if an available violation warrants relief. No fixed retrial deadline is set.

Yee distinguishes an additional argument about a preserved question from a new constitutional claim. The special-circumstance argument concerns this preserved harmlessness question, but Roy's objection to its first-instance use still requires consideration. The asserted oral concession is not established, and the objection alone creates no forfeiture.

The instructions must be read together. CALJIC 8.80's intentional-aid requirement applies conditionally to a defendant who was not the actual killer; it is not an unconditional finding of Roy's purpose to facilitate robbery. Absence of personal knife use does not itself eliminate every actual-killer theory. CALJIC 8.81.17's robbery-related purpose of the killing differs from Roy's own aiding purpose. The state reversal prevents restoration of the special-circumstance punishment; it does not automatically determine every evidentiary implication of the findings. General specific-intent or diminished-capacity instructions do not silently insert the omitted purpose clause.

The Court therefore adopts neither an automatic special-circumstance cure nor a categorical exclusion of the findings from consideration. It supplies no new trial finding, admissibility ruling on a juror declaration, exhaustion bar, or factual resolution from an unavailable transcript passage. Preservation, independent habeas requirements and any event-date statutory transition remain distinct. This remand addresses the identified method defect, without reviving a resolved gate or ordering new factfinding merely because a source is unavailable.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **UAW v. Scofield, 382 U.S. 205:** Applies the requirement that a petitioner under §1254(1) be an actual party below; official identification satisfies it here without permitting review by any interested nonparty.
- **Director, OWCP v. Perini North River Associates, 459 U.S. 297:** Preserves the distinction between statutory party status and injury; a State's enforcement interest alone does not answer the party question.
- **Smith v. Barry:** Applies practical identification in a timely filing while retaining mandatory information about the actual litigant; no late party is added.
- **United States v. Gaudin:** Applies jury allocation of an offense element and respects its reservation of universal structural-error treatment.
- **Sullivan v. Louisiana:** Distinguishes a particular purpose omission from invalidation of the reasonable-doubt standard for all findings; its structural rule remains intact.
- **O'Neal v. McAninch:** Applies its State-borne Chapman rule to this preserved personal-intent omission without requiring accompanying argument, and retains conditional relief with a reasonable retrial opportunity. Its unadopted general extension to all ordinary constitutional trial errors does not become law.
- **Chapman v. California:** Retains the State's beyond-reasonable-doubt burden to show that the error did not contribute to the actual verdict.
- **Yates v. Evatt:** Applies the actual-contribution and instructional-focus requirements; the Court rejects both sufficiency alone and an exclusive necessary-findings method for this charge, subject to the stated reliability qualification.
- **Yee v. City of Escondido:** Applies the difference between an additional argument about a preserved question and a new claim without eliminating legitimate preservation and first-instance limits.

#### Law After the Decision

Effective November 4, 1996, the defined personal-intent habeas category includes this preserved omission of the defendant's purpose to facilitate robbery. It does not depend on accompanying prosecutorial argument. The State must satisfy Chapman through a permissible inquiry into the actual verdict. Necessary findings may suffice, but the controlling method also permits supported inferences meeting the express Yates condition; strong evidence alone fails when the omission prevents a reliable connection to the actual verdict. Scalia and Ginsburg's exclusive actual-findings approach does not control, and Stevens's vote to affirm does not join that approach.

The existing official-party requirement remains unchanged. This record satisfies it through the appellate identification of the Attorney General's office and the timely official petition, without a rule making every caption conclusive. No general Chapman rule for all habeas errors, new innocence gate, automatic special-circumstance cure, exhaustion ruling, statutory-transition ruling or universal structural-error rule is established. No holding on harmlessness of this actual record is made. The conditional-relief rule remains operative if the State fails its showing on an available violation.

#### Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins Part II and supports vacatur and remand because the Ninth Circuit's necessity formulation does not replace the whole-record Chapman inquiry required for the preserved personal-intent claim. Gaudin protects jury determination of elements without making every omission structural; Sullivan's destruction of the reasonable-doubt verdict differs from this assessable omission. O'Neal puts on the State the burden to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the violation did not contribute to the verdict. He would neither supply intent himself, shift the burden to Roy nor demand innocence. If a properly available violation fails the State's showing, relief for the affected convictions must permit a reasonable retrial opportunity. Preservation, exhaustion, independent habeas requirements and statutory transition remain separate. He makes no final harmlessness finding and sets no retrial deadline. He does not join Parts I or III's additional reasoning.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins Parts I and II but would affirm the habeas-relief judgment on this record. His Rose concurrence and his agreement with Yates require rigorous inquiry into the error's contribution to the actual verdict. The distinct omitted purpose, the supported inference of companion assistance without a robbery purpose, the disputed advance-plan account and the absence of an established independent purpose finding prevent the State from proving harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. This does not establish that every defense assertion is true or that the jury rejected all of Hudspeth's testimony. Stevens accepts that the Ninth Circuit's general necessary-findings formula is too broad; he does not adopt it as the exclusive rule for every omission. A proper actual-contribution inquiry nevertheless supports the judgment already reached. No identified unfinished material task justifies another remand merely to repeat an adverse Chapman determination. He would leave conditional relief for the two Mannix convictions in place, with a reasonable retrial opportunity and no immediate unconditional release.

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Ginsburg.** They join Parts I and II but would affirm. For this omitted-purpose error, harmlessness requires that the jury's other determinations effectively embrace Roy's required purpose, or that the evidence and findings establish that the jury could not have reached what it did find without also finding that purpose. The State must establish that connection beyond a reasonable doubt. Carella's separate opinion and Scalia's Yates opinion explain why independent appellate certainty that a properly instructed jury would convict cannot replace the jury's actual findings; Sullivan and Gaudin preserve the jury's constitutional role. Whole-record review identifies what the actual verdict embraces; it does not authorize an appellate substitute verdict.

Knowledge and assistance do not necessarily establish purpose. Neither the Clark findings nor general capacity answers the missing Mannix purpose. The conditional nonkiller clause, the distinction between intentional assistance to first-degree murder and purpose to facilitate robbery, the robbery-related purpose of the killing, and the cross-reference to deficient aiding instructions prevent treating the special circumstances as an established equivalent finding. The asserted preservation objection does not itself supply forfeiture, and the record does not identify an unresolved genuine finding question requiring another remand. A valid equivalent finding could make an omission harmless, so their rule is not automatic structural reversal. They would affirm conditional relief only for the two Mannix convictions, allowing a reasonable retrial period. They adopt no innocence prerequisite or discretionary-habeas rule, and Ginsburg joins no such proposition.

### Lopez v. Monterey County, No. 95-1201

Merits decision, November 6, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 8, 1996. On appeal from the three-judge United States District Court for the Northern District of California. The court authorized March 1996 municipal-court elections under a countywide, at-large system without preclearance of all operative changes and joined California for further litigation about the source of the system. Review concerns whether a covered County must preclear state-mandated changes despite having no policy discretion; the direct voting change, designation and prospective-laches defenses; and the limits on an enforcement court's use of an unprecleared governmental plan and a wholly court-developed temporary remedy. The plaintiffs seek reversal, prospective nonuse and lawful preclearance or interim proceedings.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The order authorizing the March 1996 countywide, at-large election is vacated. The district court must enjoin prospective administration of that system unless and until every operative post-November 1, 1968 voting change necessary to it has received §5 preclearance. It must determine the exact scope of the 1983 submission, identify changes still unprecleared, and require their expeditious submission through the Attorney General or the United States District Court for the District of Columbia. It must consider any necessary interim arrangement only within independent lawful remedial authority and the limits stated below. The Court orders no completed election undone, judicial office vacated, judicial act invalidated or particular replacement plan adopted.

#### Opinions and Joins

Justice Kennedy delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Its Parts I–IV correspond to the four controlling holdings below. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela files a concurrence, joined by no other Justice, adding his constitutional response to the designation objection and specifying his boundary for a wholly judicial plan.

#### Holdings

**Common authority:** Parts I–IV of Kennedy's unanimous opinion control the four holdings below. The additional grounds in the concurrence do not form part of those holdings.

##### I. State compulsion does not exempt a covered County's administration

**Rule:** A covered political subdivision administers a voting change when its officials give the change operative effect within the subdivision. Section 5 therefore requires Monterey County to obtain preclearance before administering a postbaseline judicial-election change even if noncovered California compels the change and leaves the County no discretion; the duty is the County's and does not require California to preclear its laws in the abstract.

The Court holds that execution of an electoral change is distinct from enactment of it. Section 5 separately reaches a covered jurisdiction that enacts or seeks to administer a change. A condition of local policy discretion would erase the second route whenever a noncovered State required its covered subdivision to operate the new system. The statute contains no such condition. Monterey County conducts these public judicial elections and gives the countywide electorate operative effect; the voters experience the same change regardless of its legislative origin.

Presley supplies the distinction between a change in voting and a transfer of internal governing duties. Morse confirms the importance of the practice's actual electoral operation while preserving the designated substantive-preclearance process. Neither already decided this local-discretion question, and the Court adopts no general state-delegation test. The holding answers the legal question even assuming California law entirely displaced the County ordinances; it does not decide that disputed state-law characterization. Nor does it impose a statewide submission obligation, determine discriminatory purpose or retrogression, or confer general federal power to redesign California's judiciary.

##### II. Electorate changes and the limits of prior submission

**Rule:** Replacing nine district electorates with one countywide electorate for municipal judges is a change affecting voting under §§5 and 14(c)(1). A later related submission does not preclear earlier changes that it failed to identify; the district court must determine the precise reach of the 1983 submission as to the final ordinance while treating the earlier unidentified consolidations as unprecleared.

The Court holds that this consolidation changes who elects each judge, rather than merely the work performed after election. At the November 1, 1968 baseline, voters elected the judges of their separate districts. The six ordinances adopted between 1972 and 1983 ultimately placed the judges before one countywide electorate. Presley preserves coverage for that direct alteration of electoral choice. Allen and Perkins supply the same distinction through their treatment of electoral mechanisms and boundaries.

McCain and Clark require an actual, identifiable submission of the change for which preclearance is claimed; ambiguity is not blanket approval of earlier practices. The record establishes that California's 1983 submission did not identify or describe the earlier ordinances. Whether it reached the final 1983 ordinance must be determined from the submission's actual terms. The County cannot obtain approval of the whole consolidation sequence merely by pointing to that later submission. Coverage and lack of preclearance establish the nonuse obligation, not a finding that the system has discriminatory purpose or retrogressive effect.

##### III. Effective designation and prospective enforcement

**Rule:** Section 4(b)'s administrative coverage determination may not be collaterally retried in this §5 enforcement action; statutory bailout under §4(a) is a distinct route. Delay cannot authorize prospective administration of an unprecleared change, although reliance, completed acts and practical timing may bear on lawfully available retrospective relief or the form of a lawful transition.

The Court holds that the County's published designation remains operative in this case. Congress separately restricted review of the coverage determination and provided a judicial bailout process. The County has not obtained release from coverage through that process. Its objection cannot convert this enforcement suit into a collateral retrial of the administrative findings.

Nor does the age of the ordinances excuse their future use without submission. Section 5 conditions administration on preclearance; the passage of time supplies no substitute. Clark enforces that statutory sequence, and Hampton County confirms that elapsed time and electoral implementation do not themselves remove the review obligation. The practical cost of delay warrants a fitted transition, not continued use of the same uncleared governmental plan as an ordinary electoral system. The Court makes no factual bailout determination, awards no damages, and decides no backward-looking relief concerning a completed election, judicial tenure or completed judicial acts.

##### IV. Enforcement and a genuinely judicial temporary remedy

**Rule:** A §5 enforcement court may determine coverage and submission status and enforce nonuse, but may neither grant substantive preclearance nor authorize an unprecleared County or State electoral plan by calling it court ordered. A necessary, temporary plan wholly formulated by the court may operate without separate §5 preclearance only when independent lawful remedial authority supports it; it must comply with the Constitution and Voting Rights Act, displace state law no further than necessary, and yield to a lawful precleared arrangement.

The Court holds that judicial approval does not change the governmental origin of an electoral plan. Connor distinguishes a wholly judicial remedy; McDaniel prevents a court order from insulating governmental policy choices from submission. Here the district court reinstated substantially the County's at-large system and authorized ordinary six-year terms. It did not formulate a wholly judicial temporary remedy. Clark's reservation for an extreme circumstance supplies no basis on this record: prolonged notice of the problem, abandonment of the County's D.C. preclearance action and ordinary terms do not establish a last-minute necessity. This decision recognizes no broader emergency exception.

Morse and Shaw preserve Congress's separate preclearance channels. The enforcement court cannot decide purpose or effect itself, even in light of the County's stipulation concerning its inability to prove nonretrogression. Administrative difficulty does not enlarge its authority.

The earlier four-division arrangement is a material option for consideration. It retained a single municipal court and received preclearance on March 6, 1995; its terms reportedly end in January 1997. Johnson v. Miller prevents racial predominance in a shared territorial electorate, standing alone, from supplying a constitutional defect. But neither that rule nor preclearance answers the separate objections concerning electoral and jurisdictional linkage, minimum population, divided municipalities and candidate residence, or establishes independent remedial authority. The Court selects no interim plan. Those matters, the exact 1983 submission and any actual officeholder circumstances require lawful determination below.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Presley v. Etowah County Commission; Mack v. Russell County Commission:** Distinguishes internal allocations of governing authority from the direct electorate change here; the former exclusion remains intact.
- **Morse v. Republican Party of Virginia:** Applies its bounded concern with actual electoral operation and its separate preclearance channels without enlarging its party-convention holding; no general delegation test or new private-action rule is adopted.
- **Allen v. State Board of Elections:** Applies protection of electoral choice to the changed means of electing judges.
- **Perkins v. Matthews:** Applies coverage of changes in electoral boundaries and the electorate, without equating coverage with discrimination.
- **McCain v. Lybrand:** Requires identification of the submitted change and rejects blanket approval of unidentified earlier practices.
- **Clark v. Roemer:** Enforces nonuse pending preclearance and preserves, without applying, its narrow extreme-circumstance reservation; the present ordinary six-year elections do not qualify.
- **NAACP v. Hampton County Election Commission:** Passage of time and implementation do not eliminate the submission requirement; actual circumstances remain relevant to lawful relief.
- **Connor v. Johnson:** Retains the distinction for a wholly judicial remedial plan.
- **McDaniel v. Sanchez:** A court's adoption of governmental policy does not transform it into an exempt judicial plan.
- **Shaw v. Reno, designated-channel holding:** Substantive preclearance remains with the Attorney General or D.D.C.; no enforcement-court substitute is authorized.
- **Johnson v. Miller:** Racial predominance in a shared territorial electorate alone does not establish constitutional injury or trigger strict scrutiny; separate statutory and constitutional objections remain open.

#### Law After the Decision

Section 5 now expressly reaches a covered County's nondiscretionary administration of state-mandated voting changes. The election must not proceed under the unprecleared governmental system merely because the State requires it, delay has occurred or a federal court has ordered it used. Existing direct-voting coverage, specific-submission, designated-channel and genuinely judicial-remedy rules remain. The new construction neither places all California law under §5 nor changes the substantive purpose/effect burdens. The temporary divisions remain an option for lawful consideration, with their independent defects and authority unresolved. No new general rule governing retrospective equitable relief is announced.

#### Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the Court's entire opinion and judgment. He also concludes that South Carolina v. Katzenbach, which sustains the Act's coverage and preclearance machinery, and City of Rome, which sustains its operation against covered governmental units, foreclose the facial constitutional objection to the County's operative designation. This is distinct from the Court's statutory bar on collateral retrial of the coverage findings. He further states that the wholly judicial exception does not apply when a court adopts material electoral policy choices proposed, required or previously selected by the County or the State. Necessity and independent remedial authority must support any genuinely court-formulated temporary plan; he selects no plan and decides no purpose or effect question. No other Justice joins these additional formulations.

The November 1, 1968 baseline, 1972–1983 consolidation sequence, disputed 1983 submission and order under review are the established record posture. The [appellants' merits brief, No. 95-1201](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0689), document 08, identifies the temporary division proceedings (JA123–137), the challenged order (JA165–173, 180) and the March 6, 1995 preclearance letter (JS App53–55). This appeal establishes no intervening Supreme Court stay, completed March election, cancellation, holdover or vacancy; the decision's prospective commands do not depend on any such event.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Supreme Court appeal is completed. The district court must enforce nonuse, determine the actual submission history, require expeditious use of the designated preclearance channels and consider any independently authorized interim arrangement. No deadline, permanent electoral plan, completed-election consequence or specific judicial tenure outcome is fixed here.

**End of entry: Lopez v. Monterey County, Merits decision, November 6, 1996.**

### INS v. Yueh-Shaio Yang, No. 95-938

Merits decision, November 13, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. Argued October 15, 1996.

The Ninth Circuit's June 22, 1995 judgment vacated the Board of Immigration Appeals' discretionary waiver denial and required reconsideration excluding fraud it regarded as intertwined with entry; rehearing was denied October 5, 1995. The INS seeks reversal, and Yang seeks affirmance of the restriction on discretionary consideration. The question is whether former §1251(a)(1)(H) permits consideration of immigration fraud connected with and extending beyond the entry fraud that made the applicant deportable. The corrected 1996 immigration-review provisions also require a threshold determination of review authority.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's categorical restriction is reversed. Further proceedings must recognize that the continuing fraud pattern is a permissible discretionary consideration, subject to the statute, binding agency rules and policies, and reasoned decisionmaking. The mandate removes compelled reweighing resting on the erroneous categorical exclusion. It does not itself grant or deny the waiver, require the Board to reopen or reconsider on a new ground, or direct removal.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing or position | Author or Justice | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reaches the merits | Eight-Justice review rationale |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Unanimous permissible-factor and policy rules |
| Concurrence in the threshold resolution | Stone-Zsela | — | Agrees that the merits may be reached | Does not join Part I; publishes no separate written opinion |

#### Holdings

##### I. Review under the corrected 1996 transition provisions

**Rule:** Review of this existing deportation proceeding remains available under former INA §106 and 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) on November 13, 1996. Corrected IIRIRA §306(c)(1) gives new §242(g) reach across past, pending and future proceedings when that provision commences; it does not advance commencement of the new jurisdictional rule from April 1, 1997, and the other review restrictions establish no present bar on this record.

**Authority:** Part I supplies the eight-Justice controlling rationale identified above. Stone-Zsela concurs only in the threshold resolution and does not join its reasons.

The Court reads the enacted timing instructions together. Section 309(a) sets April 1, 1997 as the general commencement date, expressly excepting §309 and §§303(b)(2), 306(c), 308(d)(2)(D) and 308(d)(5). Corrected §306(c)(1) applies the amendments as provided in §309, then separately extends §242(g) to old as well as new proceedings. The immediate-operation argument relies on those exceptions and the unrestricted reference to pending claims. But the exceptional clause specifies covered proceedings without supplying a replacement commencement date. Its present operation as an applicability instruction has independent work: after commencement, §242(g) reaches proceedings otherwise protected by grandfathering. This reading does not presume that jurisdiction can never change during litigation.

Corrected §309(c)(1), subject to its succeeding provisions, preserves the earlier proceedings and judicial review. No paragraph (2) or (3) agency election is established. Paragraph (4) requires a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996; it lists §§212(c), 212(h), 212(i), 244 and 245. The 1995 posture supplies no new triggering order, and that list omits former §241(a)(1)(H). Permanent §242(a)(2)(B)(i) instead lists §§212(h), 212(i), 240A, 240B and 245; clause (ii) separately addresses other statutorily discretionary authority, except §208(a) grants. Neither permanent clause governs this event.

Section 306(c)(2) protects existing §106 judgments and orders from invalidation or compelled reconsideration by paragraph (1); it does not independently establish appellate jurisdiction. Section 306(d)'s AEDPA-related criminal amendment supplies no bar without a covered conviction, which this record does not establish. The Court leaves open §242(g)'s scope after commencement, including its three subjects—commencing proceedings, adjudicating cases and executing removal orders—and its exception for review provided in §242. *Landgraf* and *Rivers* supply attention to Congress's actual temporal directions, not a new unanimous adoption of every earlier temporal rationale.

##### II. Eligibility does not exclude the continuing fraud pattern from discretion

**Rule:** Former INA §241(a)(1)(H), 8 U.S.C. §1251(a)(1)(H), permits the Attorney General to consider a continuing pattern of immigration fraud when choosing among eligible waiver applicants, even though particular acts are connected with entry. Eligibility does not erase that conduct or compel a waiver; the Court neither permits automatic denial to every eligible applicant because of the qualifying fraud nor decides whether the minimum qualifying fraud alone could sustain a particular individualized denial.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion controls unanimously.

The statute makes eligibility and favorable discretion distinct. It reaches covered entry fraud or misrepresentation, whether willful or innocent. An applicant must be the spouse, parent, son or daughter of a citizen or lawful permanent resident, have possessed an immigrant visa or equivalent document, and otherwise have been admissible at entry. The exceptions for labor-certification and immigrant-documentation grounds under §§1182(a)(5)(A) and (7)(A) apply only when those grounds directly resulted from the fraud. The separate exclusion for assistance in Nazi persecution or participation in genocide remains. An actually granted waiver also reaches entry-inadmissibility grounds directly resulting from the fraud. These provisions define eligibility and the consequence of relief; they do not remove conduct from the discretionary decision.

*Jay v. Boyd* supplies the distinction between statutory eligibility and discretionary selection. *INS v. Errico* construed a mandatory predecessor; its family-preservation concern does not convert the later discretionary provision into an entitlement. *National Center for Immigrants' Rights* supports discretion exercised within the statute while preserving required individual consideration.

The Board accepted eligibility and expressly disregarded the initial entry fraud. It considered the wife's false-identity scheme and sham marital arrangements, the later naturalization fraud, further marital arrangements and lack of candor, together with favorable residence, business, property, family and health considerations and Taiwan connections. Those are the Board's findings and assessments. The Ninth Circuit allowed dishonest testimony to count but wrongly excluded collaboration and naturalization conduct because of their connection with entry. The argument that waiver discretion must remain meaningful therefore does not justify its categorical rule. A continuing pattern distinguishes applicants without making every eligible applicant lose. The Court does not reweigh the balance.

##### III. Permissible factors remain subject to reasoned and consistent administration

**Rule:** Statutory permission to consider a factor does not excuse arbitrary decisionmaking, pretext, disregard of a binding rule, or an unexplained departure from a genuinely settled agency policy. Here the Board rationally distinguished continuing immigration fraud from the initial entry fraud it disregarded; the record supplies no policy reversal requiring a new administrative balance.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion controls unanimously.

The Court separates statutory authority from the lawfulness of its exercise. *Smiley*, applying the administrative-change principles preserved from *Good Samaritan*, requires examination of the agency's actual reasons, prior guidance and legitimate reliance. A court cannot supply a litigation explanation for an unexplained change. An agency may revise a policy openly with adequate explanation, subject to binding law; the existence of discretion alone supplies no answer.

The Board retained its initial-fraud distinction and explained the significance of the continuing pattern. Its actual reasons defeat the claim that this decision irrationally abandoned that distinction. The Court neither finds a different binding policy nor commands a new explanation for an unproved reversal. The mandate removes the Ninth Circuit's compelled reweighing based on categorical exclusion. Any genuinely remaining preserved objection is for ordinary proceedings within the record and mandate; the Court directs neither a waiver nor its denial, reopening, a new hearing, or a fresh Board balance. This decision concerns the statutory immigration function presented, not classifications outside it.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Landgraf v. USI Film Products* and *Rivers v. Roadway Express*: applied to the actual congressional timing instructions; their distinct temporal rationales are not combined into a broader unanimous framework.
- *Jay v. Boyd*, 351 U.S. 345: applied for eligibility's separation from discretionary selection; it remains authority for that distinction.
- *INS v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214: distinguished because its mandatory predecessor differs from the discretionary text here; its own construction is not overruled.
- *INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights* (December 16, 1991): applied by analogy for statutory boundaries and legally required individual consideration; its release-condition holding is not transformed into a waiver decision.
- *Good Samaritan Hospital v. Shalala* and *Smiley* (June 3, 1996): applied for reasoned administrative interpretation and explained treatment of settled policy and reliance; statutory discretion does not displace those limits.
- *INS v. Doherty* (January 15, 1992): its fractured disposition supplies no controlling substantive reopening rationale for this waiver question; no separate rationale is promoted to Court law.

#### Law After the Decision

Effective November 13, 1996, courts may not exclude a continuing immigration-fraud pattern from former §241(a)(1)(H) discretion merely because its acts are connected with entry. The eligibility conditions, the distinct consequence of a granted waiver, meaningful individual discretion and ordinary review of actual agency reasons remain operative. The Court leaves the minimum-fraud-only individualized case unresolved and permits no universal eligibility-based denial. The specific transition construction preserves review at this event; it decides no post-April §242(g) scope question. No waiver is awarded or denied by this Court, and the agency balance is not judicially redone.

The [Government petition and reproduced agency and lower decisions](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0678) and [Ninth Circuit opinion, 58 F.3d 452](https://openjurist.org/58/f3d/452) support the accepted eligibility, agency reasons, balance and judgment below. The [reported case chronology](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-938.ZS.html) supplies the argument and decision dates. Former §1251(a)(1)(H), IIRIRA §§306 and 309, and [Public Law 104-302 §2](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ302/html/PLAW-104publ302.htm) supply the enacted eligibility and transition provisions; the technical correction is effective September 30, 1996.

The Board's findings, rather than a disputed fine detail of the naturalization form, support the account of misconduct. No new final administrative order, agency election or covered criminal conviction is established. The case does not depend on an unproved change of binding policy or an unestablished removal event.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit receives the cause for proceedings consistent with the permissible-factor ruling and the mandate limits. The Supreme Court merits decision is complete; no retained-jurisdiction return, new deadline, stay or removal command is ordered.

### Ohio v. Robinette, No. 95-891

Merits decision, November 18, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 8, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Ohio. Ohio seeks reversal of suppression and reinstatement of the conviction; Robinette seeks affirmance or a totality-based remand. The questions concern a categorical warning that a traffic detention has ended, the actual transition to a consensual encounter, and validity of consent. The Ohio judgment invoked the Fourth Amendment and Article I, §14 of the Ohio Constitution, held the detention unlawful and unattenuated, and separately imposed a release-warning rule. Both grounds are before the Court.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, who would vacate and remand, and Stevens, who would affirm suppression.

Eight Justices, including Stone-Zsela, support returning the case for application of the proper federal standards, but his vacatur vote is not a vote for reversal. The Court rejects the categorical federal warning requirement and the motive-based premise of the exit-order analysis. The Ohio courts must consider the actual objective transition, voluntariness and any necessary causal separation from an unlawful detention. The mandate neither orders admission of the evidence nor reinstates the conviction, and it requires no new hearing as such.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion: Parts I–IV |
| Partial joins in the Court opinion | Stone-Zsela | — | Would vacate and remand | Part II.B, objective continued-detention inquiry; Part III, no mandatory federal script and totality consent; Part IV's remand inquiries and limits, excluding reversal as the formal disposition |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stone-Zsela | — | Vacatur and remand | Preserves actual encounter analysis and his distinct formal remedy; adds no ground on Part I or Part II.A |
| Opinion concurring in the judgment | Ginsburg | — | Reversal and remand | Accepts review under Long and the federal no-script/totality correction; emphasizes independent Ohio law and reserves actual search validity |
| Dissent | Stevens | — | Affirmance of suppression | Accepts review and rejects an indispensable federal script, but finds continued unlawful detention and no attenuation on this record |

Parts I and II.A have six direct joins. Part II.B, Part III and the identified remand framework in Part IV have seven direct joins. Ginsburg's judgment concurrence and Stevens's agreement on the absence of a federal script are not counted as whole-opinion joins. No controlling rationale depends on combining separate opinions.

#### Holdings

##### I. Review of the interwoven federal and state grounds

**Rule:** When a state judgment fairly appears primarily federal or interwoven with federal law and does not clearly state an independently sufficient state ground, the Court may review its federal basis. Ohio's intertwined constitutional explanation supplies no clear independent ground barring review here; Ohio remains free to supply greater protection on genuinely independent state-law grounds.

**Authority:** Part I controls through the six direct joins identified above. Stone-Zsela agrees to exercise review without joining this explanation; Ginsburg and Stevens accept review in their separate writings.

The Court holds that Ohio's reference to its own Constitution does not end the jurisdictional inquiry. Michigan v. Long supplies the rule for opinions that rest on federal law or interweave federal and state law without a clear independent basis. Arizona v. Evans retains that rule. The Ohio opinion invokes both provisions and develops the suppression analysis with federal authorities; it does not announce that an independent Ohio ground alone sustains the judgment. Federal review therefore lies. Deciding the federal question neither erases the Ohio provision nor prevents the Ohio courts from adopting a clearly independent state rule in further proceedings.

##### II. Objective legality during and after a traffic stop

**Rule:** An officer's uncommunicated investigative motive or private decision to issue only a warning does not invalidate an objectively lawful traffic stop or an exit order while lawful traffic business is being completed. Once that justification ends, further detention requires independent legal grounds; the court must examine the objective circumstances to determine whether the person could terminate the encounter, and return of a license is neither an automatic end of seizure nor an automatic license to continue it.

**Authority:** Part II.A's motive and exit-order rule has the six full-opinion joins identified above. Part II.B's distinct continued-detention rule has those six plus Stone-Zsela. He supplies no additional reason for Part II.A.

The Court holds that an objectively justified beginning and a lawful continuation are separate questions. Whren rejects subjective purpose as an extra condition for an ordinary probable-cause traffic stop while preserving limits on manner, scope and duration. Mimms permits an exit order during a lawful traffic stop without a separate justification for that direction. The Ohio court could not deem the direction unlawful merely because the deputy privately intended a warning or hoped to ask unrelated questions.

That correction does not establish that detention lawfully continued through the later consent request. Bostick requires an objective assessment of whether the person could decline the request or terminate the encounter. The returned license and completed warning bear on that inquiry, but so do the immediacy and actual wording of further questioning, tone, positioning and continuity of official authority. Neither the officer's secret willingness to permit refusal nor Robinette's subjective impression alone decides the seizure question. Supported trial findings matter; the Court does not replace the record with a categorical conclusion in either direction. Ornelas's distinction between historical fact and ultimate constitutional sufficiency remains applicable within its decided scope; this decision announces no universal appellate-review rule for every mixed or consent question. The encounter's classification remains for proper application below.

##### III. No indispensable federal release warning; voluntary consent and attenuation remain distinct

**Rule:** The Fourth Amendment does not invariably require an officer to give a free-to-go warning before seeking consent to search. Consent must be voluntary under all the circumstances, and when an unlawful detention preceded or accompanied it, voluntariness alone does not establish that the consent was sufficiently independent of that illegality to permit use of the evidence.

**Authority:** Part III controls with the seven direct joins identified above. Ginsburg and Stevens separately reject an indispensable federal script; neither additional agreement is needed to establish the Court's rule.

The Court holds that a useful warning is not automatically an indispensable constitutional condition. Schneckloth requires voluntary consent under the total circumstances and treats knowledge of the right to refuse as relevant without making advice of that right universally mandatory. A fixed release formula would replace that inquiry rather than perform it. The absence of a clear release signal nevertheless remains important evidence; rejecting a script does not make every post-warning request consensual.

The supported trial findings concerning the manner, wording and positions of the parties, Robinette's education and the conclusion of traffic business must be considered with the returned license and actual continuation words. His testimony includes both an initial belief that he could leave and a later feeling that he could not refuse. Neither fragment decides voluntariness by itself. The Court holds neither that this consent was valid nor that it was invalid.

Royer and Brown keep consent's voluntariness separate from its causal independence of unlawful detention. If the court finds an unsupported continued seizure, it must address that additional question; voluntary consent does not automatically erase the illegality, and illegality does not establish that every later consent is incurably tainted. Suppression remains available when the applicable inquiry fails. Nothing in federal law prevents Ohio from requiring clearer warnings under independent state law or police policy.

##### IV. The remedy corrects the federal rules without deciding admissibility

**Rule:** Rejection of the warning rule and the motive-based exit-order premise requires further proceedings on the actual objective detention, voluntary consent and any necessary attenuation question. It does not itself establish the search's validity, require admission of the evidence or reinstate the conviction.

**Authority:** Part IV has the six full-opinion joins identified above; Stone-Zsela joins its stated inquiries and limits while selecting vacatur instead of reversal. Ginsburg separately supports reversal with the same unresolved-application boundary. The seven-Justice formal reversal coalition remains distinct from the joins in these reasons.

The Court holds that Ohio's two grounds must be addressed separately. The federal script requirement fails, and the detention ruling cannot rest on undisclosed motives during lawful traffic business. Yet those errors do not decide whether the later encounter was consensual, lawfully justified or unlawfully extended, or whether consent was both voluntary and independent of any illegality. The state courts must apply the rules to the supported findings and complete encounter. They may consider genuinely independent state law. A new hearing is not commanded merely by this remand, and the Court enters no final evidence-admission or conviction order.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Michigan v. Long:** Applies the presumption permitting review of interwoven federal grounds absent a plain independent-state-law basis. No exception to the ordinary final-judgment requirement is created.
- **Arizona v. Evans:** Retains that jurisdictional rule and the distinction between correcting a federal rule and deciding its unresolved factual application; its court-record-error exclusion rule is not extended here.
- **Whren v. United States:** Applies objective legality to the initial traffic business and preserves independent limits on manner, scope and duration.
- **Pennsylvania v. Mimms:** Applies the permission to order a driver out during a lawful stop; no indefinite post-stop detention authority follows.
- **Florida v. Bostick:** Requires assessment of the actual objective capacity to decline requests or terminate the encounter, without a categorical seizure or release rule.
- **Ornelas v. United States:** Preserves its historical-fact/ultimate-sufficiency distinction within its actual scope; no general review rule for all consent questions is added.
- **Schneckloth v. Bustamonte:** Applies total-circumstances voluntariness and treats knowledge as relevant rather than an indispensable warning requirement.
- **Florida v. Royer:** Preserves the need to consider whether consent follows or remains a product of unlawful detention.
- **Brown v. Illinois:** Preserves attenuation as distinct from voluntariness; neither a voluntary response nor the occurrence of illegality alone answers the remedial question.

#### Law After the Decision

Federal law imposes no universal release script, but the objective seizure, voluntary-consent and necessary attenuation inquiries remain distinct and enforceable. The decision supplies no categorical rule that a returned license ends a seizure, that a traffic offense authorizes later unrelated detention, or that a polite request ensures admissibility. The actual search result remains unresolved under the corrected federal standards, and genuinely independent Ohio protection remains available.

#### Separate Writings

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He would vacate the Ohio judgment and remand. He joins the no-script rule, the objective continued-seizure inquiry, and the Court's requirements to examine voluntariness and any necessary causal relationship to unlawful detention. A State cannot convert uninterrupted official restraint into consent merely by describing it as a request. Immediate questioning, documents, tone, positioning and the absence of an objective release signal bear on whether the motorist remained seized and whether the choice was real. A clear warning is strong evidence and may be required by state law or policy. He reaches no final admissibility result and preserves suppression where the applicable inquiries fail. He agrees to federal review and to setting aside the motive-based exit-order ground without adding a reason on those components; his chosen formal disposition remains vacatur, not reversal.

**Justice Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She accepts federal review under governing Long despite her earlier preference in Evans for a different jurisdictional presumption. The Ohio opinion's interwoven analysis supplies no clearly independent state basis. She agrees that federal law requires no universal release script and that actual consent must be assessed under the total circumstances. Her agreement does not decide that Robinette's search was valid or that no detention continued. She emphasizes that Ohio may use its own Constitution to require a clear warning, provided it rests the rule on an independent state ground. Federal correction of this judgment does not displace that authority. She does not join Stevens's conclusion that this record already requires suppression.

**Justice Stevens, dissenting.** He accepts review and rejects an indispensable federal warning formula, but would affirm suppression on the actual encounter. His Bostick dissent's attention to the practical force of police authority, together with Royer and Brown's separation of consent from unlawful detention, leads him to treat the continued official direction after completion of traffic business as restraint requiring an independent justification. The deputy's question before departure continued that restraint; no separate suspicion or intervening circumstance supplies a lawful extension or breaks the connection to consent. Returning the license and Robinette's initial sense of freedom do not decide the effect of the subsequent direction. The deputy's courtesy, lack of a drawn weapon and Robinette's education bear on voluntariness, but furnish no intervening break from the detention. Stevens does not invalidate the initial stop or a lawful exit order because of hidden motive, and he proposes no automatic suppression rule after every illegality. His disagreement is with the Court's refusal to affirm suppression on this record, not with Ohio's freedom to require warnings under independent state law.

#### Source Notes

The [petition and joint appendix in No. 95-891](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0675), documents 02 and 06, supply the complete Ohio judgments and trial ruling (Pet. App. 1–31), return of the license before the requests (JA11–13), Robinette's mixed testimony about freedom and refusal (JA26–27, 29, 31), and the deputy's manner and Robinette's education (JA28, 33). The trial court reviewed a stipulated videotape and found a nonoverbearing encounter and clear completion of traffic business; those findings are distinguished from the appellate constitutional classification. The actual transition wording remains part of the totality. An isolated compound question and suppression memorandum do not establish a specific spoken release formula against the state opinion's account.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Supreme Court review concludes. On remand, the Ohio courts must apply the stated federal standards to both lower grounds and the supported record, while retaining their independent state-law authority. No new hearing, admission of evidence, conviction reinstatement or final suppression result is ordered.

### Hopwood v. Texas, Fifth Circuit No. 94-50664

Merits decision, November 25, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

The Fifth Circuit's decision, 78 F.3d 932, reviewed the judgment at 861 F. Supp. 551. On review of the Fifth Circuit’s judgment of March 18, 1996; rehearing denied April 4, 1996.

**Questions:** Whether racial and ethnic educational diversity can be a compelling interest in public-university admissions; whether race-conscious preferences may remedy past discrimination and what predicate and necessity must be shown; and whether the law school’s 1992 system was constitutional and what relief the affected applicants may obtain. The related intervention appeal, No. 94-50569, is outside the grant.

**Record and posture:** Four white Texas applicants—Cheryl Hopwood, Douglas Carvell, Kenneth Elliott and David Rogers—challenge the 1992 process in an individual, nonclass suit. They sought declaration, injunction, admission, compensatory damages and punitive damages under the Equal Protection Clause, §§1981 and 1983 and Title VI. The named defendants include State entities and officials in their official capacities. The District Court found the separate discretionary-review machinery unlawful, awarded one dollar nominal damages to each applicant, declaratory relief and fee-free reapplication, and refused compensation, punitive damages, admission and a broad injunction. The Fifth Circuit remanded individual causation under a school-borne preponderance burden, rejected diversity and the offered remedial grounds, retained the punitive and injunction denials, and left Eleventh Amendment immunity undecided.

In March 1992, resident presumptive-admit index thresholds were 199 for white and other nonpreferred applicants and 189 for Black and Mexican-American applicants; presumptive-deny thresholds were 192 and 179. Earlier preferred-group thresholds varied. Score categories admitted exceptions: some presumptive-admit files moved to discretionary review, and some low-score files could move upward. The preferred groups had separate discretionary review and waiting lists. Enrollment aspirations and observed percentages were not proved fixed reserved seats. How different waiting lists were compared for final places is not fully established. Hopwood’s 199 index led to discretionary review after an assessment of her educational background; the other applicants had indexes of 197. The later program changed the old arrangements, but its full operation is not before the Court.

#### Holdings

##### I. The established unequal admissions process

**Rule:** The State’s racial admissions classifications must satisfy strict scrutiny: the asserted objective must be compelling and the means narrowly fitted to it. The 1992 law-school process fails because it subjected applicants to unjustified, separate racial decision systems rather than genuinely competitive individual consideration; unequal opportunity to compete is an injury without proof that an applicant would have received a seat.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion controls unanimously. It concerns the actual separate competitive process, without declaring each index differential independently unconstitutional or prohibiting every consideration of race.

City of Richmond v. J. A. Croson Co. requires a State to substantiate the justification and fit of its racial classifications; Wygant v. Jackson Board of Education requires attention to the evidence, alternatives and burdens on individuals. Bakke’s rejection of reserved seats and its recognition of competitive consideration distinguish a genuine comparison of applicants from separate racial allocation. Adarand’s equal-competition holding establishes the personal injury from unequal competition; its distinct federal merits rule does not govern Texas’s classification.

The 1992 process used different presumptive thresholds, separate discretionary committees and waiting lists, racial coding, and enrollment aspirations. There were individual exceptions to the score bands, and the aspirations were not an established fixed set-aside. Those qualifications do not answer the proved injury: applicants received individualized consideration within materially different racial decision systems, without a sufficient justification for separating their competitive treatment. Neither general educational benefits nor the State’s history establishes the necessary connection between that treatment and its asserted purpose. Correcting the appellate court’s categorical rules therefore does not reopen the established violation. The Court makes no finding that race fixed every final seat, that every feature independently failed, or that any of these four applicants certainly would have been admitted.

##### II. A supported educational-diversity interest is constitutionally available

**Rule:** A public university’s interest in concrete educational benefits from a genuinely diverse student body can be compelling on a properly supported record. The university must establish the educational objective and demonstrate that its actual racial classification is narrowly fitted to it; a diversity label, racial balance, or an assumption that racial identity determines viewpoint does not suffice.

**Authority:** Part II controls with the six direct joins identified above. Kennedy supports vacating the categorical appellate prohibition but reserves the affirmative interest question. Scalia and Thomas oppose the holding. Its authority does not depend on combining separate rationales.

Bakke did not produce five votes for every part of Justice Powell’s educational-diversity reasoning. It did produce five express joins in Part V-C’s recognition that a properly designed program using competitive consideration of race and ethnic origin may serve a substantial state interest, and in rejection of an injunction against every use of race. The Court preserves that actual proposition and now adopts the more specific constitutional rule stated here; it does not attribute today’s holding to a nonexistent earlier majority.

A university may substantiate educational benefits from interaction among students with different experiences. It must show what the benefit is and why the means advance it, while accounting for the burden imposed on applicants. The argument that all racial consideration necessarily assigns a viewpoint to every member of a group does not defeat a supported, genuinely individual educational judgment. It does require rejecting racial proxies, numerical representation as an end, and assumed validity from academic preference alone. Strict scrutiny continues to govern the means. That distinction permits recognition of the interest while invalidating the actual separate process. The categorical prohibition also controls the admissions counterfactual used on remand, so correcting it has work to do in these live individual claims. No later Texas program is approved, and no university is ordered to use race.

##### III. The responsible State and the continuing dismantlement duty

**Rule:** A State that operated a legally segregated university system must eliminate traceable policies that continue to foster segregation when their effects can practicably be removed consistently with sound educational practices; good faith and facial neutrality alone do not discharge that duty, and the State bears the showing of dismantlement. The responsible actor cannot categorically be confined to the law school as an administrative unit, but a remedial racial classification requires a demonstrated connection and fit to a continuing wrong rather than generalized societal discrimination or racial imbalance alone.

**Authority:** Part III controls with the eight direct joins identified above. Scalia does not join the affirmative institutional rule or further relevant inquiry.

Fordice and Ayers establish the State’s continuing responsibility for its former dual university system, including the interaction of inherited policies. They do not demand fresh discriminatory intent for each established remnant or allow a State to prove discharge solely by adopting neutral rules. They equally do not make present racial imbalance itself a violation. Croson requires evidence of the wrong and the fit of the proposed racial remedy; a new formal adjudication of intentional wrongdoing is not invariably a prerequisite to supported voluntary repair. Jenkins preserves violation-linked educational repair and causally bounded remedies without authorizing general educational improvement or an unproved interdistrict remedy.

The District Court’s university-level findings remain in the record. Neither the law school’s administrative identity nor the absence of recent overt officially sanctioned discrimination alone resolves their legal significance. The courts below must determine whether a traceable, continuing remnant bears on the lawful admissions counterfactual or a properly presented remedy, and whether a proposed response is fitted to it. Findings retain their ordinary review; their legal adequacy is a distinct question. No remnant, necessary preference, or completed discharge is found here. The already established 1992 process violation stands regardless of that inquiry. The remand does not reopen unrelated parts of Texas education or authorize a new general structural proceeding in this four-person suit.

##### IV-A. Individual causation and the same-decision burden

**Rule:** Once these applicants have established the unconstitutional admissions process, the school must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that each applicant would not have been admitted under a specified constitutional process to defeat relief for the denied admission and its consequences. Plaintiffs still must establish the existence and amount of compensable loss under an available cause against a proper defendant; failure of the school’s defense yields neither an automatic damages amount nor unconditional admission.

**Authority:** All eight Associate Justices join Part IV-A's burden and proof rule. Stone-Zsela concurs in the individual-remand judgment on his separate causation ground.

Mt. Healthy supplies the same-decision defense after an unconstitutional consideration is established; Arlington Heights applies that causal distinction to a discriminatory decision. Carey distinguishes compensation for actual injury from nominal recognition of a completed violation. The District Court’s Title VII analogy cannot leave ultimate persuasion about the same denial with applicants who have already established the unconstitutional process. Nor does equal-competition standing prove entitlement to the seat.

Bakke’s admission relief followed the university’s concession that it could not carry its burden; there is no equivalent established outcome for these four applicants. The inquiry into what would have happened under a lawful admissions process must follow this Court’s substantive rules. It cannot assume that every possible consideration of race is forbidden. Scalia and Thomas join the general allocation and quantum while adhering to their separate, narrower view of constitutionally permissible admissions; their votes do not support Part II. The District Court must reconsider the tension between its nonadmission conclusion and its finding of an uncommunicated late offer to Elliott. It must not treat that offer as communicated or accepted. Carvell’s tuition testimony identifies a possible loss to evaluate, not an awarded sum. A successful same-decision defense does not erase the separate unequal-process injury.

##### IV-B. Purposeful treatment and statutory intent

**Rule:** Deliberately using race to give applicants unequal admissions treatment is intentional racial discrimination for the Title VI issue presented, even when officials act in good faith and intend educational or remedial benefits. That conclusion does not establish every element of a statutory remedy, a proper defendant, or a waiver of immunity.

**Authority:** All eight Associate Justices join Part IV-B. Stone-Zsela agrees only with the disposition retaining the determination and remanding the remaining claim-specific questions.

The racial treatment here was expressly designed and administered. The relevant intent question is whether the different treatment was purposeful, not whether officials were hostile to the applicants. Bakke’s attention to the individual applicant’s statutory protection is consistent with separating purposeful unequal treatment from the availability of a particular award. A benevolent aim may be offered in justification of a classification; it does not turn the classification into an unintended disparate effect. Sections 1981 and 1983 and Title VI retain their distinct requirements. The Court adjudicates no independent disparate-impact challenge to the index and no claim belonging to the unsuccessful intervenors.

##### IV-C. The punitive request on this record

**Rule:** Smith v. Wade permits punitive damages in its applicable civil-rights setting for evil motive or intent, or for reckless or callous indifference to federally protected rights; lack of malice alone is not the whole standard. The punitive denial is affirmed on this record’s accepted good-faith finding and unsettled legal setting, without establishing a universal good-purpose defense or deciding the availability of punitive relief under every pleaded statute.

**Authority:** All eight Associate Justices join Part IV-C and its complete Smith standard. Stone-Zsela agrees with affirming the punitive denial without joining its rationale.

The applicants invoked reckless indifference, and the precise formulation used by the District Court is not fully established. The Court therefore does not affirm an animus-only test or suppose that an asserted beneficial purpose always answers recklessness. Smith’s alternative culpability paths remain intact. The accepted good-faith determination, considered in the legal setting of the challenged conduct, supports the bounded denial actually before the Court. Purposeful racial treatment sufficient for the statutory-intent issue is not itself a finding of the culpability necessary for punishment. No later official’s state of mind is determined, and no new defendant or punitive cause is supplied.

##### IV-D. Source-specific relief and undecided defenses

**Rule:** The established unequal process supports declaratory and nominal recognition independently of proof of entitlement to admission, subject to the available cause, proper defendant and immunity rules. Preserve that relief and fee-free lawful reconsideration if still practically available, while remanding the remaining claim-specific questions; no compensation, admission, immunity waiver, or personal-capacity liability is awarded here.

**Authority:** Part IV-D controls unanimously.

Carey preserves nominal recognition when actual compensable injury has not been proved. Adarand’s competition-injury rule does not determine the distinct availability of a remedy. The named officials were sued in their official capacities, and the court below expressly reserved Eleventh Amendment immunity. The four plaintiffs’ claims under Title VI and §§1981 and 1983 cannot be merged into an undifferentiated money judgment against every named defendant. Proper defendants, waiver, any properly arising personal-immunity question, statutory damages, fees and the precise remaining relief must be resolved under their own sources. The Court creates no individual-capacity claim from the pleadings before it. Fee-free reapplication is consideration under a lawful process, not a right to a seat; admission relief additionally depends on the individual causal findings and equitable circumstances.

##### V. Prospective relief and the bounded remand

**Rule:** The refusal to issue a broad prospective injunction remains intact, and directions purporting to govern unexamined future programs beyond resolution of these 1992 claims are vacated. Any later request for prospective relief requires its own sufficiently concrete future exposure, supported record and equitable basis; neither the discontinued procedure nor the existence of retrospective claims alone supplies those requirements.

**Authority:** Part V controls unanimously. Scalia and Thomas's disagreement with the substantive diversity rule supplies no assent to a new injunction or adjudication of the later program.

Adarand separates injury from past unequal competition from the real renewed exposure necessary for prospective relief. The four individual damages claims keep the 1992 controversy live, but do not authorize a perpetual order concerning every future admissions practice. The later procedure is described as using a small administrative group without the old presumptive scores; its full operation and these plaintiffs’ exposure are not established. Changed procedures therefore establish neither universal mootness nor a future violation. The Court decides no later design and enters no injunction. The corrected substantive rules govern the lawful counterfactual and properly presented relief below. They do not commission an advisory review of other institutions, educational levels, employment practices or an unidentified replacement program.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

No separate intervention disposition or independent intervenor Title VI index claim is before the Court. The Court does not decide whether any particular holistic program with race as one factor is constitutional, prescribe a universal admissions model or numerical test, require race-conscious action, or adjudicate outreach, barrier removal, race-neutral contextual review or demographic data collection. It does not find a current Texas remnant, a particular applicant’s lawful admission outcome, the amount of actual loss, or a claim-specific immunity waiver. Federal classifications remain governed by their distinct existing authorities; admissions at other educational levels and race-conscious employment decisions are outside this decision.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Bakke, 438 U.S. 265:** Preserves the invalidity of the reserved-seat system, the express five-Justice Part V-C proposition permitting properly designed competitive consideration, and the distinction between a process injury and an admission remedy. The Court adopts its affirmative educational-interest rule today; it does not call Powell’s entire rationale an earlier majority or use Marks to manufacture one.
- **City of Richmond v. J. A. Croson Co., 488 U.S. 469:** Applies strict scrutiny to the State’s classification, requiring concrete justification and fit. General societal discrimination remains insufficient; the case does not create an absolute administrative-unit limit for an established state university duty.
- **Wygant v. Jackson Board of Education, 476 U.S. 267:** Applies its requirements of evidence, fit and attention to individual burdens. Remedial purpose supplies no exemption, and no rule requiring a new formal violation finding before every supported voluntary repair is adopted.
- **Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña, June 12, 1995:** Applies equal-competition injury and separate prospective-exposure requirements. Its federal merits holding preserving Metro’s important-objective/substantial-relationship review is left intact and supplies no exception for this state program.
- **Metro Broadcasting, Inc. v. FCC, 497 U.S. 547:** Remains in force at its federal scope under Adarand; this decision neither overrules it nor transfers its standard to Texas.
- **United States v. Fordice; Ayers v. Fordice, June 26, 1992:** Applies the State’s affirmative dismantlement duty, discharge burden, traceability, continuing-effects and practicable-removal limits. It supplies no presumption that Texas currently has a particular remnant and no approval of the challenged preferences.
- **Missouri v. Jenkins, June 12, 1995:** Preserves violation-linked educational repair, causal limits and the distinction between good intentions and completed compliance. Its approved measures do not independently authorize these admissions preferences.
- **Mt. Healthy City School District Board of Education v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274:** Applies the defendant’s same-decision burden after an unconstitutional consideration is established.
- **Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp., 429 U.S. 252:** Applies the same-decision causal distinction; its ordinary intent analysis for a new violation does not replace Fordice’s inherited-duty rule.
- **Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247:** Applies the distinction between actual compensable injury and nominal recognition; a proved constitutional defect is not an automatic compensatory award.
- **Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30:** Preserves both evil-motive-or-intent and reckless-or-callous-indifference paths. The punitive denial creates no animus-only test or general benevolent-purpose immunity.

#### Law After the Decision

Effective November 25, 1996, public-university educational diversity is a constitutionally available compelling objective when concretely established, with strict scrutiny of the actual means and individual burdens. This is an affirmative holding by six Justices, beyond Bakke’s previously joined competitive-consideration proposition. It validates no later program or racial balance requirement.

The State’s existing Fordice duty survives administrative division among its institutions. The categorical law-school-only cutoff is removed; traceability, continuing effects, educational justification, practicability and causal fit remain essential. The particular 1992 unequal process remains unlawful. No current remnant or justified preference is found.

These individual claims proceed under the school’s preponderance same-decision burden, with separate proof of actual loss, causes, defendants and immunity. Good faith does not erase purposeful racial treatment under the Title VI issue, and purposeful treatment does not itself establish punitive culpability. The full Smith alternatives remain in force. No broad future-program order issues. Federal Metro/Adarand law and unrelated admissions and employment settings remain unchanged. The separate opinions’ broader prohibitions, operational formulations and repair safeguards are noncontrolling except to the extent expressly adopted in the Court’s identified parts.

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** Kennedy joins Parts I, III and IV–V but does not join Part II. He would assume a potentially compelling educational interest and invalidate the actual process for inadequate tailoring. Croson supports rigorous application without an unnecessary categorical prohibition; his Metro reasoning rejects assigning a contribution to an applicant merely from racial identity. The combination of different thresholds and separate decision rules remains unjustified despite individual exceptions within each track. He agrees that the appellate diversity prohibition must be removed from this remand but reserves the affirmative compelling-interest question. He neither requires identical numerical evaluation in every future program nor approves the later design.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part; Thomas joins Section A.** Section A rejects nonremedial racial preferences as a justification for burdening an individual applicant. The equal-protection right belongs to the person, and educational aims do not turn racial ancestry into an entitlement or establish that group membership predicts educational contribution. The actual five-Justice Bakke Part V-C proposition is acknowledged; these Justices would expressly disapprove it to the extent it authorizes nonremedial preferences. They therefore dissent from vacating the diversity prohibition and from Part II, while joining the narrow invalidity holding and the individual causation/remedy provisions. They accept the same-decision allocation and preponderance requirement, but their substantive view excludes nonremedial preferences from the lawful counterfactual. Individual experiences may still be considered under lawful nonracial criteria.

In Section B, which Thomas does not join, Scalia declines the broader institutional rule and further remedial inquiry in Part III. Even assuming a broader responsible unit, the record does not establish that these preferences were necessary to remove a traceable barrier. He recognizes Fordice as existing authority and does not seek to overrule it, but would resolve this offered defense without the additional remand inquiry. Thomas instead joins Part III’s bounded recognition of the State’s duty; that join does not equate enrollment targets with removal of inherited barriers. Neither Justice supports a new injunction or punitive award, treats benevolent purpose as eliminating statutory intent, or deems every constitutionally valid remedial act forbidden merely because it considers race.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** The Chief Justice joins Parts I–III, IV-D and V. He agrees with the individual-relief remand and punitive denial, without joining Parts IV-A–C. He would assess claimed individualization by actual operation: the order of decisions, thresholds, separate review, numerical aims, discretion, beneficiary categories, whether assumptions can genuinely be rebutted, and workable alternatives. A small formal weight for race is no answer when it determines outcomes near the admission threshold. Conversely, a program lacking quotas, separate tracks and numerical aims is not invalid merely because race is one consideration among several; no particular such program is approved. The combined 1992 design made race decisive across broad categories without the supported necessity and individualized fit he requires.

He understands a permissible educational objective to require concrete benefits from meaningful institutional inclusion, an explanation of the mechanism connecting the classification to those benefits, and attention to actual burdens. Racial arithmetic, generalized societal discrimination, reputation, hostility outside the institution, and assumptions that identity predicts viewpoint cannot substitute for that showing. For corrective measures he additionally requires objective evidence of the persisting effect, scope fitted to it, meaningful continuing opportunity for others, respect for legally protected existing interests, and termination when the basis ends. The State bears its justification burden. These additional formulations are his separate grounds, not additions to the Court’s controlling rule.

His causation position distinguishes the established unequal process from a claimed lost seat or monetary loss. A defendant may defeat admission-related compensation by proving that a specified lawful process would have produced the same denial, without erasing the violation. He would preserve nominal and declaratory recognition and fee-free lawful consideration if practically available, leaving the proper cause, defendant, immunity and precise relief for determination below. He agrees with the disposition of the separate statutory-intent component without adopting the Court’s statutory rationale.

#### Holdings

##### No live geographic placement controversy

### United States v. Jose, No. 95-2082

Summary appellate disposition, December 2, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Submitted without oral argument. On review of the Ninth Circuit's dismissal of the Government's appeal from a completed conditional summons-enforcement order. Two summonses concerned Jose as trustee of two trusts. The question is immediate appellate review of the five-day notice condition before internal IRS circulation, transfer or copying. The Ninth Circuit accepted section 1291 jurisdiction but dismissed for ripeness; its dissent would have reached the condition's merits. The United States seeks reversal and merits review; Jose invokes the absence of a concrete proposed transfer and defends the condition.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Both summonses are within the remand; the Court neither invalidates the condition nor decides its ultimate merits.

#### Opinions and Joins

The Court issues a per curiam opinion joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Holdings

##### Immediate review of the completed conditional enforcement judgment

**Rule:** An order completing a summons-enforcement proceeding is final under 28 U.S.C. §1291, including its presently binding notice condition. The Government's challenge to this condition is ripe before an interdivision transfer occurs because the judgment already fixes its legal obligation; it need not violate the order or await contempt to obtain review.

**Authority:** The unanimous per curiam opinion controls on ordinary finality and the concrete present restraint.

The district court enforced two summonses directed to Jose as trustee of two trusts, while requiring five days' notice before circulating, transferring or copying the produced material to another IRS division, including Criminal Investigation. Jose could then file and serve a motion to prohibit the proposed action. The enforcement proceeding ended with that conclusive obligation. The Ninth Circuit expressly accepted section 1291 jurisdiction but dismissed for ripeness because no transfer had been attempted and the investigation was represented to be civil. Those facts do not make the existing judicial condition hypothetical. Removing it would give the Government effectual legal relief. Church of Scientology distinguishes meaningful appellate relief from entitlement on the enforcement merits. Digital Equipment's limits on collateral appeals are preserved: this is an ordinary appeal from a completed proceeding, not a special right arising from compliance costs. Quackenbush likewise requires the actual appellate route and conclusive effect to be identified. The Court does not hold that every final order necessarily presents a ripe controversy; this particular binding restraint does.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The remand concerns the notice condition's legality and preserved related enforcement-power issues. The uncontested findings that the civil-purpose summonses are valid and should be enforced are not reopened. Privacy interests and hypothetical future criminal uses are not adjudicated by this threshold ruling. Zolin's equal division supplies no controlling answer about conditions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Church of Scientology of California v. United States (November 16, 1992): applies the distinction between effectual appellate relief and ultimate enforcement entitlement; its reservation of conditions and future-use restrictions remains.
- Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc. (June 6, 1994): preserves the cumulative collateral-order requirements and rejects a free-standing hardship exception; this order is final in the ordinary completed-proceeding sense.
- Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. (June 3, 1996): applies source-specific appellate analysis and conclusive legal effect; no additional collateral-order category is created.
- United States v. Zolin, 491 U.S. 554: its equal division over enforcement-condition authority supplies no controlling answer to the reserved merits question.

#### Law After the Decision

The existing final-judgment and live-controversy rules permit immediate review of this actual conditional enforcement judgment. The holding does not expand collateral-order jurisdiction or decide the permissible scope of IRS protective conditions.

#### Source Notes

The district-court order and [Ninth Circuit opinion, 71 F.3d 1484](https://openjurist.org/71/f3d/1484), supply the two summonses, operative notice terms, civil-purpose representation and acknowledged appellate jurisdiction. [The reported procedural recitation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-2082.ZPC.html) cross-checks that record. No attempted interdivision transfer or inevitable investigative injury is found.

The Government's appeal returns to the Ninth Circuit for the preserved merits of the notice condition. The condition is not invalidated by this threshold ruling; lawful further appellate proceedings determine its treatment.

### Humane Society v. Babbitt, D.C. Circuit No. 93-5339; district-court No. 92-cv-0952

Merits decision, December 9, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the D.C. Circuit, 46 F.3d 93. Review was granted October 7, 1996. Argued and decided December 9, 1996 under the expedited schedule. The three questions concern personal aesthetic injury from loss of access to a particular animal, causation and redressability when federal permission enables private action, and procedural standing without a separately established concrete interest. The Society challenges the August 1991 Endangered Species Act (ESA) amendment to Hawthorn's certificate and seeks suspension and a declaration against the commercial-activity interpretation. The November 1990 donation preceded that amendment; the original-transfer claim was abandoned on appeal, and the July 1991 CITES certificate was not challenged. The D.C. Circuit vacated the district court's statutory merits judgment and directed dismissal for lack of Article III jurisdiction.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The district court's statutory merits judgment remains vacated and the action is dismissed for want of Article III jurisdiction. The Court makes no ESA merits ruling.

#### Opinions and Joins

Souter delivers the opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Holdings

##### Independent failure of traceability

**Rule:** Assuming sufficient personal aesthetic injury from Lota's absence, the Society has not shown that the challenged August 1991 ESA certificate amendment caused the lost zoo access. The independent earlier donation produced that loss, and this record establishes no mechanism connecting the later amendment to its continuation.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's unanimous opinion supplies this independently sufficient jurisdictional ground.

Lujan requires support for the plaintiff's own injury and its connection to the challenged action at the litigation stage presented. A person can have a concrete aesthetic interest in observing a particular animal; species-wide depletion, ownership and a scientific profession are not prerequisites. The Court nevertheless assumes actual injury here rather than finding that the hypothetical regular observer described in the question exists in the evidence. The zoo's donation preceded the amendment, which governed later uses by Hawthorn. The Society abandoned its separate appeal concerning the original transfer and did not challenge the CITES certificate. Neither action can be restored to the case by calling this a challenge to continued absence. Government permission can be causally effective through supported, predictable private conduct; a government command is not invariably necessary. But this record supplies no such link between the later amendment and the lost Milwaukee observation. Sweet Home's treatment of actual injury to individual animals under a habitat regulation does not supply the missing human standing link for this different transportation permission.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide whether the actual members' affidavits establish injury in fact. It adopts no categorical rule against third-party causation, no purchased-ticket requirement, and no general prohibition on standing to observe one animal.

##### Independent failure of likely beneficial relief

**Rule:** The Society also fails independently to show that suspending the challenged certificate or declaring the transportation interpretation unlawful would likely benefit a member's impaired observation. Complete restoration is unnecessary, but a regulatory change must offer some likely concrete improvement to the member's interest.

**Authority:** Part II of the same unanimous opinion supplies this independently sufficient remedial standing ground.

Invalidating the amendment would leave Hawthorn able to retain Lota at its Illinois premises. It would not annul the donation, compel the zoo to receive her, or make renewed Milwaukee access substantially likely. No supported alternative benefit to an established observer appears in the affidavits or requested relief. Church of Scientology permits meaningful partial relief even when full restoration is impossible, but changing another person's legal options is not beneficial relief to a member by definition. Even with favorable reasonable inferences from the existing materials and assumed injury, the missing connection remains. The Winikoff letters were considered because the objection to their unsworn form was not preserved; considering them does not supply absent facts. The Owens letter addresses the Society's access, not its authors' personal observation. A remand to create a new return mechanism or observer would not correct an established error in this record.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No categorical conclusion is reached about remedies in another properly supported action. The Court orders no return, certificate cancellation, damages or other substantive relief.

##### Procedural and representative claims retain the concrete-interest connection

**Rule:** An asserted ESA procedure does not cure the missing connection between this challenged amendment and a represented member's concrete interest. Assuming that the asserted conservation-permit procedure exists, a claimant need not prove that proper procedure certainly changes the ultimate agency decision, but must still show the threatened personal interest that the procedure protects; representative litigation does not dispense with that constitutional requirement.

**Authority:** Part III of the same unanimous opinion rejects these proposed ways around the causal and remedial defects.

Lujan preserves procedural protection for threatened concrete interests without requiring certainty of a different final result. It does not authorize general judicial supervision based on an interest in lawful conservation administration. No individual entitlement to Lota's Milwaukee residence or separate informational right is established here. Assuming the asserted section 1539 procedure for this analysis does not convert that section's permission into a found statutory command. Brown Group preserves concrete injury, traceability and redressability for represented members; its displacement of Hunt's prudential participation limit through WARN's express authorization supplies no ESA exemption. The procedure asserted in this case does not connect the later amendment to the completed transfer or establish likely benefit to the zoo observer. The Court therefore need not construe the commercial-activity provision or the asserted permit duty.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Whether section 1539 actually imposes the claimed procedure, the commercial-activity interpretation, the unchallenged CITES certificate, the abandoned transfer claim and Hawthorn's Chaney objection remain undecided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (June 12, 1992): applies its controlling elements, stage-specific evidence and concrete-interest/procedural-right rules while preserving reasonable inferences and cognizable personal aesthetic use. Its three-Justice absent-agency discussion is not made controlling.
- Church of Scientology of California v. United States (November 16, 1992): applies the sufficiency of meaningful partial relief; distinguishes a constraint on a third party that supplies no demonstrated benefit to the claimant.
- United Food & Commercial Workers v. Brown Group (May 13, 1996): preserves represented members' constitutional standing requirements; its WARN-specific displacement of Hunt's prudential participation condition is not extended to an ESA exemption.
- Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission, 432 U.S. 333: retains the need for a member with constitutional standing.
- Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter (June 29, 1995): its actual-injury and statutory-source distinctions remain; its habitat-harm holding and separate deference rationale do not decide transportation permission or human standing here.

The Court applies constitutional causation, redressability and procedural-standing rules to this certificate challenge. Actual injury is assumed and unresolved; the discussion of observing a particular animal does not find an injured member or independently decide the suit. No ESA commercial-activity or permit-procedure construction is adopted. Jurisdictional dismissal does not decide a genuinely different claim supported by adequate standing facts and does not reopen the abandoned transfer count.

### In re Askin, Fourth Circuit No. 94-5428

Merits decision, December 9, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On granted merits review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit's February 2, 1995 affirmance of a civil-contempt judgment, reported at 47 F.3d 100. Argued October 8, 1996.

The questions are whether warrantless reception of the other participant's cordless-radio link violates the noncordless participant's Fourth Amendment rights; whether interception and use were prohibited by the Title III provisions governing the acquisition; and whether a witness may refuse criminal-trial questions directly derived from unlawful interception of his own communications, with what findings, remedial limits and just-cause consequences for civil contempt. The refusal occurred at the criminal trial after use immunity, not before a grand jury. The related criminal prosecution is not consolidated. The attorney-client and Sixth Amendment theories mentioned in the lower history are outside the granted questions.

#### Judgment

**Fourth Circuit's judgment affirming civil contempt vacated and cause remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Fourth Circuit's categorical statutory-oral and constitutional rulings cannot sustain its affirmance. The former wire/electronic exclusions and the nonretroactive completed-acquisition ruling remain binding legal conclusions. The eight-Justice implementing remand requires lawful disposition of the preserved claim through the separate oral-coverage, necessary personal-interest, prohibition, remedy and question-specific derivation inquiries. Stone-Zsela concurs in vacatur and remand on his different statutory and constitutional-remedy grounds.

The remand may lead to lawful reinstatement or setting aside of the civil-contempt adjudication. The Court orders no automatic suppression, blanket testimonial privilege, disposition of the separate criminal-contempt indictment, bar-status change, damages, new confinement, release from unestablished current confinement, new hearing or fixed return date.

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope and relationship to judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer in full | Parts I through V and the bounded implementing remand; eight-Justice controlling rationale throughout. |
| Partial join in the Court's opinion | Stone-Zsela | — | Joins II-A, III's completed-acquisition ruling, and IV's rejection of the two categorical constitutional grounds; concurs in the judgment vacating and remanding. Does not join I's reasons, II-B, III's later-act/oral-route discussion, IV's reserved broader application, or V and its distinct remand rationale. |
| Opinion concurring in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | — | Agrees with the disposition, would affirm complete former-Title-III noncoverage of the acquired segment, and proposes a more definite conditional personal-privacy rule and an independent constitutional direct-fruit just-cause rule. |

#### Holdings

##### I. The contempt adjudication remains reviewable

**Rule:** Completion of the underlying trial does not moot review when setting aside the outstanding civil-contempt adjudication can afford meaningful relief from identified collateral consequences. The possible effect on Askin's West Virginia Bar status and the already pending related criminal-contempt proceeding preserve a live controversy here without any assumption of continuing civil confinement.

**Authority:** Part I controls with the eight full-opinion joins identified above. Stone-Zsela agrees only with proceeding to the merits and supplies no additional threshold rationale.

*Church of Scientology* permits review where some meaningful relief remains, and *Calderon v. Moore* applies that principle even when relief cannot undo every past event. Ending confinement would not alone supply prospective relief, and abstract reputational concern would not suffice. But the challenged adjudication still has the concrete consequences identified in this record. Vacating it can matter to those consequences. The Court decides neither the criminal-contempt charge nor professional status and assumes no later disposition of either matter.

##### II-A. The former wire and electronic definitions exclude the acquired radio portion

**Rule:** Former 18 U.S.C. §2510(1) and (12)(A) exclude the radio portion transmitted between a cordless handset and its base from the statutory wire and electronic categories, including both speakers' words acquired at that link. The other participant's use of a landline or cellular telephone does not overcome those particular exclusions; the ruling does not classify every segment of the complete call as unprotected.

**Authority:** Part II-A controls unanimously.

The Court applies the statute to the portion the agents acquired. General language covering transmission in whole or part cannot erase the specific handset-to-base exclusions. Askin's protected endpoint therefore does not make the intercepted portion a wire or electronic communication under the former text. Former §2510(12) also separately excluded wire or oral communications, tone-only paging and tracking-device communications; those distinctions are not interchangeable. The oral category has separate terms and is addressed in Part II-B. This construction neither authorizes acquisition at another point nor decides constitutional privacy.

##### II-B. The former oral definition requires its own inquiry

**Rule:** The former exclusions from the wire and electronic categories do not categorically foreclose oral-communication coverage. Former §2510(2) requires an exhibited expectation against interception under circumstances justifying that expectation and excludes statutory electronic communication; because former §2510(12)(A) excludes this radio portion from that electronic category, the lower court must determine whether the distinct expectation requirements are met.

**Authority:** Part II-B controls with the eight full-opinion joins. Stone-Zsela does not join and would affirm complete former-Title-III noncoverage of the acquired segment.

The electronic exclusion within the oral definition uses the statute's category, not the everyday description of technologically transmitted sound. Treating the radio portion as necessarily electronic would disregard the very statutory exclusion the Government invokes. Yet exclusion from one definition does not establish either of the oral definition's expectation requirements.

The strongest objection is that oral coverage would undo Congress's two express exclusions and make their 1994 deletion unnecessary. It does neither. Conditional oral protection requires additional personal expectation findings, whereas deletion later supplies category protection without those findings. The two exclusions retain their full operation.

The lower court's assertion that Congress treated cordless calls only as wire or electronic communications supplied no independent determination under the complete oral definition and a correct personal inquiry. Askin's chosen endpoint and private interpersonal conversation are relevant, as are actual signal accessibility and established knowledge. Neither knowledge nor ignorance may be presumed. The Court vacates the categorical ruling but finds neither that Askin had nor that he lacked the required expectation, and creates no mandatory multifactor test.

##### III. The 1994 deletion does not reclassify completed acquisition

**Rule:** Public Law 103-414 §202 does not make completed preenactment interception unlawful by retroactively deleting the former category exclusions. A genuinely distinct later disclosure, use, evidentiary reception or refusal must be assessed on its own actual date and statutory predicate; pending appellate review alone establishes no such later-act violation, and preexisting oral-communication protection remains available on its own terms.

**Authority:** Part III's completed-acquisition ruling has nine joins. Its treatment of unestablished later acts and preservation of the oral route has the eight full-opinion joins; Stone-Zsela supplies no additional later-act rationale and rejects oral coverage here.

The October 25, 1994 amendment deleted the cordless exceptions but did not amend §2510(2). Title I §111(b)'s four-year delay names §§103 and 105, and §111(a) likewise concerns its own title; neither delays Title II §202. Immediate enactment, however, does not direct retrospective reclassification. The interception preceded the early January 1994 indictment. Applying new interception prohibitions to that completed acquisition would attach a new legal consequence without the necessary congressional direction. *Landgraf* and *Rivers* require attention to the actual regulated conduct; neither a corrective purpose nor a pending appeal supplies missing temporal direction. Agreement on this case-specific result does not eliminate the distinct temporal methods in those earlier opinions.

Disclosure, use, reception and refusal can be separate events. But §§2511(1)(c) and (d) ordinarily require knowledge or reason to know that the interception violated the subsection, and §2515 has its own chapter-violation condition. A later use therefore cannot simply be assumed to convert an acquisition outside coverage into an unlawful interception. No exact post-amendment use or refusal date is established; the April 6 subpoena is not that event. A preserved independently applicable later-act claim requires its actual predicate. The Court makes no such finding and orders no inquiry merely because review remained pending. The older oral category is a separate possible source of coverage, unaffected by rejection of retrospective expanded categories.

##### IV. Statutory noncoverage and another caller's endpoint do not decide personal constitutional privacy

**Rule:** A statute's failure to prohibit an interception is not affirmative constitutional permission, and the other speaker's vulnerable endpoint alone does not establish that Askin surrendered every legitimate privacy interest in his own conversation. If the constitutional question remains necessary after the statutory inquiry, the lower courts must assess his personal interest, actual exposure and any legally available justification on the record, without presuming his knowledge or ignorance.

**Authority:** Part IV's rejection of the two categorical grounds controls unanimously. The eight full-opinion joiners adopt the stated sequence and reserve a final privacy determination; Stone-Zsela's further conditional privacy rule appears only in his separate writing.

*Katz* protects private conversation without requiring trespass. *Wellons* requires examination of the claimant's own legitimate interest; another person's relation to the medium cannot replace it. *Pinson*, as applied in *Myers*, rejects a receiver-location shortcut within its technological home-information setting, but protects neither every emission nor every cordless call. *Alderman* and *Rakas* keep the personal-interest inquiry distinct from another person's rights and from remedy.

The Government's strongest argument is that both sides of this call traveled over a signal readily received with ordinary equipment. *White* recognizes the risk of disclosure through an unreliable participant. But that analogy supplies no finding that a participant cooperated with the Government or that Askin knowingly exposed his words. Actual conversational content differs from an inference drawn from exterior warmth. Conversely, selecting a landline or cellular endpoint and asserting a private conversation do not alone establish an objectively justified expectation.

The lower court treated statutory exclusion as congressional permission and applied its constitutional deference and unitary-conversation reasoning categorically. Those legal shortcuts must be corrected; no invalidation of the former statute is necessary. The Court does not finally hold that a search occurred, that the acquisition was unjustified, or that suppression follows. No warrant is established, but other exceptions require their actual legal and factual predicates rather than presumptions either way.

##### V. Statutory just cause is conditional; the constitutional remedial extension is reserved

**Rule:** Under *Gelbard*, a witness may establish just cause under §1826(a) when his own covered wire or oral communication was intercepted in violation of Title III, after consideration of applicable authorization or exceptions, and §2515 prohibits reception of the compelled testimony as intercepted contents or derived evidence. The surviving potential category here is oral, and each refused question's derivation and the witness's personal remedial entitlement must be determined; the Court leaves unresolved whether independently unconstitutional acquisition alone supplies just cause for this immunized witness's criminal-trial refusal.

**Authority:** Part V and its bounded implementing remand control with the eight full-opinion joins. Stone-Zsela agrees with Gelbard's statutory principle but rejects oral coverage here and proposes the separate constitutional just-cause rule described in his writing; he does not join Part V.

*Gelbard* supplies a statutory defense, not an automatic constitutional testimonial privilege. Section 2515 protects wire or oral communications on its own violation condition; electronic coverage alone does not obtain that remedy. Use immunity does not repeal it. The Government's admitted monitoring satisfies §3504's occurrence-response requirement without admitting illegality or derivation.

Askin refused questions about knowing McNulty, Brumbaugh and Dabbs, using cocaine, and receiving cocaine from McNulty. A subject's appearance in the calls does not establish exclusive derivation, independent source or attenuation. Each actual question requires the appropriate connection and personal entitlement; no blanket refusal follows. Naming an exception likewise does not prove it.

*Calandra* declines constitutional exclusion for grand-jury questioning and distinguishes injury from remedy; derivative questioning is not itself a new search. This case concerns a criminal trial, so its grand-jury holding supplies neither an automatic bar nor an automatic right to refuse. Direct exploitation of this speaker's words to coerce his testimony supports the proposed extension; his nonparty, immunized status and the usual personal-use limits support the objection. The potentially dispositive statutory inquiry permits reserving that constitutional remedial issue.

*Leon* and *Krull* establish no automatic good-faith result: there was no warrant, and the former coverage exclusions did not themselves direct a constitutionally valid search. Any preserved reliance claim must fit an existing exception on objective facts. Independent source, attenuation and other lawful grounds likewise require their own predicates. No subjective-bad-motive requirement or new technology exception is adopted. The remand permits lawful reinstatement or setting aside of the civil-contempt adjudication after the necessary inquiries; it commands neither final discharge nor a new hearing.

#### Precedent Treatment

- *Church of Scientology* (November 16, 1992) and *Calderon v. Moore* (June 17, 1996): applied to concrete surviving relief from the outstanding adjudication; completion of trial is not itself dispositive.
- *Landgraf* and *Rivers*: applied to the completed acquisition and the distinct possibility of genuinely later conduct; the result does not merge their separate temporal methods or retrospectively add new joins to them.
- *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347: applied to the claimant's own conversational privacy; trespass and endpoint ownership are not prerequisites.
- *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745: distinguished as to willing-participant disclosure; its recipient-risk principle does not establish cooperation or knowing public exposure on this record and remains operative in its proper setting.
- *United States v. Pinson* (May 8, 1995), as applied in *United States v. Myers* (June 11, 1996): applied against categorical inference from external reception, preserving their limited home-information holdings; no universal emitted-information rule is adopted.
- *United States v. Wellons* (May 30, 1995): applied for separate personal-interest, justification and remedy inquiries; the relation of a third person to the medium does not decide the claimant's interest.
- *Alderman v. United States*, 394 U.S. 165, and *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128: applied to personal rather than vicarious rights; neither establishes entitlement to exclusion in every proceeding.
- *Gelbard v. United States*, 408 U.S. 41: applied to the conditional statutory §2515/§1826(a) defense for the witness's own communications and derived testimony; it does not settle the independent constitutional extension.
- *United States v. Calandra*, 414 U.S. 338: its grand-jury rule remains intact; its injury/remedy distinction informs but does not decide the reserved criminal-trial constitutional issue.
- *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897, and *Illinois v. Krull*, 480 U.S. 340: remain potential objective reliance doctrines only within their actual legal and factual conditions; neither supplies an automatic defense here.

#### Law After the Decision

Effective December 9, 1996, the former cordless exceptions govern the acquired wire/electronic segment, but do not foreclose the old oral category's separately established expectation requirements. The 1994 amendment does not retroactively reclassify completed acquisition. Statutory coverage, constitutional privacy, justification, personal remedy, question-specific derivation and just cause remain separate inquiries. A new controlling rule rejects the lower court's categorical oral and personal-privacy shortcuts; no final privacy, illegality or suppression finding follows. The conditional *Gelbard* route remains available. No majority adopts a constitutional right for an immunized trial witness to refuse direct-fruit questions; Stone-Zsela's proposed extension is noncontrolling. The duty to answer lawfully compelled questions remains, subject to established just cause.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He agrees that the former wire/electronic exclusions cover the intercepted radio segment carrying both voices and that the 1994 deletion cannot retroactively make completed surveillance unlawful. His statutory position treats the place of acquisition as controlling under the former cordless exclusion. He would affirm the lower court's complete former-Title-III noncoverage ruling for that acquired segment and does not join the Court's additional oral-coverage route. Statutory noncoverage, on his view, neither authorizes interception at another point nor determines constitutional privacy.

He joins rejection of the categorical Fourth Amendment ruling and would state a further conditional rule: absent participant consent, a willing informant or knowing exposure, a landline or cellular caller does not surrender privacy merely because the other participant uses a vulnerable cordless handset without the caller's knowledge. *Katz* protects the speaker's own private words. *White* assigns the risk that an interlocutor will reveal or transmit them; it does not eliminate privacy merely because unknown agents can independently intercept a retransmission the caller did not select or know about. This rule leaves knowing exposure, participant consent and the cordless user's own expectation distinct. The record does not establish Askin's knowledge, so he would not finally apply the rule.

He accepts *Gelbard*'s statutory just-cause principle, but proposes a separate constitutional ground for this criminal-trial setting. When compelled questions are direct fruits of unlawful acquisition of the witness's own protected calls, and no recognized exception provides a lawful basis, refusal should constitute just cause under §1826(a). Coercing this speaker's testimony through his own unlawfully intercepted words gives the exclusionary rule a direct deterrent function. Question-specific derivation, personal rights and ordinary remedial exceptions keep the proposal bounded. *Pinson* and *Wellons* do not themselves establish this extension; *Calandra*'s grand-jury rule is left intact.

He would require the lower court to determine knowledge or knowing exposure, direct derivation of each question, and any preserved independent source, attenuation, objectively reasonable good-faith reliance or other lawful basis. If the governing premises establish lack of knowledge and direct derivation from unlawful acquisition without an applicable exception, the contempt adjudication must be set aside. He grants no automatic immunity from questions and no final discharge on this record. The Court's eight-Justice opinion leaves that independent constitutional just-cause question unresolved.

#### Source Notes

The [Fourth Circuit opinion, 47 F.3d 100](https://openjurist.org/47/f3d/100) supplies the endpoints, monitoring, use immunity, refused questions, civil-contempt posture and identified collateral consequences. The [1994 Code's §2510 and amendment notes](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title18/pdf/USCODE-1994-title18-partI-chap119-sec2510.pdf) identify the former category exclusions and the oral definition's electronic exclusion added in 1986. The main Code text already incorporates the later amendment and does not govern the earlier acquisition wholesale. [Public Law 103-414](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-108/pdf/STATUTE-108-Pg4279.pdf), §§111, 202 and 203, supplies the October 25, 1994 changes and their title-specific structure.

The acquisition preceded the early January 1994 indictment. The record does not establish exact interception dates, a material post-amendment use or refusal date, Askin's knowledge of the cordless endpoint, each question's derivation, the separate personal suppression-interest issue or predicates for a remedial exception. Those questions limit the judgment and remain for the lawful proceedings described above. No later criminal-contempt disposition or continuing confinement is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The cause returns to the Fourth Circuit for proceedings consistent with the separate coverage, necessary constitutional, remedy and derivation inquiries. A lawful contempt disposition depends on the findings and legal predicates described above. No final discharge, new hearing, new confinement or fixed follow-up date is commanded.

**End of entry: In re Askin, Merits decision, December 9, 1996.**

### Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis, No. 95-1263

Merits decision, December 10, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit. Argued November 12, 1996. The question is whether lack of complete diversity at removal requires vacating a federal judgment despite actual complete diversity before trial and through judgment where the plaintiff timely sought remand. Lewis sued Caterpillar and Whayne in Kentucky court; Liberty Mutual intervened. Removal occurred June 21, 1990, remand was denied September 24, 1990, and the Whayne/Liberty settlement occurred June 8, 1993. Whayne was dismissed before the November 15–22, 1993 trial. The jury returned a defense verdict. The Sixth Circuit vacated the federal judgment and directed state-court remand solely for the initial diversity defect, leaving five other appellate issues undecided.

#### Judgment

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting | Opposing | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jurisdiction-based vacatur and state-court-remand direction | Reversed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Restore the district-court judgment against this jurisdictional ground |
| Five unaddressed appellate issues | Remanded for decision, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | — | Ordinary appellate review remains; the verdict is not finally affirmed against those challenges |

#### Opinions and Joins

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Scope and relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Earlier preserved removal error and actual pretrial jurisdiction; unanimous reasoning |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | O'Connor | Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Reasons for returning the five undecided appellate issues; the Chief Justice agrees with that disposition only |

#### Holdings

##### The erroneous refusal to remand remains error

**Rule:** Complete diversity was absent when Caterpillar removed, and Lewis's timely motion required remand under the applicable removal and remand statutes. The later dismissal of the nondiverse party does not make the earlier ruling correct, waive Lewis's preserved objection, or require him to have sought extraordinary pretrial relief.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion controls unanimously on the preserved removal error.

Lewis and Whayne were Kentucky citizens, and Liberty's surviving subrogation claim kept Whayne in the action when Caterpillar removed on June 21, 1990. The district court should have granted the timely remand motion instead of denying it on September 24. Lewis's separate contention that his own settlement was partial need not be resolved because Liberty's claim already defeated complete diversity. Sections 1441 and 1447 retain their actual requirements; finality is no permission to disregard them. Things Remembered and Quackenbush preserve the mandatory-remand and review rules at their proper procedural stages. This case concerns refusal to remand followed by a later trial, not appellate review of an actually entered remand covered by section 1447(d).

**Limits and questions not reached:** No collateral challenge to an entered covered remand is authorized. No late alternative removal argument or independent fee or sanction entitlement is decided.

##### Actual jurisdiction before trial preserves the completed judgment against this earlier defect

**Rule:** When complete diversity actually arises through the nondiverse party's lawful dismissal before trial and persists through judgment, an earlier erroneous refusal of a timely remand motion does not by itself require vacating the completed federal judgment. The rule depends on actual section 1332 jurisdiction during trial and judgment; consent, finality or efficiency cannot cure a judgment entered without that jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Part II controls unanimously on this bounded extension to a preserved removal objection.

Whayne's dismissal following the June 1993 settlement left complete diversity before the November trial and through judgment. Finn required correction where the nondiverse party remained through judgment; it does not require destruction of a judgment that possessed original jurisdiction when tried. Grubbs distinguishes a completed judgment's jurisdictional foundation from earlier removal error, but involved trial without objection. The Court expressly extends that distinction to this timely preserved objection only after the actual pretrial cure. Newman-Green recognizes that party dismissal can cure statutory diversity, subject to its separate dispensability and prejudice limits. This case does not require an appellate court to dismiss a party or manufacture jurisdiction over a completed nondiverse trial. The strongest contrary argument gives Lewis's preserved forum objection a judgment-destroying remedy. Preservation makes the earlier error reviewable, but does not dictate that consequence after the obstacle ended before merits adjudication. Willy separates independently authorized collateral consequences from merits jurisdiction; it supplies no jurisdiction and does not save an uncured trial. Existing jurisdiction and the final-judgment interest, not economy alone, sustain this limited result.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No universal cure for every removal defect follows. The decision authorizes no collusive manufacture of diversity, finds no manipulation, and does not extinguish a separately authorized fee or sanction request.

##### Restoration against jurisdictional vacatur leaves the ordinary appeal for decision

**Rule:** The Sixth Circuit's jurisdiction-based vacatur and state-court-remand direction are reversed; the district-court judgment is restored against that jurisdictional ground, subject to the unresolved ordinary appeal. The case returns to the Sixth Circuit to decide the five preserved issues it did not reach: prior-accident discovery restrictions, the discovery-sanction request, excluded exhibits, excluded witnesses and the omitted failure-to-warn instruction.

**Authority:** Part III's remand explanation has the eight direct joins identified above. Stone-Zsela agrees with the remand disposition without joining Part III. All nine support reversal and the stated disposition.

The granted jurisdictional question does not decide the other appellate challenges to the defense verdict. Restoring a judgment against an erroneous jurisdictional vacatur does not make it immune from ordinary appellate correction. The Court therefore returns those distinct questions rather than finding the discovery, evidentiary, sanction or instruction objections meritless without deciding them. No fee, sanction or new trial is awarded here. The Sixth Circuit must decide the preserved issues under their governing law.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not finally affirm the defense verdict against the five remaining challenges or decide any of their merits.

#### Precedent Treatment

- American Fire & Casualty Co. v. Finn, 341 U.S. 6: distinguishes its jurisdictional defect persisting through judgment from actual complete diversity before this trial; its prohibition on jurisdiction by consent remains.
- Grubbs v. General Electric Credit Corp., 405 U.S. 699: extends its final-judgment distinction beyond its no-objection facts only to the preserved error and actual pretrial cure here; it is not described as already deciding this case.
- Newman-Green, Inc. v. Alfonzo-Larrain, 490 U.S. 826: preserves dismissal-based diversity cure and its dispensability/prejudice limits; no appellate dismissal or retrospective creation of this trial's jurisdiction is needed.
- Willy v. Coastal Corp. (March 9, 1992): applies the separation between independently authorized collateral consequences and merits jurisdiction; its holding does not itself confer jurisdiction or validate an uncured merits judgment.
- Things Remembered, Inc. v. Petrarca (December 5, 1995), and Quackenbush v. Allstate Insurance Co. (June 3, 1996): preserve the covered-remand review bar and source/stage distinctions; no bar concerning an entered covered remand is displaced.

#### Law After the Decision

The earlier statutory remand duty and Lewis's timely objection remain valid. The Court adds a bounded rule that actual diversity before trial and through judgment prevents this preserved earlier removal error alone from destroying the completed judgment. Original-jurisdiction requirements, lawful party dismissal, covered-remand review limits and ordinary appellate remedies remain. No doctrine of universal harmless removal error or unconditional verdict affirmance results.

#### Source Notes

[The Sixth Circuit opinion, 68 F.3d 474](https://openjurist.org/68/f3d/474), and [the complete reported record chronology](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1263.ZO.html) support removal, the preserved motion, pretrial party dismissal and the five remaining issues. The operative premise is actual complete diversity before trial and through judgment. No collusion or manipulation is established, and the separate partial-settlement contention at removal need not be resolved.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Sixth Circuit with the jurisdictional vacatur and state-court-remand direction reversed. The district-court judgment is restored against that ground and remains subject to resolution of the five preserved ordinary appellate claims. No disposition of those claims, new trial, fees or sanctions is ordered by this Court.

**End of entry: Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis, Merits decision, December 10, 1996.**
