### Kolster v. INS, First Circuit No. 96-1194

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the First Circuit's December 4, 1996 dismissal of a petition challenging a deportation order. Heard on the merits and decided June 23, 1997. The questions are whether AEDPA §440(a) governs the pending petition, whether withdrawal of ordinary appellate review is lawful, and what judicial consideration remains for substantial legal and constitutional objections. Kolster disputes the legal eligibility rule that prevented consideration of §212(c) relief; he does not merely seek a more favorable discretionary balance. The domicile merits are outside this review.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The direct petition ends. The Court does not decide the domicile calculation, grant a waiver or adjudicate, convert or transfer a collateral petition.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Complete transition construction; eight direct joins |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance | Completed-event analysis and retained §2241 authority; six direct joins |
| Opinion of the Court, Part IV | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous confined mandate | Entire part |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Affirms on distinct temporal grounds; reserves collateral construction | Joins Court Parts I and IV, not II–III |

Stone-Zsela agrees with Part I's disposition without joining its reasoning.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The saved proceeding remains subject to AEDPA's independent restriction

**Holding and operative rule:** Corrected IIRIRA §§306(c)(1) and 309(c)(1) preserve the former review scheme for this proceeding begun before April 1, 1997, subject to their particular exceptions; §242(g) reaches old proceedings but does not itself withdraw review of this legal objection to a completed adjudication. Section 306(c)(2) protects an actual judicial judgment or order entered under former INA §106, applying that section as amended by AEDPA §440, against an operation of §306(c)(1) that would invalidate it or require reconsideration; that protection neither supplies jurisdiction, reopens merits review nor bars ordinary appellate review of the judgment's correctness.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer: eight direct votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with this transition disposition without joining its reasoning.

**Controlling explanation:** Yang supplies the April 1 commencement rule; Reyes-Hernandez, Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro distinguish the executive decisions or actions to commence proceedings, adjudicate cases and execute orders from review of a completed legal adjudication. Section 309(c)(2) permits election of new procedures only before the evidentiary hearing has commenced and with notice at least thirty days before it. Paragraph (3) permits termination and reinitiation only before a final administrative decision. No qualifying election is established. Paragraph (4) requires a final exclusion or deportation order entered more than thirty days after September 30, 1996; the January 30, 1996 order does not meet that condition. Public Law 104-302 made §306(c)(1)'s general operation subject to §309 while retaining §242(g)'s cross-temporal reach; it left §306(c)(2) unchanged. The First Circuit's December 4, 1996 dismissal applied former §106 as amended by §440(a), so paragraph (2) protects it against invalidation or required reconsideration attributable to paragraph (1), not against this ordinary correctness review. These savings do not erase AEDPA's separate restriction merely because the old procedural scheme otherwise survives.

##### II. Section 440(a) governs this ordinary petition

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA §440(a) withdraws ordinary appellate review of Kolster's final deportation order based on the admitted drug and aggravated-felony grounds, independently supported by the conviction documents, notwithstanding his February 28, 1996 petition. On the established facts, application of the channel restriction imposes no supported new legal disability on his plea, concession or another protected completed event; this conclusion establishes neither a universal actual-reliance requirement nor a rule that pleas or eligibility for discretionary relief can never support nonretroactivity.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas support the dismissal on a different temporal ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf requires statutory temporal direction first and, absent sufficient direction, an inquiry into new consequences of completed acts. Rivers preserves that provision-specific method. Section 440(f)'s express timing clause concerns subsection (e), not a saving clause for subsection (a). Reyes-Hernandez protected an actual surrendered colorable defense; Kolster identifies none, and conviction documents independently supported deportability. Even under his preferred domicile calculation, his September 1992 plea preceded seven-year eligibility by three years. The Court does not infer his subjective motives from those facts. They establish no lost legal option of the kind asserted. Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro establish that filing and an expectation of review alone do not preserve this channel; their holdings do not make surrender of a defense the only possible protected event. Hughes Aircraft concerns creation of a previously unavailable private enforcement action, a new consequence not established here. A jurisdictional label alone supplies no answer, and §440(d)'s distinct substantive eligibility amendment remains unadjudicated.

##### III. Independent habeas jurisdiction survives for substantial legal and constitutional objections

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 440(a)'s replacement of a particular INA review channel does not clearly repeal the independent jurisdiction enacted in 28 U.S.C. §2241. When custody, proper respondent, forum, exhaustion and other applicable predicates are satisfied, that jurisdiction permits consideration of a substantial legal or constitutional claim that executive custody or deportation lacks lawful authority, including a claim that an unlawful eligibility rule prevented consideration of discretionary relief, and permits effective relief if the claim warrants it.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas do not join this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** Felker distinguishes repeal of a specified appellate mechanism from the separate statutory habeas authority that Congress leaves in place. McCardle and Yerger likewise require attention to the channel actually withdrawn; Heikkila distinguishes restricted ordinary immigration review from habeas. The replacement of former §106(a)(10) does not identify or clearly repeal §2241's separate grant. Unlike Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro, this case presents a developed legal objection: the agency refused to consider a waiver because of its construction of eligibility. Judicial reweighing of discretionary equities is different. A detention-only remedy required to assume the challenged legal rule valid would not answer this objection. Congress may channel presentation and impose valid procedural conditions, but the retained court must be able to determine the substantial authority claim actually presented and afford effective relief when warranted. This construction permits withdrawal of ordinary review without approving insulation of that claim from meaningful judicial consideration. The objection that no collateral petition is before the Court limits the disposition: the Court decides statutory capability, not whether Kolster satisfies its predicates or deserves relief. No freestanding jurisdiction arises from §1651, and no constitutional violation follows automatically from every statutory error. The Court reserves a demonstrated failure of this retained route and does not define every constitutional minimum or reopen factual and discretionary questions generally.

##### IV. The direct petition ends without a decision on eligibility

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Circuit's dismissal of the direct petition is affirmed. The Court neither decides the lawful-domicile calculation nor orders a waiver, agency reconsideration, conversion or transfer of the petition, collateral relief, a stay or execution of deportation.

**Authority:** Part IV of Souter's opinion is joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The absence of direct-review jurisdiction prevents adjudication of the eligibility merits through this petition. Recognition of a conditional collateral source does not establish custody, a proper respondent or forum, exhaustion, or an actual collateral filing. Hincapie-Nieto and Salazar-Haro preserve this channel-specific remedy. The sentencing judge's recommendation does not bind the immigration decisionmaker. Any properly brought collateral challenge must establish its own predicates before its legal claim and requested relief can be decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Yang supplies the commencement rule and separates legal eligibility from discretion; no agency-merits decision follows here.
- Reyes-Hernandez supplies the limited §242(g) construction and is distinguished on the actual lost-defense premise; other completed-event predicates remain possible.
- Hincapie-Nieto applies to filing and expectations alone; its reservation concerning an unpresented legal defect is now addressed at the conditional statutory scope stated here.
- Salazar-Haro supplies the full transition protection and is distinguished because this record presents a developed legal eligibility objection.
- Landgraf supplies the controlling temporal sequence; Rivers preserves its provision-specific operation. Neither is replaced by the concurrence's relevant-activity approach.
- Hughes Aircraft is distinguished because new private enforcement exposure differs from the channel change established here.
- Felker's separation of withdrawn and retained statutory channels is extended to the independently enacted immigration habeas authority at issue; its meaningful-review principle does not make a nominal substitute sufficient.
- McCardle and Yerger support channel-specific repeal analysis; Heikkila supplies the distinction between ordinary immigration review and habeas.
- Flores retains its custody-specific safeguards and does not itself decide review of a deportation order's legal basis.

#### Law After Decision

Section 440(a) withdraws this ordinary petition, while §2241 remains an independently enacted, conditional route for substantial legal and constitutional challenges to executive custody or deportation authority. The legal eligibility question may be considered through that route only if its actual jurisdictional and procedural predicates are met. Neither the substantive seven-year rule nor §440(d)'s separate temporal operation is decided. Section 306(c)(2) protects qualifying judicial judgments and orders under former §106 as amended by AEDPA §440 only against invalidation or required reconsideration caused by §306(c)(1); it creates no jurisdiction and bars no ordinary correctness review. No precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, concurs in part and in the judgment. The relevant statutory activity is the court's present exercise of ordinary review, so the new restriction governs that activity. The concurrence joins the transition construction and confined mandate but does not adopt the Court's general completed-event analysis or its construction of an unfiled collateral route. The three Justices recognize the difference between a legal eligibility objection and discretionary reweighing; they reserve the source, scope and predicates of collateral review and do not pronounce an unidentified remedy adequate or approve elimination of every meaningful forum.

#### Source Notes

The First Circuit's opinion, 101 F.3d 785, supplies the record and lower judgment. AEDPA §440, IIRIRA §§306 and 309 as corrected by Public Law 104-302, and 28 U.S.C. §2241 supply the statutory provisions. No custody finding, filed habeas petition or adjudication of the domicile merits is supplied by this record.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The First Circuit's dismissal stands. A properly brought collateral challenge must establish its own custody, respondent, forum, exhaustion and other applicable requirements. The mandate orders no agency reconsideration, waiver, transfer, stay, release or execution of deportation.

**End of entry: Kolster v. INS, merits decision, June 23, 1997.**

### Lindh v. Murphy, No. 96-6298

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 14, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, which applied amended chapter 153 to Lindh's pending habeas application and denied relief. The question is whether amended §2254(d) and the related chapter 153 provisions govern a noncapital application filed before AEDPA's April 24, 1996 enactment. Lindh filed July 9, 1992; his appeal was pending when the Act became law. The underlying Confrontation Clause claim is not decided.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Seventh Circuit must adjudicate Lindh's appeal under the habeas law governing the application before AEDPA. No writ or other merits relief is awarded.

#### Opinions

Ginsburg delivered the opinion of the Court. Scalia filed a dissent, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, that would affirm the temporal determination without independently adjudicating the Confrontation Clause claim.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The chapter 153 amendments at issue do not govern this pre-enactment noncapital application

**Holding and operative rule:** AEDPA's chapter 153 amendments, including amended §2254(d), do not govern Lindh's noncapital federal habeas application filed before April 24, 1996. The application-date construction preserves separately directed provisions, chapter 154's express pending-case rule and the successive-petition provisions governed by Felker; it does not exempt every pending proceeding from every AEDPA provision.

**Authority:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Breyer. Six Justices directly adopt the statutory rule, its limits and reversal with remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf makes Congress's temporal choice the first inquiry. Ordinary statutory construction may establish that choice without a prescribed formula. Congress enacted the neighboring ordinary-habeas and capital-habeas chapters together and expressly directed chapter 154 to pending cases in §107(c), while omitting that direction for chapter 153. That deliberate contrast supplies the application-date inference here. The contrary reading stresses the capital chapter's distinct State-qualification system. Those differences do not deprive Congress's adjacent and express treatment of pending applications of significance. Applying the same pending-case reach to chapter 153 would erase that contrast. The changed standards governing an already filed request for relief reinforce the need to honor the enacted distinction; the Court need not classify every amendment as substantive or procedural to decide the case. Felker applied a particular authorization-review restriction to proceedings begun after enactment and expressly reserved other Title I temporal questions. It neither fixes chapter 153's present reach nor displaces the structural inquiry. Lindh filed on July 9, 1992, almost four years before enactment. His appeal must therefore be considered under the habeas law governing that application before AEDPA. The Court expresses no view on the psychiatrist cross-examination claim, bias, harmlessness, exhaustion, chapter 154 State qualification or ultimate entitlement to a writ. No constitutional right to permanent retention of every filing-date judicial rule is announced.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Landgraf supplies the prior inquiry into statutory temporal direction; ordinary construction, including the enacted chapter contrast, resolves that inquiry here. Its default completed-event framework remains intact.
- Rivers preserves the provision-specific method; the decision establishes no timing rule for every AEDPA amendment.
- Felker is distinguished because it applied its own authorization-review provisions to proceedings begun after enactment and reserved other Title I questions. Its successive-petition and retained-original-authority holdings remain in force.
- Hughes Aircraft preserves examination of the actual statutory change and does not replace the structural inquiry or the distinct temporal methods previously expressed.

#### Law After Decision

Pre-enactment noncapital applications such as Lindh's remain governed by pre-AEDPA chapter 153 law. Applications filed after enactment, chapter 154 cases within its express clause, successive-petition provisions governed by Felker and provisions carrying their own temporal commands retain their distinct treatment. The Court overrules no precedent and decides no underlying habeas merits.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, dissents. Chapter 154 addresses a distinct capital scheme dependent on State qualification, so its express pending-case clause does not reliably prescribe the contrary operation for chapter 153. Amended §2254(d) regulates the court's present authority to grant relief; the dissent would apply it to this still-pending adjudication. Hughes Aircraft's expansion of private enforcement exposure differs from this restriction on current judicial relief. The dissent would affirm at the temporal scope reviewed, without deciding the confrontation claim anew or making every AEDPA provision immediately applicable.

#### Source Notes

The application date and procedural history are stated in Lindh v. Murphy, 96 F.3d 856. AEDPA §§101–107, particularly §107(c), supply the enacted temporal comparison. This decision does not require findings about State qualification for chapter 154 or the merits of the cross-examination claim.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Seventh Circuit for adjudication under pre-AEDPA standards. The Court expresses no view on the Confrontation Clause claim or ultimate entitlement to habeas relief.

**End of entry: Lindh v. Murphy, merits decision, June 23, 1997.**

### Metro-North Commuter Railroad Co. v. Buckley, No. 96-320

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit. At the close of Buckley's evidence the District Court entered judgment under Rule 50; the Second Circuit vacated and remanded on April 1, 1996, permitting emotional-distress and medical-monitoring claims. Buckley had extensive negligent asbestos exposure but no manifested asbestos disease. The questions are whether that contact satisfies Gottshall's physical-impact category and whether FELA permits the monitoring-cost recovery recognized below. Negligence was conceded; compensable injury, causation and damages were not.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Present emotional-distress claim | Reversed, 9–0 in judgment | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |  | Present distress theory cannot proceed; later distinct injury is not decided |
| Independent monitoring recovery recognized below | Reversed, 6–3 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg | No entitlement to the unqualified damages approved below; different properly supported arrangements remain open |

The case is remanded consistently with these holdings. No damages award, monitoring fund, schedule or automatic trial is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Reverses both components | Impact boundary and bounded monitoring rejection in full; six votes |
| Concurrence in the judgment in part and dissent in part, Part I | Ginsburg | Stevens | Supports distress reversal on different grounds | Impact accepted; severe-injury proof insufficient |
| Same separate writing, Part II | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens | Opposes monitoring reversal | Medically necessary incremental-cost claim and bounded further proceedings |
| Concurrence in the distress judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports distress reversal; also joins Ginsburg's monitoring dissent | Assumes impact without deciding it and finds distress proof insufficient |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Symptomless asbestos exposure does not satisfy the physical-danger boundary for this distress claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Physical contact with asbestos that has caused no manifested disease and presents only a risk of later disease does not qualify as the physical impact required for this FELA negligent-emotional-injury claim. An increased risk of disease in the future likewise does not alone establish Gottshall's alternative requirement of immediate risk of physical harm; the present emotional-distress claim cannot proceed.

**Authority:** Part I of Thomas's Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: six direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg support reversal of the distress determination on evidence-sufficiency grounds, without joining this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** Gottshall requires the employee to cross an independent physical-danger boundary and to establish negligence, cognizable injury and causation. Its impact category is a common-law boundary, not a rule making every particle contact a compensable impact. The extensive contact and medically supported future risk here do not supply an immediately harmful impact or the alternative immediate danger. The contrary reading correctly observes that contact occurred, but would turn the reserved future-disease category into an unrestricted route around the boundary merely by describing toxic exposure as literal touching. Urie preserves compensation for occupational disease; it does not equate the prospect of disease with present disease. Buell leaves the precise emotional-injury question to development under FELA, while Monessen and Ellis require attention to common-law limits and reject insurer liability. The Court does not import the threatened-impact branch's fear-for-self qualification into the actual-impact branch. Nor does it require psychiatric treatment or expert mental-health testimony in every case, decide every physical-manifestation question, or foreclose a later distinct manifested disease or otherwise qualifying injury. Conceded negligence and relaxed FELA causation do not eliminate the separate injury boundary. This legal ground resolves the present claim without deciding whether Buckley's distress evidence independently satisfies a universal severity test.

##### II. FELA does not authorize the independent, unqualified monitoring damages recognized below

**Holding and operative rule:** Negligent asbestos exposure, increased future disease risk and the monitoring recommendations in this asymptomatic record do not establish an entitlement under FELA to the independent, unqualified lump-sum medical-monitoring recovery recognized by the Second Circuit. Such damages cannot be attached to the unsuccessful emotional-distress claim; the Court leaves more carefully limited programs, reimbursement of actual necessary costs, later illness and materially different records undecided.

**Authority:** Part II of Thomas's Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer: six direct votes. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg dissent from reversal of the monitoring determination.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 51 requires injury resulting from railroad negligence. Gottshall keeps injury, negligence and causation separate; useful precautions against a future risk do not automatically establish the statutory damages category asserted here. Common-law guidance under Monessen does not freeze negligence law at its enactment, but the sources do not establish the unrestricted independent recovery approved below. Buckley argues that additional surveillance is a present expense rather than compensation for a disease that may never develop. That distinction has force as a description of the proposed spending, but it does not itself resolve the statutory injury question or the problems of speculative and duplicative lump-sum recovery. Nor can monitoring damages be recovered as an element of a distress claim that fails. Urie's coverage of actual occupational disease and Rogers's causal-contribution rule supply no missing injury predicate. The Court does not find that Buckley already receives equivalent benefits or that no properly supported alternative arrangement could ever be considered. It decides the damages entitlement sustained by the appellate judgment, not every possible monitoring remedy, and orders no particular fund, program, test schedule or automatic trial on a different claim.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Gottshall is applied to its reserved toxic-contact category: literal symptomless exposure with only later disease risk is not the qualifying impact. Its independent negligence, injury and causation requirements, and threatened-impact fear-for-self limit, remain distinct.
- Buell's reserved emotional-injury inquiry is developed at this bounded scope; its treatment of independently cognizable FELA claims remains intact.
- Urie retains occupational-disease coverage; prospective disease risk is distinguished from manifested occupational disease.
- Monessen supplies common-law guidance without making every tort rule immutable.
- Ellis preserves negligence-based liability rather than workplace insurance.
- Rogers preserves FELA's causal-contribution rule without replacing the separate compensable-injury requirement.

#### Law After Decision

FELA does not permit this symptomless-exposure distress claim or the independent, unqualified lump-sum monitoring recovery approved below. The Court's six-Justice rationale controls; the three evidence-sufficiency positions do not supply an additional majority holding. Later distinct illness, otherwise qualifying distress and properly supported different monitoring arrangements remain open. No rule requires professional psychiatric treatment in every case or declares every medical-monitoring remedy unavailable.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Ginsburg, joined by Stevens on distress, accepts Buckley's extensive bodily contact as physical impact. She nevertheless would reverse because the evidence, viewed favorably under Rule 50, does not establish sufficiently severe emotional injury. Anger, fear, medical risk and corroborating worker complaints are real evidence; they do not on this showing establish that injury. The lack of treatment or professional mental-health evidence bears on this record without becoming a universal prerequisite.

On monitoring, Ginsburg is joined by Stevens and Stone-Zsela. Medically necessary additional surveillance can be a present economic loss distinct from fear or future disease. They would preserve further proceedings requiring negligent exposure, materially increased serious-disease risk, competent medical proof that reasonable care calls for monitoring different from ordinary care, causation and actual incremental costs. Relief must correspond to monitoring actually necessary and available, avoiding speculative or duplicative recovery and considering comparable services if proved. They fix no amount, schedule, fund or offset and would not deny a supported tailored remedy merely because the complaint requested money.

Stone-Zsela separately concurs in the distress judgment. He assumes the disputed impact question favorably without deciding it, but finds the evidence insufficient to establish severe emotional injury even with its corroboration and lawful favorable inferences. Seriousness cannot replace Gottshall's independent physical-danger gate. He would dismiss the present distress theory without prejudice to a later distinct injury and joins Ginsburg's monitoring dissent.

#### Source Notes

The record and Rule 50 posture are described in the Second Circuit's opinion, 79 F.3d 1337. FELA, 45 U.S.C. §51, supplies the injury and negligence requirements. The record does not establish a particular monitoring schedule, cost, offset, existing benefits package or damages award.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Second Circuit must implement the reversal of the present distress theory and the monitoring-damages entitlement it recognized. Remand does not itself require adjudication of an unpleaded or unsupported alternative arrangement, guarantee a trial or prevent consideration of a different legally supported claim through its proper procedure.

**End of entry: Metro-North Commuter Railroad Co. v. Buckley, merits decision, June 23, 1997.**

### Plyler v. Moore, Fourth Circuit No. 96-6884

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Heard on the merits and decided June 23, 1997. On review of the Fourth Circuit's November 14, 1996 judgment sustaining termination of a federal prison-conditions consent decree entered in March 1986. The State moved under original PLRA §3626(b)(2). The questions concern Article III, the continuing rather than completed operation of the decree, whether its additional agreed protections are independent federal rights that prevent termination, and due process. The appeal presents those legal objections without a supported preserved claim that qualifying findings or a current or ongoing violation were disregarded.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The original §3626(b)(3) safeguard remains part of the governing rule. The Court orders no new evidentiary hearing and makes no finding that every prison condition presently complies with federal law.

#### Opinions

Kennedy delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for all holdings:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt the following rules and qualifications.

##### I. The original PLRA supplies an independent, conditional termination rule

**Holding and operative rule:** For covered federal prison-conditions prospective relief, including relief entered before April 26, 1996, original §3626(b)(2) entitles a defendant or intervener to immediate termination when the relief was approved without findings that it was narrowly drawn, extended no further than necessary to correct the federal-right violation and was the least intrusive necessary means. Under original §3626(b)(3), relief must nevertheless continue upon written, record-based findings that it remains necessary to correct a current **or** ongoing federal violation, is no broader than necessary, and is narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means; existing substantive findings may satisfy the statute without ritual wording.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 802 expressly reaches existing prospective relief. Lewis requires examination of the actual findings and the original preservation clause; enactment alone dissolves nothing. This motion uses (b)(2), not (b)(1)'s waiting-period route or (e)'s automatic-stay mechanism. The State must establish the statutory basis for termination, and the court must independently apply (b)(3). Rufo recognizes continuing authority to modify a decree upon a significant change in fact or law, but its equitable threshold is not an additional prerequisite to this independent statutory route. Nor does relief's exceeding a constitutional minimum alone establish that it is unnecessary: a remedy may require measures beyond the minimum conduct rule to correct a proved violation. The decree's age or lack of a repeated statutory phrase likewise supplies no automatic answer. The actual appeal presents legal objections, without an established preserved failure to consider qualifying findings or a supported current or ongoing violation requiring a new hearing. The Court affirms at that scope, making no statewide compliance finding and adjudicating no accrued monetary liability.

##### II. Conditional termination changes governing law without displacing Article III adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 3626(b)(2), applied with the full original (b)(3) safeguard, does not violate Article III by changing the legal authority for continued prospective enforcement of this decree. Courts retain responsibility to decide the federal violation, the actual findings and the necessity and scope of continued relief; Congress does not command false facts or a particular result under unchanged law.

**Controlling explanation:** Robertson permits a genuine change in governing law while preserving judicial application. The statute changes which prospective remedies federal courts may continue to enforce and makes the outcome depend on conditions courts must determine. The command to terminate when those conditions are met is a consequence of the changed rule, not a legislative finding that South Carolina's prisons comply. The decree expressly retained supervision and modification, and the State previously invoked that process. Rufo and the prospective-relief setting of Wheeling Bridge explain why future operation remains subject to lawful changes. The prisoners' final-judgment objection does not transform this continuing decree into an accrued damages judgment. Morgan Stanley and Plaut sustain a distinct, bounded statutory reinstatement procedure for completed private limitations dismissals; that procedure is neither the source of authority here nor a general license to reopen adjudicated merits. A statute eliminating independent determination of the actual predicates would present a different question. The Court decides no cancellation of accrued damages and no general power to contradict completed judicial findings.

##### III. Negotiated decree protections are not automatically independent federal rights

**Holding and operative rule:** Incorporating agreed protections into a federal consent decree does not make every protection exceeding underlying federal law the independent federal right whose violation prevents termination under §3626(b)(2). Continuing prospective enforcement remains subject to the statute, while relief necessary to correct an actual federal violation must be preserved under the full original (b)(3) conditions.

**Controlling explanation:** Rufo recognizes that lawful consent obligations may exceed constitutional minima and remain enforceable under the court's authority; it does not give each obligation immunity from a later valid statute governing that authority. Treating the decree itself as the federal-right violation sufficient to preserve every term would nullify the termination rule for consent decrees. The distinction concerns the source of the protected right, not whether every remedial detail must itself duplicate a constitutional command. Lewis permits measures needed to correct the demonstrated wrong, within the statute's findings and tailoring requirements. Joseph A. enforces an agreed performance-based termination condition where no prison-conditions predicate for the PLRA existed; its distinction between performance and modification does not displace this enacted route. The Court does not adjudicate a separate contract, state-law remedy or independently accrued right, and does not authorize termination of relief that satisfies (b)(3).

##### IV. The presented due-process claim does not bar termination

**Holding and operative rule:** Beneficiaries of this mutable prospective decree have no vested entitlement to perpetual federal enforcement of its unchanged terms despite valid later law. On the presented legal challenge, the judicial motion process, opportunity to oppose termination and appellate review satisfy due process; no additional evidentiary remand follows from an unestablished possibility that a statutory preservation condition was overlooked.

**Controlling explanation:** The asserted protected interest must be identified at its actual scope. The parties obtained an enforceable decree, but its continuing operation remained subject to supervision, lawful modification and the subsequently enacted termination rule. That does not promise unchanging prospective relief. The prisoners could contest the statutory predicates and constitutional authority through the judicial proceedings now reviewed. The Court presumes no unrecorded hearing and decides no actual denial of an opportunity to present a preserved factual claim. Lewis's protection of meaningful access and of relief justified by the original current-or-ongoing safeguard remains operative. A demonstrated failure to apply that safeguard would be a different case; mere invocation of state sovereignty would not excuse it. Separately accrued or contractual rights, general equal-protection questions, other PLRA provisions and the legality of any particular present prison condition remain outside this decision.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Lewis supplies the complete original PLRA conditions and the rule that substantive findings need no ritual wording. Its reserved Article III question is resolved here only for this conditional prospective-termination operation; its meaningful-access protections remain.
- Rufo remains controlling for equitable modification based on significant changed circumstances and suitable tailoring. The PLRA provides an independent route, without an extra Rufo threshold.
- Robertson supplies the distinction between changed law and compelled adjudication under unchanged law; actual statutory predicates remain for courts.
- Wheeling Bridge supports the distinction between continuing prospective relief and completed adjudication.
- Morgan Stanley retains its bounded reinstatement rule for specified completed private limitations dismissals; that separate procedure does not supply this prospective-termination authority.
- Plaut retains its application of Morgan Stanley, without authorizing unrestricted reopening or establishing immunity of this prospective decree.
- Joseph A. is distinguished because it concerned proof of an agreed performance condition without an established PLRA prison-conditions predicate.
- Landgraf's distinction between prospective operation and new consequences of completed acts remains; §802 expressly reaches existing prospective relief.

#### Law After Decision

Original §3626(b)(2), with original (b)(3), validly governs this ongoing prison-conditions decree. A court must determine the actual findings and preserve necessary, narrowly drawn, no-broader-than-necessary and least-intrusive relief for a current or ongoing federal violation; neither a consent decree's age nor missing ritual language suffices. The decree does not create a perpetual right to unchanged federal enforcement. This decision does not reach automatic stays, preliminary-order expiration, prisoner-release orders, accrued damages, separate state or contractual rights, or the constitutionality of all PLRA provisions. No precedent is overruled.

#### Source Notes

The decree and proceedings are described in the Fourth Circuit's opinion, 100 F.3d 365. The governing text is the original April 26, 1996 PLRA, including §3626(b)(3)'s current **or** ongoing formulation. The record of this legal appeal establishes no new statewide compliance finding or preserved omitted-condition error.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit's judgment sustaining termination remains in force at its prospective federal-enforcement scope. No new compliance finding or evidentiary remand is ordered. Independently established federal rights and any separately available state or contractual remedies are not extinguished by this judgment.

### Princeton University Press v. Michigan Document Services, Sixth Circuit No. 94-1778

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Heard on the merits and decided June 23, 1997. On review of the en banc November 8, 1996 judgment affirming infringement, rejecting willfulness, vacating damages and remanding damages and injunctive implementation. Michigan Document Services, operated by James Smith, sold the coursepacks. The questions concern fair use of six scholarly excerpts sold in coursepacks, commercial and educational purpose, harm to the actual excerpt-permissions market, and willfulness when copying rests on a good-faith fair-use belief. The original $5,000-per-work damages award was vacated below.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fair use and infringement for the six reproductions | Affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |  | Infringement determination stands |
| Enhanced willfulness and statutory damages | Rejection of willfulness affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |  | Existing damages vacatur remains; ordinary statutory damages to be determined without enhancement premise |
| Prospective injunction | Remand direction affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer |  | Separate, sufficiently specific injunction must preserve lawful fair use |

#### Opinions

Scalia delivered the unanimous opinion of the Court.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for all holdings:** Scalia's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine directly adopt each rule, application and remedial qualification below.

##### I. These six coursepack reproductions do not establish fair use

**Holding and operative rule:** A commercial supplier's reproduction and sale of these substantial, unaltered excerpts for assigned reading is not fair use under §107 when all four factors are considered together: genuine educational purpose and published scholarly content do not outweigh the actual quantity and role of the copying and its substitution in the demonstrated excerpt-permissions market. Neither commerciality, lack of transformation, educational purpose, a fixed copying percentage nor a demand for license fees alone decides fair use.

**Controlling explanation:** Campbell requires one integrated inquiry and preserves fair use as an affirmative defense. On purpose and character, the professors' pedagogical selection and students' study matter, but the shop sells the same expression to perform the originals' assigned-reading function. The supplier's reproduction is not identical to a student's personal study. Transformation is not indispensable; its absence here identifies the unchanged substitutive function rather than an automatic disqualification. On nature, published factual scholarship favors educational use relative to unpublished creative expression, but facts and their protected expression remain different. On amount, the six passages range from 17 to 95 pages and 5% to 30% of the works. Their quantity and qualitative role must be evaluated against the asserted teaching purpose; no percentage alone establishes liability. Taken as assigned-reading substitutes, these substantial passages weigh against the defense. On market effect, the record identifies an existing, ordinarily exploitable coursepack-permissions market for the same excerpts. Comparable unlicensed substitution would impair that market; the Court does not assume that students would otherwise buy every entire book. The shop's strongest objection is circularity: any proprietor could demand a fee and call its absence market harm. Campbell forecloses that reasoning for protected criticism and otherwise fair use. Here the independent substitute function and demonstrated licensing practice establish the connection, rather than the bare demand. Sony's home-use protection and Harper & Row's attention to legitimate markets remain contextual rules. Nonstatutory classroom-copying guidelines supply no maximum allowance or independent infringement rule. Considering all four factors on this record, the educational advantages do not establish the defense for any of the six challenged reproductions. No general rule decides individual student, nonprofit, reserve-room or digital copying.

##### II. Intentional copying does not establish willful infringement by itself

**Holding and operative rule:** Enhanced statutory damages under §504(c)(2) require knowing or reckless infringement, not simply intentional copying that ultimately fails a fair-use defense. On this record, the publishers' noncontest of Smith's actual good faith and the reasonably arguable fair-use position defeat the enhanced willfulness determination; ordinary statutory damages remain for determination without that erroneous premise.

**Controlling explanation:** Liability and culpability answer different questions. Smith deliberately copied and sold the excerpts, but intentional acts do not themselves establish knowledge or reckless disregard of infringement where a genuine, legally tenable defense is asserted. The publishers contested the reasonableness of his view rather than his actual good faith. The substantial dispute over the legal treatment of educational copying makes enhancement unwarranted here even though the Court rejects the defense. Campbell's integrated method does not collapse a losing defense into willfulness. Conversely, merely announcing sincerity cannot insulate deliberate disregard or objectively reckless infringement. Rejection of enhancement also does not establish the separate statutory conditions for innocent-infringer treatment. The en banc court vacated the original $5,000-per-work award; the Court preserves its remand rather than affirming a damages amount no longer in force. The lower court must determine ordinary statutory damages and any properly supported distinct innocent-infringer question without assuming either enhanced or reduced damages from the fair-use result alone.

##### III. The injunction must identify infringement without extinguishing lawful fair use

**Holding and operative rule:** On remand a separately entered, sufficiently specific injunction must describe the prohibited infringing conduct and leave lawful future fair use available. The decision neither immunizes later coursepacks nor categorically forbids coverage of later copyrighted works; it supplies no replacement injunction language.

**Controlling explanation:** The present infringement judgment supports prospective relief against infringement, but the scope of relief must remain distinguishable from the scope of lawful copying. A command read to prohibit every future use of protected material would exceed the liability rule, because §107 remains applicable to future uses on their actual facts. The en banc court's separate-entry and specificity directions must therefore be implemented along with the damages remand. The question whether a sufficiently specific injunction may cover later works is distinct from whether it can bar fair use; the Court adopts no categorical restriction on the former. The Court decides no future transaction or materially different copying arrangement.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Campbell supplies integrated consideration of all four §107 factors, the affirmative-defense burden, the absence of an indispensable transformation condition, and the distinction between actual substitution and a veto over criticism. The same method applies to this different assigned-reading use.
- Sony retains its home-time-shifting result and contextual inquiry; neither its commerciality language nor students' end use automatically decides the copy shop's reproduction.
- Harper & Row preserves attention to protected expression, qualitative substantiality and legitimate markets, without creating a categorical commercial-use rule.
- ProCD preserves the difference between an actual personal promise and copyright rights against the public. No promise by this shop is established, and that decision's fair-use reservation is not treated as permission or prohibition here.

#### Law After Decision

These six commercial assigned-reading reproductions infringe after the full four-factor analysis. Established substitute licensing may demonstrate cognizable harm, but a demanded fee cannot establish its own legal entitlement. Education remains relevant; profit, percentages and lack of transformation remain nondispositive. Willfulness requires its separate knowing-or-reckless showing, and ordinary statutory damages and a fair-use-respecting injunction remain for implementation. No precedent is overruled and no universal coursepack rule is announced.

#### Source Notes

The en banc opinion, 99 F.3d 1381, supplies the excerpt ranges, existing permissions market, uncontested actual good faith and operative damages/injunction mandate. Sections 107 and 504(c)(2) supply the distinct fair-use and enhanced-damages inquiries. The review concerns the stated fair-use and willfulness questions and the operative damages and injunction mandate.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower courts must determine statutory damages without the rejected enhancement premise and enter a separate, sufficiently specific injunction preserving lawful fair use. The Court fixes no amount or replacement language and adjudicates no future copying transaction.

**End of entry: Princeton University Press v. Michigan Document Services, merits decision, June 23, 1997.**

### Richardson v. McKnight, No. 96-318

Merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 19, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, which affirmed denial of qualified immunity on June 7, 1996. The question is whether guards employed by a private, for-profit prison contractor may invoke the qualified immunity available to comparable public officers. McKnight alleges injury from excessively tight restraints. The appeal presents legal eligibility; state action is assumed for this appeal and the alleged constitutional violation remains undecided.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

Denial of immunity permits ordinary further proceedings. It establishes no liability, damages or automatic trial entitlement.

#### Opinions

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court. Kennedy filed a dissent, joined by Scalia and Thomas, that would reverse categorical ineligibility and remand for an individual custodial-function immunity inquiry, reserving ultimate immunity.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for both holdings:** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six Justices directly adopt the eligibility rule, its limits and the bounded affirmance/remand.

##### I. These commercial prison employees are not eligible for Harlow immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Guards employed full time by a private, for-profit firm that independently undertakes substantial continuing prison management under limited direct governmental supervision and potential market competition are not entitled to Harlow qualified immunity merely because they exercise delegated governmental custody. Neither the historical showing nor the functional justification establishes that immunity in this institutional setting; brief private assistance, adjunct service, close official supervision and integration into government remain open.

**Controlling explanation:** Wyatt separates attribution of conduct to the State from eligibility for an immunity. Performing a public task therefore begins rather than ends the inquiry. No firmly established general historical entitlement to the modern immunity from suit is demonstrated for these employees; the Court need not declare every historical private jailer liable or equate every prior defense with Harlow immunity. The functional setting also differs from public service. Potential competition, contractual accountability, insurance and private employment arrangements supply mechanisms for managing performance, staffing and liability that bear on the asserted need for immunity. Those considerations do not prove perfect competition, actual coverage of these guards or complete freedom from litigation costs. The guards' strongest argument is that an individual making a coercive custodial decision may hesitate under threat of damages regardless of the employer's identity. The Court recognizes the shared task but concludes that its independent commercial organization materially changes the claimed necessity for the additional immunity from suit. Wyatt's debt-collection setting is not identical; its demand for a separate historical and functional justification applies, while the actual operational distinctions supply the present answer. Fletcher likewise requires justification for the particular function rather than immunity by title. Harlow remains applicable to eligible public officials; the Court neither abolishes that defense nor adopts a replacement notice or conduct test. Eligibility must exist before the content of qualified immunity can be applied. This holding concerns these employees and this setting, not every private person whose act may be attributed to government.

##### II. Denial of immunity does not establish a constitutional violation or personal liability

**Holding and operative rule:** The Sixth Circuit's denial of qualified immunity is affirmed, and the case is remanded for ordinary further proceedings on any properly unresolved state-action issue, the constitutional merits, causation and damages. The availability, elements and burdens of a distinct private good-faith defense remain undecided; no corporate-immunity or corporate-liability determination follows.

**Controlling explanation:** The appeal presents a separable legal immunity question on the assumed facts. Behrens and Johnson preserve that legal inquiry without allowing appellate selection of disputed facts. McKnight's allegation that the restraints were excessively tight is not a finding that the Constitution was violated. State action is assumed only for this appeal, not conclusively established for every further purpose. Wyatt distinguishes immunity from suit from a potential defense on the merits and leaves the latter to its own source and requirements. Fletcher and Crawford-El preserve the need to establish the actual constitutional elements and independent defenses after a ruling removes one immunity obstacle. The Court therefore neither orders an automatic trial nor awards damages, decides insurance or indemnification, or assigns a new burden for an unadjudicated good-faith defense. The claims may continue under the ordinary rules consistent with the absence of Harlow immunity for these guards.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Wyatt supplies the separation of state attribution, Harlow immunity and a possible independent good-faith defense; its historical/functional inquiry is applied to this different commercial custodial setting.
- Harlow retains its protection for eligible officials; it is not extended to these employees and no replacement immunity test is adopted.
- Fletcher supplies function-specific justification and separation of immunity from ultimate liability; its qualified-immunity reservation for an eligible prosecutor does not establish universal private-contractor eligibility.
- Behrens preserves legal collateral-order review on proper factual assumptions, without deciding factual truth or merits sufficiency.
- Johnson v. Jones preserves the boundary against factual-sufficiency appeals; this decision resolves a legal eligibility issue.
- Elder preserves independent consideration of relevant law at the proper conduct-date scope, without supplying eligibility or retrospective notice.
- Crawford-El preserves ordinary proof of actual constitutional elements and independent defenses; immunity concerns create no additional evidentiary burden here.

#### Law After Decision

These full-time commercial prison employees lack Harlow immunity in the independent operating setting presented. Their public task alone does not establish immunity, and loss of that defense alone establishes no constitutional wrong. Different private-assistance or close-supervision arrangements, state-action predicates, a distinct private good-faith defense and corporate questions remain unresolved. No precedent is overruled, and the qualified-immunity framework for eligible officials remains unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents. The guards perform delegated coercive custody, and an individual making that public decision may hesitate under threat of personal liability regardless of the employer's corporate form. Wyatt's self-interested private use of seizure machinery is different. Competition may discipline the employer without protecting the guard's discretionary judgment; profit and assumed insurance coverage therefore do not justify categorical ineligibility. The dissent would recognize eligibility and remand for the ordinary particularized immunity inquiry. It would not award immunity, approve excessive force, extend protection to every private state actor or decide the corporation's position.

#### Source Notes

The Sixth Circuit's decision is McKnight v. Rees, 88 F.3d 417. Section 1983 and the historical and functional immunity precedents supply the legal inquiry. The assumed state-action posture, alleged restraint injury and general contractor setting establish no specific insurance coverage, indemnification or constitutional violation.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The action returns for ordinary consideration of any properly unresolved state-action question, the constitutional merits, causation, damages and independently available defenses. The Court reserves whether a private good-faith defense exists and, if it does, its elements and burdens. No damages award, mandatory trial or corporate disposition is ordered.

**End of entry: Richardson v. McKnight, merits decision, June 23, 1997.**

### Akins v. FEC, D.C. Circuit No. 94-5088

Merits decision, June 25, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

On review under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) of the en banc D.C. Circuit judgment of December 6, 1996, amended January 3, 1997. Merits heard and decided June 25, 1997. The questions concern voter informational standing, review of the Commission's complaint dismissal under FECA, the major-purpose limitation on political-committee status, and the membership and transaction predicates for disclosure.

Six politically active voters challenged dismissal of their complaint that AIPAC had to register and disclose as a political committee. The Commission treated a major electoral purpose as necessary despite its assessment of likely campaign payments above the statutory threshold. A separate corporate-prohibition enforcement decision rested on uncertainty about membership communications. The district court sustained the major-purpose construction; the en banc court rejected it and recognized standing. No final finding that all alleged payments are covered expenditures is made here.

All nine Justices participated in the merits hearing and decision.

#### Judgment

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The continuing denial of particular electoral information is a concrete injury

**Holding and operative rule:** Voters denied particular campaign-financing information allegedly required by FECA, which they seek for their own electoral participation, have a concrete informational injury even when many voters share it. Here the Commission's legal ground blocks a statutory process capable of supplying information still sought; the possibility of a later lawful discretionary choice does not defeat traceability or redressability.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer: seven direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Public Citizen recognizes concrete denial of useful information without requiring that the injury be numerically exclusive. Lujan still requires the plaintiffs' own concrete interest, causation and likely redress, supported as the litigation stage requires. These voters identify electoral use of particular information that remains withheld. They assert more than opposition to AIPAC's policies or a general demand that government obey the law. The contrary argument treats any elector's interest in campaign oversight as generalized. That characterization overlooks the specific information and practical use alleged here; complaint status alone would not suffice. Bennett requires examination of the actual remedial chain despite retained agency choice. Correction of the legal premise actually used can restore the disclosure process, although the Court guarantees neither prosecution nor ultimate classification. Steel Co. involved reports already supplied and a completed deprivation that the requested remedies did not redress. Its result does not govern information still sought here. Bennett does not establish the facts of this case, and standing does not decide whether every requested item is legally owed.

##### II. FECA permits review of this dismissal's legal premise

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 437g(a)(8) permits an aggrieved complainant to obtain judicial review of a complaint dismissal within sixty days and permits correction of a legally erroneous coverage premise. A court may declare the dismissal contrary to law and may direct conformity within thirty days; if the Commission fails to conform, the complainant may bring an action to remedy the violation involved in the original complaint.

**Authority:** Part II has the same seven-Justice coalition as Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress expressly provided this channel, unlike a claim based only on a general wish to supervise executive enforcement. Brown Group distinguishes an enacted enforcement route from the separate Article III requirement; Bennett requires use of the channel that actually authorizes the claim. The political-committee dismissal rests on a legal construction. Lawful enforcement priorities do not insulate that construction from the review Congress provided. Conversely, review does not compel prosecution, disclosure or a penalty. The distinct 120-day inaction route is not invoked. The Commission's close-membership explanation for its separate corporate-prohibition decision cannot become an unexercised replacement rationale for this dismissal. Legi-Tech preserves actual lawful Commission decisionmaking and statutory prerequisites; it supplies neither an automatic remedy nor a generic review bar. Genuine statutory ambiguities and remaining lawful discretion remain for appropriate agency consideration. The Commission's reading of constitutional decisions is not conclusive merely because it administers FECA.

##### III. Major purpose is not a categorical exemption for otherwise covered candidate financing

**Holding and operative rule:** Buckley and MCFL do not establish an organization-wide major-purpose exemption from political-committee treatment for genuinely covered candidate financing under §431(4)(A). That provision reaches a committee, club, association, or other group of persons receiving contributions aggregating in excess of $1,000 in a calendar year or making expenditures aggregating in excess of $1,000 in a calendar year, subject to the actual definitions, exclusions and constitutional limits; it does not literally make every group that makes a contribution a political committee.

**Authority:** Part III, including III-A for actual candidate cash transfers and genuine conduits and III-B for other genuinely covered candidate-connected financing, is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer: five direct votes with Souter. Kennedy supplies a sixth vote only for III-A. Stone-Zsela reserves both subparts. No combination of separate rationales is necessary to make the five-Justice rule controlling.

**Controlling explanation:** Buckley's narrowing protects independent electoral discussion from being swept indiscriminately into regulation, while distinguishing resources supplied to candidates. MCFL's independent-spending setting likewise does not give every predominantly issue-oriented organization immunity for its covered candidate financing. The strongest contrary argument concerns the organizational and associational burdens of political-committee status beyond a single transaction. Those concerns require the actual statutory predicates and preserve supported challenges to particular duties; they do not justify a categorical exemption merely because lobbying occupies most of a budget. A candidate payment may fall within §431(9)'s expenditure definition, but its purpose, amount and exclusions must be established. Colorado Republican requires the real statutory candidate connection: cooperation, consultation, concert, request or suggestion where that contribution theory is invoked. Ideological alignment, lobbying, policy cooperation or partisan identity alone is insufficient. Genuine independent expression remains protected. The Court neither classifies AIPAC conclusively nor sustains every organizational or donor-disclosure burden on every application. The Commission must examine actual transactions after the relevant exclusions, rather than stop at its categorical major-purpose premise.

##### IV. Membership exclusions and their distinct reporting proviso govern the remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Commission must determine membership, covered transactions, candidate connection, calendar-year thresholds and applicable exclusions before imposing political-committee duties. Section 431(9)(B)(iii)'s membership-communication exclusion retains its separate reporting proviso; §441b(b)(2)'s exclusions and the §431 cross-references retain their different terms.

**Authority:** Part IV has the same seven-Justice coalition as Parts I and II. The statutory construction in Part III governs the Court's remand notwithstanding Stone-Zsela's and Kennedy's narrower preferences.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 431(9)(B)(iii) excludes communications by a membership organization or corporation to its members, stockholders, or executive or administrative personnel if it is not organized primarily to influence nomination or election to federal office. The proviso requires a membership organization, including a labor organization, or a corporation to report costs directly attributable to communications expressly advocating election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate when those costs exceed $2,000 per election, except a communication primarily devoted to other subjects. Reporting follows §434(a)(4)(A)(i) and, for a general election, §434(a)(4)(A)(ii). This is neither the $1,000 calendar-year committee threshold nor an exception transplanted into §441b. Section 441b(b)(2) separately excludes corporate communications on any subject to stockholders and executive or administrative personnel and their families, and labor-organization communications to members and their families. Its nonpartisan registration and get-out-the-vote exclusion covers the same respective recipients. Its separate-fund exclusion covers establishment, administration and solicitation of contributions to a separate segregated fund used for political purposes by a corporation, labor organization, membership organization, cooperative or corporation without capital stock. The loan exception concerns a national or State bank's ordinary-course loan made under applicable banking law and regulations. Sections 431(8)(B)(vi) and (9)(B)(v) cross-reference activity allowed by §441b without importing the $2,000 proviso. The agency must apply each provision to the actual record. No final membership finding, coordination finding, threshold calculation, later membership rule, donor list, liability or penalty is supplied by this decision.

The Court itself directs no registration, report, penalty or prosecution and determines no final statutory violation.

### Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, No. 96-270

Merits decision, June 25, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, whose May 10, 1996 judgment vacated certification, settlement approval and the related injunction. The question is whether settlement-only certification relaxes Rule 23's predominance and adequate-representation requirements and whether this asbestos class satisfies them.

The complaint and proposed settlement were filed together on January 15, 1993, without an intention to litigate. Nine named plaintiffs sought to represent currently injured persons, exposure-only claimants and family members with differing claims under many States' laws, without subclasses. The agreement prescribed disease categories and payment ranges, annual caps and no inflation adjustment, while restricting some state-law claims. The settling parties seek reinstatement; the objectors defend decertification. Class-related standing and notice objections are presented but do not require decision once certification fails.

Justice O'Connor took no part in the consideration or decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** Stevens and Breyer.
**Not participating:** O'Connor.

Certification, settlement approval and the classwide injunction remain vacated. Stevens and Breyer would vacate the Third Circuit's judgment and remand for settlement-sensitive review of the district court's findings; they would not reinstate or approve the class or settlement. No individual damages claim or future agreement is adjudicated.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Affirmance | Entire opinion, Parts I–IV |
| Same lead opinion | Thomas | Stevens, Breyer | Threshold and governing-rule agreement only | Parts I and II; not the adequacy or predominance applications in Parts III and IV |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Breyer | Stevens | Would vacate and remand for appellate review | Settlement's real operation and proper review of the district court's findings; no approval of the settlement |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Certification may be resolved before the particular class-dependent standing questions

**Holding and operative rule:** A nonmerits Rule 23 defect that prevents this proposed settlement class from binding absent persons is logically antecedent to the Article III questions arising from those persons' inclusion in that class. The Court may deny certification on that ground without assuming jurisdiction to adjudicate any person's substantive claim; an individual action that proceeds must independently satisfy Article III.

**Authority:** Part I of Thomas's Court opinion is joined by all seven other participating Justices: eight direct votes. O'Connor does not participate.

**Controlling explanation:** Steel Co. forbids assumed constitutional power to decide disputed statutory merits. The present order of decision does not exercise that power. Denying the representative device establishes that this proposed class cannot bind its absent members, without determining anyone's damages entitlement, future injury or individual right to sue. The standing objections specific to the proposed class therefore need not be answered to deny certification. This limited sequencing rule does not authorize a court to assume jurisdiction over an individual action or decide its merits first. The Court likewise leaves the broader constitutional notice objection unanswered because Rule 23 independently defeats this class.

##### II. Settlement does not replace Rule 23's requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** A settlement-only class must satisfy the applicable requirements of Rule 23(a) and (b), including typicality, adequate representation and predominance under Rule 23(b)(3). Because no trial is proposed, a court need not examine difficulties managing a trial; settlement's actual operation and bargain may inform the remaining inquiries, but their requirements cannot be relaxed or replaced by overall fairness or the desirability of settlement.

**Authority:** Part II has the same eight-Justice coalition as Part I. The disagreement in the separate writing concerns the appellate application and remedy, not permission to dispense with certification requirements.

**Controlling explanation:** Falcon requires rigorous attention to the actual prerequisites for representative adjudication. A settlement eliminates the need to manage a trial but still binds persons absent from its negotiation. The court must therefore examine who is represented and which claims and interests the bargain resolves. Rule 23(e)'s fairness review does not substitute for Rule 23(a) and (b). The settling parties' argument that the bargain creates a common interest in compensation does not make distinct substantive rights identical. Matsushita and Richards distinguish genuine representative authority from notice, opportunity to object or a shared objective. Castano requires the actual law-and-proof showing, without a categorical ban based on class size, novelty or settlement pressure. Settlement may bear on the real class and its interests; the Court does not treat it as relevant only to a mechanical manageability inquiry. Nor does the rule disfavor settlement classes that independently meet the prerequisites.

##### III. The undivided representation is inadequate on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** This proposed undivided class fails Rule 23(a)(4) because the same representatives and counsel do not adequately protect the materially conflicting bargaining interests of currently injured and exposure-only claimants. Notice, the opportunity to opt out and the settlement's overall economic attractions cannot cure that defect.

**Authority:** Part III is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg: six direct votes with Thomas. Stevens and Breyer do not join this application.

**Controlling explanation:** Hansberry requires genuinely adequate representation before absent persons may be bound. Matsushita preserves that requirement independently of notice and opt-out protections, and Richards rejects shared interests as a substitute for lawful representation. Here currently injured claimants seek ample immediate payments. Those who may develop disease later need protection against inflation, diagnostic uncertainty, annual caps and the loss of future claims. The agreement's fixed categories and payments, caps and absent inflation adjustment make these divergent bargaining incentives concrete. One undivided representative group cannot exchange the future protections for present benefits without independent protection of the interests surrendered. Shutts's notice and exclusion safeguards serve a different function and do not align those incentives. The availability of compensation therefore cannot answer the defect. The Court does not prescribe a universal subclass architecture, prohibit settlement of future claims, decide constitutional notice sufficiency or resolve Matsushita's reserved collateral-review question. A materially different proposal may use separately represented subclasses, narrower groups or another adequate structure, but no redesign is approved here.

##### IV. Individual claims and governing-law differences defeat predominance

**Holding and operative rule:** The proposed class also fails Rule 23(b)(3) because its common asbestos-exposure and settlement objectives do not predominate over the materially different state laws, exposure histories, causation, injuries, defenses and individual claims. Removing trial-management concerns does not remove those substantive differences.

**Authority:** Part IV has the same six-Justice coalition as Part III and independently supports affirmance. Stevens and Breyer withhold this application as well.

**Controlling explanation:** Castano requires a demonstrated grouping of the governing law and proof rather than an abstract common description. Common exposure does not resolve product identification, latency, injury, limitations, defenses or damages across the many covered claims and States. The agreement's common payment mechanism is a compromise of those rights, not proof that common questions predominate over them. Falcon's insistence on the actual certification prerequisites remains relevant even when no trial is contemplated. The contrary contention that settlement removes the need to try individual questions confuses trial management with the substantive predominance requirement. Both remain distinct under Rule 23(b)(3). This application establishes no categorical prohibition of nationwide, mass-tort, issue or settlement classes, and it decides neither the validity of any particular state-law claim nor the fairness of a future bargain. Metro-North's FELA limits do not resolve the state-law claims collected here or make all exposure-only claimants legally uninjured.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Steel Co. retains its prohibition on assumed jurisdiction to decide disputed merits; this nonmerits, class-dependent threshold ruling is distinguished.
- Falcon supplies rigorous application of Rule 23's actual prerequisites; settlement does not displace them.
- Hansberry supplies the requirement of genuinely adequate representation; conflicting settlement interests must receive real protection.
- Shutts preserves notice and opt-out safeguards, which do not independently cure inadequate representation.
- Matsushita preserves the constitutional representation prerequisite separately from settlement recognition and notice; its actual-adequacy and collateral-review reservations are not decided here.
- Richards supplies the distinction between shared objectives and lawful binding representation without making every federal class requirement a universal constitutional command.
- Castano applies the required governing-law and proof inquiry to this proposed class while retaining its rejection of categorical national-class or novelty prohibitions.
- Rhone-Poulenc retains its separate extraordinary-writ limits and does not dilute Rule 23; Adams supplies no settlement merits ruling.
- Metro-North remains a FELA decision and does not determine the distinct state-law claims in this settlement.

#### Law After Decision

Settlement-only classes remain available when Rule 23's applicable requirements are satisfied. The absence of trial eliminates the need to address trial-manageability problems, but neither supplies predominance nor cures conflicts in representation. This class fails each of those independent requirements. The decision neither decides absent persons' individual claims nor mandates a particular settlement architecture. No precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Breyer, joined by Stevens, concurs in the nonmerits order of decision and the mandatory Rule 23 requirements but dissents from affirmance. Settlement's actual consequences matter to more than the absence of trial, and the appellate court must assess the bargain and the district court's findings with that setting in view. The differences between present and future claimants, including inflation and future-payment tradeoffs, warrant careful adequacy review. They do not warrant the Supreme Court's first resolution of the large record in place of proper appellate examination. Matsushita and Richards demand lawful representation without deciding this settlement's actual adequacy; Castano likewise does not adjudicate these findings. Breyer and Stevens would vacate and remand for settlement-sensitive Rule 23 review. They would neither restore the certification automatically nor direct approval, and leave notice and ultimate fairness unresolved.

#### Source Notes

The dates, settlement structure and judgment below are reported in Georgine v. Amchem Products, Inc., 83 F.3d 610, and the public record recited at [521 U.S. 591](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/96-270.ZS.html). Those recitations support the background facts and participation. Rule 23 supplies the governing text. The ruling requires no determination of an absent person's individual injury or the validity of a future settlement.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Third Circuit's vacatur of certification, settlement approval and the classwide injunction stands. The parties may pursue individual claims, individual settlements or a materially different proposed class and settlement under the applicable law. The Court orders no new subclasses, compensation, approval or adjudication of individual damages rights and expresses no view on a revised agreement's fairness.

**End of entry: Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, merits decision, June 25, 1997.**

### City of Boerne v. Flores, No. 95-2074

Merits decision, June 25, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 19, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, reviewing an interlocutory constitutional ruling accepted under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b). The question is whether Congress's Fourteenth Amendment §5 power authorizes RFRA's application to States and local governments in this permit dispute.

St. Peter Catholic Church sought a permit to enlarge its building. The City denied the permit under a historic-preservation ordinance it asserted covered the church. The Archbishop invoked RFRA. The district court ruled on February 1, 1995 that Congress lacked the asserted §5 authority; the Fifth Circuit reversed on January 23, 1996 for application of RFRA. The City seeks dismissal of the statutory claim. The question presented concerns congressional power, not the particular permit denial's burden or justification.

All nine Justices participated in the argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Souter and Breyer.

O'Connor and Breyer would obtain focused briefing and reargument before a final merits judgment. Souter would do the same and, reargument being refused, would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted. None of these three votes is a vote to uphold RFRA. On remand the RFRA claim against the City must be dismissed. Separately pleaded and preserved Free Exercise, speech, property and state-law claims remain subject to their own analysis. No permit, exemption or damages award is ordered.

The Court declines reargument and decides only the presented State and local Section 5 question.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, Ginsburg | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion; enforcement power, RFRA application, prior-law effect and remedy |
| Concurrence | Stevens | No other Justice | Additional ground for reversal | Religious-preference Establishment Clause objection only |
| Concurrence | Scalia | No other Justice | Joins reversal and the Court's rationale | Separate defense of Smith; no additional Court holding |
| Dissent | O'Connor | No other Justice | Would order reargument | Smith critique; conditional §5 agreement only if Smith is correct |
| Dissent | Souter | No other Justice | Would order reargument; failing that, dismiss the writ | Reserves Smith and RFRA validity |
| Dissent | Breyer | No other Justice | Would order reargument | Reserves §5 validity even assuming Smith; does not adopt all of O'Connor's assertions |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for all holdings:** Kennedy's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. Six Justices directly adopt the following rules, limits and remedy. The opinion does not contain a separate historical reconstruction of the Fourteenth Amendment's adoption.

##### I. Section 5 permits prevention and remedy, not replacement of a constitutional right

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may enforce the Fourteenth Amendment through preventive and remedial legislation reaching some conduct that a court would not independently find unconstitutional. Its means must be congruent and proportional to the constitutional injury it seeks to prevent or remedy; the enforcement power does not permit Congress to replace the judicially enforceable content of the substantive right with a different constitutional standard.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 5 gives Congress substantial choice in making constitutional protection effective. South Carolina v. Katzenbach and Katzenbach v. Morgan recognize preventive measures that extend beyond adjudicated violations; City of Rome likewise preserves effective enforcement in its setting. Their breadth does not erase the distinction between enforcing a right and changing it. The Court examines the constitutional guarantee, the threatened violation and the practical statutory design. It asks whether the remedy bears a supportable substantive relationship to that injury, not whether Congress compiled a prescribed quantity or form of evidence. Congress need not prove a violation in every affected jurisdiction, make formal findings, or require individualized proof for each prohibited act. Geographic limits and expiration provisions can illuminate fit but are not indispensable conditions. The contrary position correctly emphasizes that prevention would be ineffective if confined to conduct already adjudged unconstitutional. The Court accepts that premise; what it rejects is an enforcement power without a limiting connection to the right being enforced. The independent Fifteenth Amendment power remains governed by its existing precedents and is not further resolved here.

##### II. RFRA's universal State and local command exceeds that enforcement power

**Holding and operative rule:** RFRA's general application to State and local laws cannot be sustained under §5 because it substitutes its substantial-burden, compelling-interest and least-restrictive-means standard across the field for the constitutional boundary retained in Smith, without the required enforcement relationship. The Fifth Circuit's contrary power ruling is reversed, and the RFRA claim against the City must be dismissed.

**Controlling explanation:** Smith remains the constitutional rule for genuinely neutral and generally applicable laws. Lukumi independently protects against objective religious targeting and substantial secular underinclusion; RFRA does not merely enforce those limits. RFRA demands a compelling governmental interest pursued by the least restrictive means whenever covered government action substantially burdens religious exercise, even under neutral and generally applicable laws. Its State and local coverage extends across subjects, governmental functions and rules without a limiting constitutional class, administrative setting or supported remedial problem that fits that universal substitution. The objection concerns the relation between its broad command and the constitutional wrong, not a numerical deficiency in legislative examples. Congress's disagreement with Smith cannot itself authorize rewriting the guarantee through §5. Broad preventive legislation remains possible when appropriately fitted to constitutional injury. The Court does not reconsider Smith, decide the permit denial's neutrality, motive, substantial burden or justification, or deny every other available source of congressional authority to protect religious exercise. Federal RFRA remains outside this case, including its rule concerning later federal enactments. No general hearing requirement or substitute exemption code is created.

##### III. The reserved power question limits future statutory applications without reopening completed judgments

**Holding and operative rule:** Church of Scientology and Swanner did not decide the constitutional power question and therefore do not establish §5 authority for RFRA's universal State and local application. Their prospective statutory applications cannot govern future State and local RFRA claims resting only on that power after this decision, but their completed judgments retain finality and their independent grounds and statutory reasoning retain force within a valid field of operation.

**Controlling explanation:** Both decisions expressly reserved RFRA's constitutionality while applying its claimant-specific statutory standard. Resolving that reservation does not overrule a constitutional holding that neither decision made. The superior constitutional allocation now prevents continued reliance on §5 alone for the universal State and local command. Concrete reliance on final judgments is protected by leaving them intact; general reliance on a future unauthorized federal claim cannot supply missing legislative power. The Court orders no blanket reopening, retrospective recovery or reconsideration of independent constitutional or state-law grounds. Distinct transaction or remedial reliance claims require their own lawful source and are not presumed. Agostini's retention of Aguilar, Ball and Lemon concerns a different Establishment Clause setting and supplies no earlier RFRA power ruling. Plyler's distinction between continuing decree terms and underlying federal rights likewise does not authorize Congress to redefine those rights. The remand leaves every separately pleaded and preserved claim for its own analysis and decides no permit entitlement.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Smith remains controlling for genuinely neutral and generally applicable laws; the Court neither overrules it nor decides its correctness anew.
- Lukumi preserves the separate constitutional rules for objective religious targeting, substantial secular underinclusion and demanding justification. Boerne's actual conduct is not classified under those rules here.
- South Carolina v. Katzenbach retains its recognition of effective preventive enforcement in its constitutional setting; its remedies do not establish an unlimited power to redefine rights.
- Katzenbach v. Morgan retains broad preventive authority tied to enforcement, without authorizing replacement of the substantive guarantee.
- City of Rome remains intact within its Fifteenth Amendment setting; no further independent Fifteenth Amendment rule is announced.
- Church of Scientology's constitutional reservation is resolved for §5-based universal State and local application; completed judgments and independent grounds remain intact, and its statutory reasoning survives where validly operative.
- Swanner receives the same prospective limitation; its independent and reserved claims are not extinguished.
- Agostini leaves Aguilar, Ball and Lemon in place at their established scopes and supplies no RFRA source-of-power ruling.
- Plyler retains its rule for conditional termination under the original PLRA; its prospective-remedy reasoning does not settle congressional authority to alter the constitutional right.

#### Law After Decision

RFRA's universal State and local application cannot rest on §5. Congress retains preventive and remedial power with a congruent and proportional relation to constitutional injury; no universal requirement of formal findings, geographic limits, sunsets or individualized violations is adopted. Smith and Lukumi continue to define the relevant constitutional baseline. Federal RFRA, final judgments and independently available claims remain undisturbed. The earlier RFRA applications are limited prospectively in the invalid field, rather than erased or converted into prior constitutional holdings.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs and adds an Establishment Clause ground. RFRA gives religiously motivated claimants a statutory exemption argument unavailable to atheists or agnostics asserting otherwise comparable secular interests. Applying Wallace's neutrality principle, he regards that preference for religion over irreligion as an additional constitutional defect. He does not assume that this church automatically qualifies for an exemption, condemn every religious accommodation, or decide federal RFRA's disposition in this State and local case. No other Justice joins this ground.

Scalia concurs in the Court's full operative reasoning and separately defends Smith. He rejects the claim that historical practice establishes the general judicial exemption regime proposed by the dissent. Protecting religious exercise from discriminatory treatment does not make courts general administrators of exemptions from neutral, generally applicable law. His additional historical defense is his own; the Court's enforcement holding does not depend on it.

O'Connor dissents from immediate decision and would order focused briefing and reargument on Smith, the neutral-law burden question, Sherbert and Yoder. She considers Smith wrong and would restore compelling-interest review of substantial burdens from neutral laws. She agrees Congress cannot redefine constitutional rights and conditionally accepts RFRA's §5 invalidity if Smith is correct. That conditional view does not supply a present merits vote: she would first reconsider the baseline. She grants no permit relief and does not now uphold RFRA.

Souter dissents. His doubts about Smith warrant properly focused briefing before either endorsing or rejecting it in a case whose §5 question depends on the constitutional baseline. He would order reargument; because the Court declines, he would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted. He does not accept RFRA's invalidity even on an assumed Smith baseline, affirm the Fifth Circuit or hold RFRA valid.

Breyer dissents and would obtain reargument on the constitutional baseline before deciding Congress's power. He shares the need to reconsider Smith but does not adopt every historical assertion in O'Connor's critique or her conditional conclusion that RFRA exceeds §5 if Smith is correct. He reserves RFRA's validity under either baseline and does not claim Congress may simply redefine rights.

[The public recitations at 521 U.S. 507](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-2074.ZS.html) support the permit background, identifiers and argument date. The power ruling depends on the statute's operation and constitutional scope, not a new empirical inventory of legislative violations. The permit dispute's factual merits remain unresolved.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for dismissal of the RFRA claim against the City. Independently pleaded and preserved Free Exercise, speech, property or state-law claims retain their own legal analysis. The Court determines no right to a building permit, exemption, damages or new evidentiary hearing. Completed judgments in earlier cases and federal RFRA are not reopened.

### Glickman v. Wileman Brothers & Elliott, Inc., No. 95-1184

Merits decision, June 25, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 2, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, whose June 27, 1995 opinion, amended September 18 and October 3, held the generic advertising assessments unconstitutional under Central Hudson's advancement and fit requirements.

The Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act marketing orders replace important aspects of ordinary competition with collective decisions about price, quality, quantity, inspection, research, packaging and promotion. Federally supervised producer committees assess regulated California nectarine, peach and plum growers and handlers for program expenses, including generic product advertising. Respondents remain free to advertise or criticize independently and are not identified as the generic message's authors. The question is whether these compulsory assessments violate the First Amendment and whether the Ninth Circuit applied the correct framework. The Secretary seeks reversal; respondents seek relief from the advertising assessments.

All nine Justices participated in the argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Souter and Thomas.

The challenged assessments survive the First Amendment objection on this record. The mandate concerns the reviewed speech judgment; it does not reopen resolved or unreviewed statutory and administrative issues or itself order a collection award. Claims involving materially different false or ideological content, personal attribution, discrimination, interference with independent speech or stand-alone advertising remain outside the holding.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion; integrated-program ground and express limits |
| Dissent | Souter | Scalia | Would affirm the First Amendment judgment | Entire dissent, Parts I and II |
| Same dissent | Souter | Thomas | Would affirm | Part I's compulsory-subsidy ground; not Part II's Central Hudson framework |
| Separate dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Additional speech-protection ground for affirmance | Part I: compelled financing does not lose speech protection |
| Same separate dissent | Thomas | No other Justice | Additional framework disagreement | Part II: rejects discounted Central Hudson review; retains the conditional lesser-test observation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Germane generic promotion may be funded as part of this collective marketing program

**Holding and operative rule:** A comprehensive agricultural marketing program may require regulated producers to finance generic, nonideological product promotion germane to its substantially collective economic functions when the message is not attributed to dissenting producers and they remain free to advertise and criticize independently. On this combination of facts, Central Hudson's framework for restrictions on a speaker's commercial message does not require invalidation of the assessment; common benefit or majority approval alone would not suffice.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. Six Justices directly adopt this program-specific ground, its qualifications and reversal with remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The orders already displace important individual decisions concerning price, quality, supply and marketing. Generic commodity promotion is a germane expense of that genuinely collective system. Abood and Keller distinguish permissible common-function charges from compelled support for unrelated ideological activity; Lehnert's germane-function inquiry supports the distinction, while Gibson preserves chargeable common activity and safeguards disputed nongermane dues. Respondents contend that advertising remains expression even when government requires payment rather than personal speech. The Court accepts that compelled funding can implicate expression without personal attribution or ideology. It nevertheless finds this integrated, germane expense permissible, rather than treating every expressive assessment as a restriction on respondents' own message. Respondents remain free to promote their products, criticize the campaign and speak independently; the advertisements do not identify them as authors. Hurley's protection of private expressive composition is therefore not infringed on these facts. Rosenberger expressly reserved compelled-fee questions and supplies no independent authorization for this assessment. Central Hudson, 44 Liquormart and Turner preserve scrutiny of supported restrictions on speech; they do not require an effectiveness trial for every germane administrative expense in this collective program. The Court makes no finding that generic advertising is superior to individual advertising. Its holding does not approve advertising-only arrangements in an otherwise independent market, ideological advocacy, false statements, viewpoint penalties, personal attribution or interference with independent expression. A materially different showing concerning those features would remove the factual basis for this disposition. The Court neither denies commercial and corporate speakers First Amendment interests nor holds that compelled funding is always ordinary nonexpressive regulation. It reverses only the reviewed speech judgment and supplies no ruling on unchallenged regulatory provisions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Abood retains the distinction between chargeable common functions and compelled ideological support; the Court does not create a universal exception for commercially useful speech.
- Keller's germaneness principle informs this common expense without making the integrated-bar setting identical to agricultural regulation.
- Lehnert's common-function analysis supports the limited integrated-program rationale; shared benefit alone does not justify every public-relations charge.
- Gibson retains protection of disputed nongermane dues and the permissibility of germane professional-regulatory activity; its safeguards are not displaced.
- Hurley is distinguished because these assessments do not compel inclusion in respondents' own expressive product or make them apparent authors; its private-composition holding remains intact.
- Rosenberger leaves compulsory-fee challenges unresolved and therefore does not decide the funding objection here.
- Central Hudson remains operative for covered restrictions on commercial expression; its restriction-of-message framework does not control this combination of collective-program facts.
- 44 Liquormart retains its advancement, fit and commercial-speech holdings at their actual proposition-level scope; it creates no categorical rule against this germane common expense.
- Turner retains its cable-carriage justification and fit analysis without creating a general economic-regulation exemption or governing this distinct compelled-financing arrangement.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged generic advertising assessments are valid as germane expenses of this substantially collective marketing scheme on the stated nonideological, nonattribution and independent-speech facts. The decision does not validate stand-alone compulsory advertising or all expressive spending within a regulated industry. Compelled funding may still implicate speech, and materially different content, attribution, discrimination or speech restrictions require their own analysis. No precedent is overruled, and no new efficacy finding is made.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Souter dissents, joined by Scalia throughout and by Thomas on the sufficient compulsory-subsidy ground. Paying for expression remains a First Amendment burden even if the message is commercial, not personally attributed and not ideological. Abood, Keller and Lehnert justify charges through a legally required representative function, not every useful public-relations campaign. The program's regulatory breadth and shared commercial benefit therefore do not adequately justify this separate forced expressive expenditure. The dissent would affirm the judgment against the advertising assessments while leaving independently valid nonexpressive regulation untouched. In Part II, Souter and Scalia additionally retain Central Hudson's inquiry into substantial interest, direct advancement and reasonable fit, with the burden on government and without a least-restrictive-means or mandatory-study requirement. That framework discussion supplies no separate factual holding about this program's efficacy, geographic coverage or credits.

Thomas separately dissents. In Part I, joined by Scalia, he emphasizes that compelled financing does not cease to implicate speech merely because it uses money or promotes commerce. In Part II, joined by no other Justice, he rejects reduced constitutional protection and Central Hudson's discounted balancing approach. His observation that failure under a lesser standard would also establish failure under his stronger rule does not adopt that lesser framework or add a factual application absent from the record. He would affirm on the shared compulsory-subsidy ground, without prohibiting every compulsory regulatory fee or adjudicating unchallenged parts of the orders.

The lower ruling and amendments are reported in Wileman Bros. & Elliott, Inc. v. Espy, 58 F.3d 1367. The Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act, 7 U.S.C. §§601–624, and the marketing orders supply the regulatory setting. [The public record recited at 521 U.S. 457](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-1184.ZS.html) supports the background and chronology. The disposition rests on the collective structure, generic nonideological promotion, lack of attribution and retained independent expression. It makes no new finding about comparative sales effectiveness or detailed geographic and credit arrangements.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit for proceedings consistent with reversal of its First Amendment judgment. The generic advertising assessments are sustained at the stated scope, without an automatic enforcement award or reopening of resolved or unreviewed matters. Materially different preserved claims retain their own legal and procedural requirements.

### Lawyer v. Department of Justice, No. 95-2024

The March 19, 1996 approval and decree are vacated for the confined existing-claim and current-law proceedings stated in Holding IV. ##### I. The personal challenge to this decree remains reviewable

**Holding and operative rule:** A voter with an established personal relationship to the challenged electoral assignment and decree may obtain review of the decree and the disposition of his own claims even when his design-only constitutional theory fails. This three-judge court's grant of districting injunctive relief is directly reviewable under 28 U.S.C. §§2284 and 1253; neither receipt of part of the requested practical relief nor failure on that substantive theory eliminates the controversy.

**Controlling explanation:** Miller separates personal exposure to an electoral assignment from success on the asserted constitutional claim, and Vera applies that distinction to direct appeals from districting injunctions. Lawyer contests the decree governing his own district and the treatment of his own claims. He is not a stranger objecting to another person's settlement. Replacing Plan 330 answers part of his request but does not answer whether the judicial means or replacement lawfully dispose of his objections. Lujan requires a personal, causally connected and redressable stake; the established assignment and challenged decree supply that relationship here. This is no general rule of resident standing. The serious questions concerning decree authority and a nonconsenter's claims do not become wholly insubstantial because the governing cases reject one merits theory. The Court reaches those questions and orders no Article III, mootness or insubstantiality dismissal.

##### II. General consent authority does not settle every districting-authority objection

**Holding and operative rule:** A lawful consent decree may impose obligations broader than the relief obtainable after a trial without invariably requiring a prior adjudication of liability, but it must resolve a dispute within subject-matter jurisdiction, remain within the pleadings' general scope, advance the underlying law's objectives and comply with law. One litigant cannot veto the other parties' lawful settlement merely by withholding consent; their agreement nevertheless cannot extinguish his properly raised valid claim or impose duties on him without consent.

**Controlling explanation:** Firefighters supplies both settlement latitude and these limits. Consent has legal force; a negotiated obligation need not duplicate a constitutional minimum or a remedy a court could have ordered after trial. That does not authorize a court to give every agreed state-policy change compulsory force. Florida's officials and legislative chambers acted through authorized litigation representatives. Their settlement choice was real, but the March 19 order supplied Plan 386's compulsory districting force. No legislative joint resolution or superseding state judgment adopting that plan is established. Growe requires respect for actual timely state redistricting and actual superseding judgments; it does not transform a proposal into an enacted replacement. Likewise, Lawyer's opportunity to object is material but does not itself establish authority to dispose of any properly retained valid claim. Richards and Matsushita preserve the distinction between an opportunity to participate and lawful authority over a person's own rights. The Court need not decide whether a federal districting decree invariably requires an established violation or whether authorized litigation settlement necessarily fulfills every requirement of the State's redistricting opportunity. The separate opinions disagree on those additional questions. The common consent principles alone do not sustain this approval's stated legal assessment.

**Precedent treatment:** Firefighters is applied at its full qualified scope, without converting its Title VII holding into a resolution of every federal reapportionment objection; Growe's actual-state-process rule remains; Richards and Matsushita preserve lawful authority to bind without creating a new claim here.

##### III. This approval must be reconsidered under the governing substantive law

**Holding and operative rule:** When approval of a districting consent decree expressly rests on finding a materially and fairly contestable constitutional dispute under a racial-predominance rule that governing precedent rejects, that approval cannot stand on its stated assessment. The court must reconsider the actual legally available basis and properly retained claims; this does not categorically prohibit settlement of weak, disputed or ultimately unsuccessful claims or require each agreed term to be independently compelled by federal law.

**Controlling explanation:** Shaw, Miller and Vera establish that race-conscious design of a shared territorial constituency, even when predominant and contrary to traditional criteria, does not alone establish the asserted equal-protection deprivation or trigger strict scrutiny. Independent exclusion, intentional dilution, materially unequal participation, inferior civic status or another recognized wrong retains its own elements. There is no universal requirement of a later loss or a second governmental act. The lower majority identified the required dispute through the rejected design-only theory. Defendants denied liability and conceded a reasonable basis only for settlement; the separate lower concurrence supplied no majority adjudication or unconditional admission. No independently sufficient adjudicated claim is identified in the approval.

The strongest defense of affirmance is that lawful consent may exceed a trial remedy, the State genuinely favored this compromise, and Plan 386 need not itself violate the Constitution. Those propositions do not answer the particular legal error: the approval assessed its predicate under an unavailable constitutional rule. A hoped-for reconsideration by this Court is not existing authority for a lower court. Agostini preserves lawful voluntary obligations beyond constitutional minima; Plyler distinguishes negotiated terms from their underlying federal right. Neither decides original authority for this decree, and the PLRA does not apply here. ### United States v. O'Hagan, No. 96-842

Merits decision, June 25, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 16, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, which on August 2, 1996 reversed the securities-fraud and mail-fraud convictions, rejected deceptive misappropriation under §10(b), held Rule 14e-3(a) unauthorized, and separately reversed the money-laundering convictions. The United States seeks review of the securities and mail-fraud rulings, not the money-laundering disposition.

O'Hagan was a partner in a firm representing Grand Metropolitan in a contemplated tender offer for Pillsbury. Although he did not work on the transaction, he obtained confidential information through the firm, bought Pillsbury stock and call options before announcement, and realized more than $4 million after the market responded. The questions are whether his concealed use of entrusted information constitutes his own deception in connection with securities trading and whether §14(e) authorizes Rule 14e-3(a)'s preventive tender-offer prohibition. Recognition of either legal theory leaves unresolved preserved trial objections for remand.

All nine Justices participated in the argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Deceptive use of entrusted information in securities trading can be primary §10(b) fraud

**Holding and operative rule:** A person who, with scienter, owes an information source a duty of loyalty and confidentiality, feigns fidelity, and secretly uses material nonpublic information entrusted in that relationship for securities trading commits deception in connection with the transaction when the securities use consummates the concealed disloyalty. The source need not be the trading counterparty; a qualifying duty, deception and transaction connection are indispensable, and full disclosure of the trading plan to every person owed the relevant duty removes this theory's deception.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens's Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer: six direct votes. Stone-Zsela supplies a seventh judgment vote on his narrower source-participation ground, which is not a limitation adopted by the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Chiarella makes a duty essential when silence is asserted to be deceptive; possession of superior information alone creates no general duty to the market. Carpenter recognizes that confidential information can be property and its secret conversion can defraud its source. The additional §10(b) connection exists here because trading is the use through which the entrusted information is secretly converted into personal gain. The source deception and securities use are thus linked, even though the source is not the buyer or seller opposite O'Hagan. The contrary view separates a completed source fraud from a later trade. That account misses the alleged feigned fidelity and concealed trading use that consummate this breach. O'Hagan obtained the bidder's confidential tender-offer information through its law firm and used it to acquire the affected target's stock and options. This is his alleged primary deception, not liability based on status, innocent receipt, equal access or assistance imputed from another person's fraud. Central Bank's confined private affirmative-assistance action supplies neither the primary offense nor a criminal mental-state shortcut. Disclosure to one principal does not necessarily discharge a duty to another; disclosure sufficient to remove this securities deception does not authorize conduct forbidden by other law. The Court decides the legal theory, not every possible confidential relationship or whether all preserved instruction, sufficiency and harmless-error objections fail. The Eighth Circuit's categorical rejection is reversed on that bounded basis.

##### II. Section 14(e) authorizes the bounded preventive rule in this tender-offer setting

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 14(e)'s express authority to prescribe means reasonably designed to prevent tender-offer fraud permits Rule 14e-3(a), in the source-misappropriation setting presented, to prohibit the covered trading without proof of a fiduciary breach as an additional element. The rule's actual commencement, information, source, knowledge, instrument and public-disclosure conditions remain necessary; the delegation does not authorize treating every informational disparity as fraud or regulate outside the tender-offer and antifraud domain.

**Authority:** Part II of the same Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, Stone-Zsela and Scalia: eight direct votes. Thomas dissents from this application while accepting that the statute permits some preventive rules.

**Operative conditions and distinct alternatives:** A person must have taken a substantial step or steps to commence, or commenced, a tender offer. The trader must be someone other than the offering person and must possess material information relating to the offer, knowing or having reason to know both that it is nonpublic and that it was acquired, directly or indirectly, from the offering person, the issuer, or an officer, director, partner, employee or other person acting on behalf of either. The prohibition covers purchasing, selling or causing purchase or sale of the securities sought or to be sought in the offer, securities convertible into or exchangeable for them, and options or rights to obtain or dispose of those securities. Its disclosure alternative requires the information **and its source** to be publicly disclosed a reasonable time before trading. Private disclosure to the source, which can eliminate §10(b) deception, does not itself satisfy that separate public-disclosure alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The enacted authority concerns prevention as well as completed fraud. Secret tender-offer trading presents timing and proof problems: material information may pass from transaction participants before public announcement, and proof of the precise source breach may be difficult. The rule responds with a bounded prohibition tied to the offer, identified sources, material nonpublic information and the trader's actual or reason-to-know knowledge. Requiring proof of every completed fiduciary fraud as an additional element would deprive the preventive delegation of its intended work. MCI requires an agency to remain within the authority the statute grants; it does not erase this express prevention authority or replace Chevron generally. The strongest objection is that a source condition is not itself proof of a breach and that an agency may not simply redefine fraud. The Court agrees with both propositions but finds this rule reasonably designed to prevent fraud in the present setting; the source and knowledge conditions delimit the preventive measure rather than purporting to prove a completed breach. The ruling does not resolve principal-authorized warehousing or every application of Rule 14e-3(a). Preserved pre-offer scope and due-process arguments, trial objections and other unaddressed challenges remain for the Eighth Circuit; an unpreserved contention is not decided in the first instance.

##### III. The criminal-punishment qualification and unresolved trial questions remain operative

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 78ff(a) requires a willful violation for criminal punishment and bars imprisonment for that rule violation when the defendant proves lack of knowledge of the rule. That lack-of-knowledge protection does not erase the underlying rule, create general immunity from fines or require the government to prove knowledge of the rule as an element of every violation.

**Authority:** This qualification is part of Part II and has the same eight-Justice coalition. It states the limit under which the criminal Rule 14e-3(a) ruling is sustained, not a factual adjudication of O'Hagan's knowledge.

**Controlling explanation:** Authorization of a preventive rule does not establish every prerequisite to criminal punishment. The knowledge or reason-to-know conditions concerning the information and its source remain distinct from willfulness and from the defendant's statutory protection against imprisonment for an unknown rule. Neither the trader's possession of information nor recognition of the rule's validity conclusively resolves those questions. Central Bank's severe-recklessness standard concerns a confined private accessory action and cannot be transplanted into this criminal prosecution. Princeton's separate copyright willfulness rule likewise supplies no universal criminal standard. The Eighth Circuit must consider preserved questions left unresolved by its categorical legal rulings; the Court orders no new jury finding, automatic imprisonment or unconditional reinstatement.

##### IV. The mail-fraud reversal cannot rest on the rejected securities premise

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth Circuit's reversal of mail-fraud counts 1–20, insofar as it rested on the securities theories' legal invalidity, must be reversed now that the Court rejects that premise. The case is remanded for consideration of preserved alternate grounds not reached below; money-laundering counts 55–57 were not presented for review and remain undisturbed.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court opinion is joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer and Stone-Zsela: seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas join the mail-fraud reversal and remand, making that judgment unanimous, but not this entire rationale; Thomas's independent Carpenter ground is separate.

**Controlling explanation:** The lower court's asserted dependence of mail fraud on the rejected securities theories does not support its judgment once those categorical securities rulings fall. That is sufficient to require renewed consideration without deciding every alternate trial objection. The Court does not infer a jury finding on an unaddressed issue, declare every instruction valid or make an ultimate sufficiency determination. Recognition of a lawful legal theory supports reinstatement only to the extent the convictions survive the remaining properly preserved challenges. The petition's limits independently preserve the money-laundering disposition; a reversal of related mail-fraud counts cannot enlarge the mandate to unreviewed counts.

Section 10(b) reaches deceptive trading against an information source within the six-Justice rule, without requiring that the source be the counterparty or participate in a securities transaction as Stone-Zsela separately proposes. #### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts II and III and in the §10(b) judgment. His source-bound rule requires information entrusted by a source participating in an actual or contemplated securities transaction, a defined duty of fidelity or confidentiality, scienter, concealed planned use, and trading in securities materially affected by that source's transaction. Trading must consummate or monetize the deception. O'Hagan's firm represented the contemplated bidder, and the target's securities were affected by that very tender offer, so the required connection exists. The additional condition gives independent work to the securities nexus and avoids a general federal law of confidential information. He reserves family, friendship, government-service, ordinary-employment and commercial relationships in which the source is not participating in the transaction. Full disclosure to the source removes the theory's deception; innocent receipt and equal access do not establish it. He does not attribute his limit to Central Bank or narrow that decision's separate private-assistance rule.

Scalia concurs in the Rule 14e-3(a) holding and the mail-fraud result but dissents on §10(b). Criminal lenity prevents punishment under an insufficiently clear extension of source deception beyond deception of a party to the securities transaction. That objection does not defeat §14(e)'s express prevention authority, which supports the bounded tender-offer rule. Nor does it establish immunity from mail fraud. He joins the mail-fraud reversal and remand without adopting Thomas's entire independent analysis or the Court's securities-dependent explanation. He does not adopt every part of Thomas's agency-theory objection.

Thomas concurs in the mail-fraud result and dissents from both securities rulings. For §10(b), he regards the source fraud as complete independently of any eventual trade; the government's proposed distinctions do not supply a coherent statutory securities connection, and a court cannot replace the agency's actual theory with an improved one. For §14(e), he accepts power to adopt some preventive rules but rejects this justification and application even if misappropriation could otherwise violate §10(b). Establishing that information came from the bidder does not demonstrate why proving the relevant breach is so difficult that the rule should omit it; knowledge of source is not knowledge of breach. He does not invalidate every preventive rule, classic target-insider application or differently justified protection. On mail fraud, Carpenter independently permits prosecution of the fraudulent taking and use of confidential information as property, regardless of whether the same conduct satisfies the securities statutes. He therefore supports reversal and remand for preserved alternate objections without automatic reinstatement or a new factual finding.

The Eighth Circuit must reconsider the reviewed securities and mail-fraud counts under the legal rules stated here and address preserved instruction, sufficiency, harmless-error, scope, due-process and other alternate grounds it did not reach. Reinstatement is appropriate only insofar as the judgments survive those legally available objections; no conviction is restored free of them by this mandate. The money-laundering reversal remains untouched. No other trading relationship or tender-offer application is adjudicated merely by upholding the theories presented.
