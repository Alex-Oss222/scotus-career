## Event

**Castano v. American Tobacco Co.**, Fifth Circuit No. 95-30725, 84 F.3d 734. October Term 1996. Merits decision, January 14, 1997; merits hearing January 14, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, following its May 23, 1996 decision on an accepted interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1292(b). The Court reviews the accepted interlocutory appeal, rather than a mandamus proceeding.

The nationwide tobacco-addiction class covers smokers and specified derivative or representative claimants since 1943. The District Court certified core-liability and punitive questions while excluding injury, causation, reliance, defenses and individual compensatory damages. The Court of Appeals reversed and directed dismissal of the class complaint. The class seeks reinstatement; manufacturers seek decertification. The questions concern predominance and superiority; the rigorous analysis required for a novel mass-tort theory; material state-law variations; and management, individual proof and settlement pressure. The separate denial of Rule 23(b)(2) equitable and medical-monitoring certification was not cross-appealed.

## Holdings

**Authority common to all holdings:** Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, is joined throughout by the other eight Justices identified in Opinion Topology. All four holdings command nine votes.

#### I. Certification requires a demonstrated legal and evidentiary basis

**Holding and operative rule.** Before certifying a Rule 23(b)(3) class, a court must determine from the actual claims, governing law and anticipated proof that common questions predominate and class adjudication is superior to the available alternatives, together with satisfaction of Rule 23(a). Conditional certification does not permit postponing that showing, and a claim's novelty neither dispenses with rigorous analysis nor categorically bars certification until individual verdicts exist.

**Controlling explanation.** Falcon requires rigorous examination of the Rule's prerequisites. Eisen forbids awarding or denying certification merely because the court predicts success on the merits; it does not make the inquiry pleadings-only when the substantive elements determine whether a common trial can work. The proponents must identify what the class proceeding would decide and how that work connects to remaining claims and defenses. An untested liability theory makes that explanation especially necessary, but the Rule contains no tort-maturation requirement. Common evidence of manufacturer conduct cannot itself establish that the resulting questions predominate over the unresolved legal and individual work. Administrative convenience cannot replace a prerequisite. Matsushita preserves constitutionally adequate representation as distinct from notice or opt-out rights, and Richards preserves the lawful basis needed to bind absent persons; neither supplies a finding of actual adequacy or conflict in this case. A renewed proposal must satisfy the representation requirements as well as predominance and superiority.

#### II. The present core-liability class cannot stand

**Holding and operative rule.** This nationwide core-liability certification fails Rule 23(b)(3) because the court deferred the material state-law inquiry and did not show how common issues, individual addiction, reliance, causation and defenses would fit into lawful, manageable adjudication. Differences among state laws do not automatically prohibit every multistate class, but proponents must demonstrate materially common answers or workable groupings rather than assume uniformity or rely on subclass labels.

**Controlling explanation.** Shutts prevents applying a single State's law to materially disconnected claims merely for convenience; it does not select the governing law here. The pleaded fraud, misrepresentation, negligence, warranty and consumer-protection claims require examination of their actual duties, defenses and burdens. The District Court certified core conduct while excluding injury, causation, reliance, defenses and individual compensatory damages. Without explaining how the common answers would materially resolve the legally varied claims, the court could not establish predominance or make a grounded superiority comparison. The proponents' strongest point is that common manufacturer evidence is substantial and individual litigation expensive. But an imagined future trial structure cannot supply the missing showing for the order entered. Workable legal groupings or genuinely separable common issues may matter on a different proposal. The Court requires a credible account of claims and proof, not a universal separate document bearing the title trial plan. It does not decide the full relationship between Rule 23(b)(3) and (c)(4), including whether issue certification may ever proceed when an entire cause of action fails an action-wide predominance inquiry.

#### III. The punitive component and superiority finding also fail

**Holding and operative rule.** The certified punitive-ratio component fails because its state-law predicates, burdens, relation to liability and coordination with individual proceedings were not demonstrated; those same gaps defeat the present superiority finding. Settlement pressure from a procedure that bypasses defenses or detaches punitive assessment from established claims may reinforce concrete certification defects, but aggregate size or ordinary settlement pressure alone is not a categorical bar.

**Controlling explanation.** The certification order incorporated important elements of the proposed plan, including its punitive-ratio arrangement, without adopting that plan in full. The court did not establish what one jury could lawfully decide about punishment before the remaining individualized liability work, or how later proceedings would use that decision consistently with each governing law. Predicted savings from millions of trials cannot substitute for identifying the work the common trial would actually accomplish. The manufacturers' concern about repeated jury determinations underscores the need for that account, but repetition of evidence is not automatically reexamination of a decided fact. The Court rests on Rule 23 and does not decide the constitutionality of every punitive ratio, issue class or bifurcated trial. The proposed Liggett settlement does not establish a completed release or make the certification controversy moot. No manufacturer liability, individual recovery, punitive amount, Article III severance question, new Seventh Amendment rule or complete resolution of every asserted Erie objection is decided.

#### IV. Decertification does not require a categorical ban on reconsideration

**Holding and operative rule.** The rejection of the present core-liability and punitive certification is affirmed, but the mandate is vacated insofar as it requires dismissal of the class pleading and bars reconsideration merely because the tort has not matured through individual trials. The case is remanded for ordinary proceedings on individual claims or consideration of a materially narrower, supported class proposal that independently satisfies all applicable Rule 23 requirements; the existing class remains uncertified.

**Controlling explanation.** The lower court identified decisive defects in this proposal, then treated the asserted novelty of the tort as making correction on remand categorically unavailable. The Rule requires a present showing, not a quota of prior trials or an existing crisis in individual litigation. Rejecting an unsupported class does not decide whether a materially different proposal can establish its prerequisites. The proponents retain their burden; remand creates no presumption of certification, discovery entitlement, preferred trial plan or deadline. It adjudicates no individual claim on the merits. The separate denial of Rule 23(b)(2) equitable and medical-monitoring certification was not cross-appealed and is not reopened.

## Precedent Treatment

- General Telephone Co. of Southwest v. Falcon, 457 U.S. 147: applied to require rigorous determination of the actual certification prerequisites, including where the inquiry overlaps substantive issues.
- Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 417 U.S. 156: preserved as forbidding certification based merely on a preliminary prediction of merits success; it does not forbid the substantive inquiry needed to determine whether class adjudication can work.
- Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts, 472 U.S. 797: applied against assuming one State's substantive law for materially disconnected claims; no particular choice-of-law result is directed.
- Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Epstein, February 27, 1996: its adequate-representation requirement remains distinct from notice and opt-out rights; its judgment-recognition holding does not itself decide prospective certification or the actual adequacy of this class.
- Richards v. Jefferson County, June 10, 1996: preserved for the lawful basis necessary to bind absent persons; it does not constitutionalize every Rule 23 defect or resolve the reserved certification questions.

## Law After Decision

Rule 23 requires the concrete law-and-proof showing before certification. Neither novelty nor ordinary aggregation pressure creates an independent exclusion, and differing state laws require demonstrated analysis rather than an automatic national-class prohibition. The present certification is defeated by its own missing prerequisites. The partial vacatur preserves lawful reconsideration without reinstating or presuming any class, modifying individual substantive burdens, or creating a federal tobacco tort.

## Event

**Chamber of Commerce v. Reich**, D.C. Circuit No. 95-5242, 74 F.3d 1322. October Term 1996. Merits decision, January 14, 1997; merits hearing January 14, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, which on February 2, 1996 reversed the District Court's dismissal and rejected the challenged procurement policy. No Supreme Court docket number is assigned.

The employer organizations seek declaratory and injunctive relief against the Secretary of Labor's implementation of Executive Order 12954. Their complaint was not amended to plead an APA challenge to the later regulations. The questions are whether traditional equity permits this subordinate-officer challenge despite the absence of a pleaded APA action; whether any statute precludes it; and whether the Procurement Act authorizes the replacement-based contracting restriction consistently with the NLRA. Presidential APA status remains an open question.

## Holdings

**Authority common to all holdings:** Justice Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, is joined throughout by the other eight Justices identified in Opinion Topology. Each proposition below therefore has nine votes; judgment agreement and reasoning agreement coincide.

#### I. Review of the implementing officer

**Holding and operative rule.** A person injured by an officer's implementation of a restriction outside delegated statutory authority may seek prospective equitable restraint under federal-question jurisdiction unless Congress has displaced that remedy. Section 702 waives sovereign immunity for this nonmonetary official-capacity suit against the implementing Secretary even without a pleaded APA review cause, while preserving other legal and equitable review limits and relief prohibitions in another consent-to-suit statute; any injunction must identify the officer or successor responsible for compliance.

**Controlling explanation.** Section 1331 supplies jurisdiction; traditional officer-directed equity supplies the action; §702 addresses sovereign immunity. None independently creates a damages claim or a cause for every statutory error. McAnnulty, Stark and Harmon establish that the absence of an express review provision does not itself eliminate prospective officer relief. Larson and Dugan support the confined distinction between authorized action and action beyond delegated power, without making every allegation of error sufficient. The employers challenge an operative disqualification imposed by the Secretary, not merely the wisdom of an authorized purchase. Their claim presents a judicially manageable boundary between two federal statutes. Dalton bars reopening its particular completed base-closure package; the Procurement Act contains no comparable structure foreclosing this action. Franklin preserves review of a completed subordinate act despite later presidential involvement. The origin of this restriction in a presidential order does not answer whether its implementing officer has statutory authority. The Court neither decides the President's APA status nor authorizes coercive relief against him.

#### II. The procurement delegation does not authorize this labor-policy sanction

**Holding and operative rule.** The Procurement Act's general economy-and-efficiency delegation does not authorize Executive Order 12954's government-wide disqualification based on permanent replacement of economic strikers, because that sanction alters the bargaining choices Congress preserved under the NLRA. This statutory holding leaves Congress free to change those choices and preserves ordinary review of materially different proprietary conditions and lawful centralized purchasing standards.

**Controlling explanation.** Mackay Radio recognizes the use of permanent replacements for economic strikers within the NLRA's remaining protections; it does not equate those strikers with unfair-labor-practice strikers. Insurance Agents and Machinists identify the economic weapons Congress left to the bargaining parties. A general purchasing delegation must operate with that allocation, not silently displace it. Boston Harbor protects a lawful bargain justified by the public purchaser's project interests; a procurement label alone does not turn general labor policy into proprietary conduct. This order makes replacement itself the disqualifying event and reaches affiliates capable of supplying the contract, even when the striking workers do not perform that contract. The Government's interest in dependable supply and retained experience is legitimate, but the policy's actual operation goes beyond a supported performance bargain. Its threshold and discretionary exceptions do not cure that statutory mismatch. The Court neither imposes a universal one-project rule nor invalidates purchasing standards because of their size. It does not guarantee any employer government business. The ground is construction of the federal enactments together, not mechanical application of state-preemption labels or a constitutional limit on Congress.

**Operative qualifications.** The order concerns contracts above $100,000. After finding permanent replacement of lawfully striking workers, the Secretary may find termination for convenience appropriate unless the contracting agency head objects; termination is not mandatory. Debarment retains exceptions when the precipitating dispute has been resolved as determined by the Secretary or the agency head finds a compelling reason to lift it. Debarment normally reaches the organizational units found to have used permanent replacements; the regulations define a unit to include the responsible prime-contracting division or element and any affiliate capable of supplying the contracted goods or services. In deciding whether a dispute ended, the regulations consider a formal settlement or agreed dispute-resolution procedure, informal resolution, the strikers' return, and other factors tending to show the dispute has ended. The Court does not adopt the lower court's reading of those factors as a universal requirement of union approval or find that every application compels unlawful recognition.

#### III. The relief follows the statutory defect

**Holding and operative rule.** The judgment reversing dismissal and rejecting the challenged implementation is affirmed, and the case is remanded for appropriate prospective declaratory and injunctive relief against the implementing Secretary confined to the unlawful procurement restriction. No injunction against the President, damages, contract award, striker reinstatement or employee-specific bargaining remedy is ordered.

**Controlling explanation.** The reviewable defect concerns the Secretary's implementation of an unauthorized restriction. Prospective officer relief addresses that defect without transferring purchasing decisions to the courts or deciding individual labor rights. The lower court must formulate the appropriate decree consistently with §702's responsible-officer requirement and retained legal and equitable limits. No independent nondelegation ruling, mandatory procurement-findings procedure or decision on a materially different proprietary measure is necessary.

## Event

**In re Deuel**, Federal Circuit No. 94-1202, 51 F.3d 1552; application Serial No. 07/542,232, Heparin-Binding Growth Factor. October Term 1996. Merits decision, January 14, 1997; merits hearing January 14, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit at the government's request. The petition was held through July 1, 1996; limited review was granted October 7, 1996. No Supreme Court docket number is assigned.

The Board of Patent Appeals and Interferences affirmed the examiner's §103 rejection of claims 4–7 on November 30, 1993. The Federal Circuit reversed on March 28, 1995. Claims 5 and 7 recite particular human placental and bovine uterine cDNA sequences; claims 4 and 6 cover broader DNA genera encoding the identified proteins. The questions concern whether prior protein information and known isolation methods can establish obviousness without prior disclosure of the claimed nucleotide sequence, and when a predictably effective method is sufficient despite uncertainty about precise molecular structure. The actual prior protein disclosure contains only nineteen amino acids, not the complete sequence. The controversy concerns reversal of existing application rejections, not infringement or an order to issue a patent.

## Holdings

#### I. Product obviousness turns on supported ordinary skill, not exact prior disclosure

**Holding and operative rule.** Under §103(a), the claimed subject matter as a whole is compared with the prior art and the capabilities of a person of ordinary skill when the invention was made; absence of the product's exact nucleotide sequence from the art is not a safe harbor. A known method may establish obviousness with the relevant art when evidence connects a sufficiently identified target, appropriate source material and a predictably effective route to the claimed product, but a useful research objective and a familiar general technique alone do not establish that connection.

**Controlling explanation.** Graham requires consideration of the prior art, differences from the claim, ordinary skill and relevant objective evidence. Bonito Boats preserves nonobviousness as a threshold beyond literal novelty. Section 103 expressly addresses inventions not identically disclosed under §102, so requiring the art to print the precise product would collapse that distinction. Conversely, the availability of cloning does not establish that every product ultimately obtained by it was within ordinary skill. The Office must support the route from the actual art to the actual subject matter; the applicant's success cannot retrospectively supply that evidence. The manner in which an invention was made does not itself defeat patentability. Predictable-method evidence is therefore relevant and may be sufficient with the necessary supporting art, while generalized encouragement to investigate is not enough. Genetic-code redundancy bears on what the art disclosed and what a worker could predict; it does not establish nonobviousness by arithmetic alone. The Court rejects the appellate opinion's categorical separation of familiar methods from product obviousness, without finding that the Office supplied the missing technical proof here.

#### II. The rejection of the specific sequences lacks the necessary evidentiary connection

**Holding and operative rule.** The reversal of the §103 rejection of claims 5 and 7, which recite the particular human placental and bovine uterine cDNA sequences, is affirmed because the agency did not support the connection between Bohlen's incomplete protein disclosure, Maniatis's general screening method and these claimed products. A label of routine cloning and the applicants' failure to disprove unsupported agency assumptions do not discharge the Office's initial burden.

**Controlling explanation.** Bohlen supplies the first nineteen amino acids of brain mitogens, not complete protein sequences or the claimed DNA. Maniatis supplies general library screening rather than the target-specific library selection and probe design needed here. The agency assumed identity between the disclosed proteins and the claimed growth factors and treated tissue and species differences as immaterial without adequate support. A motivation to clone a useful protein's gene leaves those gaps unresolved. Graham's ordinary-skill inquiry permits proven laboratory capabilities to matter; it does not authorize the Court or litigation counsel to invent the missing factual bridge. Even under the correct noncategorical standard, the grounds actually given fail. No findings of commercial success, unexpected results or others' failed research are supplied to create a different basis for decision.

#### III. The encoding genera require their own analysis and fail on the agency's stated grounds

**Holding and operative rule.** The reversal of the §103 rejection of claims 4 and 6, covering DNA encoding the identified 168-amino-acid proteins, is separately affirmed because the agency articulated no independently supported genus ground and left unresolved the same target-identification and recovery assumptions. A genus does not become nonobvious merely because every member was not previously identified: a supported obvious species may matter, and the Court does not declare these genera nonobvious against all possible art.

**Controlling explanation.** The broader claims cannot automatically receive every defense applicable to the two particular sequences. Their actual scope may make a rejection easier to establish, but scope cannot substitute for a supported rejection. The Office supplied no independent explanation showing that a product within these genera would have been obvious on this record. New technical assertions by counsel cannot cure that omission. Markman requires attention to the operative claim boundary while retaining the distinction between construction and factual work; it supplies no DNA-obviousness test and decides no standard for all PTO fact review. Separate examination of the broad claims therefore leads to the same disposition for an independently stated reason, without converting them into narrow sequence claims or declaring the entire genus patentable.

#### IV. Affirmance removes these rejections without ordering allowance

**Holding and operative rule.** The Federal Circuit's judgment reversing the existing §103 rejections of claims 4–7 is affirmed on the grounds and record presented; no patent issuance, new rejection or mandatory reopening to rehabilitate the unsupported grounds is ordered. Independently proper further examination remains available under ordinary notice and procedure, and §101 eligibility, §112 disclosure or enablement, priority and infringement are not decided.

**Controlling explanation.** Correcting an erroneous categorical legal rule may warrant remand when a necessary factual determination remains blocked by it. Here the agency's unsupported premises independently fail for both claim groups, so correcting the appellate overstatement does not restore the rejections. Any different statutory defect needs its own ground, notice and record. The 1995 amendment retained the product rule in §103(a) and added qualified treatment of biotechnological process claims; the claims here are products, and no process-claim election is assumed. The Court does not turn the amendment into a product exception. The judgment respects lawful examination without awarding exclusivity for research effort or deciding a reserved question.

## Precedent Treatment

- Graham v. John Deere Co., 383 U.S. 1: applied for the prior-art, claim-difference, ordinary-skill and relevant objective-evidence inquiry; the Court adds no exact-sequence prerequisite or biotechnology exception.
- Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc., 489 U.S. 141: applied for the distinction between novelty and nonobviousness, including what ordinary skill can deduce.
- Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., April 23, 1996: applied only for preserving actual claim boundaries and the separation of construction from factual work. Its embedded-construction allocation remains intact; the decision supplies no obviousness rule, universal PTO fact-review standard or mandatory construction hearing.

The Federal Circuit's categorical suggestion that familiar methods cannot establish product obviousness without advance exact structural disclosure is rejected. Its reversal of the particular rejections is affirmed on the record-specific grounds stated here. No earlier Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

Section 103(a) governs these product claims without a special DNA safe harbor or presumption against biological inventions. Supported evidence of a predictive route can establish obviousness even without an advance printed sequence; a research objective plus general technique cannot alone carry the Office's burden. The present sequence and genus rejections fail independently on the agency grounds supplied. That result grants no patent and does not foreclose a different, properly supported examination ground with ordinary procedure.

## Event

Babbitt v. Youpee, No. 95-1595. Merits decision, January 21, 1997. Argued December 2, 1996. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, whose September 5, 1995 judgment, 67 F.3d 194, affirmed declaratory and injunctive relief against amended §207 of the Indian Land Consolidation Act. ## Opinion Topology

## Holdings

#### The amended escheat provision still takes the presented succession interests without compensation

**Holding and operative rule:** Amended §207 of the Indian Land Consolidation Act effects an uncompensated taking as applied to these fractional trust-land interests: it eliminates intestate succession and confines devise to existing co-owners of the same parcel, leaving these owners unable to transmit valuable interests to their chosen descendants even though each devise preserves an existing fraction. The prospective low-income trigger and rebuttable presumption, retained lifetime powers, and statutory option for a federally approved tribal code do not cure that operation where no operative substitute code is identified for this estate.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; eight direct rationale votes support this holding and its remedy. Justice Stevens dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Hodel v. Irving, which protects the valuable power to transmit property at death while permitting Congress to address the serious problem of fractionated Indian lands. Penn Central supplies the examination of economic impact, investment-backed expectations, and the character of the governmental action. Low annual income does not establish that these interests lack value: their reported aggregate value is $1,239. Expectations of investment return are not strong, but that consideration does not answer the extraordinary destruction of succession.

Congress made real changes. Subsection (a) reaches an interest of 2 percent or less incapable of earning $100 in any one of the five years from death. Earnings below $100 in any one of the preceding five years create a rebuttable presumption of that future incapacity. Subsection (b) permits devise to another owner of an undivided interest in the same parcel. Subsection (c) permits a Secretary-approved tribal disposition code to take precedence over subsection (a), but prohibits approval of a code that fails to prevent further descent or fractionation of escheatable interests.

Those changes remain insufficient here. Intestacy is wholly barred, the devise class excludes these descendants, and no operative alternative code is identified for this estate. Each devise passes one existing fraction to one descendant; none divides that fraction further. Retained lifetime enjoyment and transfer do not adequately replace succession. Congress's important consolidation objective cannot by itself answer the uncompensated loss. Nearly six years to arrange a lifetime transfer or qualifying devise do not cure the inadequate transmission choices this mechanism preserves. The Court therefore sustains the existing declaratory and injunctive protection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not adopt respondents' proposed categorical extension of Lucas, hold every inheritance restriction a taking, value compensation, prescribe a consolidation plan, or decide a different tribal code. Purchase, compensated acquisition, voluntary consolidation, and other constitutional measures remain available; no particular alternative receives advance approval. The Court decides no independent individualized-notice or due-process claim.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Hodel v. Irving, 481 U.S. 704 (1987):** Applies its protection against the extraordinary uncompensated destruction of succession and resolves the amended statute that Irving reserved. Its recognition of legislative power to address fractionation remains intact; no universal beneficiary-choice right is adopted.
- **Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104 (1978):** Applies its impact, expectations, and governmental-character analysis in the succession setting; no factor becomes conclusive by itself.
- **Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, June 29, 1992:** Leaves its complete-economic-use rule and background-law exception unchanged. The Court does not extend that category to destruction of every separate property attribute.
- **Dolan v. City of Tigard, June 24, 1994:** Leaves its individualized permit-exaction doctrine unchanged; this case presents no development-permit dedication.
- **Andrus v. Allard, 444 U.S. 51 (1979):** Retains its treatment of the particular ban on sale of lawfully possessed artifacts. Retention of some property powers does not displace Irving's analysis of the succession restriction here; the Court makes no broader revision of Allard.
- **Texaco, Inc. v. Short, 454 U.S. 516 (1982):** Does not make the opportunity for advance planning conclusive of the different uncompensated succession burden here. Its treatment of the mineral-interest lapse law is not overruled.

## Law After Decision

Effective January 21, 1997, Irving's succession protection governs the amended mechanism as applied to these interests. The co-owner devise route, income provisions, and tribal-code option do not sustain this uncompensated escheat. Congress and the tribes retain authority to pursue constitutional consolidation measures. The decision adds this application of Irving; it creates no categorical rule governing every inheritance restriction and changes no Lucas or Dolan test.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens dissents. His concurrence in Irving accepted Congress's power to impose consolidation conditions but objected to their abrupt application without a reasonable opportunity to arrange affairs. Texaco v. Short supports allowing an owner time to avoid a legislated loss. Youpee died nearly six years after the October 30, 1984 amendment, which preserved lifetime use and transfer, permitted devise to an existing co-owner, and narrowed the affected interests through its earnings provisions. Stevens regards those changes and that opportunity as sufficient to distinguish Irving.

The difficulty of transmitting these interests to family members is substantial, but Stevens would not turn ordinary beneficiary choice into a right to continue existing fractionation indefinitely. The fact that these particular devises do not create additional fractions does not foreclose legislation directed at reducing the existing problem. He would reverse and remand on the presented takings ground, without ordering a new probate distribution or deciding an individualized notice claim. He does not find that Youpee actually knew of the statute, culpably abandoned the interests, or held property of no value. He also rejects extending Lucas to every restriction of a separate property attribute.

The Ninth Circuit's affirmance and the underlying declaratory and injunctive protection stand. No further Supreme Court proceeding, compensation valuation, or court-designed consolidation process is directed.

#### I. The optional program does not establish the claimed parental-liberty infringement

**Holding and operative rule:** The described school condom-availability program does not violate parental liberty or familial privacy merely because parents cannot require the school to exclude their children or notify them of participation. Program entry is optional, abstention carries no educational or disciplinary consequence, and the program does not prohibit parental teaching, alter custody, compel treatment or sexual conduct, or require assent to a message; the junior-high counseling condition applies only after a student elects to request condoms.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Meyer and Pierce, which protect parental direction of upbringing and education, together with Yoder's protection against the particular compulsory educational interference with religious family life and Prince's recognition that parental authority does not exclude every public role concerning children. Those decisions do not establish the particular school-enforced veto sought here.

The school provides the item directly, not merely information about it. That difference matters, but it does not itself displace parental authority. Parents remain free to teach against participation and instruct their children to abstain. Students may comply without losing instruction, grades, benefits, or standing at school. Compulsory school attendance does not turn every separable optional resource into compelled participation.

A junior-high student requesting condoms must receive counseling before receipt. The Court does not erase that condition or approve unknown counseling content. Nothing in the stated condition requires entry into the program, a prescribed affirmation, sexual activity, or a medical procedure. No independent coercive-counseling claim is established here.

A. St. P. C. protects against a complete, potentially indefinite parental-contact cutoff; Baby Richard protects against serious avoidable harm in the manner of compulsory custody transfer; M.L.B. protects necessary-record access in a provided first appeal from permanent parental termination. None of those deprivations occurs here. Practical difficulty in securing a child's adherence to parental teaching does not, on these facts, establish a constitutional right to compel the school to enforce the requested refusal.

**Limits:** This holding does not decide confidential medical treatment, prescription drugs, compelled sex education, distribution to a child unable to consent, or a program using penalty or deception to undermine parental authority. It neither denies parental liberty nor recognizes an independent constitutional entitlement to receive condoms.

#### II. The stated program imposes no established constitutional burden on religious exercise

**Holding and operative rule:** A program that students may decline without sacrificing ordinary education or another benefit, and that requires neither religiously prohibited participation nor assent, does not burden these families' free exercise merely by making the described condoms available contrary to their religious teaching. The stated program establishes no religious targeting, unequal treatment, penalty for religious refusal, or comparable official pressure to participate.

**Controlling explanation:** Lyng and Bowen v. Roy distinguish sincere conflict with government activity from interference with protected religious exercise; they do not place all government services beyond constitutional review. Here the school demands no endorsement of contraception or premarital sex, no use of condoms, and no surrender of worship or religious instruction. The junior-high receipt condition does not compel anyone to enter the program and supplies no established contrary religious affirmation.

The parents' concerns about peer influence and compulsory attendance deserve consideration. Lee's treatment of substantial official pressure at school-directed graduation prayer remains intact. Students here may avoid the separable resource without giving up the educational occasion or apparently joining a state-directed religious exercise. The asserted generalized peer influence does not establish comparable official pressure under these program terms. The holding therefore does not depend on a universal rule that only formal punishment can coerce.

The claim fails at its constitutional burden threshold. The Court need not decide whether Smith would independently defeat a claim involving an actual burden under a neutral law. Invoking parental liberty and free exercise together supplies neither the missing infringement nor automatic strict scrutiny. Lukumi's religious-targeting protections and Settle's prohibition on supported religious exclusion remain fully applicable to materially different facts.

**Limits:** The Court does not approve religious targeting, compelled participation in sexual instruction, denial of an exemption from a mandatory practice, or benefits conditioned on conduct forbidden by faith. The separate statutory inquiry under RFRA is not decided.

No injunction, new evidentiary proceeding, or prescribed parental procedure follows.

**Controlling explanation:** An exemption from compelled conduct differs from a parental right to bar a child's voluntary access. The child can already decline this program without consequence; the claimed school-enforced veto requires a substantive constitutional source that the presented parental and religious claims do not supply. Casey permitted a qualified parental-consent arrangement for abortion while preserving an independent judicial route; permission for such a statute does not command parental control over every preventive item.

Under the adopted no-infringement grounds, disputed policy wisdom and comparative health effects do not create a material constitutional fact requiring trial. **Limits:** The Court does not prohibit voluntary parental accommodation, resolve different consent or disclosure laws, or decide an independent RFRA claim. **Common authority:** Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. All nine directly adopt each holding and its explanation; no synthesis of separate opinions is required.

#### Assent to this packaged-software transaction

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Wisconsin UCC §2-204, a seller may invite acceptance by use after review when the buyer has reasonable advance notice that terms govern, receives the full terms before meaningful use, has a practical opportunity to reject the transaction and obtain a refund, and objectively manifests assent by continuing to use the product. Those conditions establish assent to this noncommercial-use covenant; payment alone did not complete a different bargain that the seller later amended unilaterally.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this agreement arose through the transaction the parties actually made. The package announced restrictions; the manual and program supplied them; operation required an indication of assent; and return for a refund offered a practical means of rejection. Zeidenberg nevertheless used the consumer product to operate a commercial service and made two later purchases. Section 2-204 permits conduct to establish agreement. It does not require every term on the outside of the box, but neither does it permit a seller to insert new obligations after an already completed agreement.

The purchaser's contrary argument treats payment as the necessary final act of assent in every retail transaction. That does not account for this communicated offer and its review-and-return sequence. Lack of individual negotiation also does not itself resolve enforceability. Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute, 499 U.S. 585 (1991), rejects that automatic objection in its setting but does not decide contested notice or Wisconsin formation here. American Airlines v. Wolens, January 18, 1995, permits enforcement of the actual voluntary bargain under its own preemption clause; it requires identifying the undertaking rather than manufacturing one.

Acceptance of goods under §2-606 is distinct from assent to contract terms. This single-form transaction does not decide §2-207's operation when parties exchange materially different forms. Nor does the Court decide terms first disclosed after irrevocable use, terms without a practical rejection route, or website terms lacking objective assent. Unconscionability, fraud, illegality and other generally applicable defenses remain available when genuinely preserved. No developed defense to this covenant requires additional proceedings merely to search for one.

**Precedent treatment:** Carnival Cruise is applied only against an automatic nonnegotiation objection, leaving its distinct maritime setting and the actual-notice inquiry intact. Wolens supplies the actual-undertaking analogy, not a Wisconsin rule of formation or immunity for every license.

#### Copyright subject matter and the distinct personal promise

**Holding and operative rule:** A fixed factual compilation can fall within §301's copyright subject-matter inquiry even when its facts or particular compilation lack copyright protection; absence of protection alone therefore does not end preemption analysis. Section 301 nevertheless does not preempt enforcement of this genuine, party-specific noncommercial-use promise, because the accepted undertaking changes the right asserted and binds only the parties, rather than imposing an equivalent exclusive right under §106 against persons who made no promise.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that subject matter and equivalence are separate questions. The database's assumed lack of copyright does not remove the compilation from the first inquiry. On the second, this action requires an undertaking governing purchased access, not copying alone. Zeidenberg accepted the consumer transaction on the condition that he would not use it commercially. Enforcing that exchange does not give ProCD ownership of the facts or a claim against an independent acquirer who never assented.

The objection that mass contracts could recreate copyright in unprotected information requires attention to the right actually enforced. A contract label or an additional pleading allegation does not invariably avoid §301. Here the real promise changes the parties' legal relationship; it is not merely a relabeled claim to control copying by the public. Section 301(b)(3) preserves nonequivalent rights, not every right described as contractual. Wolens, January 18, 1995, and Cipollone v. Liggett Group, 505 U.S. 504 (1992), supply clause-specific analogies distinguishing voluntarily assumed obligations from externally prescribed duties. Neither substitutes for §301's enacted equivalence inquiry.

Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service, 499 U.S. 340 (1991), denies copyright in facts based merely on collection effort; that limit remains intact. The assumed database premise does not adjudicate the copyrightability of every compilation element or enlarge the distinct program copyright. Nor does a holding under §301 answer whether an independent federal rule forbids a particular covenant. Nominal promises that do not qualitatively change the asserted right, first sale, reverse engineering, fair use, misuse, public policy, competition law and compulsory-license questions are not decided for materially different transactions. This judgment gives no right against nonassenting persons.

**Precedent treatment:** Feist's originality and factual-public-domain limits remain unchanged. Wolens and Cipollone's warranty component are extended by analogy only after independently construing §301; their differently worded preemption clauses create no categorical contract exemption.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute, 499 U.S. 585 (1991):** Its treatment of standard terms supports rejecting invalidity solely from lack of negotiation. It does not replace the notice, rejection and assent inquiry under Wisconsin law.
- **American Airlines v. Wolens, January 18, 1995:** The actual-undertaking distinction is applied by analogy; the airline clause and its limits retain their separate force. Ordinary law must first establish the promise, and courts may not enlarge it through external policy.
- **Cipollone v. Liggett Group, 505 U.S. 504 (1992):** The voluntary-warranty component supplies an analogy about the source of an obligation, not an exemption for all common-law claims or all contracts. Its claim-specific authority and cigarette-statute boundaries remain unchanged.

The Court rejects both a rule that payment necessarily ends formation in every such transaction and a rule validating every enclosed term. ## Event

Bibles v. Oregon Natural Desert Association, No. 96-713; October Term 1996. Reasoned summary merits disposition, February 18, 1997. On petition for a writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 83 F.3d 1168. ## Judgment & Remedy

The advocacy-access rationale may not enter the balance, but any properly supported disclosure value concerning the Bureau's own conduct and the actual privacy interests remain for assessment. The Court orders neither release nor final withholding and issues no stay. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the legal correction and remand but would reverse rather than vacate. ## Opinion Topology

## Holdings

#### I. Advocacy access is not the relevant disclosure interest

**Holding and operative rule:** In the ordinary third-party inquiry under FOIA Exemption 6, obtaining an agency mailing list to send recipients competing information is not itself a public interest that weighs in favor of disclosure. The relevant interest is the demonstrated contribution the requested information makes to public understanding of the agency's own conduct, including any genuine information that its selection of recipients reveals.

**Authority:** The Court's unsigned opinion adopts this rule and the explanation below. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the rule and explanation; his disagreement concerns the formal disposition stated separately. The institutional opinion supplies controlling authority without a published roll call.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, which limits the ordinary third-party public-interest inquiry to understanding government activity, and Department of Defense v. FLRA, which retains that inquiry when applying Exemption 6. A useful private project does not become information about government merely because a government list would help accomplish it. The Ninth Circuit recognized requester neutrality but then treated access to the Bureau's audience for competing communications as a substantial public benefit. That reasoning makes the asserted future use do work the statute assigns to the information disclosed.

The association's stronger argument is that the recipients' identities may reveal whom the Bureau chooses to inform. That is a different claim. Its value depends on what the list actually adds to public knowledge of the Bureau; neither its official source nor the desire to correct the Bureau's message establishes that value automatically. The Court therefore rejects the outreach rationale while leaving a supported agency-transparency showing available on remand. The Court does not reopen Reporters Committee's retained statutory rule or declare every possible use of a mailing list irrelevant.

**Precedent treatment:** Reporters Committee is applied at its ordinary third-party scope, without substituting its Exemption 7(C) threshold. FLRA is applied for requester neutrality and incremental agency transparency, without transferring its separate Privacy Act prohibition to this case.

#### II. Correcting the public-interest premise does not complete the privacy balance

**Holding and operative rule:** On remand the government must establish that disclosure would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy under Exemption 6, balancing the actual privacy interests against any demonstrated incremental agency-transparency value. Subscription to the official newsletter alone does not establish consent to unrestricted dissemination; neither final withholding nor final disclosure follows from rejecting the outreach interest, and Exemption 6 permits withholding when satisfied rather than commanding secrecy.

**Authority:** The Court's unsigned opinion adopts this remand rule and explanation. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the rule and explanation and agrees that the lower court must conduct the actual balance, while preferring reversal to vacatur of its judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Department of State v. Ray, as preserved in FLRA, requires attention to concrete privacy and the actual addition to public understanding. Receiving official mail does not by itself surrender control over dissemination of a name and address to all requesters. Nor can the intended recipient's assumed willingness to receive this association's material establish willingness to receive contacts from everyone who could obtain the same information. These points preserve a real inquiry; they do not fix the weight of the privacy interest here.

The government retains its statutory burden. Exemption 6 covers personnel, medical and similar files and requires a clearly unwarranted invasion; the different law-enforcement standard in Exemption 7(C) does not govern. FLRA's conclusion that its particular home-address requests failed to require disclosure was record-specific. Its resulting Privacy Act prohibition arose from a separate statute and invoked exception, neither of which is adjudicated here. Only private individuals' information remains disputed because the organizational information has been released. The lower courts must reconsider that live dispute without the unlawful outreach interest. The Court neither creates a categorical mailing-list exemption nor orders release or nondisclosure.

**Precedent treatment:** Ray remains a concrete-balance authority, and FLRA remains a record-specific application rather than categorical home-address secrecy. No earlier Supreme Court decision is overruled.

## Event

California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Construction, N.A., Inc., No. 95-789; October Term 1996. Argued November 5, 1996; decided February 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 57 F.3d 712. The question is whether ERISA §514(a) preempts the application of California's public-works wage rule permitting lower apprentice wages only for apprentices enrolled in state-approved programs. The District Court granted summary judgment to the state defendants; the Ninth Circuit reversed on ERISA grounds, leaving independent NLRA and contingent damages issues unresolved.

## Participation

All nine sitting Justices participated at argument and decision: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. No nonparticipation is established.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's ERISA-preemption judgment and remands. Justice Souter delivers the opinion of the Court.

## Judgment & Remedy

Reversed and remanded, 9-0. Supporting: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The ERISA ground for preventing this prevailing-wage application is removed. The mandate does not direct final judgment on every claim: the independent NLRA theory, its contingent §1983 damages consequence, genuinely remaining fee issues, nonpreemption defenses and wage calculation retain their proper lower-court postures. The particular §1988 fee denial based on an asserted ERISA right not pursued under §1983 is not automatically reversed, and the previously unpreserved contractual-estoppel ground is not revived.

## Opinion Topology

Justice Souter writes the opinion of the Court, Parts I-III, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The complete opinion uses the concrete plan-command and economic-effects inquiry stated in Holdings. No separate opinion is filed.

## Holdings

**Common authority:** Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, Parts I-III, is joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Each proposition below has nine direct rationale joins; no narrower-opinion calculation is needed.

#### I. The wage criterion does not refer to ERISA plans

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §514(a) does not preempt the challenged public-works wage application as a reference to ERISA plans: the lower apprentice rate turns on state approval of an apprenticeship program, not on its ERISA status or the benefit content of a covered plan. The Court accepts this program's covered status for purposes of deciding preemption without holding that every apprenticeship or training arrangement is an ERISA plan.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers distinguishes state laws whose legal operation identifies covered plans or uses their benefits as a benchmark from laws operating independently of that status. Board of Trade supplies the contrasting case: its replacement-coverage obligation used a covered plan's benefit content as the mandatory measure. California's criterion concerns approval of apprenticeship programs as such, and programs subject to that criterion need not all be ERISA plans. Its operation therefore does not depend on the existence of a covered plan.

The contrary argument begins with this program's covered status and the fact that approval conditions affect its operation. But a law does not acquire an ERISA-specific reference merely because a covered plan encounters it. That would replace the statutory inquiry with the presence of an ERISA litigant. The particular program may be assumed covered without making that assumption the legal measure of the State's wage rule. The Court does not decide every requirement in California's apprenticeship system or the classification of other training arrangements.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers is applied for independently operating state cost rules. Board of Trade is distinguished because its mandatory covered-plan benchmark is absent; its holding remains intact.

#### II. The wage choice does not establish a preempted plan command

**Holding and operative rule:** On this record, §514(a) does not preempt California's requirement that contractors pay prevailing journeyman wages when they do not use apprentices from an approved program. The approval-linked wage incentive neither dictates covered-plan benefits, funding, structure, administration or remedies nor is shown to have economic effects so acute that the retained wage choice becomes an effective plan command; a direct command or demonstrated coercive application remains outside this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Travelers requires examination of a law's actual operation. Its hospital-payment holdings preserve ordinary economic influence while reserving effects that operate as substantive coverage commands or effectively restrict choice. A contractor here may retain an unapproved program and pay the prevailing journeyman wage. The State's incentive favors approved training, but no supported finding shows that the higher wage eliminates that alternative or requires a change in a covered plan's internal terms. Later approval of the program was not retroactive and does not erase the dispute over the earlier wages.

Dillingham's strongest contention is that the wage differential pressures programs to conform to substantive state training standards. Influence is real, but it does not by itself establish the compulsion Travelers requires. Treating every substantial cost consequence as preemption would discard that decision's distinction. Traditional state responsibility for wages and apprenticeship informs the statutory setting; it does not immunize every public-works measure or establish a percentage safe harbor. The Court applies the concrete command-and-effects inquiry, not a formula under which any verbal relationship to a covered plan suffices. It neither creates a competing ERISA enforcement remedy nor sustains regulation of every plan merely because the State acts through a contractor.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers's indirect-effects rule and acute-effects reservation are applied without change. Board of Trade remains controlling when plan benefits supply the mandatory legal benchmark, which this wage application does not use.

#### III. Reversal of the ERISA ground leaves independent issues for remand

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's ERISA-preemption judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with the absence of §514(a) preemption of this wage application. The mandate removes that federal obstacle to enforcement without directing final judgment on the independently unresolved NLRA theory, contingent §1983 damages, any properly remaining fee issue, nonpreemption defenses or the underlying wage calculation.

**Controlling explanation:** The District Court granted judgment to the state defendants on the ERISA and NLRA theories. The Ninth Circuit reversed on ERISA, rejected the Fitzgerald Act saving theory, and did not decide the independent NLRA issue or its contingent damages consequence. Removing the ERISA ground therefore does not supply a decision on those unreviewed questions. The separate rejection of the particular §1988 fee request based on an asserted ERISA right not sought to be enforced under §1983 is not automatically reversed, and the State's contractual-estoppel contention, declined below because it was first raised on appeal, is not revived.

The contrary remedial path would reinstate final judgment on every claim merely because the sole appellate preemption ground fails. That would decide more than the granted ERISA question and eliminate issues the Court of Appeals has not reached. Conversely, remand does not authorize a new challenge to the settled absence of §514(a) preemption on these facts. Because that provision does not reach this application, the Court need not decide §514(d) or whether the Fitzgerald Act would save an otherwise preempted rule. Nor does the State obtain blanket immunity merely from public ownership of the project.

**Precedent treatment:** Travelers governs the ERISA ground at its actual scope. Neither Boston Harbor nor Chamber of Commerce v. Reich supplies an automatic public-contract immunity or decides the independent NLRA and damages issues left for remand.

## Precedent Treatment

- New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co., April 26, 1995: applied to the concrete difference between ordinary economic influence and a direct or effective plan command. Its acute-effects reservation and unresolved direct-self-funded questions remain intact; no numerical threshold or general-applicability immunity is added.
- District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, December 14, 1992: distinguished because California's wage criterion does not use covered-plan benefit content as a mandatory measure. The covered-plan benchmark holding remains binding.
- Building & Construction Trades Council v. Associated Builders & Contractors (Boston Harbor), March 8, 1993: its protection of the lawful proprietary labor agreement supplies no blanket ERISA immunity for every public-works condition. The Court does not resolve the independent NLRA claim by applying a procurement label.
- Chamber of Commerce v. Reich, January 14, 1997: its particular limit on federal procurement authority does not decide this ERISA application or the separate NLRA claim and associated remedies. Its refusal to make a procurement label dispositive remains undisturbed.

## Law After Decision

California may apply the reviewed wage consequence without the ERISA bar imposed below, subject to independently unresolved issues and ordinary enforcement requirements. State approval of apprenticeship programs as such is not a reference to ERISA status, and this record's wage incentive is not an actual or effective plan command. Direct plan regulation, an ERISA-specific legal benchmark, competing enforcement remedies and demonstrated acute compulsion remain outside the holding. No categorical immunity for wage laws, apprenticeship laws, public purchasing or indirect economic effects is adopted.

## Separate Writings

No separate writing is filed. The Court's opinion does not revive an unlimited reading under which every relationship to a covered plan establishes preemption.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit for the independent issues it did not resolve and proceedings consistent with the ERISA holding. No final wage amount, damages award, fee entitlement or new deadline is ordered. The Court need not reach §514(d) or the Fitzgerald Act saving theory because §514(a) does not preempt this application. The program's later approval remains nonretroactive.

## Holdings

#### I. The assessed purchaser may bring this challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** A purchaser directly liable for a tax has standing to challenge an exemption that allegedly burdens its interstate purchases. The adverse assessment supplies the purchaser's own economic injury, caused by the challenged tax rule and redressable through lawful relief from that assessment, whether or not payment has been established.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** General Motors contests a charge imposed on its own purchases. Its injury does not become another seller's claim merely because the tax also affects competing suppliers. Bacchus recognizes a taxed purchaser's ability to challenge a discriminatory exemption; Fulton likewise examines the taxpayer's own liability although the distinction concerns another market participant. Relief from an unlawful assessment would remove the asserted burden. Ohio's seller-only objection therefore fails. Standing establishes access to review, not the truth of the claimed comparison between the sellers. The Court does not find an assessment amount or prior payment and orders no refund on the strength of standing alone.

**Precedent treatment:** Bacchus Imports, Ltd. v. Dias supplies purchaser standing; Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner confirms that the claimant may contest its own tax burden without being the disfavored seller. Their merits and remedial boundaries remain.

#### II. This record does not establish the asserted discrimination

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio's exemption for sales by regulated local distribution companies does not violate the Commerce Clause on this record, because their compulsory rate, reliability and service obligations materially distinguish their regulated supply business from independent marketers' commodity sales. Commodity overlap alone does not establish equivalence of those offerings where the record does not establish a distinct industrial-sales category separated from the obligations relied upon; the holding confers no general immunity on utilities or all of their competitive transactions.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Stone-Zsela and Stevens do not join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court must examine the practical competitive relationship, rather than accept a utility label or the fact that local marketers also pay the tax. West Lynn requires examination of an integrated fiscal preference; Carbone requires attention to the actual service market. Here the favored sellers must maintain regulated supply and meet reliability and human-needs obligations rather than select only profitable customers. Those duties materially affect their offering. General Motors bought virtually all its gas from interstate marketers and often obtained local delivery, but the record gives little detail about the industrial market and does not establish the asserted unregulated negotiated LDC sales during the assessment period. The common gas commodity does not by itself remove the established service differences.

The objection that duties in a captive market cannot justify preference in a separate competitive market would require considering such a supported separation. This record does not establish it. The Court neither finds that every industrial sale funds a particular public duty nor presumes that any alternative would endanger service. The 1996 enactment cannot prove the character of 1986–1990 purchases. Oregon Waste and Fulton continue to require demanding justification and actual tax equivalence once discrimination is established; no compensatory defense is adopted here. A materially different proved sales category remains for its own case. No independent incidental-burden balancing claim or congressional-authorization theory is decided.

**Precedent treatment:** West Lynn's integrated-operation rule and Carbone's practical service-market inquiry apply without categorical utility immunity. Oregon Waste's discrimination-and-justification rule and Fulton's cumulative compensatory conditions remain unchanged; the Court does not reach justification after rejecting the asserted comparison.

#### III. The economic classification has a rational basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Ohio may distinguish regulated local distribution companies from independent natural-gas marketers under ordinary economic equal-protection review because the companies' actual rate and service duties rationally relate to the tax classification. Competitive overlap does not eliminate that legitimate distinction, and this rational-basis holding does not reduce the independent Commerce Clause requirements.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Stone-Zsela and Stevens reserve this question on their proposed Commerce Clause dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** Beach permits an ordinary economic classification when a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis supports it, without requiring perfect fit or formal legislative findings. Ohio's rate, reliability and service obligations supply such a basis here. An industrial buyer's opportunity to purchase from either class does not make it irrational to distinguish the regulated service business from independent marketers. The Court does not equate rationality with proof that utility taxes exactly offset the challenged charge. Nor does the Court attribute this analysis to the Ohio court, which treated equal protection as submerged in its Commerce Clause discussion. The ordinary economic challenge fails on this separate ground; independent constitutional prohibitions retain their force.

**Precedent treatment:** FCC v. Beach Communications supplies the ordinary economic-classification rule and its limits; it does not displace Commerce Clause scrutiny.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bacchus Imports, Ltd. v. Dias: applied to the assessed purchaser's own standing; local-product tax preferences remain subject to its substantive rule.
- West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy: its examination of integrated fiscal operation remains controlling; the present record differs in the established obligations of the compared service businesses.
- C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown: its practical service-market comparison is applied; neither a nominal utility classification nor equal taxation of local outsiders is sufficient by itself.
- Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality: the discrimination trigger and demanding justification survive unchanged; no new public-service exception is created.
- Fulton Corp. v. Faulkner: preserves all three cumulative compensatory-tax requirements and lawful fact-dependent relief; no exact compensation or payment is found here.

The economic classification independently survives equal protection. A taxpayer may challenge its own assessment even when the alleged competitive discrimination affects suppliers. His comparison focuses on the transaction bought by a noncaptive industrial customer: LDCs and interstate marketers can compete to supply that commodity even though LDCs also serve captive customers. West Lynn's examination of the actual fiscal preference and Oregon Waste's rule against unjustified discrimination require a connection between the favored transaction and the service burden said to justify it. ## Holdings

#### I. A spouse's contingent death-benefit claim does not trigger predeath forfeiture

**Holding and operative rule:** An injured worker's spouse is not a person entitled to LHWCA death-benefit compensation before the covered death and qualifying survivorship that create that statutory right. A spouse's predeath third-party release therefore does not trigger §33(g)'s employer-approval forfeiture of the later death-benefit claim merely because the release covers a possible future wrongful-death action.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Cowart makes an existing substantive right, rather than payment or a formal award, the entitlement threshold. Mrs. Yates had no vested §9 death-benefit right when the predeath releases were signed. Her husband was alive, and the required covered death and her qualifying survivorship might never occur. Her consortium claim and possible wrongful-death claim are not the distinct federal death-benefit entitlement; her husband's disability entitlement does not become hers. The later death and causation stipulation cannot retroactively supply those missing prerequisites.

Ingalls argues that approval protects its exposure and that the words concerning compensation to which a person would be entitled include potential beneficiaries. That comparison measures the compensation of a person already entitled when settling; it does not erase the threshold identifying that person. The employer's protective policy cannot add a forfeiture the enacted condition does not reach. Cowart's current approval, filing and notification requirements remain distinct: covered settlements below entitlement require prescribed prior written employer-and-carrier approval and thirty-day filing, while judgments and settlements at least equal to the compensation entitlement require notification rather than that prior approval. The Court does not hold that every third-party recovery requires approval or apply later procedural amendments automatically to releases ending in January 1984. It decides no vested disability claim, postdeath settlement, benefit amount, §33(f) offset allocation, contract credit or deference question.

**Precedent treatment:** Estate of Cowart v. Nicklos Drilling Co. is applied at its existing-right threshold; its payment-independent entitlement rule and separate approval/notification branches remain in force.

#### II. The Director may respond in this private-party review

**Holding and operative rule:** The Director of OWCP, as the Secretary of Labor's designated representative, is a proper respondent under the then-effective Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 15(a) in an employer-initiated appeal from a Benefits Review Board order while the private parties maintain a live controversy. This respondent role does not authorize an independent Director appeal based only on a general administrative interest or decide whether the Director could alone continue the controversy after all private adversity ends.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 15(a) requires the agency to be named respondent and includes an agency, board, commission or officer within that term. The Department administers the Act while the Board performs its independent adjudicative work. That internal division does not require the adjudicator itself to advocate or exclude the Department's designated litigating representative from the existing review. The 1996 designation regulation identifies the Director as the Secretary's proper party in §21(c) proceedings. Section 921a provides Secretary-appointed representation in the stated court proceedings but expressly excepts this Court. Those representation provisions identify who acts for the Department; Rule 15 supplies the respondent-status ground.

The Board-only objection confuses the allocation of decisional responsibility within the Department with its representation in appellate review. The Director need not have authored or defend the Board's order to represent the Department's view. Newport News denied independent §921(c) review based on general administrative interest and reserved respondent status. Ingalls initiated this review; it and Mrs. Yates supply the live controversy. The holding neither manufactures aggrievement by regulation nor extends jurisdiction through party nomenclature. It grants no relief outside the live appeal, resolves no special-fund case and adopts no merits-deference rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Director, OWCP v. Newport News Shipbuilding & Dry Dock Co. retains its independent-review limit; the respondent question it reserved is now resolved only for this live private-party review.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Thomas.** Both join Part I's refusal to forfeit the death-benefit claim based on predeath releases. They would reverse only the ruling that the Director is a party respondent. In their reading, Rule 15 names the agency whose order is reviewed; here that entity is the independent Benefits Review Board. The Department's common administrative umbrella does not turn the Director into the Board or make its order his. A command to name the agency whose order is reviewed does not independently confer substantive party capacity on every officer designated to represent the Department. Section 921a and the regulation address representation where capacity exists, and cannot create the missing capacity; section 921a also expressly excepts proceedings in this Court. Rule 1(b)'s prohibition on enlarging or diminishing jurisdiction corroborates the limit on using procedural rules to create an entitlement to participate. Newport News's restriction on independent review and reservation of respondent status do not supply an affirmative respondent right. ## Event

Robinson v. Shell Oil Co., No. 95-1376. Argued November 6, 1996; decided February 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, reviewing its en banc judgment, 70 F.3d 325 (1995), No. 93-1562. The question is whether employees in Title VII §704(a) includes former employees alleging retaliatory postemployment conduct by a former employer. Shell discharged Robinson in 1991; while his EEOC racial-discharge charge was pending, a prospective employer sought a reference. His complaint alleges false information and negative references in retaliation. The claim was dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6) solely because he was no longer Shell's employee. ## Public Action

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. ## Opinion Topology

## Holdings

#### Former employees fall within this retaliation protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Title VII §704(a)'s protection of employees includes former employees alleging postemployment retaliation by their former employer because they engaged in Title VII-protected activity. Former-employee status therefore cannot alone support dismissal of Robinson's claim; inclusion within the protected category does not establish retaliatory conduct, motive, causation, actionable harm or a remedy.

**Authority:** Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Title VII's definition identifies an employment relationship but does not supply one temporal answer for every statutory use. Section 704(a) contains no current-employment qualifier. Its protection for making a charge or participating in enforcement must be read with the discharge and remedial provisions: discharge complaints ordinarily continue after employment ends, and §706(g)'s reinstatement of employees confirms that the noun can include people no longer employed. References and blacklisting can punish that continued resort to enforcement. This enacted context resolves the temporal meaning; desirable policy does not override an unambiguous exclusion.

The express applicants category does not require the opposite result. Applicants need never have had an employment relationship, whereas former employees did; the separate category does not transform the latter into strangers. Nor does a uniform statutory definition require one time of reference. Walters counts existing relationships on each specified working day under §701(b); separated workers remain excluded from subsequent daily counts. Section 704(a) contains no corresponding daily-count requirement. Darden's worker-status inquiry and Town & Country's compatible-service analysis likewise require attention to each statute's own work, rather than a universal temporal definition.

The complaint alleges a false, unfavorable reference after Robinson's EEOC charge. Those allegations have not been adjudicated. The Court reverses the categorical coverage dismissal and remands for unresolved conduct, prohibited reason, causation, legally required harm, defenses and relief. It defines no general actionable-harm standard, new employment-nexus element, universal postemployment tort or deference rule. The separate discriminatory-discharge merits are not reopened.

**Precedent treatment:** Walters's genuine-relationship daily count is preserved at its §701(b) scope; Darden and Town & Country supply attention to legal relationship and statutory context without deciding the temporal reach of §704(a).

## Precedent Treatment

- Walters v. Metropolitan Educational Enterprises, Inc.: distinguished by the §701(b) daily time units; its exclusion of separated workers from later daily employer-size counts remains intact and its reservation of retaliation liability is respected.
- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden: preserves its ERISA common-law agency status inquiry; no universal rule terminating statutory protections at separation is inferred.
- NLRB v. Town & Country Electric, Inc.: its attention to actual compatible employment and each statute's exclusions supports context-sensitive interpretation; its NLRA holding does not itself decide Title VII temporal coverage.

## Law After Decision

Former employees are within §704(a)'s retaliation protection at the scope stated. Employment separation does not itself defeat that coverage; it still ends later daily relationship counts under Walters. Coverage remains distinct from proving a prohibited retaliatory act and obtaining relief. No general standard of actionable postemployment harm, causation rule, liability finding or new remedy is adopted.
