QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Does the Kansas Sexually Violent Predator Act, 
Kan. Stat. Ann. § 59-29a01 et seq. - which provides for the 
civil commitment and long-term care and treatment of 
sex offenders who are found beyond a reasonable doubt 
both (1) to be suffering from a mental abnormality or 
personality disorder and (2) to present a continuing dan- 
ger to society through a likelihood of committing future 
sexually violent offenses — violate substantive due pro- 
cess principles? 


2. What level of constitutional scrutiny applies to a 
claim that civil commitment of a sexually violent predator 
for care and treatment deprives him of a liberty interest 
in violation of substantive due process principles?

This Court’s jurisdiction to review the final judgment

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Procedural History 


Petitioner, the State of Kansas, initiated this action 
against Respondent, Leroy Hendricks, on August 17, 
1994. J.A. 4. The State sought to have Hendricks, a con- 
victed child molester, civilly committed to a state mental 
institution for long-term care and treatment under the 
Kansas Sexually Violent Predator Act, Kan. Stat. Ann. 
59-29a01 et seq. The trial court rejected Respondent's con- 
stitutional challenges to the Kansas statute, J.A. 120-24, 
and, following a trial, the jury returned a verdict finding, 
beyond a reasonable doubt, that Hendricks is a sexually 
violent predator. Id. at 357, 360. The trial court ordered 
Hendricks committed to Larned State Hospital for care 
and treatment. Id. at 361. 


On March 1, 1996, the Kansas Supreme Court 
reversed, concluding that the Kansas statute violates sub- 
stantive due process principles. Pet. App. 1a-59a. The 
State requested that the Kansas Supreme Court stay its 
mandate pending the State’s efforts to secure review of 
that court’s decision in this Court. On March 22, 1996, the 
Kansas Supreme Court directed that its mandate be 
stayed for 30 days to permit the State to seek certiorari 
review in this Court. Pet. App. 60a. On April 22, 1996, 
this Court stayed the Kansas Supreme Court's decision 
pending the outcome of the proceedings in this Court. On 
June 17, 1996, this Court granted review of the State’s 
petition in No. 95-1649, and Hendricks’ conditional cross- 
petition in No. 95-9075, and consolidated the cases for 
oral argument.

Statement of Facts 
1. The Kansas Sexually Violent Predator Act 


The Kansas Sexually Violent Predator Act (“the 
Act”), Kan. Stat. Ann. § 59-29a01 et seq., Pet. App. 
61a-72a, was enacted in 1994 to address the problem of 
recidivist sexual offenders.! The Act grew out of the 
efforts of an Ad Hoc Sexual Offender Task Force and 
followed the highly publicized, brutal rape and murder 
of Stephanie Schmidt, a Kansas college student, by a 
convicted rapist recently released from prison. See Pet. 
App. 25a. The sponsor of the Kansas bill (S.B. 525), Sena- 
tor Vancrum, declared that the Act was designed “to 
protect our communities from those offenders who pose 
the greatest danger to society by providing for treatment 
and commitment of sexually violent predators until they 
are no longer a danger.” J.A. 460. He further stated that 
“(t]he bill is narrowly tailored to focus on the smaller 
number of habitual sex offenders who, because of their 
psychological makeup, pose an immediate danger to the 
public as soon as they are released from prison.” Id. at 
461; see also id. at 545-47. Several law enforcement officials 
also testified to the high recidivist rates for sex offenders. 
See id. at 475, 490, 492-96.2

The version of the Act in effect at the time of 
Hendricks’ proceeding provided that 60 days before the 
anticipated release of a “sexually violent predator,” the 
agency with authority over the release must notify the 
prosecutor in the county where the person was charged. 
§ 59-29a03. (The current version requires that the Attor- 
ney General be notified). The prosecutor then has 45 days 
to file a petition invoking the Act’s procedures. 
§ 59-29a04. (Under the current version, all sex predator 
proceedings are handled by the Attorney General's office 
rather than by the county attorneys and the time periods 
are somewhat different). The next step is for the district 
court to determine whether there is probable cause to 
believe that the person is a sexually violent predator and, 
if so, to order the person transferred to an appropriate 
facility for an evaluation. § 59-29a05. 


Promptly after the filing of the petition, the district 
court must conduct a trial to determine whether the 
person is a sexually violent predator. § 59-29a06. In the 
trial, the person is entitled to the assistance of counsel 
and the provision of a mental health professional to eval- 
uate the person at public expense if necessary. Id. If the 
person makes a jury demand, then the trial is before a 
jury. Id. The trier of fact must determine whether the 
person is a sexually violent predator beyond a reasonable 
doubt. § 59-29a07. If that determination is made, then the 
person is committed to the custody of the secretary of 
social and rehabilitation services for “control, care and 
treatment until such time as the person’s mental abnor- 
mality or personality disorder has so changed that the 
person is safe to be at large.” § 59-29a07(a). 


The Act further provides that the mental condition of 
a person committed under the Act must be reevaluated 
on an annual basis, including district court review and a 
determination whether there is probable cause to believe 


7 


that it is safe to release the person. § 59-29a08. If the court 
concludes that there is probable cause to believe that the 
person may safely be released, then the burden of proof is 
on the State to demonstrate beyond a reasonable doubt 
that the person’s mental abnormality or personality dis- 
order remains such that it is not safe to release the per- 
son. Id. The person also may file a petition seeking release 
at any time, § 59-29a11, or those having custody of the 
person may determine that the person’s condition has so 
changed that release is appropriate and authorize the 
person to petition the court for release. § 59-29a10. The 
procedures and burden of proof in such a proceeding are 
the same as for a hearing on annual review. Id.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Because the Kansas Sexually Violent Predator Act 
neither offends principles of justice so deeply rooted in 
the traditions of our people to rank as fundamental nor 
relies upon a suspect classification, the statute is pre- 
sumptively constitutional and this Court should apply 
reasonableness review to Hendricks’ substantive due pro- 
cess claim. This Court’s decisions involving substantive 
challenges to laws involving mental illness and civil com- 
mitment, like its decisions involving such challenges to 
the substance of state criminal laws, uniformly have 
applied a reasonableness level of review. The Court 
repeatedly has recognized that limited federal review 
accords the proper deference to the States’ traditional 
authority over the substance of their civil commitment 
and criminal justice systems. Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 
71 (1992), can and should be read as endorsing rather 
than altering this longstanding level of constitutional 
review. 

The Kansas Act satisfies the basic substantive due 
process requirements this Court has set forth in past 
cases. These requirements apparently include proof of (1) 
dangerousness and (2) mental illness (neither of which the 
Court has defined in previous cases). First, the Kansas 


19 


Act requires evidence of past conduct and a prediction of 
likelihood to commit such offenses in the future in order 
to establish dangerousness, and there is no serious ques- 
tion that Hendricks’ 40-year criminal record of sexually 
molesting children —- coupled with his own testimony in 
this case — establishes his past, present and future danger- 
ousness to some of the most vulnerable members of soci- 
ety. Second, for substantive due process purposes, the 
requirement of mental illness — a term this Court has never 
defined in its cases — is satisfied by the statutory require- 
ment of proof that the person subject to commitment 
suffers from a “mental abnormality” — such as pedophilia, 
see DSM-IV at 527-28 — that is connected to both past and 
potential future dangerous conduct. To the extent there 
are differences of expert medical opinion regarding the 
diagnosis and classification of mental disorders, the 
Court previously has warned that courts must pay partic- 
ular deference to reasonable legislative judgments. Surely 
it is reasonable for a state legislature to rely on legal 
concepts that incorporate recognized mental disorders, 
such as the pedophilia from which Hendricks suffers. 


Finally, even if the Court were to apply strict scru- 
tiny, the Kansas Act passes constitutional muster because 
it is narrowly tailored to serve the dual compelling inter- 
ests of providing treatment to sexual predators and pro- 
tecting society from such individuals. Certainly, the Act is 
more narrowly tailored than a criminal sentence of life 
imprisonment without parole, which indisputably would 
be a constitutional option for the State to implement in 
the circumstances presented. The State of Kansas reason- 
ably has determined to provide Leroy Hendricks with the 
specialized treatment his condition requires while pro- 
tecting the children of Kansas from the dire consequences 
of his unregulated conduct until such time as his mental 


20 


abnormality or dangerousness abates. The Kansas Sexu- 
ally Violent Predator Act is constitutional.

A. Reasonableness Review Applies To The Substan- 
tive Due Process Question 


Statutes properly enacted by state legislatures are 
presumed constitutional, Heller v. Doe, 509 U.S. 312, 319 
(1993), unless they involve either fundamental rights or 
suspect classes. Id. See also City of Cleburne v. Cleburne 
Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985). “So-called 
‘substantive due process’ prevents the government from 
engaging in conduct that ‘shocks the conscience’ or inter- 
feres with rights ‘implicit in the concept of ordered lib- 
erty.’ ” United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 746 (1987) 
(quoting Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952), and 
Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325-26 (1937)). This 
Court's “primary guide in determining whether the prin- 
ciple in question is fundamental is, of course, historical 
practice.” Montana v. Egelhoff, No. 95-566, slip op. at 5, 116 
S. Ct. 2012, 20___ (1996) (plurality opinion). Hendricks’ 
facial challenge to the Kansas statute, “is, of course, the 
most difficult challenge to mount successfully, since the 
challenger must establish that no set of circumstances 
exists under which the Act would be valid.” Salerno, 481 
U.S. at 745. The fact that an “Act might operate uncon- 
stitutionally under some conceivable set of circumstances 
is insufficient to render it wholly invalid, since we have 
not recognized an ‘overbreadth’ doctrine outside the lim- 
ited context of the First Amendment.” Id. 

This case involves neither a fundamental right nor a 
suspect class and therefore is subject to reasonableness 


21 


review. Hendricks — as the party challenging a properly 
enacted statute —- thus bears the burden of establishing 
that the law is unconstitutional by demonstrating that 
there is no conceivable basis for the Act. Heller, 509 U.S. 
at 320. Importantly, this Court traditionally pays “partic- 
ular deference to reasonable legislative judgments,” Jones 
v. United States, 463 U.S. 354, 365 n. 13 (1983); see also 
Marshall v. United States, 414 U.S. 417, 427 (1974), when 
expert opinions differ on an issue such as what consti- 
tutes “mental illness.”

With Due Process 


1. Due Process At Most Requires Proof Of Danger- 
ousness And A Mental Condition That Reason- 
ably Can Be Characterized As “Mental Illness” 


This Court has held that the Due Process Clause of 
the Fourteenth Amendment “contains a substantive com- 
ponent that bars certain arbitrary, wrongful government 
actions ‘regardless of the fairness of the procedures used 
to implement them.’ ” Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113, 125 
(1990) (quoting Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327, 331 


34 _ 


(198€)). The constitutionality of the Kansas Sexually Vio- 
lent Predator Act thus turns upon the interpretation of a 
line of this Court’s decisions that address substantive due 
process claims in the mental health context. As explained 
below, these decisions apparently establish that the State 
must prove that Hendricks is both (1) dangerous and (2) 
mentally ill. See Foucha, 504 U.S. at 75-76. The decisions 
have not, however, defined the term “mental illness,” and 
certainly have not confined it to any particular clinical 
categories. 

In O'Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563, 576 (1975), 
this Court held that “a State cannot constitutionally con- 
fine without more a nondangerous individual who is 
capable of surviving safely in freedom by himself or with 
the help of willing and responsible family members or 
friends.” The jury in Donaldson specifically found that 
“Donaldson was neither dangerous to himself nor dan- 
gerous to others.” Id. at 573. Donaldson stands for the 
proposition that the mentally ill cannot be involuntarily 
committed absent proof of dangerousness, i.e., they can- 
not be warehoused, for example, simply to get them out 
of the public’s sight. 

In Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979), a pro- 
cedural due process case, the Court held that involuntary 
commitment proceedings require proof that the individ- 
ual satisfies the State’s substantive standards for commit- 
ment by clear and convincing evidence. The Texas law at 
issue required proof of both mental illness and danger- 
ousness. Id. at 426. Although Addington was not a sub- 
stantive due process case, later decisions of this Court 
have treated the Texas statutory elements of mental ill- 
ness and dangerousness as substantive due process 
requirements. For example, Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 
354 (1983), held that a defendant acquitted of a crime by 
reason of insanity could be confined to a mental institu- 
tion until such time as he either regained his sanity or no 
longer presented a danger to himself or society, citing 


35 


Addington for the proposition that “the Due Process 
Clause requires the State in a civil-commitment proceed- 
ing to demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that 
the individual is mentally ill and dangerous.” Id. at 362. 


Foucha, upon which the Kansas Supreme Court prin- 
cipally relied, repeated this formulation, holding that 
“keeping Foucha against his will in a mental institution is 
improper absent a determination in civil commitment 
proceedings of current mental illness and dangerous- 
ness.” 504 U.S. at 78. Like the earlier cases, however, the 
Court had no occasion to define the requisite “mental 
illness” in Foucha because “Louisiana [did] not contend 
that Foucha was mentally ill at the time of the trial court’s 
hearing.” Id. Moreover, the Louisiana statute at issue in 
Foucha, unlike the Kansas statute at issue here, placed the 
burden of proof on Foucha to prove that he was not 
dangerous, and the record contained no affirmative testi- 
mony that Foucha would in fact pose a danger to the 
community, only testimony by mental health profes- 
sionals that they would not state with certainty that he 
posed no danger. Id. at 81-82. 


2. The Kansas Act Satisfies The Dangerousness 
Requirement 


In this case, there is no question that the dangerous- 
ness requirement is satisfied. The Act can be invoked only 
when a person “has been convicted of or charged with a 
sexually violent offense,” Kan. Stat. Ann. § 59-29a02(a), 
and “suffers from a mental abnormality or personality 
disorder which makes the person likely to engage in the 
predatory acts of sexual violence... .” Id. Thus, the 
statute requires proof of more than predisposition or the 
possibility of predisposition; in fact, it requires evidence of 
past sexually violent behavior or conduct and a mental 
condition that creates a likelihood of such conduct in the 
future if the person is not committed and treated. 


36 


Indeed, neither the plurality nor the dissenting opin- 
ion in the Kansas Supreme Court expressed any doubt 
that the Act satisfies the constitutional “dangerousness” 
requirement, nor did Hendricks raise such a contention in 
his response to the State’s petition for a writ of certiorari 
in this Court. Moreover, the Act’s dangerousness element 
is more substantial than the general Kansas civil commit- 
ment statute which requires a showing that the person 
“liJs likely, in the reasonably foreseeable future, to cause 
substantial physical injury or physical abuse to self or 
others or substantial damage to another’s property, as 
evidenced by behavior causing, attempting or threatening 
such injury, abuse or damage.” Kan. Stat. Ann. 
§ 59-2902(g). The Act’s requirement of proof of either 
criminal charges or a conviction, in addition to proof that 
the predator is likely to commit such offenses in the 
future, requires the State to prove considerably more than 
is required under the general civil commitment statute. 
Under the Act, the State had to prove, beyond a reason- 
able doubt, that Hendricks poses a danger to people - 
such as young children — not just a danger to “property.” 
Hendricks has not, and realistically could not, question 
the constitutional adequacy of the Kansas civil commit- 
ment statute in this respect. See, e.g., In re Albright, 17 
Kan. App. 2d 135, 138-43, 836 P.2d 1, 3-6 (1992) (rejecting 
constitutional challenge to § 2902(g)).

In Allen v. Illinois, 478 U.S. 364 (1986), this Court held 
that commitment proceedings under the Illinois Sexually 
Dangerous Persons Act, a sexual psychopath statute, 
were not “criminal” within the meaning of the Fifth 
Amendment's privilege against self-incrimination. In 
reaching that conclusion, the Court recognized that the 
Illinois law’s purpose — like the Kansas law in this case - 
was to provide treatment, not punishment, for persons 
adjudged sexually dangerous. Id. at 370. The Court con- 
cluded that the petitioner had not demonstrated that the 
conditions of confinement were “incompatible with the 
State’s asserted interest in treatment,” id. at 373, and the 
Court observed that committed individuals were treated 
differently than convicted felons. Id. at 373-74.

The Kansas statute satisfies both requirements of the 
strict scrutiny test. First, the States without a doubt have 
compelling interests in treating sexual predators and pro- 
tecting the public from them. No court has suggested 
otherwise, nor can such a suggestion seriously be made. 
Leroy Hendricks alone has been convicted of committing 
sexual offenses against no fewer than ten young children, 
ranging in age from 7 to 14. The impact of his conduct on 
his young and vulnerable victims is difficult to fathom 
and unquestionably significant. 


Second, the Act is narrowly tailored to advance the 
State’s dual interests of treating the individual and pro- 
tecting the community. The Act applies only to persons 
who have committed sexually violent offenses and who 
suffer from an identifiable mental disorder which renders 
them likely to continue committing acts of sexual vio- 
lence. § 59-29a02(a), (b). The Act does not apply to those 
who have unacceptable fantasies; it only applies to per- 
sons who have actually been charged with or convicted of 
sexually violent offenses. § 59-29a02(a). In accordance 
with the goal of treatment, the Act requires that persons 
committed under it be located and receive treatment 
either in a facility outside the state prison system or, if 
housed in correctional facility, in segregation from the 
inmate population. § 59-29a07(a). The Act specifically 
requires regular reviews of each committed individual's

Certainly, the Kansas Sexually Violent Predator Act is 
more narrowly tailored than would be a statute imposing 
life imprisonment on all repeat child molesters, an option 
that is indisputably constitutional. Likewise, it is more 
narrowly tailored than simply expanding the “mental 
illness” definition already contained in the traditional 
Kansas civil commitment statute. Because the latter 
option undoubtably would give rise to substantial consti- 
tutional questions of the type raised in this case, life 
imprisonment without parole for all repeat sex offenders 
may be the only realistic alternative remaining to the 
States if laws such as the Kansas Sexually Violent Preda- 
tor Act violate substantive due process principles. 


The Kansas Sexually Violent Predator Act is constitu- 
tional. It provides for the civil commitment - with exten- 
sive procedural safeguards — of sexually violent predators 
such as Hendricks, who has a medically recognized men- 
tal disorder, who repeatedly has committed sexually vio- 
lent offenses, and who, by his own admission, will 
continue to prey on vulnerable children if released with- 
out treatment. The State of Kansas reasonably has 
decided to provide Hendricks with the specialized psy- 
chiatric treatment he so desperately needs while protect- 
ing the children of Kansas from the shattering 
consequences of his sexual abuse. The Constitution 
should not be interpreted to condemn these efforts.
