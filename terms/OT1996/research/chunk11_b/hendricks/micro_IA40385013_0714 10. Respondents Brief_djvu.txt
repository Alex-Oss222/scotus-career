STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


In 1994 the Kansas Legislature passed the Sexually 
Violent Predator Act (SVP Act). The Act is aimed at 
providing a means to keep sex offenders who are per- 
ceived to be dangerous incarcerated after the expiration 
of their prison terms. Unlike other sex offender com- 
mitment schemes, the SVP Act does not provide a dis- 
positional alternative to traditional prosecution and sen- 
tencing. Instead it provides for the institution of the 
commitment proceeding only after the offender is about to 
be released from incarceration. 


In 1984, Mr. Hendricks entered into a plea agreement 
with the State of Kansas providing that he be sentenced to 
a term of 5 to 20 years incarceration for two counts of 
taking indecent liberties with a child for fondling two 
thirteen year old boys who came into the electronics store 
where he worked. A third count arising out of the same 
incident was dismissed. The State did not request conse- 
Cutive sentences or enhanced sentences.’ The State did 
nut request evaluation or treatment of Mr. Hendricks at 
a state psychiatric facility. Pet. App. 3a.” 

Mr. Hendricks served his sentence without incident. 
He earned all possible good time and was scheduled to be 
released on September 11, 1994. J.A. 5. Upon his re- 
lease, he would have been under supervision requiring 
him to live in a halfway house and receive group and 
individual counseling. J.A. 183, 186.

Decision of the Kansas Supreme Court 


Respondent appealed the commitment. He argued that 
the Act, though labeled “civil” was punitive in purpose 
and effect and therefore violated the prohibition against 
double jeopardy and ex post facto laws. He also argued 
that he had been denied equal protection of the law be- 
cause those committed under the SVP Act were subject 
to more onerous conditions, and greater difficulty of re- 
lease, than persons committed under the State civil com- 
mitment laws. Respondent further asserted that the Act, 
by requiring indefinite incarceration of persons without 
a finding of both dangerousness and mental illness, vio- 
lated substantive due process. Pet. App. 10a. The Kansas 
Supreme Court agreed that the Act violated substantive 
due process." 


’ The State persists in labeling the majority opinion of the 
Kansas Supreme Court a plurality opinion. Justice Lockett, the 
member of the majority who wrote the joining concurrence, states 
very plainly, “In this opinion, I will set out my reasoning for 
joining Justice Allegrucci’s majority opinion.” 912 P.2d at 138, 
Pet. App. 2la. Further, a portion of the paragraph from Justice 
Lockett’s opinion being quoted by the State on page 15 of its brief 
is omitted. That portion left out by the State reads, “. . . I must 
join the majority.” 912 P.2d at 140, Pet. App. 24a. 


The Kansas Supreme Court first determined: 


it is clear that the overriding concern of the legis- 
lature is to continue the segregation of sexually vio- 
lent offenders from the public. Treatment with the 
goal of reintegrating them into society is incidental, 
at best. The record reflects that treatment for sex- 
ually violent predators is all but nonexistent.” The 
legislature concedes that sexually violent predators 
are not amenable to treatment under K.S.A. 59-2901 
et seq. [The act for the care and treatment of men- 
tally ill persons.) If there is nothing to treat under 
K.S.A. 59-2901, then there is no mental illness. /n 
re the Care and Treatment of Hendricks, 259 Kan. 
246, 912 P.2d 129, 136 (1996), Pet. App. 16a. 


The Court explained: 


We must determine if Hendricks is denied substan- 
tive due process based not on his lack of character 
but, rather, on the merits of his challenge. Mental 
illness is defined by K.S.A. 59-2902(h) as meaning 
any person who (1) [is] suffering from a severe 
mental disorder to the extent that such person is in 
need of treatment; (2) lacks capacity to make an 
informed decision concerning treatment; and (3) is 
likely to cause harm to self or others. Here, neither 
the language of the Act nor the State’s evidence sup- 
ports a finding that ‘mental abnormality or person- 
ality disorder’ as used in 59-29a02(a), is a ‘mental 
illness’ as defined in 59-2902(h). Absent such a 
finding, the Act does not satisfy the constitutional 
standard set out in Addington and Foucha. Justice 
White, speaking for the majority of the United States 
Supreme Court in Foucha, clearly stated that to in- 
detinitely confine as dangerous one who has a per- 
sonality disorder or antisocial personality but is not 
mentally ill is constitutionally impermissible. 504 
U.S. at 78. Similarly, to indefinitely confine as dan- 
gerous one who has a mental abnormality is consti- 
tutionally impermissible. 


9 


In addition, the State’s own evidence is that Hen- 
dricks was being committed even though he does not 
suffer from mental illness. Hendricks is not mentally 
ill, and the criminal offenses for which he was im- 
prisoned were not the result of mental illness. There- 
fore, as applied to Hendricks, the constitutionality of 
the Act depends upon a showing of dangerousness 
without a finding of mental illness. Clearly, the due 
process standard of Addington and Foucha, is not 
met by the Act as applied to Hendricks. We conclude 
that the Act violates Hendricks’ substantive due 
process rights. 912 P. 2d at 138, Pet. App. 20a. 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


T . .' > Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment 
has been recognized by this Court to include substantive 
limitations on the states. If this were not true, a state 
government could deprive its citizens of fundamental con- 
stitutional rights for insubstantial reasons, so long as it 
provided a hearing for individual adjudication. But even © 
the most elaborate hearing, with what appear to be abun- 
dant procedural protections, cannot be allowed to mask 
the unconstitutionality of laws and actions that exceed 
the power of government over people. Similarly, the sub- 
stantive component of Due Process jurisprudence can act 
as a check on state government when it purports to be 
acting in a constitutionally permissible fashion but is ac- 
tually attempting surrepitiously to accomplish a purpose 
that is forbidden by direct constitutional prohibitions. 


The State of Kansas wished to continue to incapacitate 
Mr. Hendricks and other persons targeted by the SVP 
Act, even though its authority to do so had expired at 
the end of their criminal sentences, because it feared that 
they might be prospectively dangerous if released. As was 
demonstrated in our earlier brief as Cross Petitioner, Kansas 
lacked the authority to extend the criminal because of the 
combined effect of the ex post facto and Double Jeopardy 
Clauses of the Constitution. Kansas law already provided 


10 


a remedy—its civil commitment statute—that would allow 
it to seek the confinement and treatment of individuals 
upon a showing that they were mentally ill and dangerous 
to themselves or to others. But the State was not satisfied 
with this as a remedy, because it would have required it 
to demonstrate that persons like Mr. Hendricks had a 
mental illness, which it could not. 


Faced with this dilemma, the State concocted a new 
Statutory scheme that had some of the appearances and 
used some of the language of a civil commitment statute, 
but which lacked the central core of a constitutionally 
legitimate commitment law: a required finding of mental 
illness and the promise of attempts to treat the illness. 
In their place, the new scheme required only a showing 
of something it called “mental abnormality”, the principal 
component of which was that the individual must be shown 
to be the kind of person who engages in the very kind of 
criminal behavior that he had been punished for prior to 
the expiration of his sentence. In other words, motivated 
by its politically driven desire to continue the punishment 
and incapacitation of persons like Mr. Hendricks, and re- 
gretting the level of punishment it had prescribed for their 
crimes before they were committed, the legislature con- 
structed an elaborate ruse. 


The constitutionality of this statutory scheme will be 


measured by the substantive limitations imposed by the 
Due Process Clause. 


The threshold question is the level of scrutiny to be 
employed. This Court has made clear that heigthened, 
rather than deferential, scrutiny is warranted when a 
law deprives an individual of a fundamental liberty inter- 
est. And there can be no doubt that a law that incar- 
cerates an individual deprives him of the fundamental 
right to physical freedom. Since the law is subject to 
the skeptical test of heightened scrutiny, the State must 
demonstrtate a compelling state interest, and must show 
that no less drastic means could accomplish that interest. 


11 


A key feature of heightened scrutiny is that the Court 
evaluates the actual purpose of the legislation, and not a 
hypothesized or post hoc rationalization. 

This Court has made clear that a state may not con- 
fine an individual under the guise of civil commitment 
unless it demonstrates that he is both mentally ill and 
dangerous. The Kansas legislature attempted to evade 
that clear requirement by claiming that it was incarcerat- 
ing those who exhibited “mental abnormality”. This at- 
tempted deception is transparent, and the State’s definition 
of “mental abnormality” reveals that it is a circular de- 
scription of people who commit certain crimes. The 
Kansas Supreme Court has authoritatively and correctly 
interpreted the SVP Act as requiring no finding of mental 
illness. 


It has been suggested to this Court that even a commit- 
ment statute that requires no finding of mental illness and 
no provision of treatment must be approved because some 
mental patients may have received no treatment (at least 
as we understand that concept today) in the eighteenth 
and early nineteenth centuries. It is certainly true that our 
nation’s traditions and history have provided guidance to 
this Court in interpreting the meaning of Due Process. 
But the more expansive suggestion that the mere fact 
that a practice may have some antecedents in our history 
automatically requires its approval as constitutional today 
is inconsistent with important decisions of this Court, both 
within the mental health area and beyond. 


Stripped of both its historical camouflage and the dis- 
ingenuous claim that it is merely a therapeutic endeavor 
designed to treat a condition that is sort of equivalent to 
true mental illness, the SVP Act is revealed for what it 
really is: an impermissible attempt to continue punish- 
ment past the expiration of an inmate’s criminal sentence. 
This is not permitted by the Constitution.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE KANSAS STATUTE VIOLATES THE SUB- 
STANTIVE PROTECTIONS OF THE DUE PROCESS 
CLAUSE. 


It is well settled that the Due Process Clause of the 
Fourteenth Amendment imposes substantive, as well as 
more familiar procedural, limitations on state actions that 
impair the liberty interests of individuals. This Court has 
repeatedly observed that this “substantive component... 
bars certain arbitrary, wrongful government actions ‘re-. 
gardless of the procedures used to implement them.’” 
Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992), quoting 
Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113, 125 (1990). This 
substantive protection is not an empty formality that a 
state can satisfy by mere recitation of a governmental 
purpose that is not illegitimate or with a routine and 
formulaic insistence on deference to state policy choices. 
Rather, the core of substantive due process in this Court’s 
modern jurisprudence is that some limitations on individ- 
ual liberty are beyond the state’s authority even if it 
purports to accompany them with procedural mechanisms 
that appear to allow fair adjudication. In other words, if 
the state seeks to restrict individual liberty for a purpose 
that is beyond its constitutional power, no procedures, 
however elaborate or generous they might appear, will per- 
mit the state to evade that substantive limitation. 


Legitimate contemporary concerns about appropriate 
judicial restraint, reinforced by the memory of excesses in 
the implementation of the doctrine in an earlier era, have 
led this Court to ration its implementation of substantive 
due process. The Court has made clear that it will not 
casually supplant a state’s policy preferences with its own, 
but has also indicated that it will be more demanding 
when a state seeks to deprive citizens of the most impor- 
tant liberty interests. By the same token, the substantive 
protections of the Due Process Clause would be drained 
of all significance if a state could extinguish fundamental 


13 


liberties of individuals without adequate justification or if 
a state were allowed to accomplish constitutionally imper- 
missible goals through a ruse of pretending to exercise 
constitutionally valid powers.” In these limited circum- 
stances, the Court must more vigorously and skeptically 
examine the state’s actions and its proffered justification. 


A. Heightened Scrutiny Of A State’s Justification Is 
Warranted When One Of Its Laws Deprives Individ- 
uals Of Their Fundamental Right To Physical 
Freedom. 


Both historically and conceptually, physical freedom 
has always been recognized as central to our understand- 
ing of what liberty means. “Freedom from bodily restraint 
has always been at the core of the liberty protected by the 
Due Process Clause from arbitrary governmental action.” 
Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. at 80. Whatever else the 
drafters of the Due Process Clauses may have had in mind 
when they wrote the word “liberty,” their contemplation 
surely included and, indeed, began with freedom from con- 
finement. Reflecting this truism, this Court has taken 
great care not to “minimize the importance and funda- 
mental nature of this right.” United States v. Salerno, 
481 U.S. 739, 750 (1987). “The institutionalization of 
an adult by the government triggers heightened, substan-
