STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case arises out of Brown University’s May 1991 
decision to stop funding two thriving women’s intercollegiate 
athletic teams -- women’s gymnastics and volleyball -- when the 
university already offered “far more individual positions for male 
athletes than for female athletes.” App. 86a, 89a. Brown also 
announced that it was terminating funding for two men’s teams -- 
golf and water polo. Jd. The teams would be allowed to 
continue only if they could raise all of their funds from private 
sources. App. 207a. The women’s teams would have to raise 
over $62,000 annually to survive; the men’s teams had to raise 
less than $16,000. Jd. Asa result, Brown knew “full well” that 
the women’s teams (unlike the men’s teams) would be unable to 
continue. 232a. 


In April 1992, members of the women’s gymnastics and 
volleyball teams filed this class action lawsuit against Brown 
University, its President, and its Athletic Director (collectively 
“Brown”), charging that Brown’s intercollegiate athletic program 
discriminates against women in violation of Title IX of the 
Education Amendments of 1972, 20 U.S.C. §§ 1681 ef seq., 
App. 252a (“Title IX”).' The certified class consists of “all 
present and future Brown University women students and 
potential students who participate, seek to participate, and/or are 
deterred from participating in intercollegiate athletics funded by 
Brown.” App. 202a-203a. Plaintiffs sought, inter alia, both 
preliminary and final injunctive relief ensuring women equality 
of opportunity and treatment in Brown’s intercollegiate athletic 
program. 


| Title IX provides, in pertinent part: “No person in the United States 
shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the 
benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or 
activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” 20 U.S.C. §1681(a), App. 252a. 


Cohen I. On December 22, 1992, after a three-week 
hearing, the district court granted plaintiffs’ motion for a 
preliminary injunction. Cohen v. Brown University, 809 F. 
Supp. 978 (D.R.] 1992) (“Cohen I’), App. 202a. In its lengthy 
opinion and order, the district court first explored the history of 
intercollegiate athletics at Brown University. It noted that 
Brown had developed and Supported men’s teams for over 100 
years, but had created Virtually all of its women’s teams between 
1971 and 1977, immediately before and after Title IX was 
enacted. App.206a. 


The court then reviewed the applicable law, including both 
the relevant federal regulations applying Title IX to 
intercollegiate athletics, 34 C.F.R. §§ 106.41 et seq., App. 259a 
(“Title IX regulations”), and the federal government’s official 
Policy Interpretation of Title LX and the Title IX regulations. 44 
Fed. Reg. 71,413 (1979), App. 261a.? It focused on plaintiffs’ 
Claim that Brown was in violation of section 106.41(c)(1) of the 
Title IX regulations, which addresses “[w]hether the selection of 
sports and levels of competition effectively accommodate the 
interests and abilities of both sexes.” To determine whether 
Brown was in violation of section 106.41(c)(1), the district court 
applied the three-part test for compliance with that section set 
forth in the Policy Interpretation, which asks: 


(1) Whether intercollegiate level Participation 
Opportunities for male and female Students are 
provided in numbers substantially proportionate to 
their respective enrollments; or 


—e 


(2) Where the members of one sex have been and are 
underrepresented among intercollegiate athletes, 
whether the institution can show a history and 
continuing practice of program expansion which is 
demonstrably responsive to the developing interest 
and abilities of the members of that sex; or 


(3) Where the members of one sex are 
underrepresented among intercollegiate athletes, and 
the institution cannot show a continuing practice of 
program expansion such as that cited above, whether 
it can be demonstrated that the interests and abilities 
of the members of that sex have been fully and 
effectively accommodated by the present program. 


App. 282a. An institution that satisfies any one of the three 
questions is in compliance with this component of the Title [X 
regulations. Jd. 


Reviewing the evidence in detail, the district court 
concluded that “plaintiffs have shown a likelihood of success 
with respect to § 106.41(c)(1) based on Brown’s likely violation 
of the three-part test.” App 234a. Specifically, the court found 
Brown in likely violation of Part One of the three-part test 
because the ratio of men to women in Brown’s intercollegiate 
athletic program (63.4% men to 36.6% women) was not 
“substantially proportionate” to the ratio of men to women in 
Brown’s student body (51.8% men to 48.2% women). App. 
228a. Regarding Part Two, it found that Brown did not have a 
“continuing practice of program expansion demonstrably 
responsive to the developing interests and abilities of the 
[underrepresented] sex” because, among other reasons, “the only 
women’s team added since 1977 was winter track in 1982, a 
sport that merely involved providing indoor space to the existing 
women’s track team.” Jd. Finally, the district court found 
Brown in likely violation of Part Three of the test (requiring the 
school’s present program to “fully and effectively accommodate” 
the interests and abilities of the underrepresented gender) because

active in varsity-level competition at Brown, but also to survive 
as a team at all.” App. 230a.-231la.° 


After weighing other relevant factors, the district court 
granted the preliminary injunction. It reinstated the two 
women’s teams and prohibited Brown from reducing the funding 
or status of any other women’s university-funded varsity team 
pending trial. App. 249a.*

Cohen III. On remand, after a lengthy trial,’ the district 
court found Brown in violation of Title [IX and its regulations on 
two independent grounds. Cohen v. Brown University. 879 F. 
Supp. 185 (D.R.I. 1995) (“Cohen IIT’), App. 85a. First, the 
district court ruled that Brown failed to satisfy all three parts ot 
the three-part test and was, therefore, in violation of § 
106.41(c)(1), governing equal opportunities to participate. App. 
114a-144a. Second, the district court ruled that the allocation of 
offerings by gender within the “unusual two-tiered structure” of 
Brown’s intercollegiate athletic program violates the “equai 
treatment” portions of the Title IX regulations, § 106.41(c)(2)- 
(10). App. 144a.

One of the three-part test (substantial proportionality) because 
women are provided with only 38% of the opportunities to 
participate, when their undergraduate enrollment rate is 51%. 
App. 98a. In so doing, the district court specifically found that 
the reason for this disparity is that Brown offers “far more 
individual positions for male athletes than for female athletes” 
and “predetermines the number of athletic positions available to 
each gender” through a variety of practices, including “the 
practice at Brown to rely almost exclusively on recruited athletes 
to field varsity teams.” App. 89a, 119a-121an.35.

men’s teams that the umiversity chooses to sponsor.

IV. THE DECISION BELOW DOES NOT PRESENT 
AN IMPORTANT QUESTION OF FEDERAL LAW 
THAT WARRANTS REVIEW BY THIS COURT. 


Finally, Brown argues that review is warranted because the 
decision below will ultimately force universities everywhere to 
Slash men’s sports programs. See Pet. at 28. This argument 
again distorts reality; in fact, none of the courts of appeals to 
apply Title IX in the context of intercollegiate athletics has 
required a school to cut men’s sports teams. To the contrary, 
where a violation of Title IX is found, a school always has the 
option of simply increasing its women’s program -- and leaving 
the men’s athletic program intact. Schools that ignore this route 
and cut their men’s programs instead are really scapegoating 
Title IX for their own failure to allocate sufficient resources to 
women’s athletics and/or reallocate resources within their men’s 
programs, which take up the lion’s share of most universities’ 
athletic budgets. See, e.g., Brake & Catlin, The Path of Most 
Resistance: The Long Road Toward Gender Equity In 
Intercollegiate Athletics, 3 Duke J. of Gender Law and Policy 
51, 71-72 (1996). In any event, the question of whether it is a 
good idea, from a social standpoint, to reallocate resources from 
men’s to women’s sports is a policy question neither presented 
in this case nor deserving of review -- especially in the context
