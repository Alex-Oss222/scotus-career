# 96. CompuServe, Inc. v. Patterson

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

**Merits docket status: SIM COURT GRANTED — TO BE HEARD ON THE MERITS.** Review is granted on the questions stated in this packet. This case is on the OT1996 merits docket under the established simulation calendar.

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Caption:** CompuServe, Inc. v. Patterson

**Lower-court decision:** 89 F.3d 1257 (6th Cir. 1996); No. 95-3452; decided 1996-07-22. The index separately supplies the scheduled OT1996 merits-hearing date.

**Procedural category:** Simulated-grant appellate merits. **Area of law:** Civil procedure; electronic commerce; specific personal jurisdiction.

**Questions for the authorized simulation review:**

1. Whether a person purposefully avails himself of the benefits of conducting business in a forum state, consistently with the Due Process Clause, by entering an ongoing commercial relationship with a computer network service located in that state and distributing software through its electronic systems.
2. Whether personal jurisdiction over an out-of-state defendant may rest upon electronic commercial transactions and related communications when the defendant has no physical presence in the forum.
3. What relationship must exist between the defendant's electronic commercial contacts and the plaintiff's claims to support specific personal jurisdiction.

The questions stated in this packet define the established simulation grant; they are not quotations attributed to a historical Supreme Court order. Any vehicle clarification is identified in the question-change record.

**Record and proceedings:** CompuServe, headquartered in Columbus, Ohio, operated a computer network through which subscribers could obtain and license shareware. Richard Patterson, a Texas lawyer doing business as FlashPoint Development, subscribed and became a software provider. FlashPoint was his business name, not a distinct established corporate defendant. He accepted a Shareware Registration Agreement electronically by typing agreement to its terms. Incorporated terms stated Ohio formation and Ohio governing law; the record does not establish a forum-selection or consent-to-jurisdiction clause.

Between 1991 and 1994 Patterson sent thirty-two master software files to CompuServe's Ohio system, advertised through it and received fees collected by CompuServe in Ohio after a fifteen-percent deduction. CompuServe alleged exclusive marketing through its system. Patterson reported less than $650 in sales to twelve Ohio residents and said he had never visited Ohio. He later asserted common-law rights in the names WinNAV, Windows Navigator and FlashPoint Windows Navigator, accused CompuServe of infringing those names through competing software, sent electronic and ordinary correspondence and demanded at least $100,000 to resolve the dispute.

CompuServe brought a diversity-based declaratory action in the Southern District of Ohio concerning common-law trademarks and unfair or deceptive trade practices. Patterson sought dismissal on several grounds. The District Court, without an evidentiary hearing, relied in part on his affidavit and dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction; it also denied reconsideration. The Sixth Circuit reversed and remanded, finding a prima facie jurisdictional showing. It did not decide Patterson's other dismissal grounds or intellectual-property merits. Patterson filed no appellate brief and did not appear at circuit argument; those historical absences do not establish nonparticipation in this simulated Supreme Court proceeding.

**Relief and scope:** Patterson seeks reinstatement of the personal-jurisdiction dismissal. CompuServe seeks affirmance of the appellate judgment allowing the action to continue. The issue is specific jurisdiction over this commercial dispute on a papers-only motion, not general jurisdiction, venue, liability on the marks, or jurisdiction over every ordinary subscriber.

### The governing test and the actual forum contacts

The federal diversity court uses the applicable Ohio long-arm statute, including Ohio Revised Code §2307.382(A)(1)'s transacting-business provision, and the Fourteenth Amendment's due-process limit through the applicable service/jurisdiction rules. International Shoe, Hanson v. Denckla, World-Wide Volkswagen and Burger King supply the surviving predivergence framework: defendant-created purposeful affiliation, a sufficient relationship between the forum contacts and this controversy, and fair play and substantial justice. A contract or choice-of-law clause alone is not dispositive; prior dealings, contemplated obligations, actual course of performance and the nature of the disputed relationship matter. Physical entry is not an indispensable prerequisite. A plaintiff's residence and a defendant's mere foreseeability that information might reach a state are insufficient by themselves.

Simulated **Quill Corp. v. North Dakota (May 26, 1992)** supplies a relevant, bounded confirmation. Eight Justices held that purposeful sustained remote solicitation could satisfy due process for a use-tax collection obligation without physical presence. Stone did not reach that due-process issue. Its retained Commerce Clause collection rule, supported at the narrower precedent ground, and its five-Justice finding about insignificant licensed software do not create a general software-nexus rule or a defense to ordinary civil personal jurisdiction. Applied here, Quill prevents treating physical absence as mechanically dispositive, while Burger King and the specific-jurisdiction authorities still require a claim-linked examination of Patterson's own activities. No tax threshold or presumed minimum volume of software sales is imported.

The Asahi stream-of-commerce opinions were fractured. No majority adopted a universal rule that mere distribution everywhere establishes purposeful availment everywhere. Patterson's knowing, continuing relationship with this identified Ohio intermediary presents more specific alleged conduct than passive availability or an unidentified downstream sale. The state connection must still be attributable to him, and the common-law trademark controversy must arise out of or relate to the relevant commercial contacts. The reasonableness inquiry considers his actual Texas-to-Ohio litigation burden, Ohio's legitimate interest, CompuServe's interest in effective relief and the interstate interests; commercial scale alone gives CompuServe no jurisdictional privilege.

The District Court decided without an evidentiary hearing. On the governing prima facie motion standard, supported jurisdictional allegations are considered favorably to the plaintiff, rather than resolving conflicting affidavits against it as if a trial had occurred. That provisional showing is not a final determination of disputed jurisdictional facts or a judgment on liability. The Declaratory Judgment Act supplies a remedy, not independent subject-matter jurisdiction; the pleaded diversity basis and unresolved defenses retain their distinct operation.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL POSITIONS

CompuServe emphasizes Patterson's deliberate provider agreement, repeated uploads to its Ohio system, marketing, Ohio collection of fees and the dispute arising from that relationship. Patterson emphasizes physical absence, small direct Ohio sales, standardized terms prepared by CompuServe and the fact that trademark claims might exist even had he marketed elsewhere. The District Court regarded the network's Ohio location as incidental; the Sixth Circuit treated the actual ongoing provider relationship as central. The Court must decide from those contacts and this claim, without assuming that electronic communication either dissolves territorial limits or is legally less real than other deliberate interstate dealings.

## D. LAWFUL DISPOSITIONS AND RECORD LIMITS

The Court can affirm the Sixth Circuit's reversal and permit further proceedings subject to proof and unresolved defenses; reverse and restore dismissal if the alleged contacts or connection to the claim are insufficient; or order a limited factual inquiry if the motion posture legally requires it. It must not decide the validity or infringement of the asserted marks, require trial in Ohio on every future dispute, or announce jurisdiction over every state in which the software can be downloaded. A choice-of-law clause is not silently converted into consent to forum, and venue or convenience transfer remains distinct from constitutional amenability.

# 97. Vitronics Corp. v. Conceptronic, Inc.

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

**Merits docket status: SIM COURT GRANTED — TO BE HEARD ON THE MERITS.** Review is granted on the questions stated in this packet. This case is on the OT1996 merits docket under the established simulation calendar.

## A. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Whether the meaning of disputed patent-claim language must be determined primarily from intrinsic evidence, including the claims, specification, and prosecution history, before reliance upon extrinsic evidence.

2. Whether a federal court may rely upon expert testimony or other extrinsic evidence to adopt a claim interpretation inconsistent with the meaning established by the patent's intrinsic record.

3. Under what circumstances may technical dictionaries, scientific publications, and expert testimony properly assist judicial construction of patent claims.

## B. RECORD, POSTURE AND RELIEF

The dispute concerns “solder reflow temperature” in a patent for soldering printed-circuit assemblies. After hearing the trial evidence, the district judge adopted a liquidus-temperature construction; Vitronics then conceded that construction required judgment as a matter of law because it had offered no infringement proof under that reading. The jury trial ended in judgment for Conceptronic without a jury infringement verdict. The Federal Circuit read the specification as requiring peak reflow temperature and reversed and remanded for further proceedings. Prosecution history was not in evidence. The selected questions concern construction and evidentiary use; the packet does not add an equivalents claim or direct a finding of infringement.

## C. ENTERING LAW APPLIED TO THE RECORD

Simulated Markman PartI unanimously assigns legally operative claim meaning to the judge while preserving disputed infringement facts for their proper factfinder. PartII has eight votes and includes genuine technical-usage and expert-credibility disputes embedded in construction within the judge's work. Stone did not join PartII; his separate view cannot narrow that majority. PartIII rejects any general rule that any intrinsic evidence always defeats any expert account. It also leaves appellate review of subsidiary findings open.

The claim language, specification and any relevant prosecution history anchor public notice and construction. Technical evidence can explain actual usage and comprehension but cannot simply replace an evident definition with a different limitation. Here the specification's described temperatures and integrated embodiment bear directly on the disputed term. The absence of prosecution evidence is not a basis to invent a disclaimer. Dictionaries may assist understanding consistently with the patent. Later Phillips and Teva rules are unavailable, and Markman does not require a separate hearing in every case.

## D. PARTY POSITIONS, AVAILABLE DISPOSITIONS AND LIMITS

The patentee relies on the specification's integrated description and contends that the liquidus reading excludes its disclosed embodiment. The accused infringer relies on technical accounts supporting the narrower construction. The Court must evaluate whether those accounts illuminate genuine usage or contradict the instrument's resolved meaning. It may affirm a corrected construction and remand infringement, or return a genuine unresolved construction issue to the assigned judge. No construction ruling alone establishes that every claim element is present in the accused process.

[Reported lower-court opinion](https://openjurist.org/90/f3d/1576). Its operative judgment identifies the vehicle; its reasoning is not thereby Supreme Court precedent.

**Caption:** Hincapie-Nieto v. INS

**Lower-court decision:** 92 F.3d 27 (2d Cir. 1996); No. 96-4022; decided 1996-08-02. The index separately supplies the scheduled OT1996 merits-hearing date.

**Procedural category:** Simulated-grant appellate merits. **Area of law:** Immigration; pending appellate jurisdiction; habeas review.

**Questions for the authorized simulation review:**

2. Whether application of a newly enacted jurisdictional restriction to pending judicial proceedings constitutes an impermissible retroactive application absent a clear congressional statement.

He filed a petition for review on February 16, 1996. It was withdrawn without prejudice by stipulation, after which he sought reinstatement. Hincapie-Nieto fully conceded deportability, with no identified surrendered colorable defense. He seeks reinstatement of direct review. On alternative judicial protection, the Government conceded only possible review of detention if INS custody occurred and expressly denied habeas review of the deportation order itself. The court left the nature and scope of surviving review unresolved. That narrow concession cannot be restated as an established, adequate forum for every deportation challenge.

### Provision-specific temporal and review rules

In simulated **Landgraf v. USI Film Products (April 26, 1994)**, five Justices, including Stone, adopted the sequential inquiry: follow Congress's prescribed temporal reach, subject to constitutional limits; otherwise identify whether the particular provision imposes new legal consequences on a completed event. Pending litigation alone does not establish retroactivity, and a jurisdictional label alone does not defeat it. The distinct three-Justice relevant-activity approach is not the Court's general rationale. Accordingly, the court must distinguish withdrawing future ordinary appellate review under §440(a), eliminating substantive eligibility for discretionary relief under §440(d), and changing the consequences of an earlier plea or concession. The date on which each occurred matters. A disappointed hope of favorable discretion is not automatically an impaired entitlement, but the discretionary character of the ultimate benefit does not by itself resolve every completed-transaction question.

In simulated **Felker v. Turpin (June 28, 1996)**, the Court unanimously enforced §2244(b)(3)(E)'s bar on certiorari review of a successive-petition authorization decision while preserving the distinct original authority expressly left in §2241. Its temporal result concerns authorization and review proceedings begun after April 24, with six Justices including Stone joining the provision-specific Landgraf explanation. It does not decide pending immigration appeals or all AEDPA provisions. Its constitutional holding depended on actual independent consideration through the retained route and an adequate traditional ground for denying that original writ. Merely naming a possible remedy is insufficient to reproduce that reasoning. Section 1651 assists existing jurisdiction; it does not create a substitute forum.

The older immigration statute expressly authorized direct appellate review under 8 U.S.C. §1105a(a) and contained a specific custody-habeas provision in paragraph (10). AEDPA §440(a), enacted April 24, 1996, replaced paragraph (10) with a prohibition on review of final deportation orders based on specified convictions. Section 440(f)'s express effective-date provision concerns §440(e), not a universal saving clause for these petitions. The general federal habeas statute, §2241, and the traditional distinction between restricted appellate review and habeas remain separately relevant. Ex parte McCardle, Ex parte Yerger and Heikkila v. Barber supply predivergence historical context; none authorizes a court to ignore the precise later enactment. Whether §440(a) leaves §2241 review of a deportation order's legal basis, and what review the Constitution independently requires, are open questions here, not holdings already supplied by Felker.

Simulated **Reno v. Flores (March 23, 1993)** upheld specified juvenile-custody arrangements and request-based review on a facial record while preserving statutory dispatch limits, actual habeas protection, effective notice, requested review and individual constitutional challenges. The six-Justice Court rationale does not include Stone's narrower separate analysis. Applied here, Flores confirms that lawful immigration administration and genuine procedural limits can coexist with enforceable individual safeguards; it does not establish that a hypothetical detention-only proceeding can decide every objection to a final removal order. The Japanese Immigrant Case supplies the antecedent due-process protection in deportation proceedings.

No later historical Supreme Court construction of AEDPA or IIRIRA governs this simulation automatically. These are old deportation proceedings with original agency final orders entered before September 30, 1996. A later enactment must be applied through its own effective-date and transition provisions; the mere date of this Court's simulated consideration does not convert the underlying proceeding into a newly commenced removal case.

### Application to this petition

Landgraf requires assessing the changed channel on the assumed February 16 filing date. Section 440(a) addresses future appellate adjudication of the covered order; the record identifies neither a defense surrendered in reliance on direct review nor a new §440(d) denial of eligibility. Nevertheless, the removal of all judicial consideration of a preserved legal defect would raise a different issue from moving or narrowing ordinary appellate review. Felker permits the distinction only after the relevant alternative is identified and shown to perform the necessary work. Here the Government's detention-only concession does not itself establish review of the legal authority for deportation. Flores likewise preserved actual statutory and individual safeguards rather than upholding every administrative action merely because some review might exist.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL POSITIONS

Hincapie-Nieto argues that applying the repeal to his pending petition defeats the pre-enactment review path and leaves serious constitutional objections to unreviewable executive action. The Government invokes §440(a)'s enacted language, the absence of a saving clause and the prospective operation of jurisdictional restrictions; it alternatively defends the agency judgment. The lower court distinguished the Reyes-Hernandez reliance theory on the actual concession of deportability and treated some possible detention habeas as enough to reject a constitutional objection. The simulation's narrower Felker holding requires separate examination of whether that asserted substitute addresses the claim that needs judicial consideration.

The Court cannot affirm the agency merits while disclaiming jurisdiction to reach them. A §2241 petition must satisfy its own custody, respondent, forum, claim and procedural requirements; this appeal is not automatically converted or transferred into one. No immigration detention date or pending removal execution is established that would justify an invented stay.

# 99. Kinman v. Omaha Public School District

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

**Merits docket status: SIM COURT GRANTED — TO BE HEARD ON THE MERITS.** Review is granted on the questions stated in this packet. This case is on the OT1996 merits docket under the established simulation calendar.

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Kinman v. Omaha Public School District
- **Citation or Docket:** 94 F.3d 463 (8th Cir. 1996); court-of-appeals No. 95-2809
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Procedural Category:** Simulation-assigned appellate merits
- **Lower-Court Event Date:** 1996-08-26; this is a source date, not an assigned simulation event.

- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment on §1983 and reversed summary judgment on Title IX for trial. The review assignment includes both components; the original Nebraska tort dismissal was not appealed and is outside it.
- **Relief Requested:** Kinman seeks reinstatement of the constitutional claims and an available Title IX damages action. The District and supervisory officials seek judgment protecting them from attribution of the teacher's misconduct.
- **Material Record:** Kinman attended Bryan High School from 1986 to May 1990. She alleges that English teacher Sheryl McDougall exploited a relationship originating at school and engaged in unwelcome sexual conduct. Later participation and a postgraduation relationship are conceded; initiation, welcome, and officials' knowledge are disputed. Her mother requested a classroom transfer; several students and staff later reported a relationship. Officials interviewed Kinman and her mother, attempted a telephone trace allegedly placed on the wrong line, administered two polygraphs to Kinman, and eventually confronted the teacher, who denied misconduct. They did not initially suspend her under school policy. After receiving a journal, photographs and cards in 1992, they pursued termination and certificate revocation. Summary judgment requires supported disputes to be viewed favorably to Kinman; none of these allegations becomes a finding of liability.
- **Vehicle Limits:** Recipient liability, each supervisor's own conduct, the District's policy, and the teacher's personal responsibility are distinct. The original default against McDougall was vacated; no final damages amount is supplied.

### Questions Presented

1. Whether a school district receiving federal financial assistance may be held liable for a teacher's sexual harassment of a student under Title IX when school officials knew or reasonably should have known of the misconduct and failed to take appropriate corrective action.
2. Whether a school district's liability for teacher-student sexual harassment under Title IX should be governed by agency principles developed under Title VII, or by a distinct standard applicable to federal funding recipients.
3. Whether a school district and its supervisory officials may be held liable under 42 U.S.C. §1983 for failing to prevent a teacher's sexual misconduct when officials investigated complaints but allegedly failed to take adequate protective measures.
4. Whether same-sex harassment by a teacher constitutes discrimination on the basis of sex under Title IX, notwithstanding the absence of opposite-sex harassment.

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Lords Landing Village Condominium Council of Unit Owners v. Continental Insurance Co.
* **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 893 (1997); No. 96-1033
* **October Term:** 1996
- **Entry Stage and Event:** Petition-stage request for grant, vacatur, and remand based on a recent authoritative Maryland decision that the federal court may not have fully considered. No plenary merits grant is presumed.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 2, 1997 (historical)
- **Question Presented:** Whether to GVR for consideration of a recent controlling state-law decision predating the appellate judgment but brought to that court only after mandate, when the court's unexplained rejection does not clearly establish substantive consideration; no definitive insurance-coverage ruling is sought from the Supreme Court at this event.
* **Question Granted:** The disposition concerns whether an intervening authoritative state-law decision warrants a GVR rather than plenary adjudication of the insurance-coverage merits.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fourth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the insurer on August 6, 1996, treating ordinary consequences of negligence as outside accident coverage. It denied rehearing September 3 and issued its mandate September 11. After learning of Sheets, the association moved September 20 to recall or stay the mandate; the court denied that motion as without merit.
* **Relief Requested:** The condominium council seeks vacatur and reconsideration under current Maryland law. Continental seeks denial or affirmance, arguing the intervening decision does not alter the result.
- **Material Record:** The association sought payment under the developer's liability policy of a $1.1 million judgment for construction defects, misrepresentations, and warranty breaches. Maryland's highest court decided Sheets v. Brethren Mutual Insurance Co. on July 26, 1996—eleven days BEFORE the Fourth Circuit's merits judgment. Counsel learned of Sheets September 17 after the mandate. Sheets rejected key authorities used below. The ambiguity is whether the denial of recall reflected substantive consideration or a perceived procedural limitation.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** A GVR is appropriate only when an intervening development is sufficiently relevant that there is a reasonable probability the lower court may alter its judgment and reconsideration would be useful. The Court should not decide unsettled state-law merits itself.
- **Relevant Dates:** Sheets July 26, 1996; Fourth Circuit judgment August 6; rehearing denied September 3; mandate September 11; counsel learned of Sheets September 17; motion to recall or stay September 20; Supreme Court event June 2, 1997.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Need for reconsideration

* **Reasoning Below:** Sheets preceded the Fourth Circuit’s merits judgment by eleven days, but was brought to that court only after its mandate. The unexplained refusal to recall the mandate does not disclose whether the court rejected Sheets on substance or regarded the procedural stage as decisive.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Sheets undermines the reasoning below and creates at least a reasonable probability of a different result, making vacatur and remand appropriate.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** The new case is distinguishable or does not change the independent grounds supporting judgment, so a GVR would be unnecessary.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may deny certiorari if the intervening authority is immaterial, but should not decide Maryland coverage law plenarily without lower-court consideration.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** Vacatur would express no view on the correct insurance result.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may grant, vacate and remand under §2106 if Sheets creates a reasonable probability that the lower court will reject an outcome-material premise and the Lawrence equities support reconsideration. It may deny the requested GVR if Sheets is distinguishable, an independent ground sustains the judgment, or equitable costs outweigh the reason for another review. A remand would leave coverage and the effect of Sheets to the lower courts.

# 105. McMillian v. Monroe County

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** McMillian v. Monroe County
* **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 781 (1997); No. 96-542
* **October Term:** 1996
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued § 1983 municipal-liability case reviewing whether an Alabama sheriff represents the county or the State when exercising law-enforcement authority.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 2, 1997 (historical)
* **Question Presented:** For purposes of § 1983 municipal liability, is an Alabama sheriff a final policymaker for the county or for the State when conducting law-enforcement investigations and related prosecutorial functions?
* **Question Granted:** Whether Monroe County could be held liable under Monell for the sheriff’s alleged law-enforcement misconduct on the theory that the sheriff was a county policymaker in that field.
- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Eleventh Circuit affirmed dismissal of the claims against Monroe County based on Sheriff Tate's alleged law-enforcement misconduct, holding that he was not the County's final policymaker for that function. McMillian v. Johnson, 88 F.3d 1573 (11th Cir. 1996).
- **Relief Requested:** McMillian seeks reversal of the County's dismissal on the sheriff-attribution theory and further proceedings. Monroe County seeks affirmance because Alabama law assigns the relevant law-enforcement policy to the State.
- **Material Record:** After six years in prison, McMillian's murder conviction was reversed for suppression of exculpatory evidence and he was released. He sued Monroe County and officials, alleging that Sheriff Tate and investigators manufactured testimony and suppressed evidence. The District Court dismissed the County claims and the official-capacity claims against Tate and investigator Ikner because their alleged acts did not represent County law-enforcement policy. McMillian appealed the ruling as to Tate; the Eleventh Circuit affirmed. The individual-capacity claims proceeded separately. No jury verdict against Monroe County is part of this reviewed judgment. Alabama's constitutional, statutory and judicial allocation of law-enforcement authority is central to attribution.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Municipal liability requires an underlying violation caused by policy attributable to the defendant municipality. Whether an official is the relevant final policymaker is a function-specific question of state law, not a conclusion drawn from title, geography, funding, or general employment status alone.
* **Relevant Dates:** The Eleventh Circuit judgment issued in 1996; argument occurred March 19, 1997; the Supreme Court event date is June 2, 1997.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None identified as a separate matter requiring adjudication.

### Issue 1: Function-specific identification of the governmental policymaker

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** Simulated Swint preserves the separate appellate positions of officers and municipalities and leaves county-policy attribution unresolved; the Bryan County packet is pending rather than controlling.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Under Monell and Pembaur, identify the relevant function, examine positive state law and actual legal authority, and attribute policy only to the governmental entity for which the official acts in that function.

* **Material Other Authority:** Alabama constitutional text, statutes, state decisions, county control, funding, removal, supervision, and the sheriff’s authority over criminal investigations.
* **Open Point:** Any liability of individual officials, the State, or the County on a distinct policy or custom not dependent on treating the sheriff’s law-enforcement acts as county policy.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Identity of the policymaking sovereign

- **Reasoning Below:** The Eleventh Circuit held that Sheriff Tate was not a final policymaker for Monroe County in law enforcement because the County possessed no authority to make that policy.
- **Petitioner or Appellant:** McMillian argues that local election, territorial service, County funding and the sheriff's local institutional role establish County policymaking for the challenged law-enforcement activity.
- **Respondent or Appellee:** Monroe County argues that Alabama assigns the relevant authority to a state executive officer whom the County cannot direct or control; local election and funding do not make his law-enforcement acts County policy.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** The Court may resolve only the law-enforcement function and leave unrelated sheriff functions and any other county policy theory open.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** State labels are relevant but cannot conceal actual county control if the legal structure places the function in county hands.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

If Alabama law attributes the sheriff's relevant law-enforcement policy to the State, affirmance of the County's dismissal is available. If the law and actual allocation of authority make him the County's final policymaker in that function, the Court may reverse and remand for the remaining §1983 requirements. Neither answer decides liability of individual officers or every other sheriff function. The Bryan County packet remains pending and supplies no governing holding at this baseline.

# 106. Saratoga Fishing Co. v. J.M. Martinac & Co.

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Saratoga Fishing Co. v. J.M. Martinac & Co.
* **Citation or Docket:** 520 U.S. 875 (1997); No. 95-1764
* **October Term:** 1996
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued admiralty products-liability case reviewing whether equipment added to a vessel by its first user before resale counts as “other property” when a defect in the vessel destroys the equipment.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 2, 1997 (historical)
* **Question Presented:** Under the maritime economic-loss rule of East River, is equipment added to a product by the initial user after purchase “other property” when a defect in the original product later destroys both the product and the added equipment after resale?
* **Question Granted:** Whether the fishing vessel’s skiff, fishing net, and other user-added equipment were part of the “product itself” or recoverable other property.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit held that all equipment transferred with the vessel to the subsequent purchaser became part of the integrated product and denied tort recovery. Saratoga Fishing Co. v. J. M. Martinac & Co., 69 F.3d 1432 (9th Cir. 1995).
* **Relief Requested:** Saratoga Fishing seeks reversal and recovery proceedings for the user-added equipment. The manufacturer and builder seek affirmance under the economic-loss rule.
* **Material Record:** Martinac built and sold the fishing vessel to an initial user. That user added equipment including a skiff, fishing net, and spare parts. The vessel was later sold to Saratoga Fishing with the added equipment aboard. After a fire allegedly caused by a defective hydraulic system, the vessel and equipment were lost. East River bars products-liability tort recovery for damage a defective commercial product causes only to itself but preserves claims for damage to other property.
- **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** East River's product/other-property distinction controls, but the relevant product boundary after user additions and resale is disputed. The Court must compare the original manufacturer's transaction with the package later purchased by Saratoga and the provenance of each item. Defect, causation, defenses and damages remain separate from that classification.
* **Relevant Dates:** The Ninth Circuit judgment issued in 1995; argument occurred February 25, 1997; the Supreme Court event date is June 2, 1997.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None identified as a separate matter requiring adjudication.

* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** Federal maritime common law should preserve the contract-tort boundary without enlarging it beyond the transaction and risk allocation that justify it.

### Issue 1: Product itself versus other property under maritime law

* **Controlling Simulated Law:** No supplied simulated holding squarely resolves this maritime economic-loss boundary.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** East River bars maritime products-liability recovery for injury a defective commercial product causes only to itself and preserves the product/other-property distinction. Whether that product should be measured at the manufacturer's sale or the subsequent purchaser's bundled acquisition is the unresolved issue here; the first-sale definition cannot be assumed as entering law.
* **Other Governing Law:** East River Steamship Corp. v. Transamerica Delaval Inc.; admiralty products-liability principles; contract and warranty remedies.
* **Material Other Authority:** Construction and sale records, equipment lists, timing and identity of additions, resale documents, and proof of loss.
* **Open Point:** Whether each item was in fact added after the original sale, whether any item became a replacement component of the original product, and the elements and amount of liability.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Classification of the added equipment

* **Reasoning Below:** The Ninth Circuit treated everything sold together to the subsequent purchaser as one integrated product.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** The manufacturer did not make or sell the user-added equipment and had no contract allocation for its destruction; the equipment is other property under East River.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** The relevant product is the integrated vessel as acquired by the plaintiff, so tort recovery would impermissibly duplicate commercial warranty remedies.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** Classify each item by when and by whom it was supplied rather than announce that every post-sale addition is always separate.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** A component incorporated by the manufacturer or supplied as part of the original bargain remains part of the product itself.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

A manufacturer-transaction construction would permit reversal and remand for classification of user-added equipment as other property and for the remaining tort issues. A subsequent-purchase construction would support affirmance because the vessel and additions were acquired as an integrated package. The Court may distinguish ordinary user additions from original or replacement components if the record supports that narrower boundary. Either construction must explain East River's contract-and-tort allocation without deciding defect, causation or damages merely from classification.
