# 39. Ehrlich v. City of Culver City

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

**Merits docket status: SIM COURT GRANTED — TO BE HEARD ON THE MERITS.** Review is granted on the questions stated in this packet. This case is on the OT1996 merits docket under the established simulation calendar.

## A. QUESTIONS PRESENTED

1. Whether the essential-nexus and rough-proportionality requirements established by Nollan v. California Coastal Commission and Dolan v. City of Tigard apply to monetary exactions imposed as conditions of approving a particular land-development proposal.

2. Whether a municipality may require a property owner to pay recreational-facility mitigation fees as a condition of redevelopment without demonstrating a sufficient relationship between the development's anticipated impacts and the particular financial burden imposed.

3. What distinguishes individualized monetary permit conditions subject to heightened takings scrutiny from generally applicable taxes, assessments, and regulatory fees.

## B. RECORD, POSTURE AND RELIEF

Redevelopment of a defunct private recreation facility for townhouses carried a $280,000 recreation charge and a $33,200 public-art condition; a separate $30,000 park fee was not challenged. After the historical Dolan GVR, California's Supreme Court returned the recreation fee for justification and sustained the art condition. Its reasoning was fractured. The state court accepted a nexus to the rezoning's loss of the recreation use but found the amount insufficiently supported. The art ordinance allowed an approved work kept on the project site instead of a payment or donation; the successor used that onsite option while the preserved challenge continued. The owner contests the charges; the City invokes replacement recreational needs and its general arts program. Review is of the California judgment and its remand, not direct review of a completed taking or a compensation award. Under §1257, the federal question must be sufficiently final under the applicable exception and not defeated by an adequate independent state ground.

## C. ENTERING LAW APPLIED TO THE RECORD

Simulated Dolan's five-Justice majority, including Stone, requires nexus and an individualized rough-proportionality showing for demanded property dedications. Reasonable estimates suffice; exact mathematics and unique attribution are unnecessary. It expressly reserves monetary exactions and generally applicable conditions. Nollan supplies the pre-divergence nexus principle. Extending either principle to the individualized recreation charge is therefore the question, not an already binding holding. The City's interest in recreation does not by itself establish a connection between these townhouses and the expense of replacing a formerly private amenity with a public one.

The public-art requirement arises from a generally applicable program and offers an onsite alternative; those characteristics bear on whether the same conditional-surrender concern is present, but are not a categorical legislative safe harbor. The separate park payment is outside this challenge. The California Mitigation Fee Act must be separated from federal doctrine. Where the state result turns independently on state law, federal review cannot issue an advisory merits ruling; where the state court makes the federal rule controlling and the federal issue final notwithstanding quantification on remand, confined review may be available.

## D. PARTY POSITIONS, AVAILABLE DISPOSITIONS AND LIMITS

The owner's strongest claim targets individualized payment demanded as the price of a particular land-use permission. The City can demand mitigation of genuine incremental impacts, supported by reasonable estimates, without purchasing every desired public benefit at the applicant's expense. Upholding, invalidating or remanding a particular condition requires analyzing that condition's nature and record. The simulation grant does not eliminate §1257 finality or independent-state-ground scrutiny. Neither the brief nor the historical GVR supplies missing state-law findings.
