### Nixon v. United States, No. 91-740

Merits/threshold decision, January 13, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

506 U.S. 224. Argued October 14, 1992. On certiorari to the D.C. Circuit, 938 F.2d 239 (1991), affirming dismissal of Walter Nixon’s challenge to Senate Impeachment Rule XI. Nixon, a removed federal district judge, seeks a declaration invalidating his removal and recognizing his entitlement to office-related salary and privileges. The question is whether courts may review his claim that receiving testimony through a committee rather than before the whole Senate failed to satisfy the constitutional duty to try impeachments.

A committee of twelve Senators heard ten witnesses over four days and supplied a record and report to the full Senate without resolving disputed facts or recommending a disposition. The full Senate received the evidence, heard arguments including Nixon’s presentation, deliberated and convicted him on two articles by the required two-thirds vote on November 3, 1989. His complaint concerns allocation of evidence-taking, not failure to meet the express voting requirement.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the judgment, 9–0. Six Justices adopt the Court’s ground that this claim is not open to judicial decision. Three concur in the judgment separately; two of those would reach the constitutional merits.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy and scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judgment dismissing Nixon’s Rule XI challenge | Affirm, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas | No opposing Justice | Dismissal stands. No declaration invalidating removal, reinstatement, salary award or supervisory remand. |

The judgment coalition does not make the alternative constitutional-merits rationale controlling. The Court resolves the review channel for the presented evidentiary objection.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The presented committee-evidence challenge is not judicially revisable

**Controlling proposition:** The Senate’s use of a committee to receive testimony, while the full Senate receives the record, hears argument and retains deliberation and judgment, falls within the impeachment-trial procedure committed to the Senate and is not judicially revisable on this claim. This holding does not decide whether courts may enforce the oath, presidential-trial presiding-officer or two-thirds requirements, or review a purported proceeding that abandons adjudication altogether.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Six of nine Justices adopt this exact ground and its limits; White and Blackmun’s merits ground is noncontrolling, and Souter does not formally join the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this challenge asks the Judiciary to choose the Senate’s evidentiary method rather than enforce an independently prescribed constitutional procedure. Baker requires examination of the particular constitutional commitment and the standards available for judicial decision. Article I gives the Senate sole responsibility to try impeachments but does not require every Senator to receive live testimony. The committee assembled evidence; the full Senate retained deliberation and judgment. Powell enforced qualifications independently fixed by the Constitution and therefore does not authorize appellate supervision of this method. Commerce v. Montana likewise permits review of a defined population constraint, not judicial selection of another branch’s trial procedure. Political importance alone would not foreclose adjudication. Here, however, imposing the ordinary courtroom model would transfer supervisory authority the constitutional text assigns elsewhere. The Court accordingly affirms dismissal without deciding whether the procedure was constitutionally sufficient on the merits, or whether violation of an express command or abandonment of adjudication would present a reviewable claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 — applied through its issue-specific textual-commitment and manageable-standards inquiry; political subject matter alone remains insufficient.
- Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 — distinguished because independently fixed legislative qualifications differ from the evidentiary method left to the Senate.
- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, decision — preserved and distinguished; its apportionment review holding does not create impeachment-procedure review.
- United States v. Muñoz-Flores, 495 U.S. 385 — preserved; interbranch disagreement alone does not bar constitutional review.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. Baker’s inquiry is applied to this claim. Powell, Muñoz-Flores and Commerce v. Montana retain their holdings permitting judicial enforcement of the constitutional constraints they addressed.

Effective January 13, 1993, the Rule XI evidentiary-allocation objection presented here is nonjusticiable. This adds an impeachment-specific application of Baker without a general immunity for politically significant action. The Senate’s procedure receives no controlling merits approval. Judicial enforceability of independently determinate impeachment commands and the consequences of a proceeding abandoning adjudication remain open. White and Blackmun’s contrary reviewability rule and Souter’s exceptional-case formulation are noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stevens, concurring, no joins.** Stevens joins the Court’s opinion and emphasizes the constitutional decision to place impeachment in the Legislative Branch. That allocation deserves respect in evaluating this ordinary procedural objection. He sees no occasion to assume that the Senate would abandon its constitutional responsibility. His additional institutional emphasis supplies no holding concerning a future violation of an express command.

**White, joined by Blackmun, concurring in the judgment.** They would decide the claim on the merits. Powell requires courts to interpret the limits of a constitutionally assigned power; assigning the Senate the trial does not necessarily make the Senate the final interpreter of the duty to conduct one. Review does not require imposing every criminal-trial formality. The committee developed a record, the full Senate received it and heard the parties, and the full Senate made the decision. In their view that process satisfies the constitutional requirement to try Nixon. They reject the premise that possible disruption or interbranch disagreement independently removes ordinary constitutional interpretation from the courts. Their ground supports affirmance but commands only two votes.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment, no joins.** Souter regards the present allocation of evidentiary work as falling within the Senate’s broad responsibility for impeachment trials. The institutional disruption of judicial review reinforces restraint here. He would leave room for judicial intervention in an extraordinary proceeding whose arbitrariness seriously threatens the integrity of the result, such as conviction unrelated to adjudication. This reservation is a separate, noncontrolling position; the Court itself leaves that outer boundary undecided.

The Constitution’s express impeachment conditions are reproduced in that opinion. No decisional quotation is presented as verbatim; no event after January 13, 1993, is asserted.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review is complete. The affirmed dismissal remains in place; Nixon receives no relief from removal. No supervisory remand, retained Supreme Court matter or later filing is established.

### Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc., No. 93-1121

Merits decision, April 18, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1994; decided April 18, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, reviewing its August 3, 1993 judgment, 1 F.3d 1487. The question is whether §27A(b) may require reopening this final, unappealed private federal securities dismissal.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas; Justice Breyer in the judgment only.
**Opposing:** Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg.

The Court affirms the judgment of the Sixth Circuit, which affirmed the district court's refusal to reopen the final August 13, 1991 dismissal under §27A(b). That dismissal remains final. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to the extent it requires the district court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation, and the February 11, 1992 reinstatement motion was properly denied. No determination of the underlying securities claims is made; no finding of fraud, liability, or damages is entered.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined. BREYER, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment. STEVENS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SOUTER and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opinion of the Court | Chief Justice Stone-Zsela | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas | Affirmance | Entire opinion, including the Article III finality rule, its application to the August 13, 1991 dismissal, its qualifications, and its limits. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Breyer | No other Justice | Affirmance | No part of the Court's opinion; rests on the closed, identifiable class of final judgments §27A(b) reopens without general prospective application. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Souter and Ginsburg | Would reverse and remand for reinstatement | Entire dissent; no part of the Court's opinion. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress may not, solely by changing the applicable law, require an Article III court to reopen a final private judgment

**Controlling proposition:** Article III protects the conclusive disposition of a private case by a federal court once its judgment has become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may amend substantive or procedural law, prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation, and authorize judicial relief through constitutionally permissible procedures. It may not, solely by changing the law previously applicable to a completed private action, require an Article III court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to that extent.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. Those five direct rationale joins control every proposition, qualification, and limit stated here. Breyer concurs only in the judgment, on a narrower ground, and joins no proposition; Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg dissent. No combination of separate rationales is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that finality is itself an Article III protection. It does not disappear merely because Congress leaves the underlying merits undecided. Morgan Stanley's affirmance by an equally divided Court established no precedent, so the question is one of first impression. Robertson permits Congress to change the law governing pending cases and left the final-judgment question open. Klein and McCullough supply the governing principle: Congress may not prescribe the results of cases or legislatively revise completed adjudications. Petitioners' action was not pending. It was dismissed as time barred on August 13, 1991, and the dismissal became final without appeal before §27A(b)'s December 19, 1991 enactment. Section 27A(b) does not change the law for litigation still pending; it commands a court to vacate that completed judgment and reinstate the same litigation because the law that produced the dismissal has since changed. The February 11, 1992 motion sought exactly that vacatur and reinstatement. The principal contrary argument is that §27A(b) dictates no finding or liability, because courts would independently determine eligibility and then decide the securities claims, defenses, and remedies. The statute's objective class and retained adjudication answer the contention that Congress selected parties for liability, but not the finality objection: the judicial act Congress sets aside is the completed dismissal itself, and later independent adjudication cannot restore its conclusiveness. Because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, Landgraf's and Rivers's rules of statutory construction cannot avoid the constitutional question. The rule is confined to legislative commands of this kind. Congress may amend substantive or procedural law, prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation, and authorize judicial relief through constitutionally permissible procedures. A court's reopening on valid Rule 60(b) grounds is judicial action under general rules, not legislative displacement of the judgment. Section 27A(b) is therefore unconstitutional to the extent it requires reopening this dismissal; the reinstatement motion was properly denied, and the Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed. The Court makes no determination of the underlying securities claims.

**Qualifications:** The rule leaves existing law in place. Congress may change the law while a case remains pending. It may expressly make a new limitations rule applicable to pending cases, subject to other constitutional limits. It may change the law governing the future operation of an injunction within the applicable remedial framework, as Wheeling Bridge illustrates. It may not require judges to adopt particular factual findings or impose predetermined liability in any case; Klein and McCullough forbid that legislative adjudication. Relief from a final judgment through constitutionally permissible judicial procedures, including reopening on valid Rule 60(b) grounds, remains available.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court does not decide §27A(a), the application of §27A(b) to litigation still pending on direct review, any due-process question, or severability beyond withholding this application. It does not hold that every statute affecting a final judgment is invalid, decide every form of later judicial reopening, or address the Government's waiver of its own defenses. Fraud, reliance, causation, liability, damages, and ordinary merits defenses are not adjudicated; the affirmance establishes none of them.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morgan Stanley & Co. v. Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co., May 23, 1994: its affirmance by an equally divided Court, 4–4, issued no opinion and established no precedent; it is neither applied nor distinguished, and the Article III question is decided as one of first impression.
- Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society, March 25, 1992: answers, for completed private judgments, the final-judgment question Robertson left open; its rule that Congress may change the law governing pending cases remains intact.
- United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128: applies its bar on legislative prescription of judicial results; it continues to forbid legislatively dictated findings or outcomes in any case.
- McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102: applies its protection of completed judicial determinations against legislative displacement; it supports the finality rule without being extended to every statute that affects a judgment.
- James B. Beam Distilling Co. v. Georgia, 501 U.S. 529: its finality limit on judicial retroactivity is consistent with, but does not itself decide, the limit on legislative reopening; its force is unchanged.
- Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421: distinguishes new law governing the future operation of continuing equitable relief within the applicable remedial framework; that prospective setting does not authorize vacating a completed private judgment.
- United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians, 448 U.S. 371: distinguishes the Government's waiver of its own defense; it does not concern a command to reopen a private defendant's final judgment.
- Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304: remains a due-process revival authority only; it supplies no answer to the Article III question.
- Lampf, Pleva, Lipkind, Prupis & Petigrow v. Gilbertson, 501 U.S. 350: supplies the limitations rule under which the August 13, 1991 dismissal was entered and to which §27A responds; its force is unchanged.
- Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, and Rivers v. Roadway Express, Inc., 511 U.S. 298: retained; their presumption against statutory retroactivity does not avoid the question because §27A(b) expressly directs reinstatement, and Congress's power to prescribe expressly retrospective rules for pending litigation remains subject to other constitutional limits.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Morgan Stanley's affirmance by an equally divided Court established no precedent and is neither applied nor distinguished; the Court decides the question as one of first impression. Klein and McCullough are applied: Congress may not prescribe findings or results or legislatively revise completed adjudications. Robertson's reserved final-judgment question is answered for completed private judgments, and its pending-case rule remains intact. Wheeling Bridge is distinguished as concerning the prospective operation of continuing equitable relief, and Sioux Nation as concerning the Government's waiver of its own defense. Beam's finality limit on judicial retroactivity is consistent with the rule but does not itself decide it. Chase Securities remains a due-process revival authority only. Lampf supplies the limitations rule that produced the dismissal and to which §27A responds. Landgraf and Rivers are retained. No precedent is overruled.

Article III protects the conclusive disposition of a private case by a federal court once its judgment has become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may not, solely by changing the law previously applicable to a completed private action, require an Article III court to vacate that final judgment and reinstate the same litigation, and later independent judicial determination of eligibility and the merits does not save such a command. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to that extent and cannot require a court to reinstate an action whose private judgment had become final through the ordinary judicial process. Congress may still change the law while a case remains pending, expressly make a new limitations rule applicable to pending cases subject to other constitutional limits, and change the law governing the future operation of an injunction within the applicable remedial framework; it may not require judges to adopt particular findings or impose predetermined liability. Relief from a final judgment through constitutionally permissible judicial procedures, including valid Rule 60(b) grounds, remains available. Section 27A(a), litigation still pending on direct review, due process, and severability beyond withholding this application remain undecided. Breyer's closed-class ground and the dissent's statutory-entitlement position are noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Breyer concurs in the judgment and joins no part of the Court's opinion. Section 27A(b) reopens final judgments in a closed, identifiable class of cases and has no general prospective application. In his view, that combination of retroactivity, a closed class, and finality makes the command operate as a legislative disposition of particular decided cases, and it violates the separation of powers. He examines the judicial function the command displaces rather than adopting a categorical rule: he declines the Court's broader holding that finality alone bars the command and expresses no view on a reopening provision of general prospective application. He agrees that the dismissal remains final and the motion was properly denied, and he decides nothing on §27A(a), due process, fraud, liability, or damages.

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Justices Souter and Ginsburg. Section 27A(b) creates a new legal entitlement to adjudication for an objectively defined class of actions dismissed under Lampf's limitations rule. Courts independently determine whether its conditions are satisfied and then decide the securities claims, defenses, and remedies. Congress makes no finding of fraud, reliance, causation, or damages and leaves application of the rule to judges, so the statute does not perform adjudication, and the completed limitations dismissal does not bar the remedy Congress enacted. Securities limitations policy and its temporal scope belong to Congress. An express statutory source of post-dismissal judicial authority differs from reopening by judicial retroactivity alone, which Beam forecloses, although such a source does not by itself validate every reopening command. In the dissent's view, finality alone cannot defeat an entitlement that leaves every finding and the question of liability to courts. The dissent claims no general legislative power to reopen completed merits judgments and would leave Klein and McCullough intact. The dissenters would reverse and remand for reinstatement under §27A(b) and further judicial proceedings on the securities claims, without deciding §27A(a), independent due-process claims, fraud, liability, or damages.

#### Source Notes

The [Sixth Circuit opinion and Keith concurrence, 1 F.3d 1487](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/001/1.F3d.1487.92-5591.html) supply the filing, final dismissal, timely motion, §27A(b) conditions, and lower-court grounds. The Court's [Morgan Stanley judgment, May 23, 1994](../../OT1993/output/OT_1993CHUNK5.md), 511 U.S. 658, records the affirmance by an equally divided Court and its stated alignment; it issued no opinion and established no precedent. These sources establish no finding of fraud, liability, or damages.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment of the Sixth Circuit is affirmed. The August 13, 1991 dismissal remains final, and the February 11, 1992 reinstatement motion was properly denied. Section 27A(b) is unconstitutional to the extent it requires the district court to reopen that dismissal. Supreme Court review is complete; no remand is ordered, and no determination of the underlying securities claims is made.
