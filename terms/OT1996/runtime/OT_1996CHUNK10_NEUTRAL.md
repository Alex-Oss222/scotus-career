# 107. Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co.

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

**Merits docket status: SIM COURT GRANTED — TO BE HEARD ON THE MERITS.** Review is granted on the questions stated in this packet. This case is on the OT1996 merits docket under the established simulation calendar.

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Citizens for a Better Environment v. Steel Co.
- **Citation or Docket:** 90 F.3d 1237 (7th Cir. 1996); court-of-appeals No. 96-1136
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Procedural Category:** Simulation-assigned appellate merits
- **Lower-Court Event Date:** 1996-07-23; this is a source date, not an assigned simulation event.

- **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Seventh Circuit reversed dismissal, construing EPCRA to allow citizen enforcement of missed reporting deadlines although forms were submitted before suit. Standing and jurisdictional sequencing in the current assignment require independent examination; the appellate statutory answer does not establish Article III.
- **Relief Requested:** A declaration of past violation; periodic access to the facility and records at the Company's expense; copies of future EPA compliance reports; $25,000 per day per reporting violation, payable to the Treasury; and investigation/prosecution expenses claimed under EPCRA's litigation-cost provision. No compensatory payment for lost timely information is authorized by the invoked penalty remedy.
- **Material Record:** CBE identified unfiled chemical-inventory and toxic-release reports for the Company's Chicago steel facility. It sent statutory notice to the Company, EPA and Illinois authorities on March 16, 1995. The Company then filed the overdue forms; EPA did not commence enforcement within the notice period. CBE sued August 7, 1995, with filings current. The District Court treated wholly past violations as outside the citizen-suit provision. The complaint identifies members living near or visiting the facility who use the information for chemical-risk assessment and emergency preparation. It alleges late reporting and resulting effects, but no continuing violation or imminent future violation. These existing complaint facts are confirmed by the later primary opinion's account, used here for record verification only; its holding is isolated in Section III.
- **Vehicle Limits:** The notice interval, filing-date standing, statutory cause, and each requested remedy remain separate. Later filing cannot be treated as cessation after an originally live suit: the forms were complete before this complaint. No recurrence allegation may be invented.

### Questions Presented

1. Whether the citizen-suit provision of the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act authorizes private plaintiffs to sue for violations of statutory reporting deadlines when the defendant has submitted the required information before the action is commenced.
2. Whether an environmental organization and its members satisfy Article III's injury-in-fact requirement by alleging the deprivation of information that federal law requires regulated facilities to disclose.
3. Whether a plaintiff satisfies Article III's redressability requirement when the defendant has already corrected the reporting violation and the requested civil penalties would be paid to the United States Treasury rather than the plaintiff.
4. Whether a federal court may assume the existence of Article III jurisdiction to decide a disputed statutory cause-of-action question on the merits, or must establish jurisdiction before reaching that question.

Congress can protect a concrete procedural interest but cannot substitute a citizen-suit entitlement for threatened personal injury. The particular absent-agency application had only three joins and must not be generalized. ## C. LITIGATION POSITIONS, LAWFUL PATHS, AND UNCERTAINTY

CBE argues that filing deadlines are enforceable requirements, its use of mandated information was impaired, and penalties deter repetition. The Company answers that reporting was completed before the action, civil penalties compensate nobody here, and future compliance is not genuinely threatened. The notice period can encourage compliance without necessarily erasing liability; that interpretive point does not itself answer constitutional redressability.

Possible dispositions include jurisdictional dismissal without deciding statutory scope or a merits ruling only if the actual alleged injury and requested relief establish jurisdiction. Any remand for additional proceedings would need an identified unresolved matter or lawful amendment basis; it cannot rest on an invented recurrence allegation. A fee request cannot create the antecedent merits controversy. The verified lower opinion does not permit assuming a continuing violation, compensation payable to CBE, or a recurrence threat just because historical reporting was seriously deficient.

# 108. Doe v. District of Columbia

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Doe v. District of Columbia
- **Citation or Docket:** 93 F.3d 861 (D.C. Cir. 1996); court-of-appeals No. 95-7077
- **October Term:** 1996
- **Procedural Category:** Simulation-assigned appellate merits
- **Lower-Court Event Date:** 1996-08-27; this is a source date, not an assigned simulation event.

Jane Doe, then a young child living with her mother's friend, allegedly remained unprotected after relatives reported neglect to assigned social workers. She suffered severe scalding injuries before entering District shelter custody. Through a next friend she sued the District and two employees for damages, alleging CAPTA and local child-protection violations and procedural due process.

The district court dismissed the federal claims and found the local negligence claims barred by statutory notice requirements. The circuit resolved the federal questions but certified the unsettled local notice issue to the District of Columbia Court of Appeals. Thus local certification remains pending for this packet. The federal review is of Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal: well-pleaded allegations are assumed, not established as trial findings. The timing and adequacy of reports, official response, causation, and damages are unresolved.

### Questions Presented

1. Whether the Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act's requirement that participating jurisdictions provide for prompt investigation of suspected child abuse creates an individually enforceable federal right under 42 U.S.C. §1983.
2. Whether mandatory obligations imposed upon recipients of federal funding create enforceable rights for intended beneficiaries, or instead constitute conditions enforceable only through the federal government's supervision of the funding program.
3. Whether a child-protection statute establishing mandatory investigative procedures creates a liberty interest protected by procedural due process when the child remains outside government custody.

**Provision-specific statutory enforcement.** Simulated *Suter v. Artist M.* rejects the particular §671(a)(15) reasonable-efforts and §671(a)(9) reporting rights asserted there, and separately rejects the implied causes of action. It does not make all funding or state-plan conditions unenforceable, does not treat administrative funding withdrawal as automatic foreclosure, and leaves other provisions for individual examination. Its remand of §671(a)(16) is not a holding that the right exists. Here CAPTA's then-applicable §5106a(b)(2) directs prompt initiation of an investigation after an abuse report and immediate protection following a finding. The question is whether that language creates an individual entitlement or requires a funded jurisdiction to establish a system.

Pre-divergence *Wright*, *Wilder*, and *Thiboutot* remain operative: identify the beneficiary, a binding sufficiently determinate obligation, and a cognizable right, then ask whether Congress foreclosed §1983. Simulated *Livadas v. Bradshaw* applies this separation to a definite federal labor right and refuses to turn Suter into a universal enforcement bar. A remedy cannot follow from compassionate purpose alone; equally, the absence of a freestanding implied action is not dispositive where enacted §1983 supplies the asserted channel. Funding notice and the actual statutory version matter.

**Procedural liberty is separate from statutory enforceability.** Simulated *Sandin v. Conner* preserves genuine substantive constraints and meaningful protected interests while rejecting mandatory procedural wording alone. Its sufficient prison-liberty rule is not automatically a universal child-protection entitlement, and historical Sandin's replacement hardship test does not govern. *DeShaney* rejects a general affirmative constitutional duty to prevent private violence outside custody but does not erase every independently created statutory entitlement. Simulated *Pinder v. Johnson* preserves the distinction between mere omission and an official's own affirmative deprivation without declaring every increased-risk theory established. The pleaded failure to investigate cannot be relabeled custodial abuse based on custody acquired only after the injury.

**Remedy and defendant.** Even if a federal right exists, §1983 requires personal or lawful institutional attribution, causation, and appropriate damages; municipal employer status alone is insufficient. The local notice statute and the certified question have their own sources and cannot be decided through an assumed federal merits conclusion.

## C. LITIGATION POSITIONS, LAWFUL PATHS, AND UNCERTAINTY

Doe argues that the report-triggered duty identifies endangered children and concrete required action, and that an alleged complete failure to begin investigation is judicially manageable even without a universal hour-by-hour deadline. The District emphasizes the funding eligibility structure, administrative implementation, flexible investigatory content, and the absence of a guarantee against private violence.

The Court may affirm the federal dismissals, distinguish CAPTA from Suter and remand the statutory claim, or require a more precise right/foreclosure analysis. It may separately reject a due-process claim founded only on investigative procedures without declaring all noncustodial entitlements impossible. Local certification should remain intact; the Supreme Court simulation does not answer unresolved D.C. statutory notice law or invent a state-court response. Recognizing enforceability would not establish that prompt investigation would have prevented these injuries or automatically support damages against every defendant.

**An actual prior simulation decision addresses the closest issue.** *Kimberlin v. Quinlan* unanimously rejected a categorical direct-evidence requirement in motive-dependent constitutional claims. *Leatherman v.
