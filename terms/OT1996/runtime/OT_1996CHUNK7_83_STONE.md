## B. WHY DEFAULT COMES FIRST IN THIS CASE

Procedural default and Teague are distinct, nonjurisdictional limits on habeas relief. Neither becomes an ArticleIII requirement, and the received law does not impose a universal rule that default must always precede nonretroactivity. Stone's preferred order here is a case-specific exercise of review and remand authority.

The supplied record leaves disputes about the operation of Florida's rule and the State's litigation conduct. Those matters bear on adequacy, independence, preservation and whether federal review remains open. The lower court can assess the actual state procedural materials and any cause-and-prejudice or miscarriage-of-justice contention without first extending or restricting constitutional sentencing doctrine. Stone chooses that bounded inquiry because a legally sufficient, preserved bar could resolve this claim and because the current materials do not permit this Court to make the necessary default findings responsibly.

That choice does not make a Teague decision advisory merely because another nonjurisdictional defense also exists. A court may have a lawful reason to address the easier dispositive threshold first. Stone does not establish the contrary mandatory ladder; he explains why this unresolved state record makes his selected order useful here.
