# 143. Raines v. Byrd

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Raines v. Byrd
* **Citation or Docket:** 521 U.S. 811 (1997); No. 96-1671
* **October Term:** 1996
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Argued expedited direct appeal from a three-judge district court declaring the Line Item Veto Act unconstitutional at the suit of six Members of Congress.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 26, 1997 (historical)
* **Question Presented:** Do individual Members of Congress who voted against the Line Item Veto Act have Article III standing to challenge it based on alleged dilution of their legislative power before the President has used the cancellation authority against a measure producing a personal, concrete injury?
* **Question Granted:** Whether the plaintiff legislators had standing and, only if so, whether the Act violated Article I’s lawmaking requirements or nondelegation principles.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The three-judge District Court denied dismissal, found standing and ripeness, and entered summary judgment declaring the Act unconstitutional under the Presentment Clause and nondelegation doctrine. Byrd v. Raines, 956 F. Supp. 25 (D.D.C. 1997).
* **Relief Requested:** Executive officials seek vacatur and dismissal for lack of standing or reversal on the merits. The Members seek affirmance of the constitutional judgment.
* **Material Record:** Four Senators and two Representatives voted against the Line Item Veto Act, which nevertheless passed both Houses and was signed. The Act authorized the President, after signing specified legislation, to cancel certain spending and tax items subject to statutory procedures. The Members sued the day after the Act took effect, before any cancellation harmed them personally. They alleged that the Act altered the meaning and practical effect of their future votes and shifted institutional power to the President. Neither House authorized them to represent Congress, and both Houses opposed the suit’s premise or participated separately.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Article III standing is jurisdictional and cannot be supplied by Congress’s statutory authorization to sue. The Court must distinguish personal injury, vote nullification, and abstract institutional dilution. If standing fails, the constitutional merits and ripeness need not be reached.
* **Relevant Dates:** The Act was signed April 4, 1996 and took effect January 1, 1997; suit was filed January 2, 1997; the District Court ruled April 10; argument occurred May 27; the Supreme Court event date is June 26, 1997.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** None identified as a separate matter requiring adjudication.

## C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

### Issue 1: Concrete injury

* **Reasoning Below:** The District Court held that altered voting power and institutional subservience were sufficient injuries and that the claim was ripe immediately.
* **Petitioner or Appellant:** The Members’ votes were counted and lost. They allege only abstract, widely shared institutional dilution, lack authorization from their Houses, and retain legislative remedies.
* **Respondent or Appellee:** The Act changes the legal effect of every appropriation vote and removes Members from the constitutionally prescribed repeal process, causing present official-capacity injury.
* **Material Alternative or Unreached Ground:** Even if Congress eliminated prudential standing limits, the Court must dismiss unless the injury meets Article III’s personal and particularized requirements.
* **Necessary Concession or Limiting Principle:** Dismissal leaves the Act open to challenge by a person concretely injured by an actual cancellation.

## D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The threshold path is dispositive. If the Members’ votes on the Act were fully counted and their asserted harm is a generalized institutional loss shared by all Members, Coleman’s vote-nullification exception does not apply. The District Court judgment must be vacated and the complaint dismissed. The Court should state no position on Presentment, delegation, or the Act’s validity in a suit by a proper plaintiff.

# 147. Foreman v. Dallas County

# SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

## A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

- **Case Name:** Foreman v. Dallas County
* **Citation or Docket:** 521 U.S. 979 (1997); Nos. 96-987 and 96-1389
* **October Term:** 1996
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Non-argued reported direct-appeal merits disposition reviewing interlocutory and final three-judge-court orders rejecting a Voting Rights Act § 5 challenge to changes in Dallas County’s method of appointing election judges.
* **Simulated Event Date:** June 27, 1997 (historical)
* **Question Presented:** Do discretionary county changes in the partisan formulas used to appoint election judges require § 5 preclearance, did Texas’s 1985 submission preclear those methods, and what disposition is required when the record does not establish the appointment procedure in force on the coverage date?
* **Question Granted:** Direct appellate review of the denial of prospective relief and final dismissal in the consolidated § 5 enforcement matters, including appellate finality and the missing baseline record.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** A three-judge District Court held that preclearance was unnecessary because Dallas County merely exercised discretion under a precleared state statute, treated Texas’s 1985 recodification submission as sufficient preclearance, denied injunctive relief, and later dismissed the complaint.
* **Relief Requested:** The voters seek reversal, prospective nonuse of any unprecleared appointment practice, and remand to identify the lawful baseline. Dallas County and state officials seek affirmance.
* **Material Record:** Texas law authorizes counties to appoint election judges who supervise voting at precinct polling places. Dallas County changed its selection procedures repeatedly after 1983, using different party-affiliation formulas. Texas’s 1985 submission of a recodified election code described changes to the start date and duration of election-judge appointments but did not identify the County’s particular partisan-selection formulas. The record does not establish Dallas County’s method on November 1, 1972, the relevant coverage date.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The interlocutory direct appeal and appeal from final dismissal require separate jurisdictional treatment.
