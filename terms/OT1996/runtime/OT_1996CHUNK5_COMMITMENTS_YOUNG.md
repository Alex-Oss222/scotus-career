## Shared operative commitments

All eight Associates support the following propositions at the same level of generality and support affirming the judgment of the Tenth Circuit on that basis. These are provisional direct opinion joins, not merely identical judgment votes. No separate writing is needed to express a supplied material disagreement.

### 1. Community conditional liberty

When the State actually releases a prisoner to live at his own residence, work in the community, and conduct ordinary community life subject to parole-like supervision and conditions, termination of that conditional liberty requires the protections of Morrissey v. Brewer, 408 U.S. 471, 480–490 (1972). The State cannot avoid that protection merely by calling the release preparole, retaining legal custody, identifying overcrowding as a program purpose, or reserving a separate decision about formal parole.

Morrissey protects the existing conditional freedom to live outside prison. It already accounts for the original conviction, continuing sentence, restrictions, supervision, and the State's need to return a person to custody on legally sufficient grounds. Harper's residence, work, and reporting arrangement present that liberty, rather than a routine transfer between prison settings. Five months of release reinforce the concrete nature of the deprivation but are not a new minimum duration for constitutional protection.

Greenholtz v. Inmates, 442 U.S. 1, 7–12 (1979), distinguishes a request for release not yet obtained from the loss of conditional freedom already possessed. The Governor's refusal of formal parole is not automatically a constitutionally adequate revocation proceeding for existing preparole liberty. This conclusion does not hold that Harper was entitled to receive formal parole or that every form of temporary absence from prison has the same constitutional incidents.

Sandin's controlling sufficient, nonexclusive rule requires genuine substantive constraints and a meaningful deprivation for the state-created prison-entitlement route; it expressly preserves directly protected liberty. The Court applies that preservation and Morrissey's more specific rule. It adopts neither the historical atypical-and-significant-hardship formulation nor an inference that mandatory words alone create protected liberty. No new finding that the entire Oklahoma program independently satisfies Sandin's state-entitlement route is necessary.

### 2. Original conditions and the later procedure

Harper's October 1990 orientation and executed acknowledgment tied community status to compliance with release conditions. The Board simultaneously recommended parole and released him. The supported account describes five **apparently** uneventful months, followed by the Governor's denial and the March 14, 1991 telephone direction to return. Those facts support conditional community liberty; they do not conclusively establish that Harper violated no condition, that release was unconditional, or that he personally was then ineligible for parole.

The procedure on which the State relies was promulgated in August 1991, after the return to prison. The earlier procedure in effect at release was silent about the effect of a later parole denial. The August procedure therefore does not establish a parole-denial return condition communicated to Harper in October 1990. Nor can silence about that single issue establish the complete content of the governing scheme, forbid all other lawful return grounds, or make community release irrevocable. The Court rejects reliance on the later procedure to defeat this process claim; it does not invalidate the August procedure prospectively or resolve every possible substantive basis for revocation.

### 3. Process and its operative qualifications

Morrissey's ordinary protections govern termination of this liberty. They protect reliable determination of the legally asserted ground and the decision whether revocation is warranted, while preserving the State's authority to use informal administrative proceedings. The case does not add a criminal-trial model, a new evidentiary burden, a fixed numerical deadline, or an absolute requirement that every final revocation hearing precede initial arrest or detention.

**Preliminary stage.** When the participant is arrested and detained pending revocation, a reasonably prompt inquiry at or reasonably near the place of the alleged violation or arrest determines whether probable cause or reasonable ground supports detention for a parole-condition violation. A person not directly involved in the case makes that determination; the officer need not be a judge and may be a different parole officer. The participant receives notice of the inquiry, its purpose, and the alleged violations; may appear, speak, and provide relevant documents or witnesses; and, on request, may question an adverse informant in his presence. If the hearing officer determines that disclosure of the informant's identity would expose the informant to a risk of harm, confrontation and cross-examination of that informant may be withheld. The officer summarizes the proceedings and gives the reasons and evidence supporting the probable-cause determination. Formal judicial findings are not required. A supported probable-cause determination permits continued detention and return to the institution pending the final decision; no such determination is manufactured here.

**Final stage.** The State must afford an opportunity for a final revocation hearing, if desired, within a reasonable time after custody and before the effective final revocation decision. The inquiry resolves contested relevant facts and whether the established circumstances warrant revocation; the participant may deny a violation or present mitigation explaining why a violation should not result in revocation. Its minimum protections are:

1. Written notice of the claimed violations or asserted revocation grounds.
2. Disclosure of the evidence against the participant.
3. An opportunity to be heard in person and to present witnesses and documentary evidence.
4. Confrontation and cross-examination of adverse witnesses, unless the hearing officer specifically finds good cause for withholding confrontation.
5. A neutral and detached decisionmaker, such as a parole board whose members need not be judges or lawyers.
6. A written statement of the evidence relied on and reasons for revocation.

These proceedings may consider letters, affidavits, and other material that a criminal trial would exclude. Morrissey's reference to two months as not apparently unreasonable on the circumstances it discussed is not a universal deadline or safe harbor. A parolee cannot relitigate issues already determined against him in another forum, including a criminal conviction that supplies a revocation ground. No such prior determination, valid waiver, admission disposing of an actual violation, risk-of-harm finding, or good-cause finding is supplied for Harper, and none is presumed. The preliminary informant-risk qualification and final-stage specific-good-cause qualification remain distinct. Morrissey itself reserved the counsel question; this case announces no separate counsel holding or claim that all later date-eligible counsel doctrine is unresolved.

### 4. Judgment, remedy, and limits

Affirm the Tenth Circuit's judgment that this preparole status requires Morrissey process and its reversal of the denial of habeas relief. The case returns through the ordinary mandate for proceedings consistent with that protected-liberty and process determination. Oklahoma remains able to establish and act upon a legally available revocation ground through constitutionally adequate procedures. The Court does not grant formal parole, order unconditional release, decide that Harper's sentence expired, establish a particular release date, declare every possible return ground invalid, or itself prescribe a new deadline for state proceedings.

This is a merits application of procedural due process, not an exhaustion remand based on hypothetical litigation defects. The question whether any particular subsequent revocation or continuing custody is lawful is left to proceedings applying the governing law and record. The original telephone instruction without the required opportunity for process cannot itself supply the constitutionally adequate revocation determination.

## Material alternative paths and source limits

**Review timing.** Dismissal would follow if the only relevant paper were an unauthorized late rehearing submission and the original judgment's review period had elapsed. That is not the supplied sequence: express leave, treatment as timely, and the pending mandate establish an accepted rehearing proceeding. The strongest objection to accepting jurisdiction is that a lower court cannot extend the statutory certiorari limit by relabeling a late paper. Jenkins's actual-treatment inquiry and its rejection of orders solely enlarging review time preserve that limit. No such manipulative or retrospective extension is supported here. All eight support review on the actual facts, not a universal exception.

**Liberty characterization.** Reversal would be supported if the relevant interest were merely an unreceived discretionary grant of parole or an ordinary internal custody adjustment, with no distinct protected interest. Greenholtz and Meachum supply those limiting principles. The strongest argument for that account is continued service of a valid sentence coupled with the Governor's parole decision and the program's administrative purpose. The argument fails at the required characterization: Harper was living at home and working in the community under conditions resembling parole. Morrissey addresses that actual conditional freedom even though custody and sentence persist. The original-rules chronology independently prevents treating the later procedure as dispositive advance notice. All eight join this direct route.

**Independent state-law entitlement.** The record might support further inquiry under Sandin into substantive program constraints; the compliance acknowledgment supplies a relevant indicator. The strongest argument against an immediate state-entitlement finding is the absence of the full original operative scheme and the difference between release conditions and a substantive restriction on all return authority. The commitments make no such finding. They apply the preserved direct constitutional route, so an exhaustive Sandin inquiry is neither a condition to this disposition nor an independently sufficient alternative holding.

**Process and relief.** A broad rule demanding a completed judicial adversarial hearing before any physical return would exceed Morrissey's distinction between arrest, prompt preliminary review, and final revocation. Conversely, an automatic return merely because formal parole was denied would erase the protected-interest inquiry and the need for a constitutionally adequate determination. The adopted course applies the ordinary process with its actual qualifications and leaves lawful grounds and implementation to appropriate proceedings. No unsupported waiver, admission, emergency, confrontation exception, or automatic-release consequence is inferred.

The original procedure's silence concerns the effect of parole denial only.
