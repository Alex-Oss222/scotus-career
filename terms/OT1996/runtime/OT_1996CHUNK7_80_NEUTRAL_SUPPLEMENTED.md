## Questions and separate available grounds

The questions concern whether Papai's employment connection permits a reasonable finding of Jones Act seaman status and whether the final LHWCA proceeding forecloses his seaman remedies. Harbor Tug seeks reversal and foreclosure; Papai seeks affirmance of the ruling returning status to a jury. The status issue and the compensation consequences are independent possible grounds. Within the latter, **§905(a) statutory exclusivity and ordinary administrative issue preclusion are separate theories**. The employer expressly disclaims an affirmative election-of-remedies defense. Judicial or equitable estoppel, issue preclusion and initial compelled election must not be used interchangeably.

A status disposition may reserve the award questions; no entering authority requires a universal sequence. Defeating seaman remedies does not itself resolve the separately appealed post-trial vessel-negligence judgment. That appellate challenge was not reached below and must be accounted for in any proposed mandate.

## Actual injury assignment and contrary crew evidence

On March 13, 1989, Papai injured his left knee while painting the housing of Harbor Tug's Pt. Barrow at its Alameda home dock. His deposition described a belief that the portable ladder moved, then uncertainty about precisely what happened, then a recollection of movement where the ladder's wood met the metal housing. He noticed no rocking or rolling of the boat. These are his accounts; they are not an established finding of negligence. A later trial made contrary findings under the vessel-negligence theory, described separately below.

Papai knew before arrival that he was to do chipping and painting rather than what he called moving-vessel deckhand work. The assignment was expected to last one day, with no commitment to return to this tug the next day; he would go home and await a possible standby call or return to the hiring hall. He would not sail with the tug when it left. Its engines stayed idle, it remained docked, and no permanent officers or other permanent crew were aboard. He was paid hourly at straight time. These facts do not alone establish withdrawal from navigation or defeat the possibility of replacement crew membership.

He and Edwin Low initially painted together under Don Dawson's instructions. Dawson moved Low to Sea King; Papai asked whether he should continue alone on the ladder and testified that Dawson directed him to finish. Papai thereafter selected the sequence of painting. Papai described Dawson as a shoreside port captain with an office at the dock. Respondent cites later trial testimony to call Dawson responsible for operations; the reply says he was administratively above tug captains and rarely operated a tug. Those cited trial volumes are not reproduced in the recovered appendix, and a title alone does not determine Papai's assignment. Respondent also characterizes Low's reassignment as seagoing work; the copied deposition establishes reassignment to Sea King, without proving Papai himself undertook sea duty.

**The Secchitano declaration is material counterevidence, not merely a job label.** The union regional director declared that she dispatched Papai to replace a deckhand more regularly assigned to Pt. Barrow, that Papai and Low filled its two-deckhand complement, and that the dispatch was into the operational crew rather than a separate maintenance or repair gang. She said painting was ordinary deckhand work, the dispatch did not permit shoreside assignment, and shoreside workers had distinct classification, pay and seniority treatment. She also explained that idle engines did not mean withdrawal from navigation, that she had no reason to believe the machinery inoperable, and that such tugs did not have ships' articles. Her express limits of knowledge remain part of the declaration. It is evidence of the claimed employment role, not a finding that the tug sailed, that Papai performed every contractual duty, or that federal status follows from the union designation.

Dispatch timing is contested. Papai remembered a home telephone call on Sunday evening from the employer's standby list and arrival around 8 a.m. Monday. Secchitano's deposition identifies a March 13 telephone dispatch at 8:45 a.m. to report as soon as possible; respondent's statement says the employer requested two maintenance deckhands at 8:05 a.m. The source reader's visual check of the handwritten slip confirms March 13, Papai, Harbor Tug, deckhand maintenance and 8:45 dispatch, with Low also listed. These accounts are not silently harmonized into a single finding. The other copied slips cover March 13–14 and other workers, not a complete history of Papai's earlier engagements.

The Deckhands Agreement requires knowledge or ability concerning lines, towing winches, splicing, helmsmanship, lookout, chipping, painting, tools and safe work. Its reproduced provisions address maintenance and cure, casual replacement guarantees, ordinary maintenance duties and engine-room checks while underway. These are qualifications, contractual duties and evidence of the claimed relationship. They do not prove that Papai steered, kept lookout, checked engines underway or sailed on each earlier job. Conversely, ordinary maintenance within an established crew relationship is not categorically a new shoreside occupation merely because that day's task is painting.

The **same-employer repeated-engagement theory** is analytically distinct from the **common-hiring-hall fleet theory**. No common ownership or operational control of all vessels drawing on the hall is established merely by their common labor source. Whether the supported Harbor Tug engagements and replacement assignment establish the legally required connection must receive its own analysis even if broad hiring-hall aggregation is rejected. A genuine change in essential assignment and a routine task within an unchanged assignment are competing characterizations to test against the evidence; neither is supplied as a finding.

## Civil proceedings and the unresolved vessel-negligence appeal

The January 12, 1990 complaint asserted Jones Act negligence, unseaworthiness, maintenance and cure, and Joanna Papai's consortium claim, and demanded a jury. Unsafe equipment, inadequate help, work arrangements and instructions were allegations. On May 29, 1990 the District Court dismissed the Jones Act and general maritime seaman remedies with prejudice for lack of status, while expressly leaving any LHWCA or common-law negligence claims unaffected and allowing amendment. The docket dates the First Amended Complaint May 24; it asserted §905(b) vessel negligence, general maritime principles and consortium and retained a jury demand. The documents' dates are reported as they stand; no unsupported amended-filing sequence or jury waiver is inferred.

The reconsideration order was signed August 17 and filed August 21, 1990. It **certified** the status question under §1292(b); the Ninth Circuit **denied permission** for the interlocutory appeal on October 30. Respondent's shorthand that certification was denied is not the actual procedural event. After rebriefing in light of Wilander and Gizoni, the April 6, 1992 order reaffirmed summary adjudication for want of a more-or-less permanent vessel connection or substantial work on the vessel sufficient for seaman status.

The remaining negligence claim was tried to the court August 31–September 17, 1992. Reasons were delivered December 16; judgment for Harbor Tug against both plaintiffs was signed December 28 and entered December 29. The later nonjury trial does not itself explain disposition of the earlier jury demand. The judge applied Scindia's vessel-operator framework, distinguished employer acts from vessel acts, treated OSHA standards as relevant rather than controlling or automatically violated, and required proof by a preponderance. He found that the ladder did not slip and that Papai slipped, missed a rung or stepped down improperly. He rejected Low's corroboration as unreliable, considered inconsistent contemporaneous accounts and found Papai had choices regarding equipment and its use. He found no proven §905(b) or general negligence and therefore did not reach medical or economic damages.

Those are trial findings supporting the negligence judgment, not concessions by Papai or facts automatically usable against him on the earlier summary-judgment status record. His January 20, 1993 appeal challenged **both** the status ruling and the post-trial negligence judgment. The Ninth Circuit expressly did **not** reach the latter challenge because it reversed status summary judgment. Its expectation that Jones Act negligence would receive a more favorable standard and jury consideration did not adjudicate that independent §905(b) appeal.

The status ruling therefore cannot be used without explanation to direct reinstatement of the entire final judgment as though the vessel-negligence appeal had failed on its merits. Equally, this neutral packet does not overturn the trial findings, declare a jury waiver, direct damages or resolve consortium. Any disposition must trace each reviewed component and the remaining appellate task.

## Compensation proceeding: adjudication, content and dates

Compensation began voluntarily before the formal order. The ALJ recorded a stipulation that Papai received weekly disability benefits of **$238.46** from injury through the June 2, 1992 hearing. The reply says the employer filed LS-206 on March 30, 1989, Papai filed LS-203 on April 15, 1991 adding a back claim, and the employer controverted that added claim on May 30, 1991. It identifies that dispute as the hearing's trigger. Respondent says the hearing was necessary to establish the compensation lien before trial. The forms themselves are not in the recovered originals; each account remains attributed. The ALJ's actual five disputed issues were jurisdiction, average weekly wage, duration of total disability, permanent partial disability, and employer relief under §8(f).

The June 2, 1992 hearing included claimant and employer/carrier counsel, oral evidence and exhibits, later admission of Dr. Dye's deposition and post-hearing briefs. The Director filed a prehearing statement but did not appear. The ALJ **expressly adjudicated status**: Harbor Tug substantially conceded injury on navigable waters in employment but argued Papai might be a seaman excluded by §902(3)(G). The ALJ noted both parties' compensation positions conflicted with their civil positions. He declined to give the District Court status orders preclusive force because the pending §905(b) claim made them interlocutory.

The ALJ used Wilander's land/sea and vessel-connection analysis, cited Robison's permanent-assignment-or-substantial-work test with contribution, and acknowledged that Wilander displaced Bullis's aid-in-navigation requirement. He accepted that some tasks could be performed by crew but characterized the assignments as random, sporadic and transitory, found no permanent vessel or fleet connection and held Papai outside the crew exclusion. He cited Griffin, 25 BRBS 196 (1991). Thus this is not a check or compromise without a status adjudication. Whether its standard, litigation, necessity or finality permits the claimed legal consequences is a separate question.

The award did not grant every requested item. It rejected increased average weekly wage based on projected union seniority as speculative; found total disability until suitable work became available November 12, 1990; awarded scheduled partial benefits for the left knee and right foot; denied additional monetary compensation for hip/back symptoms for the reasons in its analysis; and denied §8(f) relief because the compensable disabilities were scheduled. It ordered $238.46 per week, including **106.56 weeks** of permanent partial benefits beginning November 12, 1990, credit for prior compensation, interest on past-due compensation, District Director calculations and a later fee petition. Its analysis repeatedly uses February 5, 1990 for permanent/stationary knee status, while ordered paragraphs 1–2 use February 2. The source reader confirmed the discrepancy in the originals; this packet does not select an unsupported correction.

The decision was signed **August 27, 1992** and the certificate records filing and certified-mail service **August 31**. Neither party appealed; the lower majority and both merits sides treat the award as final. Under §921(a), filing makes it effective and expiration of the thirtieth day without the specified review proceedings makes it final. Section 921(b) supplies Board review; §921(c)'s sixty days run from a final **Board** order for court-of-appeals review, not from this ALJ award as an initial appeal period. Respondent's October 27 deadline is therefore not adopted. No exact last-day calculation or adjudication of this claimant's appellate standing is needed to state the documented no-appeal posture.

Respondent says a lien filing disclosed the award on September 2, 1992 and preclusion was first urged on appeal in July 1993. The docket records an August 27 lien notice entered September 2 and a December 9 amended lien request. The Ninth Circuit judicially noticed the order; Papai challenged the propriety of notice but not its existence or the copy's authenticity. The court regarded the reply and oral argument as a sufficient opportunity to address notice. Neither that ruling nor the lien filing by itself decides waiver, estoppel, the adequacy of status litigation or all preclusion predicates.

## The lower appellate grounds and each party's strongest alternatives

Takasugi, joined by Reinhardt, held that genuine status issues remained. The majority's broader theory treated employers using the common hall as a common employment source so genuinely seaman work would not lose protection merely through daily hiring. Its narrower alternative relied on the twelve Harbor Tug engagements themselves. Poole required a fleet under common ownership or control and read changed-assignment analysis as affecting the period within an employment relationship rather than erasing the vessel-group boundary; he regarded the actual connection as legally insufficient. His use of the ALJ's account in that status analysis did not add an independent preclusion holding.

The majority also **rejected** preclusion from the formal award. It recognized Gizoni involved unadjudicated benefits, but extended its credit and anti-forced-election reasoning, citing the parties' reversed positions and the perceived unfairness of immunizing an employer who forces compensation litigation while leaving a voluntary payer exposed. This is a decided lower ground, not an issue merely presented. Its description of litigation incentives must be assessed alongside this record's years of voluntary payment and disputed added-injury account; neither description becomes a new finding about what motivated Harbor Tug.

**Harbor Tug on status:** The injury assignment was discrete, dockside maintenance with no planned sailing or continued commitment. The actual Pt. Barrow history was maintenance, the identifiable immediately prior Sea Fox job was maintenance, and broad work for unrelated employers cannot itself create an identifiable fleet. The employer invokes both nature and duration, not merely a title or presence aboard. Its reply accepts that hiring-hall dispatch can coexist with a sufficient vessel connection and distinguishes a one-day all-seagoing assignment. It does not categorically disqualify every casual or every one-day worker.

**Papai on status:** Normal maintenance may be part of continued qualified-deckhand service rather than a change of essential assignment. The replacement-crew declaration, contractual work relationship, repeated Harbor Tug engagements, narrower 1989 same-employer assertion and general actual moving-vessel testimony require favorable evaluation at summary judgment. His narrower Harbor Tug theory is independent of the broader hall theory. The limit is that contractual possibilities and general occupational percentages do not establish the tasks on every earlier engagement, and the declaration does not override undisputed actual tasks merely by announcing a classification.

**Harbor Tug's independent statutory theory:** A formal LHWCA award, including the §8(i) settlement category it discusses, triggers §905(a) exclusivity without needing ordinary issue-preclusion elements. It distinguishes initial pursuit of alternative remedies and voluntary payments from a final formal disposition. It acknowledges the failure-to-secure-compensation exception and says none is claimed. It reads §903(e) as expressly crediting Jones Act payments against compensation, not vice versa, and argues that Jones Act settlements without status findings leave that provision work to do even with preclusion. Its references to nonsymmetrical credits and fees, reciprocal consequences of prior status determinations, and settlement categories are arguments with their own premises, not an adopted bar.

**Harbor Tug's issue-preclusion theory:** This actual order expressly resolved the same status issue after counsel, evidence and briefs, as a necessary predicate to coverage, and became final without appeal. It invokes Astoria and Utah Construction for administrative preclusion absent displaced congressional intent. It distinguishes Bassett's direct-review treatment from collateral attack and contends no unsupported-finding exception would help here. This is not the same argument as exclusivity by force of the statute alone.

**Papai's responses to the two theories:** Credit and the statutory vessel-negligence remedy, in his view, undercut categorical foreclosure. He separately contests adequate adversarial litigation of status, issue identity under the correct connection rule, the effect of the parties' inconsistent positions, an award recipient's incentive or ability to appeal, and allowing a later administrative order to cut off review of the earlier judicial ruling. He advances judicial estoppel and objects to retroactive imposition of a newly announced preclusion rule. A compensation award partly favorable to him is neither proof that appeal was legally impossible nor proof of full opportunity to litigate this particular issue. His criticisms of the ALJ do not erase the order's express status analysis and hearing. The cited distinctions among Sharp, Fontenot, Figueroa, Hagens and Simms remain submissions requiring their actual opinions if a decisive rule is drawn from them.

**Sequencing and practical availability arguments:** The employer says a claimant can seek a stay, withdraw the compensation claim before trial, control sequencing or withhold settlement consent. It invokes §913(d) and argues that §922 modification no longer remained available. None establishes that Papai actually requested, obtained or was assured any such option. Section 913(d) is conditional tolling when damages recovery is denied on the specified covered-employee, covered-employer and secured-compensation grounds; it is not universal protection after any failed suit. Section 922 uses specified last-payment or rejection dates and factual-mistake/changed-condition grounds, not one year from signing every award, and excludes settlement modification. No last-payment date, exhausted modification opportunity or withdrawal right is invented. Conversely, possible modification does not automatically resolve finality against preclusion. The distinct arguments require distinct legal treatment.

## Statutory text and its qualifications

The [official extracts](../sources/chunk7-neutral-b/Papai_statutory_extracts.md) copy complete 1994 Code sections. The source readers checked their amendment and effective-date notes: last listed amendments were in 1984, with the relevant injury-based amendments applying after September 28, 1984 and pertinent review/modification amendments effective then. Thus the quoted provisions predate the 1989 injury and 1992 award. The edition year itself is not the operative effective date; no new enactment is created by this reading copy. Those note checks are inherited, not represented as this validator's independent reading of the linked originals.

- **Section 902(3):** The master/crew exclusion is clause (G). The state-workers'-compensation condition modifies (A)–(F), not (G) or the distinct small-vessel hiring exclusion in (H). Occupational listing does not override the crew exclusion.
- **Section 903(e):** Amounts paid under another workers' compensation law or the Jones Act for the same injury, disability or death are credited against LHWCA liability. The statutory direction, the in-world Gizoni credit rule and the disputed final-award consequences remain distinct.
- **Section 905(a):** Employer exclusivity reaches the worker and enumerated derivative claimants, including a spouse. Failure to secure required compensation creates an election to seek compensation or damages; in that damages action fellow-servant negligence, assumption of risk and contributory negligence are unavailable defenses. Contractor treatment has its own subcontractor-failure-to-secure condition. No such failure is alleged here.
- **Section 905(b):** A covered person's injury caused by vessel negligence can support the specified vessel action. It bars employer indemnity to the vessel and seaworthiness-based vessel liability, and makes the remedy exclusive against the vessel except LHWCA remedies. Its stevedoring restriction applies when the vessel employs the person for stevedoring and the injury is caused by persons providing that service. Separately, where the person is employed for shipbuilding, repairing or breaking and the employer is the vessel owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator or charterer, suit against that employer in any capacity or its employees is barred. These express limits prevent an unconditional rule that every employer-vessel is suable. The supplied lower judgment resolved negligence; this packet makes **no new determination that Papai's painting is repairing services within that bar**, no new preservation finding and no new alternative ground. Section 905(c)'s Outer Continental Shelf reciprocal-indemnity qualification has no supplied factual predicate here.
- **Section 921:** Filing-based effectiveness, thirty-day finality, Board review and sixty-day judicial review of a final Board order are different steps. The Board provision permits a party in interest to raise a substantial legal or factual question and makes supported factual findings conclusive on Board review. Pending review does not itself stay payments; the statute requires an ordered stay and the specified irreparable-injury predicate. Enforcement is separate. The text establishes no conclusion that every issue-preclusion predicate was satisfied.
- **Sections 913 and 922:** Filing time, knowledge, prior voluntary payment, first-hearing objection, the specified occupational-disease and disability qualifications, and conditional tort-suit tolling are not interchangeable. Modification addresses changed conditions or factual mistake within the stated last-payment or rejected-claim periods, with limits on previously paid compensation and no settlement modification. Those provisions do not themselves find that a procedural alternative was actually available and unused here.

## Balanced lawful paths and remedy boundaries

1. **Conclusive lack of status:** Apply the governing substantial nature-and-duration inquiry to the legally relevant vessel or fleet and conclude that the supported record permits only a transitory dockside relationship. Any common-control clarification must be expressed as the present interpretation, not falsely attributed as an already-decided answer in the entering slice. The strongest counterargument is the replacement-crew declaration and narrower repeated Harbor Tug evidence, which cannot be discarded by a permanence requirement, a numerical minimum or a general statement that all jobs were maintenance. Rejecting broad hall aggregation does not by itself dispose of the narrower ground. The unresolved negligence appeal still requires a stated disposition or return for consideration.
2. **A triable status relationship:** Hold the properly considered same-employer history and replacement role sufficient to require factfinding, without finding Papai a seaman. A broader alternative could accept a functionally organized vessel group if the actual legal and factual showing supports it. The strongest counterargument is that a labor pool, occupational classification and possible contractual work do not themselves supply an identifiable fleet or substantial nature; specific undisputed dockside tasks and the discrete assignment remain material. A remand must identify a supported material issue, not assume unproved sea voyages or authorize a general search for better proof.
3. **Independent §905(a) foreclosure:** If adopted, identify the statutory trigger and explain why this formal order has the proposed effect despite the crew exclusion, Gizoni's reservation, credit and alternative-remedy arguments. The strongest objection is that a categorical formal-order bar may leap beyond the statutory language or Congress's treatment of overlapping pursuit without establishing actual status preclusion. Statutory foreclosure cannot be inferred merely from a phrase describing remedies as mutually exclusive, and no settlement ruling is necessary solely because petitioner includes settlements in its argument.
4. **Issue preclusion on this order:** If ordinary administrative preclusion governs, test the precise status issue, actual litigation, necessity, full and fair opportunity, finality and any relevant statutory or equitable limit. The full order now permits a concrete inquiry; its recovery removes the old packet's missing-order blocker. The strongest objection concerns the claimed inadequacy or changed legal standard, contrary party incentives, procedural timing and review opportunity. Conversely, inconsistent positions or a later contrary legal interpretation do not by themselves erase the actual hearing and finding. A dispositive rule drawn from a cited preclusion case must be verified from its primary text and actual in-world treatment before use; counsel's descriptions are not such verification.
5. **Reserve the award question or remand a material unresolved predicate:** A sufficient status disposition can leave final-award effects open. If those effects become necessary, identify the exact genuinely missing administrative transcript, filing or legal source rather than treating every omitted page as a blocker. The strongest objection to a new factual remand is that the complete order and existing record may already resolve the needed predicates; a remand cannot be justified by a generic absence of every underlying document.

No path awards damages, declares actual negligence, supplies a jury waiver, eliminates the independent vessel-negligence appeal by silence, or adjudicates an unrelated compensation modification. The presence of final administrative benefits is real; the legal effect is what is disputed.

§905. Exclusiveness of liability






(a) Employer liability; failure of employer to secure payment of compensation


The liability of an employer prescribed in section 904 of this title shall be exclusive and in place of all other liability of such employer to the employee, his legal representative, husband or wife, parents, dependents, next of kin, and anyone otherwise entitled to recover damages from such employer at law or in admiralty on account of such injury or death, except that if an employer fails to secure payment of compensation as required by this chapter, an injured employee, or his legal representative in case death results from the injury, may elect to claim compensation under the chapter, or to maintain an action at law or in admiralty for damages on account of such injury or death. In such action the defendant may not plead as a defense that the injury was caused by the negligence of a fellow servant, or that the employee assumed the risk of his employment, or that the injury was due to the contributory negligence of the employee. For purposes of this subsection, a contractor shall be deemed the employer of a subcontractor's employees only if the subcontractor fails to secure the payment of compensation as required by section 904 of this title.


(b) Negligence of vessel


In the event of injury to a person covered under this chapter caused by the negligence of a vessel, then such person, or anyone otherwise entitled to recover damages by reason thereof, may bring an action against such vessel as a third party in accordance with the provisions of section 933 of this title, and the employer shall not be liable to the vessel for such damages directly or indirectly and any agreements or warranties to the contrary shall be void. If such person was employed by the vessel to provide stevedoring services, no such action shall be permitted if the injury was caused by the negligence of persons engaged in providing stevedoring services to the vessel. If such person was employed to provide shipbuilding, repairing, or breaking services and such person's employer was the owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator, or charterer of the vessel, no such action shall be permitted, in whole or in part or directly or indirectly, against the injured person's employer (in any capacity, including as the vessel's owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator, or charterer) or against the employees of the employer. The liability of the vessel under this subsection shall not be based upon the warranty of seaworthiness or a breach thereof at the time the injury occurred. The remedy provided in this subsection shall be exclusive of all other remedies against the vessel except remedies available under this chapter.


(c) Outer Continental Shelf


In the event that the negligence of a vessel causes injury to a person entitled to receive benefits under this Act by virtue of section 1333 of title 43, then such person, or anyone otherwise entitled to recover damages by reason thereof, may bring an action against such vessel in accordance with the provisions of subsection (b) of this section. Nothing contained in subsection (b) of this section shall preclude the enforcement according to its terms of any reciprocal indemnity provision whereby the employer of a person entitled to receive benefits under this chapter by virtue of section 1333 of title 43 and the vessel agree to defend and indemnify the other for cost of defense and loss or liability for damages arising out of or resulting from death or bodily injury to their employees.

§913. Filing of claims






(a) Time to file


Except as otherwise provided in this section, the right to compensation for disability or death under this chapter shall be barred unless a claim therefore is filed within one year after the injury or death. If payment of compensation has 
been made without an award on account of such injury or death, a claim may be filed within one year after the date of the last payment. Such claim shall be filed with the deputy commissioner in the compensation district in which such injury or death occurred. The time for filing a claim shall not begin to run until the employee or beneficiary is aware, or by the exercise of reasonable diligence should have been aware, of the relationship between the injury or death and the employment.


(b) Failure to file


(1) Notwithstanding the provisions of subsection (a) of this section failure to file a claim within the period prescribed in such subsection shall not be a bar to such right unless objection to such failure is made at the first hearing of such claim in which all parties in interest are given reasonable notice and opportunity to be heard.


(2) Notwithstanding the provisions of subsection (a) of this section, a claim for compensation for death or disability due to an occupational disease which does not immediately result in such death or disability shall be timely if filed within two years after the employee or claimant becomes aware, or in the exercise of reasonable diligence or by reason of medical advice should have been aware, of the relationship between the employment, the disease, and the death or disability, or within one year of the date of the last payment of compensation, whichever is later.


(c) Effect on incompetents and minors


If a person who is entitled to compensation under this chapter is mentally incompetent or a minor, the provisions of subsection (a) of this section shall not be applicable so long as such person has no guardian or other authorized representative, but shall be applicable in the case of a person who is mentally incompetent or a minor from the date of appointment of such guardian or other representative, or in the case of a minor, if no guardian is appointed before he becomes of age, from the date he becomes of age.


(d) Tolling provision


Where recovery is denied to any person, in a suit brought at law or in admiralty to recover damages in respect of injury or death, on the ground that such person was an employee and that the defendant was an employer within the meaning of this chapter and that such employer had secured compensation to such employee under this chapter, the limitation of time prescribed in subsection (a) of this section shall begin to run only from the date of termination of such suit.

§921. Review of compensation orders






(a) Effectiveness and finality of orders


A compensation order shall become effective when filed in the office of the deputy commissioner as provided in section 919 of this title, and, unless proceedings for the suspension or setting aside of such order are instituted as provided in subsection (b) of this section, shall become final at the expiration of the thirtieth day thereafter.


(b) Benefits Review Board; establishment; members; chairman; quorum; voting; questions reviewable; record; conclusiveness of findings; stay of payments; remand


(1) There is hereby established a Benefits Review Board which shall be composed of five members appointed by the Secretary from among individuals who are especially qualified to serve on such Board. The Secretary shall designate one of the members of the Board to serve as chairman. The Chairman shall have the authority, as delegated by the Secretary, to exercise all administrative functions necessary to operate the Board.


(2) For the purpose of carrying out its functions under this chapter, three members of the Board shall constitute a quorum and official action can be taken only on the affirmative vote of at least three members.


(3) The Board shall be authorized to hear and determine appeals raising a substantial question of law or fact taken by any party in interest from decisions with respect to claims of employees under this chapter and the extensions thereof. The Board's orders shall be based upon the hearing record. The findings of fact in the decision under review by the Board shall be conclusive if supported by substantial evidence in the record considered as a whole. The payment of the amounts required by an award shall not be stayed pending final decision in any such proceeding unless ordered by the Board. No stay shall be issued unless irreparable injury would otherwise ensue to the employer or carrier.


(4) The Board may, on its own motion or at the request of the Secretary, remand a case to the administrative law judge for further appropriate action. The consent of the parties in interest shall not be a prerequisite to a remand by the Board.


(5) Notwithstanding paragraphs (1) through (4), upon application of the Chairman of the Board, the Secretary may designate up to four Department of Labor administrative law judges to serve on the Board temporarily, for not more than one year. The Board is authorized to delegate to panels of three members any or all of the powers which the Board may exercise. Each such panel shall have no more than one temporary member. Two members shall constitute a quorum of a panel. Official adjudicative action may be taken only on the affirmative vote of at least two members of a panel. Any party aggrieved by a decision of a panel of the Board may, within thirty days after the date of entry of the decision, petition the entire permanent Board for review of the panel's decision. Upon affirmative vote of the majority of the permanent members of the Board, the petition shall be granted. The Board shall amend its Rules of Practice to conform with this paragraph. Temporary members, while serving as members of the Board, shall be compensated at the same rate of compensation as regular members.


(c) Court of appeals; jurisdiction; persons entitled to review; petition; record; determination and enforcement; service of process; stay of payments


Any person adversely affected or aggrieved by a final order of the Board may obtain a review of that order in the United States court of appeals for the circuit in which the injury occurred, by filing in such court within sixty days following the issuance of such Board order a written petition praying that the order be modified or set aside. A copy of such petition shall be forthwith transmitted by the clerk of the court, to the Board, and to the other parties, and thereupon the Board shall file in the court the record in the proceedings as provided in section 2112 of title 28. Upon such filing, the court shall have jurisdiction of the proceeding and shall have the power to give a decree affirming, modifying, or setting aside, in whole or in part, the order of the Board and enforcing same to the extent that such order is affirmed or modified. The orders, writs, and processes of the court in such proceedings may run, be served, and be returnable anywhere in the United States. The payment of the amounts required by an award shall not be stayed pending final decision in any such proceeding unless ordered by the court. No stay shall be issued unless irreparable injury would otherwise ensue to the employer or carrier. The order of the court allowing any stay shall contain a specific finding, based upon evidence submitted to the court and identified by reference thereto, that irreparable damage would result to the employer, and specifying the nature of the damage.


(d) District court; jurisdiction; enforcement of orders; application of beneficiaries of awards or deputy commissioner; process for compliance with orders


If any employer or his officers or agents fails to comply with a compensation order making an award, that has become final, any beneficiary of such award or the deputy commissioner making the order, may apply for the enforcement of the order to the Federal district court for the judicial district in which the injury occurred (or to the United States District Court for the District 
of Columbia if the injury occurred in the District). If the court determines that the order was made and served in accordance with law, and that such employer or his officers or agents have failed to comply therewith, the court shall enforce obedience to the order by writ of injunction or by other proper process, mandatory or otherwise, to enjoin upon such person and his officers and agents compliance with the order.


(e) Institution of proceedings for suspension, setting aside, or enforcement of compensation orders


Proceedings for suspending, setting aside, or enforcing a compensation order, whether rejecting a claim or making an award, shall not be instituted otherwise than as provided in this section and section 918 of this title.

§922. Modification of awards






Upon his own initiative, or upon the application of any party in interest (including an employer or carrier which has been granted relief under section 908(f) of this title), on the ground of a change in conditions or because of a mistake in a determination of fact by the deputy commissioner, the deputy commissioner may, at any time prior to one year after the date of the last payment of compensation, whether or not a compensation order has been issued, or at any time prior to one year after the rejection of a claim, review a compensation case (including a case under which payments are made pursuant to section 944(i) of this title) in accordance with the procedure prescribed in respect of claims in section 919 of this title, and in accordance with such section issue a new compensation order which may terminate, continue, reinstate, increase, or decrease such compensation, or award 
compensation.
