STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case questions the extent to which ERISA preemption 
insulates ERISA-covered welfare benefit plans from tradi- 
tional areas of state regulation when plans choose to operate 
in those areas. New York regulates all providers of hospital 
care services without regard to whether a provider is owned

by an ERISA plan. If a plan chooses to operate its own med- 
ical center, instead of purchasing services from others, the 
State’s regulation falls upon plan-owned facilities. 


In this case, the ERISA plan has argued, and the Second 
Circuit has agreed, that a generally applicable tax imposed 
upon all hospitals in New York cannot be imposed on plan- 
owned facilities simply because they are plan-owned. States 
must be able to regulate the activities in which ERISA plans 
choose to engage so long as that regulation does not imper- 
missibly intrude upon those plan functions which ERISA pre- 
emption is intended to protect, that is, plan administration and 
plan benefit structure. Any other result would confer upon 
ERISA plans a charmed existence which Congress did not 
intend. 


A. The Hospital Assessment 


In 1990, in order to offset increased health care costs and as 
an alternative to substantial Medicaid payment reductions 
which would otherwise have been required (JA 42-43, 50), 
the New York State Legislature enacted section 2807-d of 
the New York Public Health Law, referred to by the courts 
below as the Health Facility Assessment or “HFA.” 1990 N.Y. 
Laws, ch. 938.? The HFA, with certain exceptions not relevant 
here, is imposed upon all “hospitals” in New York. Hospitals 
are defined to encompass any facility engaged in providing 
services under the supervision of a physician or dentist, 
including diagnostic centers and treatment centers. N.Y. Pub. 
Health Law § 2801(1) (McKinney 1993). 


The HFA is assessed at six-tenths of one percent for diag- 
nostic and treatment centers. Assessable income is defined to 
include all gross receipts from: (1) patient care services irre- 
spective of payment source; (2) investment income; and (3)

Respondents are trustees of the NYSA-ILA Medical and 
Clinical Services Fund (the “Fund”). The Fund is a labor- 
management trust governed by Section 302 of the Labor- 
Management Relations Act of 1947, 29 U.S.C. § 186. It was 
established pursuant to collective bargaining agreements 
between the New York Shipping Association, Inc. (“NYSA”) 
and the International Longshoremen’s Association (“ILA”) 
(JA 20-21). The Fund administers a self-insured, ERISA cov- 
ered, multi-employer welfare benefit plan (“NYSA plan”). 
See 29 U.S.C. § 1002 (1). 


The Fund owns and operates two medical centers in 
New York and one in New Jersey which deliver medical, den- 
tal, and other health care services on an outpatient basis (JA 
21). The New York medical centers are incorporated sepa- 
rately as New York not-for-profit corporations and are 
licensed by the New York State Department of Health as 
“diagnostic and treatment centers” pursuant to N.Y. Pub. 
Health Law § 2805 (McKinney 1993)(JA 98). The centers 
deliver medical services principally to NYSA employed long- 
shore workers, retirees, and dependents (JA 75-76). The cen- 
ters also deliver medical services to ILA members employed 
by non-longshore employers (“outside employers”) (JA 76). 
Finally, the centers deliver medical services to members of 
another union, the Port Police and Guards Union (“PPGU”), 
pursuant to a contract between the PPGU’s ERISA plan and 
the Fund (JA 75-76, 78).

The medical centers’ income is derived from a number of 
sources. NYSA employer contributions are remitted to the 
Fund which in turn transfers them to the medical centers as 
needed. Outside employer contributions are remitted directly 
to the medical centers. Fee-for-service payments are made 
directly to the medical centers by the PPGU fund and from 
workers’ compensation carriers. Employee registration fees 
and co-payments for services are made to the centers. Finally, 
there are miscellaneous sources, such as supplier rebates and 
medical records fees (JA 77-79, 81-82). 


The medical centers filed reports and paid the hospital 
assessment for the period from January 1, 1991 through 
November 30, 1991 (JA 81-82, 84-96). The centers did not 
report as assessable income any monies transferred from the 
Fund, treating such income as a grant which is non-assessable 
income under state law (JA 80-81). The State did not contest 
that exclusion. The centers reported as assessable income out- 
side employer contributions to the centers, fee-for-service 
payments from the PPGU fund and workers’ compensation 
carriers, employee payments for certain services, as well 
as miscellaneous income (JA 80-81). For the period Jan- 
uary 1, 1991 through November 30, 1991, the centers reported 
assessable income of $1,177,670 and paid assessments total- 
ing $7,066.02 (JA 81-82). Thereafter, the centers discontin- 
ued filing reports and paid no further assessments (JA 82). 


B. Prior Proceedings 


On April 16, 1992, the Fund commenced this action against 
the New York State Commissioner of Health and other Health 
Department officials (referred to collectively as the “State”) 
for declaratory and injunctive relief and for a refund of the 
assessments already paid on the ground that the hospital 
assessment is preempted by ERISA to the extent it “imposes 
a levy on contributions and payments for health care benefits 
provided by the Fund” (9a). The parties cross-moved for sum- 
mary judgment pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. 


6 


In an opinion dated February 18, 1993, the district court 
granted the State’s motion, determining that the hospital 
assessment is not preempted by ERISA. The court reasoned 
that the HFA is a law of general application applying to all 
health facilities with only an incidental economic impact on 
ERISA plans (21a). The court concluded that the tax “does 
not interfere with calculation of benefits” or with the “deter- 
mination of an employee’s eligibility for benefits” (21a). 
Pointing to the de minimis amount of the assessment, the 
court distinguished this case from The Travelers Insurance 
Co. v. Cuomo, 813 F. Supp. 996 (S.D.N.Y.), aff'd, 14 F.3d 708 
(2d Cir. 1993), rev'd sub nom. New York State Conference of 
Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co.., 
— U.S. __, 115 S. Ct. 1671 (1995), in which three New 
York State surcharges imposed on hospital bills of varying 
amounts of 9%, 11%, and 13%, depending on the identity of 
the third-party payor, were invalidated by the Second Circuit 
as ERISA-preempted (22a). 


On June 23, 1994, the Second Circuit reversed the district 
court. The court rejected the district court’s conclusion that 
the HFA was a law of general applicability. The court stated 
that because “the HFA targets only the health care industry” 
which is, “by definition, the realm where ERISA welfare 
plans must operate, [the assessment] was bound to affect 
them” (12a). Because the Fund had chosen to provide inedi- 
cal benefits through its own medical centers, the court con- 
cluded that the HFA operated as an immediate tax on 
payments and contributions which were intended to pay for 
medical benefits (JA 16).
