Lords Landing Village Condominium Council of Unit Owners v.

I. 


THE HOLDING IN SHEETS V. BRETHREN MUTUAL, 
342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996) IS NOT DISPOSITIVE 
OF THE ISSUE OF THE DETERMINATION OF 
LIABILITY COVERAGE UNDER CONTINENTAL’S 
COMMERCIAL GENERAL LIABILITY POLICY FOR 
BREACH OF WARRANTIES AND MISREPRE- 
SENTATIONS IN VIOLATION OF THE MARYLAND 
CONSUMER PROTECTION ACT, AND THEREFORE, 
DOES NOT CONSTITUTE A CONTROLLING 
INTERVENING STATE COURT DECISION WHICH 
SPECIFICALLY REJECTS THE STANDARD USED AND 
THE CASE LAW RELIED UPON BY THE FEDERAL 
APPELLATE COURT. 


Contrary to the contention by the Petitioner that the issues 
before this Court arise from “principles of federalism”, the 
proper issue before this Court is simply whether the holding in 
Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996) 
is dispositive of the issue of liability coverage for breach of 
warranties and misrepresentations in violation of the Maryland 
Consumer Protection Act. In the event that the holding in Sheets 
v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996) is not 
dispositive and/or is not a controlling intervening state court

By its own admission, Petitioner’s case is premised 
exclusively upon the assumption that the holding in Sheets v. 
Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996) is 
dispositive of the issues of liability coverage in the instant case. 
Specifically, Petitioner argues that prior to the affirmation of 
the trial courts ruling by the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals, 
the Maryland Court of Appeals issued a controlling decision in 
Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996) 
which the Petitioner contends constitutes a controlling 
intervening state court decision which specifically rejects the 
standard used and the case law relied upon by the federal 
appellate court. This is simply not the case. A close examination 
of the holding as set forth in Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 
Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996), reveals that said holding does 
not address, in any manner, the insurer’s duty to indemnify 
within the context of a commercial general liability policy, nor 
does it address the issue of indemnity coverage within the context 
of breach of warranties and misrepresentations in violation of 
the Maryland Consumer Protection Act. As a result, said holding 
is easily distinguishable from the instant case. 


In Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 
(1996), the insureds filed a declaratory judgment to determine 
whether their general liability insurer, within the context of a 
farm owner’s policy, had a duty to defend or indemnify in a tort 
suit brought as a result of the sale of the insureds’ farm. The 
underlying suit specifically alleged that in conjunction with the 
sale of the farm, the insureds both intentionally and negligently 
misrepresented that the septic system at their farmhouse was in 


5 


good working condition. Jd. at 541. Approximately three weeks 
after the new owner took possession of the property, it was 
alleged that the septic system began leaking and flooded the 
walkway. The septic system was then condemned, and as a result, 
it was replaced at a cost of approximately $1,200. Jd. The essence 
of the underlying complaint was that had it not been for the 
alleged misrepresentations, the purchasers would not have 
moved into the house. 


After suit was filed, the insureds notified their insurance 
carrier (Brethren Mutual Insurance Company, “Brethren” and/ 
or “Insurer”) and requested that Brethren defend and indemnify 
pursuant to the terms of their farm owner’s general liability 
policy. Brethren refused and the declaratory judgment was filed. 
A Motion for Summary Judgment was then filed on behalf of 
Brethren and granted by the trial court. This was appealed by 
the insureds. Prior to the time that the intermediate appellate 
court (Maryland Court of Special Appeals) considered the case, 
the Maryland Court of Appeals issued a Writ of Certiorari sua 
sponte. While the appeal was pending, the case was settled. Jd. 
at 542. 


Prior to addressing any of the opinions set forth in the 
declaratory judgment action, the Maryland Court of Appeals in 
Brethren specifically stated that: 


Preliminarily we note that since there is no 
information in the record concerning the 
settlement of the Christensens’ suit against 
the Sheetses, we have no indication of how 
damages were calculated or whether the suit 
was settled on grounds of intentional or 
negligent misrepresentation. Hence, we can 
not determine whether Brethren would 
have had a duty to indemnify the Sheetses.

Consequently, we will discuss only 
Brethren’s duty to defend and not its duty 
to indemnify. 


(Emphasis added). Jd. As a result of this clear pronouncement 
from the court, it is unquestionable that from the very outset 
that the court in Brethren specifically recognized that its holding 
did not address the issue of the insurer’s duty to indemnify 
and expressly limited its opinions with respect to the insurer’s 
duty to defend. 


To further clarify this position, the court in Brethren 
expressly recognizing the distinct difference between the duty 
to defend as opposed to the duty to indemnify by reiterating its 
previous holding in the seminal case of Brohawn v. Transamerica 
Ins. Co., 276 Md. 396, 347 A.2d 842 (1975), wherein the 
Maryland Court of Appeals held that: 


The obligation of an insurer to defend its 
insured under a contract provision such as 
here :nvolved is determined by the allegations 
in the tort action. If the plaintiff in the tort 
suit alleges a claim covered by the policy, 
the insurer has a duty to defend. .. . Even if 
a tort plaintiff does not allege facts which 
clearly bring the claim within or without the 
policy coverage, the insurer still must defend 
if there is a potentiality that the claim could 
be covered by the policy. (Emphasis in 
original). 


Based upon the “potentiality doctrine” as set forth in Brohawn, 
it is well recognized in Maryland that an insurer’s duty to 
indemnify is separate and distinct from its duty to defend. While 
duty to defend is determined by the allegations of the underlying 


a ee 


7 


complaint and any extrinsic evidence presented or known to the 
insurer, the duty to indemnify is determined by the facts as 
they ultimately are proven in the underlying action, either 
at trial or by some other definitive means. Cheverly v. Ticor, 
100 Md. App. 606, 642 A.2d 285 (1994). As a result, it is a 
fundamental principle that an insurer’s duty to defend is broader 
than duty to indemnify. American Motorists Ins. Co. v. ARTRA 
Group, Inc., 338 Md. 560, 659 A.2d 1295 (1995). Therefore, in 
view of the fact that the court in Brethren only addressed the 
issue of the insurer’s duty to defend, and specifically stated that 
it was not addressing the issue of an insurer’s duty to indemnify, 
said holding cannot, as a matter of law, have any precedential 
value or provide any guidance concerning the issue of 
indemnification.‘ Furthermore, since the basis of the decisions 
below were based primarily upon the issue of whether 
Continental owed a duty to indemnify the Insured as a result of 
the jury’s verdict, the holding in Brethren cannot represent a 
“controlling intervening state court decision which specifically 
rejects the standard used and the case law relied upon by the 
federal appellate court.” Therefore there was no failure on behalf 
of the federal appellate court to “apply intervening judicial 
decisions of Maryland’s highest court” as the Petitioner 
contends.

Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996) 
outright rejects the use of an objective standard to determine 
what constitutes an “occurrence” or “accident” under a liability 
insurance policy and adopts a subjective standard whereby if a 
negligent act causes damage that is unforeseen or unexpected 
by the insured, the act constitutes an “accident.” However, the

Petitioner fails to acknowledge that there is absolutely no 
indication from the Maryland Court of Appeals as to how they 
would decide the issue based upon breach of warranties and 
misrepresentations within the context of indemnification. As 
the Petitioner is aware, the opinion in Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 
342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996) is not only expressly limited 
to the issue of an insurer’s duty to defend, it is also limited to 
the issue of negligent misrepresentation within the context of 
the insurer’s duty to defend. 


In the instant case, the jury expressly held that the Insured 
was liable for breach of warranties and misrepresentations 
in violation of the Maryland Consumer Protection Act, not 
Negligent Misrepresentation. In fact, all negligence counts, 
including the cause of action premised upon Negligent 
Misrepresentation in the underlying tort suit, were dismissed 
prior to trial in response to a Motion for Summary Judgment 
filed on behalf of the underlying tort defendant. In said Motion, 
the defendant argued that based upon the economic loss rule as 
set forth in Morris v. Osmose Wood Preserving, 340 Md. 519, 
667 A.2d 624 (1995), the plaintiff, as a matter of law, could not 
proceed with causes of action sounding in negligence. As a 
result, Count Four, (Negligence) and Count Nine (Negligent 
Misrepresentation) were dismissed. Thus, all causes of action 
premised upon negligence were dismissed prior to trial and 
therefore could not have been included in jury’s determination 
of liability. 


In Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 
(1996) the court also stated that: 


we hold today that an act of negligence 
constitutes an accident under a liability 
insurance policy when the resulting damage 
was an event that takes place without the

insured’s foresight or expectation.... In 
other words, when a negligent act causes 
damage that is unforeseen or unexpected by 
the insured, the act is an accident under a 
genera liability policy. 


Id. at 548. Therefore, the court has further limited its opinion to 
negligent acts that are “unforeseen or unexpected.” Yet in the 
instant case, the Insured was held liable for breach or warranties 
and misrepresentations in violation of the Maryland Consumer 
Protection Act, not causes of action premised upon negligence 
that were unforeseen or unexpected. The negligent act in 
Brethren was that the owners allegedly failed to tell the 
purchasers about the true condition of the septic system. 
However there is no evidence in Brethren to support the fact 
that the owners had any knowledge with respect to the alleged 
defective condition of the sewer system nor is there any 
indication from the court that there were facts sufficient to 
establish that the sellers should have known of the alleged 
defective condition. However, the jury in the instant case 
expressly found that the Insured “lead the Plaintiffs to believe 
that the common elements of the condominium had a quality, 
useful life or characteristics which they do not have”, App. 25a, 
and that the Insured “breached its warranty obligation to the 
individual purchasers at Lords Landing for common elements.” 
(App. 24a.) 


By the very nature of the jury’s expressed findings, it is 
clear that the basis for the Insured’s liability is easily 
distinguishable from the factual basis for the court’s 
determination of the duty to defend in Brethren. Moreover, the 
findings on behalf of the jury in the instant case do not amount 
to “negligent acts that are unforseen or unexpected.” There is 
simply no comparison. They are separate and distinct causes of 
action each requiring the establishment of separate and distinct 


10 


elements. There is no credible basis for a comparison between 
the two nor is there any indication in the jury’s verdict that the 
basis of liability stemmed from negligent acts that were 
unforseen or unexpected. Therefore, even if the holding in 
Brethren could be applied to the duty to indemnify, which is 
denied, then and in that event, said holding is still inapplicable 
as the theory of the Insured’s liability in the instant case has 
nothing to do with negligence. 


Yet another faulty assumption concerning the facts of the 
underlying tort case is illustrated by the Petitioner’s attempt to 
label the case as one arising out of negligence. This flawed 
strategy was not only expressly rejected by the trial court, this 
was also rejected by the federal appellate court. However, under 
the guise of “federalism”, this essentially is what the Petitioner 
is attempting yet again. Petitioner seeks to have this Court 
determine that the actions on behalf of the Insured were 
tantamount to negligence and/or negligent work on behalf of 
the subcontractors. Once again, the Petitioner has elected to 
ignore the underlying jury verdict, i.e. the specific basis for 
liability on behalf of the Insured. The Brethren decision was 
never intended to be applied to the facts of the instant case. 
Clearly this is improper and impermissibly extends any such 
intended meaning well beyond the contemplated intent of the 
Maryland Court of Appeals. In fact, the only indication that is 
expressly stated by the court in Brethren is that the opinion is 
limited to the insurer’s duty to defend. Therefore, the opiriion 
as set forth in Brethren cannot andedoes not provide a basis 
upon which this Court or any court can determine the issue of 
liability coverage.

PETITIONER DOES NOT RAISE AN IMPORTANT 
POLICY ISSUE. 


Petitioner argues at length that the issue before the Court is 
one “federalism” and one of the “orderly administration of 
justice”, however, the only support for this perceived theory is 
that the holding in Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 
A.2d 540 (1996) “specifically rejects the standard used and the 
case law relied on by the federal appellate court.” * Contrary to 
the Petitioner’s belief, the salient issues before this Court have 
nothing to do with federalism or the orderly administration of 
justice. Petitioner’s argument stems entirely from a self-serving 
interpretation of the holding in Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 
Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996), which is neither supported by 
the facts contained therein nor by the court’s own language. 
The holding in Brethren clearly does not “specifically reject” 
the standard used by the federal appellate court based upon the 
statement by the court in Brethren that the opinion was not 
addressing the duty to indemnify. The only result obtained from 
Brethren is that based upon the “potentiality doctrine”, the court 
decided that the insurer owed a duty to defend under the 
applicable policy with respect to the cause of action premised 
upon negligent misrepresentation. It certainly did not deal in 
any manner with the theory of liability in the instant case, i.e. 
breach of warranties and misrepresentations under the Maryland 
Consumer Protection Act. Furthermore, there is no indication 
that the federal appellate court failed to consider the holding in 
Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996). 
The most reasonable assumption is that as a result of the plain 
language as set forth in Brethren, the court decided that it did
