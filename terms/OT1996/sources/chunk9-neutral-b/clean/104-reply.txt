PETITIONER'S REPLY MEMORANDUM 


In its opposing brief Respondent argues that despite the 
decision in Sheets v. Brethren Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 
A.2d 540 (1996), a federal court applying Maryland Law is 
free to use an objective standard to determine whether an 
occurrence has taken place under a general liability insurance 
policy, unless that action involves an insurer's duty to defend 
in a case based on an underlying negligence action. 


In an attempt to persuade this Court that its position is 
correct Respondent: 


(a) misleads the Court by intentionally failing to inform 
the Court of the jury instructions on which the verdict 
sheet was based; 


(b) confuses “negligent acts” with negligence causes of 
action; and 


(c) creates a scenario that woul’ encourage forum 
shopping since principles of federalism would not 
require federal courts to follow Maryland's highest 
court's pronouncement that an objective test will 
render the policy all but meaningless. 


Much of Respondent's argument is simply a red herring 
based on the faulty premises that a “negligent act” is the 
same as a negligence cause of action. More, troubling, 
however, is Respondent's false implication that the jury 
verdict sheet proves that Respondent’s insured knew that its 
statements were false when they were made to the unit 
owners at Lord’s Landing. 


At page ten (10) of its brief, Respondent partially quotes 
from the jury verdict sheet in an attempt to show that the 
jury verdict against its insured required knowledge of the 
falsity of the insured’s misrepresentation. In using this 
truncated quote to distinguish the present case from Sheets, 
Respondent fails to advise the Court that the jury instruction 
for issue number five (5) states: 


"This section of the Act does not require the seller to 
have knowledge of falsity or intent to deceive. This 
section only requires a false deceptive statement that 
has the capacity to mislead the consumer.”App. 3a.’

Respondent's argument that Sheets is strictly limited to pure 
negligence causes of action involving a duty to defend, fares 
no better than its jury verdict argument. Respondent's 
analysis ignores the fact that negligent acts can give rise to 
a number of legal theories not just causes of action for 
negligence. For example, in rendering its opinion in Sheets 
Maryland's Court of Appeals harmonized and explained three 
negligent act cases that it had previously decided. Id. Sheets, 
342 Md. at 650, 679 A.2d at 547. 


The first of these cases was Haynes v. Am. Cas. Co., 228 
Md. 394, 179 A. 2d 900 (1962) which involved “a technical 
trespass... through the unwitting and heedless act of the 
insured’s employees in going on the land of another...” Id. 
Haynes, 228 Md. at 399, 179 A.2d at 903. By listing the 
Haynes case as a negligent act case, the Sheet's Court 
recognized that negligent act cases are not limited to cases 
brought under pure negligence theories. 


Moreover, the Haynes case did involve a suit for 
indemnification and the Sheets court recognized that at least 
one of the other cases it was harmonizing involved 
indemnification. Haynes, 228 Md at 395, 396, 179 A.2d at 
901-902 (”...the instant action was filed...seeking damages for 


breach of contract by the Appellee in not paying the 
judgment”); Sheets 228 Md at 651, 6679 A.2d at 549 (”... 
Contractor sought to recover the amount of the judgment 
from his insurer...”). Thus if Respondent's analysis was 
accepted a federal court could refuse to apply the Sheets 
holding to cases that had identical fact patterns to the very 
cases Maryland’s highest court was harmonizing. 


More importantly, in Sheets Maryland’s highest court was 
forced to re-examine the test for an occurrence or accident 
that it had used in its earlier decisions, including the very 
cases that the trial court and Fourth Circuit relied on in the 
present case. Id. Sheets 342 Md. at 654, 679 A.2d at 549- 
550. In rendering its decision the Maryland court rejected 
the objective test used by the trial court and the Fourth 
-Circnit. Id. Sheets, 342 Md. at 650-654; 679 A.2d 547-550. 
Thus the Sheet's decision has cast doubt on the use of an 
objective standard to determine whether an occurrence takes 
place and principles of federalism do require federal courts 
to follow Sheets. Huddleston v. Dryer, 322 U.S. 232, 236- 
237, 64SCt. 1015, 1017-1018 (1944).
