STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case arises from the sinking of the M/V Sar- 
atoga, a 191 foot steel-hull tuna-seining vessel. The M/V

Saratoga was in service for fourteen years before she 
suffered a catastrophic hydraulic failure, burned and 
sank. The fire started when a major hydraulic supply 
hose, previously known by the captain and chief engineer 
to be leaking, burst during fishing operations. The hose 
was a replacement hose of unknown quality, specification 
and installation. 


This action proceeded to trial on a product liability 
theory. The District Court found that this loss was caused 
in major part by poor maintenance, extensive modifica- 
tions to the hydraulics system, and the careless actions of 
the Captain and Chief Engineer which the court found 
“borders on irresponsible conduct.” (FL 143, 155, 167, 
Appx. 164a, 168a, 171a.)? Nevertheless, the Respondents 
were held 1/3 at fault for the loss. 


The M/V Saratoga was a “fishing machine” com- 
prised of the thousands of components necessary to 
locate, harvest, store, transport and deliver a “catch” of 
tuna. To accomplish this mission, a tuna seiner must carry 
and utilize a purse seine net, a skiff and other fishing 
equipment necessary to capture and bring the tuna 
aboard, where it is frozen in super-cooled brine during 
the journey to market. If any of the critical components of 
this “fishing machine” fails to perform, the vessel owner 
is either unable to catch tuna, unable to effectively store 
the catch, or unable to transport the catch to market; and 
is thereby unable to sell raw tuna for a profit.

The purse seine tuna fishing method was developed 
during the late 1950’s and early 1960's. Previously, tuna 
fishing involved the use of small, wooden-hull “bait 
boats,” so-called because the tuna were caught using 
fishing poles and bait. In the late 1950’s, a few tuna 
fishing vessel owners began pioneering the use of the 
“purse seine” method of harvesting tuna, whereby a 


purse seine net is dropped into the water, with one end 


attached to a skiff.* The skiff then remains stationary 
while the tuna seiner comes about, deploying the rest of 
the net to encircle a school of tuna. The tuna seiner circles 
back to the skiff, where the two ends of the net are joined, 
and the bottom of the net is closed, thereby trapping the 
tuna. (FL 2, Appx. 118a; Testimony, Trial Transcript Vol. 
X, pp. 49-54) 


At first, bait boat owners took their wooden bait 
boats to small shipyards to be refitted to carry a skiff, a 
net, and the hydraulic winch, boom, and other equipment 
necessary to deploy and retrieve the net. Because the 
vessel owners, and not the shipyards, developed this new 
fishing method, the vessel owners largely directed the 
retrofitting procedures. Inevitably, as the related equip- 
ment became bigger, heavier, and more powerful, the 
wooden bait boats proved to be too small a platform, and 
larger steel-hull vessels were needed to carry the new 
equipment, and the record harvests being netted. These

A handful of small shipyards, including J.M. Mar- 
tinac & Co. (“Martinac”), began custom building steel 
hull tuna seiners to order for vessel owners in the 1960's. 
(Testimony, Trial Transcript Vol. X, pp. 52-53) By 1971, 
when the M/V Saratoga was built, the construction of 
each Martinac tuna seiner was still a product of detailed 
collaboration between shipowner and shipyard, with the 
shipowner selecting and approving the selection of var- 
ious components and their physical arrangement aboard 
a vessel.* (Testimony, Trial Transcript Vol. X, pp. 55-60)

bait boats, and then commissioning construction of the 
new steel hull seiners. (FL 1, Appx. 118a.) Saratoga’s 
contention notwithstanding (Petitioner’s Brief, p. 14), 
Madruga was decidedly not an unsophisticated consumer

In March, 1974, Saratoga purchased the completed, 
fully outfitted M/V Saratoga from Madruga’s corporation 
for $2,950,000 thereby earning Madruga’s corporation a 
substantial profit. (Trial Ex. Z, Trial Transcript Vol. VII, 
pp. 13, 19) The transaction between Saratoga and Mad- 
ruga thus represents the first time that the M/¥V Saratoga 
was sold as a completed fishing vessel capable of per- 
forming its mission. Saratoga bought the M/¥V Saratoga 
as a complete, integrated unit including all of the items 
which Saratoga claims are “other property,” except for 
the ship’s lost tuna catch. (FL 11, 12, Appx. 121la, 122a.) 
There is no dispute that sale of the M/V Saratoga from 
Madruga’s corporation to Saratoga was a business trans- 
action between commercial entities; and that the object of 
Saratoga’s bargain was a fully integrated, operational 
tuna seiner, including everything necessary to conduct 
fishing operations. Saratoga bought the vessel “as is, 
where is,” with no warranty other than that “said vessel 
and her appurtenances will be in operable condition 
upon delivery of the Bill of Sale.” (Trial Ex. Z, Trial 
Transcript Vol. VII, pp. 13, 19) Presumably, the disclaimer 
of warranties on the vessel and all of her appurtenances 
was in exchange for a lower purchase price than would 


have been charged if Madruga had included extensive 
warranties. 


The M/V Saratoga burned and sank on January 
16-17, 1986,° along with all of the property acquired by 
Saratoga from Madruga, except for the skiff, which was 
deliberately scuttled after the crew of the M/V Saratoga 
were rescued.’ Saratoga also lost the expected perfor- 
mance of the vessel — the catch of tuna aboard. Because 
the vessel sank before it was able to deliver the catch in 
proper condition to the cannery for sale, Saratoga lost the 
profit it expected to receive when it originally purchased 
the vessel.
