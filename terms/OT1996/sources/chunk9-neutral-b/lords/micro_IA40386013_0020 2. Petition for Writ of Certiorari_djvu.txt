STATEMENT 


Petitioner, Lords Landing Condominium Council of 
Unit Owners', (The Unit Owners), brought suit against 
Respondent Continental Insurance Company, (Continental), 
in the Circuit Court for Prince George's County, Maryland 
for breach of a Commercial Liability Insurance Policy. The 
unit owners had obtained a $1.1 million dollar judgment 
against Continental's insured for damages arising out of work 
performed by the insured’s subcontractors in constructing the 
Condominium. The Judgment against Continental's insured 
was based on misrepresentations and omissions under 
Maryland’s Consumer Protection statute and breach of 
implied and express statutory warranties under Maryland's 
Real Property Code.? The unit owners sued Continental 
under a state subrogation statue for failing to pay that 
judgment. 


Continental removed the action pursuant to 28 USC 
§ 1446(a) to the federal district court for Maryland. 
Jurisdiction of the district court was invoked because of 
diversity of citizenship pursuant 28 USC § 1332. 


Petitioner does nct have any parent companies or subsidiaries. 


Petitioners sued Continental under §§ 10-202 - 10-203 and 11-131 of 
Maryland's Real Property Code and §§ 13-301(1) 13-301(2), 13-301(3) 
and 13-301(9) of Maryland’s Consumer Protection Counts. The jury 
found for Continental's insured on each Consumer Protection Count that 
required scienter but the jury found against Continental's insured for 
misrepresentations, omissions, or statements by the insured regarding the 
quality, useful life or characteristics of the common elements. App. 25a - 
26a. The jury found for Petitioner on each of the express and implied 
wattanty counts. App. 24a - 25a. 


The threshold question in this case was whether the 
property damage giving rise to the $1.1 million verdict was 
caused by an occurrence. The district court applied an 
objective test for occurrence and held that as a matter of law, 
property damage that is the natural, probable consequences 
of poor workmanship and omissions is not caused by an 
occurrence as defined by the policy. App. 3a. The Court of 
Appeals for the Fourth Circuit affirmed the trial court on 
August 6, 1996 and it rejected the unit owners argument that 
Maryland State law required an occurrence to be determined 
by a subjective test that viewed the accident component of an 
occurrence from the prospective of whether the insured 
intended or expected the damage. App. 3a. 


(A) Intervening Controlling Decision By Maryland's 
Highest Court 


Of primary importance to the consideration of the 
questions presented in this case is the serious doubt that an 
intervening decision by Maryland's highest court casts on the 
validity of the Fourth Circuit's affirmance of the trial court. 
Maryland’s highest court, on July 26, 1996, issued a 
controlling decision on what constitutes an occurrence or 
accident under Maryland insurance law. Sheets v. Brethern 
Mutual, 342 Md. 634, 679 A.2d 540 (1996). Neither the 
parties nor the Fourth Circuit were aware of this decision 
prior to the Fourth Circuit's ruling on August 6, 1996. See 
part B infra. The following is a comparison of the Fourth 
Circuit's culing with the intervening Maryland decision. 


1. In its August 6, 1996 opinion the Fourth 
Circuit affirmed the trial court and defined the issue as 
follows: 


The district court held that as a matter of law, 


property damage that is the natural, probable 
consequence of poor workmanship and 
omissions is not caused by an occurrence as 
defined by the policy. The court noted that 
there was no occurrence whether it was the 
subcontractor or a general contractor that was 
involved. 


Appealing the district court's judgment the 
unit owners contend that “the accident 
component of an occurrence must be viewed 
subjectively, and where the injury is not 
expected or intended from the standpoint of 
the insured an occurrence takes place.” This 
contention, however, is based on the policy's 
exclusions and fails to take into account the 
policy's threshold requirement that property 
damage be caused by an “occurrence.” 


...Accordingly, we must first address whether 
the loss was caused by an occurrence or 
accident.

REASONS FOR GRANTING PETITION 
In its July 26, 1996 opinion Maryland's highest court 


rejected the natural probable consequences test for 
determining what constitutes an occurrence and explained 


12 


that if they were “to adopt an objective standard ... such 
insurance policies would be rendered all but meaningless.” 
Maryland's highest court clarified any confusion in its prior 
case law and it specifically rejected the test for “accident” 
applied in the very cases relied on by the district court and 
Fourth Circuit below. Compare Sheets, 342 Md. at 652 - 
654, 679 A.2d at 548-550 with the Fourth Circuits’ August 6, 
1996 Opinion in this case. App. la. 


The Circuit Court's failure to follow the law of the 
State of Maryland conflicts with well reasoned and long 
established principles of federalism. The Circuit Court's 
decision denied the unit owners the benefit of the laws of 
forum state and applied a test that rendered the policy of 
insurance virtually meaningless under Maryland law. In 
addition to the important questions of federalism that this 
case raises it also provides this court with a rare opportunity 
to offer guidance to federal courts on the orderly 
administration of state law in the federal judicial system. 


L The Failure Of The Court Of Appeals To Appl, 
Intervening Judicial Decisions Of Maryland's Highest 
Court Is In Conflict With Decisions Of This Court. 


In our system of federalism Erie mandates that a 
federal court sitting in diversity apply the substantive law of 
the forum state that is in existence at the time it renders its 
decision absent a federal statutory or constitutional directive 
to the contrary. Salve Regina College v. Russell, 499 U.S. 
225, 226, 111 S.Ct. 1217, 1218 (1991); Erie Railroad Co. v. 
Thompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 58 S.Ct. 817 (1938). Similarly 
where a federal court of appeals’ decision on a point of state 
law is cast in doubt by an intervening decision of a states 
highest court while the case is on direct review it has become 
the practice of this court to vacate and remand so that the 


13 


question can be decided by judges familiar with state law. 
Stutson v. U.S., __ U.S. __, 116 S.Ct 611, 612 (1996) (Scalia 
J. dissenting) (citing Huddleston v. Dwyer, 322 U.S. 232, 
237, 64 S.Ct. 1015, 1018 (1944), Lawrence On Behalf Of 
Lawrence vy. Chater, _ U.S. ____ 116 S.Ct. 604, 606-608 
(1996); See Vandenbark v. Owens-Illinois Glass Co., 311 
U.S. 538, 540-543, 61 S.Ct. 347, 349-350 (1941); Blaauw v. 
Grand Trunk Western R. Co., 380 U.S. 127, 85 S.Ct. 806 
(1965); Conner v. Simler, 367 U.S. 486, 81 S.Ct. 1679 
(1961); See also United States v. Security Industrial Bank, 
459 U.S. 70, 81, 103 S.Ct. 407, 413 (1982) (the principle 
that... judicial decisions operate retrospectively is familiar to 
every law student); Bradley v. Richmond School Board, 416 
U.S. 696, 711; 94 S.Ct 2006, 2016 (1974) (distinction 
between applying a change in the law before time to petition 
for certiorari has passed and after final judgment); Linkletter 
v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 622, 85 S.Ct. 1731, 1734 (1965) 
(distinction between cases still pending on direct review and 
finality of judgments). 


The failure of the Fourth Circuit to apply an 
intervening judicial decision of Maryland's highest court in 
a diversity case places its August 8, 1996 decision squarely 
in conflict with the above cited decisions of this court. This 
case presents a rare opportunity for this court to reinforce the 
principles of federalism upon which diversity jurisdiction is 
based and to enforce Erie's mandate. 


The unit owners have argued throughout this litigation 
that Maryland courts applied a subjective analysis to 
determine if an occurrence took place and the Fourth 
Circuit's decision denied the unit owners the benefit of 
applying the law of the forum state to the issues presented in 
this case. 


Il. This Case Raises An Important Question Of The 
Orderly Administration Of State Law In Federal 
Courts. 


Equall important, however, is the opportunity this 
case gives the court to review the orderly administration of 
state laws in federal courts. When an intervening judicial 
decision of a state's highest court comes down so shortly 
before a federal appeals court decision that it is not applied 
by the federal court, the federal court should rehear the case 
if the state court decision is brought to its attention before 
the time that a petition for certiorari is due in this Court. If 
the appellate court waits to see if this court grants, cert, 
vacates and remands, it will only diminish this court's scarce 
resources. See Lawrence On Behalf Of Lawrence v. Chater, 
— US. __, 116 S.Ct 604, 606 (1996); See also Robinson v. 
Story, 469 U.S. 1081, 105 S.Ct. 583 (1984). See also Braniff 
Airways, Inc. v. Curtiss Wright Corporation, 424 F.2d 427, 
430 (1970). 


Considering the difficulty of securing review in this 
court in diversity cases, lower federal courts rarely obtain 
guidance on the issue of orderly administration of state law 
in the federal system. This has led to conflicting opinions 
about when circuit court's should apply the ruling of this 
Court in Huddleston v. Dwyer, 322 U.S. 232, 236, 64 S.Ct. 
1015, 1017 (1944). The Second Circuit has recognized that 
this Court has not defined “precisely what the bounds of the 
term ‘sub judice’ might be” and it has viewed Huddleston as 
authority to rehear cases based on intervening state law even 
after Certiorari has been granted. Braniff Airways, Inc. v. 
Curtiss-Wright Corporation, 424 F.2d 427 at 429-430 (2nd 
Cir. 1970), Cert denied 400 U.S. 829 (1970). The Second 
Circuit has also cited Huddleston for the proposition that 
mandates should be recalled and rehearings granted when an 


15 


intervening decision of this Court comes down before a panel 
decision is rendered. Marino v. Ortiz, 888 F.2d 12, 13 (2nd 
Cir. 1989) Cert denied 495 U.S. 931 (1990); Davis v. Smith, 
607 F.2d 535, 540 (2nd Cir. 1978). 


The Ninth Circuit, at least, in cases where decisions 
of this Court are involved, will recall a mandate and rehear 
the case in light of intervening decisions that come down 
after the federal appeals court has ruled. Zipfel v. 
Halliburton Co., 861 F.2d 565, 567 (9th Cir. 1988). 


The First Circuit, however, has questioned the 
authority of an appeals court to ever recall a mandate and 
rehear a case in light of an intervening decision of a state 
court. Boston and Maine Corp. v. Town of Hampton, 7 F.3d 
281, 252 (1st Cir. 1993). Moreover, unlike the Second 
Circuit, the First Circuit does not see Huddleston as authority 
for applying state decisional law after the mandate has issued 
but before the time for filing a petition for certiorari has run. 
Id. 7 F.3d at 282. 


In the present case the Fourth Circuit has refused to 
recall its mandate even though the intervening state court 
decision came down before it rendered its opinion. This puts 
the Fourth Circuit in direct conflict with holdings of the 
Second Circuit and decisions of this Court involving 
“pending” cases. 


In Bradley v. Richmond School Board, 416 U.S. 696, 
711, 94 S.Ct. 2006, 2016 (1974) this Court held that changes 
in the law should be given effect while the case was pending. 
The Court further pointed out that pending cases were 
distinguished from final judgments by the lapse or exhaustion 
of the “availability of appeal” or the passing of the time to 
petition for certiorari. Id. Bradley, 416 U.S. at 711, 94 S.Ct. 


16 


at 2016 footnote 14. This holding was based in large part on 
cases involving retroactive application of state decisional law. 
Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp. v. Bonjorno, 494 U.S. 
827, 849, 110 S.Ct. 2006, 2016 (Scalia J. concurring).* 


The present case then gives this Court the opportunity 
to give guidance on the issue of whether or not intervening 
state judicial decisions should be applied by federal court's of 
appeal to cases that have not become “final” as the term is 
applied in Bradley. Moreover, granting certiorari allows this 
Court to offer guidance concerning the scope of the term 
“sub judice” used in Huddleston if it is in fact more 
circumscribed than Bradley's definition of “final.” In 
addition, this case gives this Court the opportunity to offer 
guidance on whether or not federal appeals courts should 
provide less difference to intervening state court decisions in 
Erie cases than they give to this Court's decisions on federal 
questions. 


The administration of state law in federal courts 
Should be uniform. Yet there is an apparent lack of 
uniformity among the circuits in applying intervening state 
decisions before the case becomes “final.” This case presents 
the rare opportunity for this Court to address these issues and 
the Court should take it.

OPINION 
PER CURIAM: 


The unit owners of Lords Landing Village 
Condominium (“unit owners”), having obtained a $1.1 
million judgment against the condominium developer, filed 
this action against the developer's insurance company, 
Continental Insurance Company (“Continental”) to compel 
Continental to pay the judgment. Applying Maryland law, 
the district court concluded that the policy issued by 
Continental did not cover the loss because it did not arise 
from an “occurrence” within the meaning of the policy. We 
affirm. 


In the underlying action, the unit owners sued the 
condominium developer, Wellington Homes, for numerous 
defects in the condominium complex, including a faulty fire 
protection sprinkler system, faulty insulation, and rotted wood 
resulting from a subcontractors failure to prime wood 
components prior to painting. A jury rendered a verdict in 
favor of the unit owners against Wellington Homes for 
breach of express and implied warranties and 
mistepresentations and awarded the unit owners $1.1 million. 


To collect the judgment, the unit owners instituted 
this action against Continental because it had issued a general 
liability policy covering Wellington during the applicable 
period. That policy provides payment for “those sums that 
[Wellington] becomes legally obligated to pay as damages 
because of ‘bodily injury’ or ‘property damage’ to which this 
insurance applies.” And property damage is covered only if 
it is caused by an “occurrence,” defined as “an accident, 


armel aaa 


Pde Rat its Oy + Nt ea

including continuous or repeated exposure to substantially 
the same general harmful conditions.” 


The district court held that as a matter of law, 
property damage that is the natural, probable consequence of 
poor workmanship and omissions is not caused by an 
occurrence as defined by the policy. The court noted that 
there was no occurrence whether it was the subcontractor or 
a general contractor that was involved. 


Appealing the district court's judgment, the unit 
owners contend that “the accident component of an 
occurrence must be viewed subjectively, and where the injury 
is not expected to intended from the standpoint of the insured 
an occurrence takes place.” This contention, however, is 
based on the policy's exclusions and fails to take into account 
the policy's threshold requirement that property damage be 
caused by an “occurrence.” If we were to interpret the policy 
as urged by the unit owners, we would be collapsing one of 
the policy's business risks exclusions into a threshold 
requirement for coverage. The policy in question does not 
provide coverage for all property damage, but only property 
damage that is caused by an “occurrence.” Only if that 
threshold coverage is crossed must the exclusion alluded to 
by the unit owners be addressed, i.e., the exclusion for 
property damage that was “expected or intended from the 
standpoint of the insured.” Accordingly, we must first 
address whether the loss was caused by an occurrence o 
accident. 


“Under Maryland law in the context of insurance law 
‘accident’ means an ‘undersigned, sudden and unexpected 
event, usually of an afflictive or unfortunate character, and 
often accompanied by a manifestation of force, but it does 


4a 
4 LORDS LANDING V. CONTINENTAL INSURANCE CO. 


not mean the natural and ordinary consequences of a 
negligent act.‘” JA Construction Corp. v. T&T Surveying, 
Inc., 822 F. Supp. 1213, 1215 (D.Md. 1993) (quoting Ed. 
Winkler & Son, Inc. v. Ohio Casualty Ins. Co., 441 A.2d 
1129, 1132 (Md. App. 1980) (quoting 7A Appleman, 
Insurance Law and Practice § 4492 at 17 (Berdal ed 
1979))) (holding that claims against contractor for work 
performed in reliance upon negligent work of another 
contractor did not constitute “occurrence” under general 
liability policy). Occurrence “does not include the normal, 
expected consequences of poor workmanship.” Reliance 
Ins. Co. v. Mogavero, 640 F. Supp. 84, 86 (D.Md. 1986). 


We agree with the district court that the damages 
awarded in the underlying action for breach of warranties and 
misrepresentations allegedly resulting from the poor 
workmanship of a subcontractor were not caused by an 
“occurrence” within the meaning of Continental's policy. 
"This is the stuff of the construction trade (and professional 
malpractice insurance), not a risk protected against by a 
general liability policy.” IA Construction Corp., 822 F. 
Supp. at 1215 (referring to one contractor's negligence 
causing liability of another contractor); see also Mogavero, 
640 F. Supp. at 85 (“[t]his case turns upon one overriding 
principle: that [insurer] issued a general liability policy, not 
a performance bond”); cf American Home Assurance Co. v. 
Osbourn, 422 A.2d 8, 13 (Md. App. 1980) (holding that 
action against service station operator for trespass and 
conversion did not constitute “occurrence”). 


Accordingly, we affirm the judgment of the district 


court.
