QUESTION PRESENTED 


The overbreadth of the question as framed by both petition- 
ers and the United States overstates respondents’ pre-emption 
challenge and misses the point of the decision of the court of 
appeals. Respondents do not claim and the court of appeals 
did not find that the New York statute is pre-empted as it 
applies to all income received by medical centers operated by 
ERISA plans but only when it taxes “payments and contribu- 
tions which were intended to pay for participants’ medical 
benefits” (App. 9). The correct formulation of the question, 
therefore, is as follows: 


Whether a state tax on the “gross receipts re- 
ceived from all patient care services and other oper- 
ating income” of hospitals licensed to operate in the 
State, insofar as it directly applies to payments and 
contributions for healthcare benefits furnished to 
plan participants and their beneficiaries by medical 
centers owned and operated by ERISA plans, 
“relate[s] to” those plans within the meaning of 
ERISA Section 514(a), 29 U.S.C. 1144(a) (1994), 
and is thus preempted?

INTRODUCTION 


In 1974 Congress enacted the Employee Retirement In- 
come Security Act (“ERISA”), 29 U.S.C. §§ 1001-1461 
(1994), a comprehensive regime to protect the interests of 
employees and their beneficiaries in private employee benefit 
plans. ERISA’s “crowning achievement” was the elimination 


2 


of state regulation of ERISA plans. Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, 
463 U.S. 85, 99 (1983). The issue in this case is whether 
ERISA’s express pre-emption clause is to be dethroned and 
the states let loose to tax ERISA plans. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Structure of the Fund 


Respondent NYSA-ILA Medical and Clinical Services 
Fund (the “Fund”) is a jointly administered, multiemployer, 
labor-management trust fund established in accordance with 
§ 302(c)(5) of the Labor Management Relations Act, 29 
U.S.C. § 186(c)(5) (1994), and pursuant to an Agreement and 
Declaration of Trust and Plan and collective bargaining 
agreements adopted by the New York Shipping Association, 
Inc. (“NYSA”) and the International Longshoremen’s Asso- 
ciation, AFL-CIO (“ILA”) (App. 75). NYSA is the multi- 
employer collective bargaining representative for employers 
of longshore workers in the Port of New York and New Jer- 
sey (the “Port”), and ILA is a labor organization representing 
those employees. Both organizations negotiate and administer 
labor contracts prescribing the terms and conditions of long- 
shore employment in the Port. Both appoint the individual 
trustees that manage the Fund.’ Both established the Fund 
through collective bargaining to create and administer an 
ERISA-regulated employee welfare benefit plan that provides 
medical, dental, and other healthcare benefits on an out- 
patient, ambulatory basis to over 20,000 eligible beneficiaries 
consisting of ERISA plan participants and their dependents 
(App. 75). 


Four classes of persons receive healthcare benefits from the 
Fund. The first and predominant class consists of longshore 
workers and retirees and their dependents, whose right to 
Fund benefits stems from longshore employment in the Port 
under NYSA-ILA labor contracts. The second class is

Financing of the Fund 


The healthcare benefits provided by the Fund through its 
medical centers are funded for the most part by employer and 
employee contributions that are prescribed in collective bar- 
gaining agreements. The medical centers’ operating budgets, 
which total more than $19 million annually (C.A. App. 90, 
97), are financed almost entirely from these contributions. 
Some employer contributions are made to the Fund directly 
and are then funneled by the Fund to the centers to pay their 
operating expenses. Other employer contributions are paid 
directly to the medical centers (App. 77). 


Employers of longshore workers are obligated by the 
NYSA-ILA collective bargaining agreement to pay contribu- 
tions that fund fringe benefits through an assessment imposed 
on cargo and containers through the Port. These 
assessments fund all employee fringe benefits for longshore 
workers, including pension, welfare, and medical and clinical 
benefits. The portion of the assessments that fund medical and 
clinical benefits is paid to the Fund on a weekly basis. The 
Fund then transfers to each center an amount sufficient to 


meet its weekly expenses (App. 77). 


A second source of employer contributions comes from 
various ILA local unions which pay to the Fund directly man- 
hour contributions on behalf of their officers and employees. 
These contributions in turn form part of the monies trans- 
ferred weekly to the medical centers on an as-need basis 
(App. 78). 


Outside employers pay man-hour contributions for the 
medical and clinical benefits provided to their employees, 
who are eligible to be treated only at the medical center near- 
est their workplace. Each outside employer has historically 
paid its contributions directly to the medical center where its 
employees are treated (App. 78). 


Waterfront security guards in the Port are participants in 
their own ERISA welfare plan, the NYSA-PPGU Welfare 
Fund, which provides medical and clinical benefits for the 


and their dependents. Excessive costs forced the 
NYSA-PPGU Welfare Fund to close its own medical center 
in 1988. It entered into a contractual arrangement with the 
Fund to allow port guards and their dependents to be treated 
at the medical centers operated by the Fund. The NYSA- 
PPGU Welfare Fund pays for medical and dental services 
rendered to its participants on a fee-for-service basis (App. 
78). 


The final source of contributions, made only to the Fund’s 
medical center in Brooklyn, New York, consists of receipts 
from workers’ compensation insurance carriers. The Brooklyn 
Medical Center is authorized by N.Y. WorK. Comp. LAW 
§ 13-c(3) (McKinney 1992) to treat Fund participants who 
have been injured on the job and are covered by workers’ 
for the medical services it provides to plan participants, all of 
whom are eligible for treatment at the center. These workers’ 
compensation offset the costs of healthcare benefits 
which the Fund must provide and which would have had to be 
financed by contributions from the same employers paying for 
the workers’ compensation coverage (App. 78-79). 


In light of dramatic cost increases experienced in recent 
years, the Fund had to change its traditional method of being 
funded solely by employer contributions. In 1986 the NYSA- 
ILA labor contract inaugurated a new method of employee 
contributions. To help defray rising costs, the labor contract 
imposed a prescription charge of $2.00 per prescription on 
each beneficiary to whom prescription drugs are dispensed at 
any medical center. This was the first time that any obligation 
for medical benefits was placed directly upon the beneficiar- 
ies of the Fund. It produced revenue of approximately $1 mil- 
lion per annum (App. 79; C.A. App. 105). 


Costs continued to escalate at an alarming rate. As a result, 
in 1990 the longshore labor contract imposed additional obli- 
gations on Fund participants. A $20 per-family annual regis- 
tration fee was instituted, and the prescription charge 
was increased to $5.00. Even these additional employee

experienced by the Fund. Consequently, in 1992 the Fund was 
forced to close its Hoboken, New Jersey medical center (App. 
79).

Fund’s ERISA Challenge 


The Fund filed reports and paid the HFA for the first eleven 
months of 1991 (App. 80-82, 84-96; C.A. App. 134-41). The 
Fund excluded from taxable receipts the more than $19 mil- 
lion it disburses to the medical centers to pay their operating 
expenses. None of these remittances relate to the furnishing of 
services for particular patient care or constitute income from 
the operations of the centers. These transfers are no more than 
the utilization of employer contributions to provide the 
healthcare benefits furnished by the Fund. These weekly 
transfers of employer contributions are akin to grants that are 
nontaxable under New York law. See N.Y. PuB. HEALTH LAw 
§ 2807-d(3\(d) (McKinney 1993). While the State has not yet 
contested the exclusion from the tax of Fund remittances to 
the centers, see PB at 5; SG at 4 n.3, any future change in the 
State's position could expose the Fund to HFA tax liabilities 
of about $120,000 per year. 


The Fund does not contest the HFA in its entirety, only its 
application to contributions or other payments for healthcare 
benefits provided by the Fund through its medical centers. 

, the Fund does not challenge imposition of the 
HFA on miscellaneous revenue earned by the medical centers 
for activities that are unrelated to the healthcare benefits pro- 
ous income includes proceeds from the sale of waste or scrap, 
fees for reproducing medical records, and supplier rebates and 
commissions (App. 82). 


The Fund's ERISA pre-emption challenge in this case is 
limited to New York’s attempt to assess two categories of re- 
ceipts paid directly to the Fund’s medical centers: 

1) Contributions to fund the healthcare benefits 
provided by the Fund; and 

2) Payments made for benefits provided to ERISA 
plan participants and their beneficiaries. 


The first category involves amounts mandated by the terms 
of applicable collective bargaining agreements for the funding 


of the healthcare benefits provided by the Fund (App. 78). 
This category includes both employer and employee contri- 
butions that are required to be paid whether or not the benefi- 
ciaries for whom the are made ever receive 
healthcare benefits from the Fund’s medical centers. The 
employer contributions consist entirely of man-hour contri- 
butions from non-longshore, outside employers (App. 78); the 
employee contributions consist of prescription charges and 
annual registration fees imposed on plan beneficiaries through 
the collective bargaining process to help defray the rising 
healthcare costs encountered by the Fund (App. 79). 


The second category encompasses payments received di- 
rectly by the Fund’s medical centers for benefits provided. It 
includes payments from the NYSA-PPGU welfare plan for 
healthcare benefits provided to its beneficiaries and payments 
received from workers’ compensation insurance carriers for 
healthcare benefits provided to Fund participants injured on 
the job (App. 78-79). 


The receipts in both categories, which totalled about $1.16 
million for the first eleven months of 1991, were received di- 
rectly by the medical centers and were included in the HFA 
reports as taxable amounts for which the Fund paid the HFA 
tax of about $7,000 (App. 80-82). The Fund has since restruc- 
tured its payment procedures. Since early 1992 the registra- 
tion fees and the payments from the NYSA-PPGU Welfare 
Fund have been remitted to the Fund, not to the medical cen- 
ters (App. 82-83). 


The Fund protested the HFA on ERISA pre-emption 
grounds (App. 82, 97-102). When the State of New York de- 


nied the protest (C.A. App. 147-48), the Fund ceased payment 
of the tax and commenced this lawsuit both for a declaratory 
judgment that ERISA pre-empts the HFA to the extent that it

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


There are certain connections between state laws and 
ERISA plans that are so clear and unmistakable that no pro- 
longed and complicated analysis is necessary to conclude that 
these laws relate to the plans and so are pre-empted by 
ERISA. A state law that directly taxes contributions and other 
payments for healthcare benefits provided by ERISA plans 
falls within that category. To deny that the tax relates is to 
defy reality. 


The careful analysis, as prescribed by this Court in Travel- 
ers, of Congressional will by reference to the objectives of 
ERISA, its legislative history, and the language of its pre- 
emption clause yields the same result. Congress did not intend 
to permit state taxation of ERISA plans. In enacting ERISA 


Congress rejected a request by the Executive Branch to ex- 
clude state tax laws from ERISA pre-emption. Indeed, state 


11 


taxes are expressly included within ERISA’s broad pre- 
emption provision. True, state tax laws are pre-empted insofar 
as they “relate” to ERISA plans. But if a tax upon benefits 
and contributions — the very essence of ERISA plans — does 
not relate to the plans, it is difficult to conceive of a law that 
does. No case has ever ruled otherwise. Pre-emption of state 
taxation of contributions and other payments for healthcare

PRE-EMPTION OF A DIRECT TAX ON 
ERISA PLANS DOES NOT THREATEN ALL 
STATE HEALTHCARE REGULATION 


Ultimately the State and its allies take refuge in prophesies 
of gloom and doom. They predict that if this Court rules that 
the Fund is not subject to the New York HFA, it will mean 
that ERISA plans will be free of a myriad of state regulations 
affecting all kinds of benefits provided by ERISA plans from 
healthcare to legal services. See PB at 20; SG at 29; AG at 6- 
10; HA at 14-16; AFSCME at 11, 13; NGA at 15-16. They 
conjure up images of unqualified nurses, unprotected chil- 
dren, unethical lawyers, and unembalmed bodies if the deci- 
sion of the court of appeals is reversed. Some even foretell of 
a mass emancipation from state regulation of virtually all 
healthcare providers through the device of transforming them- 
selves into ERISA plans. See HA at 9-11. 


The “tenuous, remote or peripheral” standard, see Shaw, 
463 U.S. at 100 n. 21, disposes of most types of state regula- 
tion. The focus of this standard is the nature of the state law’s 
effect on ERISA plans. When the state regulation touches the 
plan only slightly on the outer limits of its activities, the state 
law’s connection to the plan may well be too tangential to 
satisfy the “relate to” test that triggers pre-emption. 


The “tenuous, remote or peripheral” standard assures 
that the Congressional objective embodied in ERISA’s 


31 


pre-emption clause is properly effectuated. Pre-emption is 
designed to proscribe state regulation when the state law re- 
lates to an ERISA plan qua an employee. benefit plan, ie., 
when the state law affects the very activities and functions 
that make the employee benefit plan what it is. Fort Halifax 
Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1, 7 (1987) (“ERISA’s pre- 
emption provision does not refer to state laws relating to 
employee benefits’, but to state laws relating to ‘employee 
benefit plans ’” (emphasis in original)). 


Obviously, Congress could not have intended to shield 
plans from the normal incidents of state regulation that affect 
private enterprises generally. ERISA plans do not operate in a 
vacuum. A necessary incident of their activities requires them 
to perform functions that are not peculiar to employee benefit 
plans but are carried on by other private entities. ERISA plans 
hire employees, rent office space, and purchase goods and 
services. Congress could not have intended ERISA’s pre- 
emption clause to foreclose state regulation of employee 
benefit plans when they act not as plans but as employers, les- 
sees, or purchasers. Cf Mackey, 486 U.S. at 833 (ERISA 
plans are not relieved from state law liability for unpaid rents, 
failure to pay creditors, or tortious conduct). By the same to- 
ken state taxes and assessments that do not implicate the ad- 
ministration of employee benefit plans but arise as a result of 
transactions which any other enterprise might engage in do 
not trigger pre-emption because they are too remote, tenuous, 
or peripheral. Sales taxes, utility taxes, and gasoline taxes are 
some of the state levies that would fall into this non-pre- 
empted category. 


The only real problem arises with respect to those state 
regulations directly involving healthcare: staffing require- 
ments, nursing and physician standards, sanitary regulations 
involving disposal of medical waste, etc. These provisions 
which apply to plans as the providers of healthcare benefits 
are affecting the plans qua plans. It does not take Jamesian 
metaphysics to see that they relate to ERISA plans. Travelers, 
115 S. Ct. at 1677. Nor can one honestly say that the 


32 


relationship is tenuous, remote or peripheral, since it goes to 
the essence of the plans’ ERISA status. 


It is precisely in this area that the Court’s decision in 
Travelers offers the solution. The touchstone is the intent of 
Congress. The starting point of that analysis is the presump- 
tion that the exercise of historic state police powers is not pre- 
empted. Travelers observed that there was no indication of a 
clear Congressional intent in ERISA to pre-empt traditional 
state healthcare regulation. None of these regulatory measures 
is specifically mentioned in ERISA’s pre-emption provision. 
It would appear, therefore, that the regulations about which 
the State and its allies are so concerned would survive. But 
that is not this case. This case involves not healthcare regula- 
tion but taxation, and as to taxation the ERISA pre-emption 
clause does contain an explicit expression of Congressional 
intent to pre-empt. As to the other types of regulation, which 
are the subject of the doomsaying of the State and its allies, 
the question of their susceptibility to ERISA pre-emption 
must be decided on a case-by-case basis. What is important 
here, however, is that it does not necessarily follow, that, if 
this Court invalidates a state tax, which Congress expressly 
included within the ambit of ERISA pre-emption, on the very 
activities that make the plan a plan, all peripheral state regu- 
lation of plan activities must also fall. 


A far more realistic prediction of mischief that might fol- 
low upon a decision of this Court arises if the Second Cir- 
cuit’s decision should be reversed. In that event the states will 
have been given the green light to directly tax the contribu- 
tions and benefits of ERISA plan. If the arguments of the 
State and its allies are accepted, it is difficult to conceive of 
any state tax that would be pre-empted. After all, state taxes, 
especially income taxes, are laws of general applicability in- 
volving traditional exercises of state police power that affect 
plans not as plans but as taxpayers. The states’ appetites for 
money are without limit. ERISA plans provide an available 
fatted calf. Ominous stirrings already appear in the amicus 
brief to this Court filed on behalf of more than half the states 
of the Union. See AG at 12-14.

CONCLUSION 


The court of appeals in this case, as did every other tribunal 
that has entertained the same question, applied established 
precedent to invalidate the immediate and direct imposition of 
a state tax levy on contributions to, and benefit payments of, 
an ERISA plan. The decision below is perfectly consistent 
with the analytical methodology adopted by this Court in 
Travelers, since it gives effect to Congressional intent, the 
touchstone for ERISA pre-emption. It rests squarely upon a 
clear and explicit Congressional policy to pre-empt state tax 
impositions on ERISA plan contributions and benefits. The 
decision below, therefore, is in perfect harmony with estab- 
lished principles of ERISA pre-emption law and should not be 
disturbed. The judgment of the court of appeals should be af- 
firmed.
