STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
A. The Hospital Assessment 


In 1990, in order to offset increased health care costs, 
the New York State Legislature enacted section 2807-d 
of the New York Public Health Law, referred to by the 
courts below as the Health Facility Assessment or “HFA ” 
1990 N.Y. Laws, ch. 938. The assessment was enacted 
as an alternative to substantial Medicaid payment reduc- 
tions which would otherwise have been required (JA 51, 
59). 


The assessment is charged to all “hospitals” which are 
defined to encompass generally any facility engaged in 
providing services under the supervision of a physician or 
dentist, and includes diagnostic centers and treatment 
centers, N.Y. Pub. Health Law § 2801(1) (McKinney 
1993). The assessment, which is six-tenths of one per- 
cent for diagnostic and treatment centers, applies with 
limited exceptions to a covered facility's gross receipts 
from patient care services and other operating income. 
N.Y. Pub. Health Law §§ 2807-d(1)(a), 2807-d(2)(c) 
(McKinney 1993). Assessable income has been further 
defined by the New York State Department of Health to 
include all cash receipts from patient care services irre- 
spective of payment source, investment income, and other 
operating income, such as non-patient food sales, parking 
revenue, medical records fees, and the like (JA 65). 
Reports of assessable income and the assessments are re- 
quired to be forwarded on a monthly basis to an admin- 
istrative agent appointed by the New York State Depart-

B. Prior Proceedings 


On April 16, 1992, the Fund commenced this action 
for declaratory and injunctive relief and for a refund of 
the assessments already paid on the ground that the hos- 
pital assessment is preempted by ERISA (JA 18-23). 


The parties cross-moved for summary judgment pursuant 
to Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. 


The district court granted the State’s motion, determin- 
ing that the hospital assessment is not preempted by 
ERISA because it is a law of general application with 
only an incidental economic impact on ERISA plans 
(21a).° Pointing to the de minimis amount of the assess- 
ment, the court distinguished this case from The Travelers 
Insurance Co. v. Cuomo, 813 F. Supp. 996 (S.D.N.Y.), 
aff'd, 14 F.3d 708 (2d Cir. 1993), rev’d sub nom. New 
York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans 
v. Travelers Insurance Co., 115 S. Ct. 1671 (1995), in 
which three New York State surcharges imposed on hos- 
pital bills of varying amounts of 9%, 11%, and 13%, 
depending on the identity of the third-party payor, were 
invalidated as ERISA-preempted (22a). 


On June 23, 1994, the Second Circuit reversed the dis- 
trict court. The court stated that because “the HFA

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 


I. THE DECISION OF THE COURT OF APPEALS IS 
IN DIRECT CONFLICT WITH SUPREME COURT 
PRECEDENT 


This Court vacated the prior judgment in this case and 
remanded it to the court of appeals for reconsideration in 
light of its opinion in Travelers. The Second Circuit, 
however, did not actually apply the Travelers analysis in 
the decision. Instead, the court simply distinguished this 
case from Travelers on the basis that “there is no third 
party between the plan and the tax” (3a). An actual ap- 
plication of the Travelers analysis in this case demon- 
strates that the Second Circuit’s opinion on remand is in 
conflict with this Court’s decision in Travelers. Moreover, 
the court’s reliance upon the lack of a third party between 
the generally applicable hospital assessment and the Fund 
as the basis for preemption is in conflict with Mackey 
v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 
825 (1988). In Mackey, this Court held that a generally 
applicable garnishment statute was not preempted despite 
its direct imposition of legal obligations upon an ERISA 
plan which resulted in economic costs and administrative 
burdens to the plan. 


8 


In Travelers, this Court held that state laws which have 
only an indirect economic impact upon the choices made 
by ERISA plans with regard to the provision of benefits 
are not preempted by ERISA. Construing ERISA’s pre- 
emption provision in light of its objective, “to avoid a 
multiplicity of regulation, in order to permit the nationally 
uniform administration of employee benefit plans,” 115 
S. Ct. at 1677-78, this Court held that state laws relate 
to plans and are preempted when they “mandate[] em- 
ployee benefit structures or their administration,” or pro- 
vide “alternative enforcement mechanisms.” 115 S. Ct. 
at 1678. 


The Court found, however, that the challenged sur- 
charges, which made Blue Cross a more attractive insur- 
ance alternative, only had “an indirect economic effect on 
choices made by insurance buyers, including ERISA 
plans.” 115 S. Ct. at 1679. Such economic influence 
“does not bind plan administrators to any particular 
choice and thus function as a regulation of the ERISA 
plan itself.” 115 S. Ct. at 1679. Thus, ERISA does not 
preempt state laws that have an impact “on the costs of 
benefits.” 115 S. Ct. at 1679. This Court also noted that 
“nothing in the language of the Act or the context of its 
passage indicates that Congress chose to displace general 
health care regulation, which historically has been a matter 
of local concern.” 115 S. Ct. at 1680. 


The Second Circuit’s original decision that the HFA 
is preempted rested upon three bases: first, that the 
HFA was unlike other laws of general applicability be- 
cause it was health care regulation and therefore affected 
plans in “the realm where ERISA plans must operate,” 
(12a); second, that the HFA directly depleted plan as- 
sets intended to provide health care benefits, (12a); and 
third, that a restructuring of the plan to avoid the tax 
would interfere with uniform administration of the plan 


9 


on an interstate basis, (13a). The first and third of these 
bases have since been explicitly rejected by this Court 
in Travelers. This Court found no congressional intent 
to have ERISA generally displace health care regulation, 
115 S. Ct. at 1680, and also held that state laws do not 
relate to plans if they do not mandate plan decisions, even 
if the law’s economic effect results in plans if they do 
not mandate plan decisions, even if the law’s economic 
effect results in plans making different choices in different 
states. 115 S. Ct. at 1679. 


The application of the Travelers analysis does not yield 
a different result in this case simply because the tax is 
applied to an ERISA plan-owned medical center and, 
therefore, the impact is characterized as “direct.” In 
Travelers, this Court focused upon whether the surcharges’ 
economic impact upon plans mandated benefits or struc- 
tures, not upon whether the economic impact was directly 
or indirectly imposed upon plans. Like the surcharges in 
Travelers, the HFA does not bind plan administrators to 
a particular choice of how to provide benefits nor does it 
have anything other than an economic impact on plans.* 
The HFA arguably has an economic impact on a plan’s 
decision to provide medical services in-kind, through a 
plan-owned medical center, rather than simply paying for 
medical services that plan participants have received else- 
where. However, in Travelers, this Court held that the 
surcharges were not preempted because they simply bear 
on the cost of benefits and the relative costs of competing 
insurance to provide them. The HFA, as it is applied to 
ERISA plan-owned medical centers, simply bears on the 
cost of benefits and the relative costs of competing 
methods to provide them. 


Nothing in Travelers supports the conclusion that valid 
laws, such as the surcharges in Travelers, which indirectly

increase costs to plans through their application to third 
parties, become invalid when the plans happen to own or 
operate the third party. Indeed, the HFA is very similar 
to the 9% assessment in Travelers which is imposed upon 
the inpatient hospital costs paid by health maintenance 
organizations (HMOs). Under the Second Circuit’s ra- 
tionale, the 9% would be preempted if an HMO was 
owned by an ERISA plan. However, none of the reasons 
articulated by this Court for finding the 9% not pre- 
empted are in any way diminished simply because a plan 
chooses to own an HMO, rather than using the more 
usual method of purchasing its services. 


The Second Circuit’s decision rests solely on its char- 
acterization of the HFA as having a direct impact upon 
the Fund. This Court, however, has not construed ERISA 
as preempting laws simply because they directly operate 
upon plans. Even prior to Travelers, this Court, in 
Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 
U.S. 825 (1988), upheld a Georgia garnishment statute 
that directly operated upon plans. Like the HFA, the 
garnishment law was one of general application, but when 
it applied to plans it directly regulated their administra- 
tive procedures and, as a result, imposed costs. As 
garnishees, plan trustees were required to become parties 
to state lawsuits. They were also subject to state sub- 
stantive law concerning spendthrift trusts. 486 U.S. at 
831. Yet this Court held that these administrative bur- 
dens and costs, which were the result of a direct applica- 
tion of the statute to plans, were not enough to trigger 
preemption. 486 U.S. at 831. Moreover, in Mackey, 
this Court noted that run-of-the-mill state lawsuits against 
ERISA plans for unpaid rent, etc. were not preempted 
by ERISA. 486 U.S. at 833. Like the HFA, those legal 
obligations are applied directly to ERISA plans as well. 
The Second Circuit’s failure to apply the Travelers analy- 
sis, and its reliance on a factor which this Court rejected 
as a basis for preemption in Mackey, has resulted in a 
decision in conflict with these cases. The Court should 
grant certiorari to resolve those conflicts.

Il. THE DECISION OF THE COURT OF APPEALS IS 
ALSO IN CONFLICT WITH A DECISION OF THE 
EIGHTH CIRCUIT. 


The decision of the Second Circuit is also in direct 
conflict with the decision of the Eighth Circuit in Boyle 
v. Anderson, 68 F.3d 1093 (8th Cir. 1995), cert. denied 
sub nom. Boyle v. Smith, 64 U.S.L.W. 3511 (March 18, 
1996). In Boyle, the court found that a Minnesota pro- 
vider tax on the gross patient revenues of hospitals is not 
preempted by ERISA. The statute contemplates that the 
tax will be paid by third party purchasers. It includes a 
pass-through provision that authorizes providers to trans- 
fer the expense as an addition to the amount due the 
provider pursuant to any provider contract. 68 F.3d at 
1098 (citing Minn. Stat. § 295.582 (Supp. 1993)). The 
law also imposes direct obligations upon plans to pay the 
tax. It provides: “all third party purchasers of health 
care services . . . must pay the transferred expense.” 68 
F.3d at 1098 (citing Minn. Stat. § 295.582 (Supp. 
1993)). Accordingly, no third party stands between 
ERISA plans and the legal obligation to pay the tax. 
Despite this, the court rejected a self-insured ERISA 
plan’s challenge to the law. 


In Boyle, the Eighth Circuit correctly examined the 
nature of the impact of the pass-through provisions of the 
law, rather than focusing on the direct or indirect source 
of the impact. First, the court correctly viewed the law 
as one of general application because the statute does not 
refer to ERISA plans, single them out for different treat- 
ment or apply to them differently than non-ERISA plans. 
68 F.3d at 1101. Recognizing that the purpose of ERISA 
preemption is to permit nationally uniform plan adminis- 
tration, the Eighth Circuit examined the impact of the law 
on plan administration. The court held that the law only 
causes plans to pay more for healthcare services already 
covered. 68 F.3d at 1103. While it may add to the 
differences in the price of health care services from state 
to state, it will not require plans to be redesigned or 


12 


cause inefficiencies. 68 F.3d at 1105. With respect to 
the economic impact of the law, the Eighth Circuit cited 
this Court’s Travelers decision extensively and noted that 
the impact of the surcharges in that case was much 
greater than Minnesota’s 2% provider tax. 68 F.3d at 
1108. Weighing these factors, the Eighth Circuit con- 
cluded that the law has oaly a tenuous, remote and 
peripheral impact on plans which did not warrant preemp- 
tion. 


The decision in Boyle cannot be reconciled with the 
Second Circuit's decision in this case. Both laws involve 
a generally applicable provider tax where, in certain situ- 
ations, the legal obligation to pay the tax falls direcly or

plans. In Boyle, the law had a direct impact on ERISA 
plans by requiring self-funded plans to pay the tax ex- 
transferred. The HFA has a direct impact

should grant a writ of certiorari to resolve this conflict.

Preemption of provider taxes as they are applied to 
ERISA plan-owned providers could also threaten the 
validity of state regulatory schemes under federal law. 
As part of its 1991 amendments to the federal Medicaid 
Act, Congress explicitly authorized states to use taxes of

The breadth of the Second Circuit’s decision is sweep- 
ing. The assessment was invalidated because, the court

tax imposed pursuant to the HFA” (3a). Were this view

world and goes far beyond what Congress intended in 
enacting the preemption provision. The Court should

District Court for the Southern District of New York

(“ERISA”), 29 U.S.C. § 1001 ef seqg., did not preempt

York’s imposition of a surcharge on hospital bills paid by 
commercial insurers and Health Maintenance Organiza-

Shield insurers. The statute at issue in Travelers did not 
impose surcharges on ERISA plans themselves, but on 
insurance carriers providing health insurance coverage 
to the plans and their beneficiaries. The surcharge con-

ble for the balance of any bill an ERISA plan or insur-

cluded that “a law operating as an indirect source of 
merely economic influence on administrative decisions [by 
ERISA plans], as here, should not suffice to trigger pre- 
emption.” Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1680. 


The facts of the present case distinguish it from Trav- 
elers, because, unlike in Travelers, there is no third party

assets directly, and thus has an immediate impact on the

HFA’s direct economic impact upon the Fund as a basis 
for finding ERISA pre-emption nor precludes a deter-

ERISA plans is sufficient for a finding of pre-emption.

The Trustees of NYSA-ILA Medical & Clinical Serv-

laration that the Employee Retirement Income Security 
Act (“ERISA”), 29 U.S.C. § 1001 et seq., preempts New 
York Public Health Law § 2807-d to the extent the latter 
provision taxes contributions and other payments for 
health care benefits received by two medical centers oper- 
ated by the Fund. The Fund now appeals from a judg- 
ment of the United States District Court for the South- 
ern District of New York (John S. Martin, Jr., Judge) hold- 
ing that ERISA does not preempt the state tax and grant- 


ing summary judgment to defendant state health 
Officials. We reverse. ? a4 


BACKGROUND 


The Fund is an ERISA-regulated multiemployer, labor- 
management trust fund established through collective bar- 
gaining to administer an employee welfare benefit plan 
that provides medical, dental, and other health car- serv- 
ices to ERISA plan participants, consisting primarily of 
longshore workers, and their dependents. The Fund pro- 
vides these health care benefits through three medical 
centers that it owns and operates, two in New York and 
one in New Jersey. The centers provide treatment ex- 
Clusively to plan participants and beneficiaries. 

_In 1990, the State of New York enacted a Health Fa- 
cility Assessment (“HFA”), which imposed a tax on the 
gross receipts from patient care services and general op- 
erations of all hospitals in New York State. N.Y. Pub. 
Health Law § 2807-d. The HFA was enacted to help 
eliminate the State’s budget deficit for the 1990-91 fiscal 
year. The HFA became effective January 1, 1991, and 
was supposed to expire on April 1, 1992, but has subse- 
quently been amended to remain in force on and after 
that date. 1992 N.Y. Laws, ch. 41, §§ 54, 163. The 
HFA applies to a wide range of medical facilities, includ- 


8a 


ing diagnostic and treatment centers such as the medical 
centers operated by the Fund. N.Y. Pub. Health Law 
§ 2801(1). The amount of the assessment, which varies 
by type of facility, is six-tenths of one percent for the 
Fund's medical centers. Jd. § 2807-d(2)(c). For pur- 
poses of the assessment, “gross receipts” are defined as 
“all monies received for or on account of hospital and 
health related services” with only limited exceptions. /d. 
§ 2807-d(3)(c). 


The Fund's medical centers receive income from a va- 
riety of sources. The bulk of their costs are covered by 
employer contributions remitted to the Fund which are 
then transferred to the centers on an as-needed basis to 
pay for the centers’ payroll and other operating expenses. 
However, the centers obtain additional revenue from cer- 
tain employer payments made directly to the centers, 
employee co-payments, other fee-for-service payments, 
patient registration fees, payments from workers’ compen- 
sation carriers, and such miscellaneous sources as the 
sale of scrap or waste, fees for reproducing medical rec- 
ords, and supplier rebates. For the period from January 
1, 1991 to November 30, 1991, the Fund’s two New 
York medical centers reported combined assessable in- 
come of $1,177,670 and paid assessments totalling 
$7,066. The Fund discontinued filing HFA reports with 
the New York State Department of Health after Novem- 
ber 1991 and has paid no assessments since that time. 


The medical centers did not include in their assessable 
income monies transferred from the Fund to the centers, 
and the State defendants have not contested the exclusion 
of these payments in this case. However, the question of 
whether monies transferred from the Fund to the centers 
are assessable has never, to our knowledge, been litigated 
or definitively resolved. Since early 1992, the Fund has 
restructured its payment procedures so that certain em- 
ployer contributions and registration fees that were for- 
merly paid directly to the medical centers are now re- 
mitted to the Fund itself. The Fund believes that this 


9a 


restructuring avoids the effect of the HFA, but is unwill- 
ing to redirect to the Fund all payments now received 
by the medical centers until this case has been decided. 


The Fund instituted this suit in April 1992 asserting 
that ERISA preempts the HFA insofar as it imposes a 
levy on contributions and payments for health care bene- 
fits provided by the Fund. The Fund does not challenge 
imposition of the HFA on the centers’ miscellaneous reve- 
nue earned from activities unrelated to the health care 
services provided to participants and beneficiaries, such 
as the income derived from the sale of scrap, records 
reproduction fees, and supplier rebates. The Fund sought 
declaratory and injunctive relief as well as restitution of 
a substantial portion of the HFA assesments it had pre- 
viously paid. 

On cross-motions for summary judgment, the district 
court ruled that the HFA is a tax of general application 
that is not preempted by ERISA because it has only an 
incidental impact on ERISA plans. The district court 
reasoned that the minimal tax imposed by the HFA “is 
not great enough to pose a serious economic threat to the 
plan which might trigger preemption.” NYSA-ILA Medi- 
cal & Clinical Servs. Fund v. Axelrod, 92 Civ. 2279 
(JSM), slip op. at 8 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 1992). Ac- 
cordingly, the district court judgment dismissing the action 
and this appeal ensued. We review the district court's 
decision de novo. See Westinghouse Elec. Corp. v. New 
York City Transit Auth., 14 F.3d 818, 821 (2d Cir. 
1994). 

DISCUSSION 


ERISA was enacted in 1974 “to promote the interests 
of employees and their beneficiaries in employee benefit 
plans,” including both pension and welfare plans. Shaw 
v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85, 90 (1983). An 
“employee welfare benefit plan” is defined in the statute 
as 


10a 


any plan, fund, or program . . . established or main- 
tained by an employer or by an employee organiza- 
tion, or by both, to the extent that such plan, fund, 
or program was established or is maintained for the 
purpose of providing for its participants or their 
beneficiaries, through the purchase of insurance or 
otherwise, . . . medical, surgical, or hospital care or 
benefits, or benefits in the event of sickness, acci- 
dent, disability, death or unemployment... . 


29 U.S.C. § 1002(1). The parties do not dispute that the 
Fund qualifies as an employee welfare benefit plan that 
fulfills its institutional mission by providing medical bene- 
fits directly to plan participants and their beneficiaries 
through Fund-operated medical centers. 


One of ERISA’s main objectives was to eliminate state 
regulation of employee benefit plans. See Ingersoll-Rand 
Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133, 138 (1990); Shaw, 
463 U.S. at 99 (quoting legislative history). To accom- 
plish this, Congress enacted an express preemption clause 
that is “deliberately expansive.” Pilot Life Ins. Co. v. 
Dedeaux, 481 U.S. 41, 46 (1987). It provides that 
ERISA “shall supersede any and all State laws insofar as 
they may now or hereafter relate to any employee benefit 
plan” covered by the law. 29 U.S.C. § 1144(a). The 
Supreme Court has observed that “{a] law ‘relates to’ an 
employee benefit plan, in the normal sense of the phrase, 
if it has a connection with or reference to such a plan.” 
Shaw, 463 U.S. at 96-97. Preemption thus applies even 
where a state law was not specifically designed to regu- 
late the terms and conditions of employee benefits plans 
or does not relate to those subjects covered by ERISA, 
such as reporting, disclosure, and fiduciary responsibilities. 
See Ingersoll-Rand Co., 498 U.S. at 137-39; FMC Corp. 
v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52, 58-59 (1990); Shaw, 463 US. 
at 98. Indeed, even where a state law has no express 
link to an employee benefit plan, it can be preempted 
“‘insofar as’ the law applies to benefit plans in particu- 
lar cases.” Morgan Guar. Trust Co. v. Tax Appeals Tri- 


lla 


bunal, 80 N.Y.2d 44, 49 (1992) (quoting Shaw, 463 
U.S. at 97 n.17). 


ERISA preemption is not without limitations, however. 
The Supreme Court has recognized that ERISA preemp- 
tion will not apply to state laws that “affect employee 
benefit plans in too tenuous, remote, or peripheral a man- 
ner to warrant a finding that the law ‘relates to’ the plan.” 
Shaw, 463 U.S. at 100 n.21. When it announced this lim- 
itation, the Court “express[ed] no views about where it 
would be appropriate to draw the line,” id., but it has sub- 
sequently observed that this limitation will apply “with 
many laws of general applicability,” District of Columbia 
v. Greater Wash. Bd. of Trade, 113 S. Ct. 580, 583 n.1 
(1992). There are a number of cases that bear out this 
observation. See, e.g., Mackey v. Lanier Collection 
Agency & Serv., Inc., 486 U.S. 825 (1988) (generally 
applicable garnishment law not preempted); Fort Halifax 
Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 (1987) (state law re- 
quiring one-time severance payment not preempted); 
Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Borges, 869 F.2d 142 (2d Cir.) 
(state escheat law not preempted when applied to recover 
unclaimed ERISA plan benefits), cert. denied, 493 US. 
811 (1989); see also Matter of Dyke, 943 F.2d 1435, 
1447 (Sth Cir. 1991) (collecting cases). Unfortunately, 
these decisions have provided no sure method for deter- 
mining whether the HFA’s impact on the Fund is too 
“tenuous, remote, or peripheral” to trigger ERISA pre- 
emption. 

The State argues that the HFA is a state tax law of gen- 
eral application that affects ERISA plans only inciden- 
tially. According to the State, the HFA is no more di- 
rected at ERISA plans than laws implementing sales taxes, 
utility taxes, and gasoline taxes, and should thus be seen 
simply as a “cost of doing business” within the state. 
Relying on Mackey and Aetna Life Ins. Co., the State 
contends that the HFA should not be preempted because 
it covers ERISA plans only by virtue of the plans’ inclu- 
sion in a broader class of entities subject to regulation. 


12a .- 


However, we believe that the HFA is distinguishable 
from these laws of general application. The HFA does 
not apply broadly to every sector of society in New York; 
nor does it apply to the Fund simply because it engaged in 
certain kinds of activities which any other member of 
society might engage in, such as purchasing gasoline or 
paying one’s employees. Rather, the HFA targets only the 
health care industry. Because this industry is, by defini- 
tion, the realm where ERISA welfare plans must operate, 
the HFA was bound to affect them. In a case such as the 
present one where the plan has chosen to provide medical 
benefits through self-run medical centers, the HFA oper- 
ates as an immediate tax on payments and contributions 
which were intended to pay for participants’ medical ben- 
efits. The HFA thus directly affects the Fund in its prin- 
cipal role as an employee welfare benefit plan. It does not 
touch the Fund only slightly on the outer limits of its 
plan activities; it affects the very operations and functions 
that make the Fund what it is, a provider of medical, sur- 
gical, and hospital care to its participants and their bene- 
ficiaries. 


We thus conclude that the HFA “relates to” the Fund 
and does so in a very direct way. The tax depletes those 
assets earmarked for the provision of health care benefits 
and, as a result, will cause the Fund to reduce benefits 
provided and/or to charge beneficiaries more in the future 
for benefits received. Both impacts will detrimentally 
affect the central mission and purpose of the Fund and 
hence cannot be characterized as remote or tenuous. See 
Travelers Ins. Co. v. Cuomo, 14 F.3d 708, 720 (2d Cir. 
1993) (“{T]he surcharges here force ERISA plans to 
increase either plan costs or reduce plan benefits. There- 
fore, they have the requisite connection to ERISA.”), peti- 
tion for cert. filed, 62 U.S.L.W. 3625 (U.S. Mar. 9, 1994) 
(No. 93-1414); E-Systems, Inc. v. Pogue, 929 F.2d 1100, 
1103 (Sth Cir.) (“The cost of the plan must therefore 
increase for the employer and/or employees or the bene- 
fits must be adjusted downwards to offset the tax bite. 


13a 


This is the type of impact Congress intended to avoid 
when it enacted the ERISA legislation.”), cert. denied, 
112 S. Ct. 585 (1991). 


Ovr decision would remain the same even if we as- 
sumed that the Fund could avoid the HFA by restruc- 
turing its finances so that funds formerly remitted by plan 
participants and employers directly to the medical centers 
would be remitted to the Fund which would subsequently 
transfer them to the centers. Wholly apart from whether 
the transmission of assets from the Fund to the centers 
could be taxed without running afoul of ERISA preemp- 
tion, an issue we need not add and do not decide, such a 
restructuring would alter the Fund’s established method of 
administration and would require it to implement proce- 
dures for its New York facilities that differ from those of 
its New Jersey facility. Moreover, these changes would 
not be insignificant since they would affect the Fund’s 
primary means of collecting contributions and paying for 
benefits. A state law that “relates to” an ERISA plan is 
not saved from ERISA preemption by the fact that a plan 
could restructure its operations to avoid the impact of the 
state law. Requirements under state law that force plans 
to change their traditional methods of administration or to 
adopt different methods on a state-by-state basis are fea- 
tures of state regulation that ERISA was designed to pre- 
vent. See FMC Corp., 498 U.S. at 59-60; Alessi v. 
Raybestos-Manhattan, Inc., 451 U.S. 504, 524 (1981); 
National Carriers’ Conference Comm. v. Heffernan, 454 
F, Supp. 914, 918 (D. Conn. 1978) (Newman, J.), Com- 
pare Aetna Life Ins. Co., 869 F.2d at 147 (discounting 
Fund’s complaint that it would need to make administra- 
tive and accounting adjustments in order to comply with 
state escheat law because the impact was not substantial 
enough). 


The district court acknowledged that the HFA will 
directly impact the Fund by leaving less money for bene- 
fits, but determined that the magnitude of the impact was 
too insubstantial to trigger preemption since the tax rate 


14a 


was only .6%. We do no agree with this reasoning. The 
magnitude of a statute’s economic impact on employee 
benefit plans may enter the analysis where the statute’s 
connection to the ERISA plan is otherwise insufficient for 
preemption. For example, a real estate gains tax that has 
only a peripheral connection to ERISA plans may still be 
subject to preemption if the high rate of tax has a sub- 
stantial economic impact on a plan. See Morgan Guar. 
Trust Co., 80 N.Y.2d at 51-53 (ten percent tax on gain 
from ERISA plan’s sale of real property preempted be- 
cause of direct impact on plan’s administration and 
investment strategy). However, the converse is not true. 
A statute that “relates to” ERISA plans cannot escape pre- 
emption simply because the magnitude of the impact is 
thought to be insubstantial. Adopting such an approach 
would contravene the policy that underlies the preemption 
provision of protecting ERISA plans from state regulation 
and would open the door to endless litigation about what 
constitutes a “substantial” impact. 


Travelers Insurance Co. v. Cuomo, supra, is not to the 
contrary. In that case, we held that New York surcharges 
on hospital rates were preempted by ERISA to the extent 
ERISA plans must pay surcharges. In the course of our 
analysis, we discussed Judge Martin’s opinion in this case 
without criticizing it. However, we cited it only for the 
proposition that “a substantial economic impact, standing 
alone, could be enough to bring ERISA’s preemption 
clause into play.” 14 F.3d at 721. We did not endorse— 
and now expressly refuse to endorse—the converse propo- 
sition urged by the State: that a regulation which has a de 
minimis economic impact on ERISA plans may withstand 
preemption regardless of what plan operations it affects. 


For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the judgment of 
the district tourt.

MEMORANDUM OPINION AND ORDER 


JouHN S. MarTIN, JR., District Judge: 


Plaintiff and defendants have moved for summary judg- 
ment. The central question presented by these motions is 
whether the Employment Retirement Income Security Pro- 
gram (ERISA), 29 U.S.C.A. §§ 1001-1461 (1985 & 
Supp. 1992), preempts the State of New York’s Health 
Facility Assessment (HFA), N.Y. PUB. HEALTH LAW 
§ 2807-d (McKinney Supp. 1992). 


l6a 


The HFA, a state tax of general application, is an 
assessment charged on gross receipts of hospitals. /d. 
§ 2807-d(1). Plaintiff is an ERISA plan which operates 
medical centers for the benefit of its plan beneficiaries. 
New York currently applies the HFA to plaintiff's medical 
centers. Plaintiff claims that its medical centers should 
not be subject to the tax because ERISA preempts state 
laws, such as the HFA, which affect ERISA plans. Plain- 
tiff also seeks to recover the tax it has thus far paid 
under the HFA. 


Defendant’s move for dismissal on grounds that the Tax 
Injunction Act, 28 U.S.C.A. § 1341 (1976), prevents this 
Court from ordering injunctive relief which interferes with 
a state tax plan. Defendants also move in the alternative 
for summary judgment on plaintiff's claims. 


Defendants’ motion for summary judgment is granted 
because ERISA does not preempt the Health Facility 
Assessment. 


DISCUSSION 


Defendants first question this Court’s jurisdiction over 
this case. ERISA provides that actions concerning ERISA 
preemption of state laws shall be brought in the United 
States District Courts. 29 U.S.C.A. § 1132(e)(1). How- 
ever, this jurisdictional grant is in conflict with a jurisdic- 
tional bar contained in the Tax Injunction Act (TIA). 28 
U.S.C.A. § 1341 (1976). The TIA provides that “[{t]he 
district courts shall not enjoin, suspend or restrain the 
assessment, levy or collection of any tax under State law 
where a plain, speedy and efficient remedy may be had in 
the courts of such State.” Jd. The conflict is resolved, 
however, in light of ERISA’s provision that “nothing in 
[this subchapter] shall be construed to alter, amend, 
modify, invalidate, impair, or supersede any law of the 
United States . . . or any rule or regulations issued under 
any such law.” 29 U.S.C.A. § 1144(d). This provision 
prevents ERISA from superseding the TIA. Retirement 


17a 


Fund Trust v. Franchise Tax Bd., 909 F.2d 1266 (9th 
Cir. 1990); Ashton v. Cory, 780 F.2d 816, 822 (9th Cir. 
1986); National Carriers’ Conference Comm. v. Heffer- 
nan, 440 F. Supp. 1280, 1283 (D. Conn. 1977); In re 
Sterling Die Casting Co., Inc., 118 B.R. 205, 207 (Bkrtcy. 
E.D.N.Y. 1990); see also Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 
463 U.S. 85, 104-05, 103 S. Ct. 2890, 2904 (1983). 


Plaintiff raises additional arguments against the TIA’s 
applicability to this action which are unpersuasive. The 
Fund urges this Court to use rules of statutory construc- 
tion to find that ERISA supersedes the TIA. Plaintiff 
notes that a more specific statute overrides one that is 
more general. Morgan v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, 550-51 
(1974). However, the Morgan rule only applies where 
there is no clear expression by Congress as to whether 
the later statute is intended to supersede the earlier one. 
Id. This rule is inapplicable in this case because of the 
clear language of ERISA § 1144 which indicates that 
Congress did not want ERISA to affect other Federal law. 


Plaintiff also argues that it should be allowed the benefit 
of an exception to the TIA. The Supreme Court has ruled 
that the United States is not restricted by the TIA from 
protecting itself and its instrumentalities from unconstitu- 
tional state exactions in federal courts. Department of 
Employment v. United States, 385 U.S. 355, 358 (1966). 
The Court extended this rule to allow Indian tribes an 
exemption from the TIA if the United States could have 
sued on their behalf. Moe v. Confederated Salish and 
Kootenai Tribes, 425 U.S. 463, 96 S. Ct. 1634 (1976). . 
Moe grants this exception to Indian tribes in part because 
of the unique trustee relationship of the United States to 
the tribes. Jd. at 473, 96 S. Ct. at 1641; Federal Deposit 
Ins. Corp. v. State of New York, 928 F.2d 56, 60 (2d 
Cir. 1991). Plaintiff misconstrues Moe te state that any 
private party is exempt from the TIA’s jurisdictional bar 
if the United States could have sued on its behalf. Federal 
Deposit Ins. Corp., 928 F.2d at 60. Since plaintiff is not 


18a 


in a trust relationship with the United States, the Moe 
exception to the TIA does not apply in this action. 


There being no reason extrinsic to the TIA to bar its 
application to this action, it is now appropriate to consider 
the terms of the TIA itself. The TIA operates to bar fed- 
eral courts from granting injunctions or declaratory judg- 
ments in matters affecting state tax schemes when there is 
a “plain, speedy and efficient remedy” to be had in state 
courts. Keleher v. New England Tel. & Tel. Co., 947 
F.2d 547, 549 (2d Cir. 1991). 


Plaintiffs arguments to the contrary notwithstanding, 
the HFA does fall within the class of state taxes excluded 
from the district courts’ jurisdiction. For TIA purposes, 
the Second Circuit broadly defines the word “tax.” 
Keleher, 947 F.2d at 549. Any state or local revenue 
collection device is a “tax” for purposes of the TIA. 7d. 
(citing Robinson Protective Alarm Co. v. City of Phila- 
delphia, 581 F.2d 371, 374-76 (3d Cir. 1978)). In the 
case at bar, the HFA is a revenue collection device of 
the state and is therefore a tax for TIA purposes. 


Once it has been established that a state tax if at issue, 
federal courts do not have jurisdiction to enjoin its enforce- 
ment or declare it invalid if there is a plain, speedy and 
efficient remedy available in the state courts. 28 U.S.C.A. 
§ 1341. It is not enough that the defendant may raise the 
invalidity of the tax as a defense to an enforcement pro- 
ceeding. Georgia R.R. & Banking Co. v. Redwine, 342 
U.S. 299, 303 n.11, 72 S.Ct. 321, 324 n.11 (1952); 
National Carriers’ Conference Comm. v. Heffernan, 440 
F. Supp. 1280, 1283 (D. Conn. 1977). 


In New York State Courts, there are two affirmative 
proceedings that the taxed entity might pursue; an Article 
78 proceeding and an action for declaratory judgment. 
However, where constitutional questions are implicated in 
an Article 78 proceeding, the proper procedure is for the 
action to be converted to one for declaratory judgment 


19a 


because an Article 78 proceeding is not an appropriate 
forum for constitutional challenges. Board of Educ. v. 
Gootnick, 427 N.Y.S.2d 777 (N.Y. 1980). Thus, in 
New York, an action for declaratory judgment is the only 
action available to the Fund to affirmatively challenge the 
constitutionality of the HFA. 

Plaintiff asserts, however that its claim will be dis- 
missed in state court because ERISA grants exclusive juris- 
diction over ERISA claims to the federal courts. Yet, 
plaintiff's speculation on the fate of its action in the state 
courts is discredited in light of the fact that New York 
State courts regularly entertain such actions. See, ¢.g., 
Matter of Morgan Guar. Trust Co. v. Tax Appeals Tri- 
bunal, 587 N.Y.S.2d 252 (N.Y. 1992); Tap Elec. Con- 
tracting Serv., Inc. v. Harnett, 556 N.Y.S.2d 988 (N.Y. 
1990); Sasso v. Vachris, 494 N.Y.S.2d 856 (N.Y. 1985); 
United Health Servs., Inc. v. Upstate Admin. Servs., Inc., 
573 N.Y.S.2d 851 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1991); People v. Art 
Steel Co., Inc., 505 N.Y.S.2d 715 (N.Y. Crim. Ct 1986). 
Nevertheless, courts have held that the uncertainty over 
whether New York State courts will entertain ERISA 
preemption actions renders the availability of a state court 
remedy anything but “plain.” Travelers Ins. Co. v. Cuomo, 
No. 92 Civ. 3999 (LJF) (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 3, 1993); 
Heffernan, 440 F. Supp. at 1283. There is no need to 
decide this issue, here however, because, reaching the 
merits of plaintiffs, claim, I conclude that the HFA is 
not preempted by ERISA. 


Since there are no disputed issues of material fact, this 
case is an appropriate one to resolve on motion for sum- 
mary judgment. This case is governed by recent Second 
Circuit pronouncements on the extent of ERISA preemp- 
tion. Although the parties make much of analogizing to, 
and discrediting, the Second Circuit’s holding in Rebaldo 
v. Cuomo, 749 F.2d 133 (2d Cir. 1984), cert. denied, 
472 U.S. 1008, 105 S. Ct. 2702 (1985), that case does 


20a 


not provide the clearest guidance. The Circuit Court has 
since refashioned its test for ERISA preemption in light 
of Supreme Court rulings subsequent to Rebaldo, although 
it has not abandoned Rebaldo. See Rogers v. Consolidated 
Rail Corp., 948 F.2d 858 (1991); Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. 
Borges, 869 F.2d 142 (2d Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 493 
U.S. 811, 110 S. Ct. 57 (1989). 

While ERISA’s preemption provision is to be inter- 
preted broadly, the presumption against preemption of 
state law is at its strongest when dealing with a law that 
is within the states’ traditional police powers. Rogers, 
948 F.2d at 859; Aetna, 869 F.2d at 144. The Second 
Circuit, in conducting a survey of ERISA preemption 
cases has generalized from them to find that there are 
certain types of cases which fall on either side of the 
preemption line. Aetna, 869 F.2d at 145-46. The Cir- 
cuit Court found 


that the laws that have been ruled preempted are 
those that provide an alternative cause of action to 


refer specifically to ERISA plans and apply solely 
to them, or interfere with the calculation of benefits 
owed to dn employee. Those that have not been 
preempted are laws of general application—often 
traditional exercises of state power or regulatory 
authority—whose effect on ERISA plans is inci- 
dental. 


Id. at 146. Further defining “what constitutes an inci- 
dental impact,” the Circuit Court stated that indirect 
economic impact alone was not enough. The impact must 
affect the “primary administrative functions of benefit 
plans, such as determining an employee’s eligibility for 
a benefit and the amount of that benefit.” Jd. 


Using this litmus test, the Second Circuit held in Aetna 
that a Connecticut escheat law was not preempted by 
ERISA. /d. at 147. The escheat law required that pen- 


authority.” Id. 


the determination of an employee’s eligibility for benefits. 
The impacts on benefit plans are incidentally economic 
resulting only in the need for administrative and account- 
ing procedures to comply with the law. 


The fact that the tax will leave less money for benefits 
is not decisive here as it was not in Aetna, where Con- 
necticut’s escheat laws also drained funds that might oth- 
erwise go to benefits. The tax is not great enough to pose 
a serious economic threat to the plan which might trigger 
preemption. See Aetna, 869 F.2d at 147. Here the tax 
is “sixth-tenths of one percent of each facility's gross 
receipts received from all patient care services and other 
operating income on a cash basis.” N.Y. PUB. HEALTH 
LAW § 2807-d(2)(c). The Fund paid, for the period 
of January | through November 30, 1991, $7,066.02 as 
the assessment on $1,177,670 of income which is subject 
to the assessment out of approximately $20,000,000 


22a 


(twenty million) total income for 1991. Fier Affidavit 
4 19. While there are no numerical standards established 
by case law to determine how much tax is too much, it 
is clear that the HFA is not significant enough to war- 
rant preemption of a state’s traditional taxing power with- 
Plaintiff notes that the New York Court of Appeals has 
reached a different conclusion. Morgan, 587 N.Y.S.2d 
252. The Court of Appeals held that a state tax on gains 
from the transfer of real property was preempted by 
ERISA because it drained plan funds, caused an admin- 
istrative burden that would be different than that in other 
states, and impacted the structure of administrative deci- 
sions on the disposal of assets. Jd. at 256-57. However, 
Morgan is distinguishable from the case before this Court. 
The tax in Morgan was ten percent (10.0%) of the con- 
sideration received for the property sold, an amount the 
Court of Appeals considered significant, Jd. at 257, as 
opposed to six-tenths of one percent (0.6%) in the case 
before this Court. The economic impact of the HFA is 
not as substantial as that found in Morgan. Compare 
Travelers Ins. Co. v. Cuomo, No. 92 Civ. 3999 (LIF) 
(S.D.N.Y. Feb. 3, 1993) (state surcharges of 9%, 11%, 
and 13% on hospital rates preempted by ERISA to the 
extent that ERISA plans must pay the surcharges). 
Thus, it is consistent with decisions of the New York 
Court of Appeals, the Second Circuit, and District Courts 
within it, to hold that ERISA does not preempt the HFA. 


Therefore, plaintiff's motion for a refund of the ap 
proximately $7,000 paid as HFA assessments is dismissed. 
CONCLUSION 


Defendants’ motion for summary judgment dismissing 
the complaint is granted because ERISA does not pre- 
empt the Health Facility Assessment. Accordingly, the
