STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


This case questions the extent to which ERISA preemption 
insulates ERISA-covered welfare benefit plans from tradi- 
tional areas of state regulation when plans choose to operate 
in those areas. New York regulates all providers of hospital 
care services without regard to whether a provider is owned

by an ERISA plan. If a plan chooses to operate its own med- 
ical center, instead of purchasing services from others, the 
State’s regulation falls upon plan-owned facilities. 


In this case, the ERISA plan has argued, and the Second 
Circuit has agreed, that a generally applicable tax imposed 
upon all hospitals in New York cannot be imposed on plan- 
owned facilities simply because they are plan-owned. States 
must be able to regulate the activities in which ERISA plans

emption is intended to protect, that is, plan administration and 
plan benefit structure. Any other result would confer upon 
ERISA plans a charmed existence which Congress did not 
intend. 


A. The Hospital Assessment 


In 1990, in order to offset increased health care costs and as 
an alternative to substantial Medicaid payment reductions 
which would otherwise have been required (JA 42-43, 50), 
the New York State Legislature enacted section 2807-d of 
the New York Public Health Law, referred to by the courts 
below as the Health Facility Assessment or “HFA.” 1990 N.Y. 
Laws, ch. 938.? The HFA, with certain exceptions not relevant 
here, is imposed upon all “hospitals” in New York. Hospitals 
are defined to encompass any facility engaged in providing 
services under the supervision of a physician or dentist, 
including diagnostic centers and treatment centers. N.Y. Pub. 
Health Law § 2801(1) (McKinney 1993). 


The HFA is assessed at six-tenths of one percent for diag- 
nostic and treatment centers. Assessable income is defined to 
include all gross receipts from: (1) patient care services irre- 
spective of payment source; (2) investment income; and (3)

forwarded monthly and eventually become part of the State’s

Respondents are trustees of the NYSA-ILA Medical and 
Clinical Services Fund (the “Fund”). The Fund is a labor- 
management trust governed by Section 302 of the Labor- 
Management Relations Act of 1947, 29 U.S.C. § 186. It was 
established pursuant to collective bargaining agreements 
between the New York Shipping Association, Inc. (“NYSA”) 
and the International Longshoremen’s Association (“ILA”) 
(JA 20-21). The Fund administers a self-insured, ERISA cov- 
ered, multi-employer welfare benefit plan (“NYSA plan”). 
See 29 U.S.C. § 1002 (1). 


The Fund owns and operates two medical centers in 
New York and one in New Jersey which deliver medical, den- 
tal, and other health care services on an outpatient basis (JA 
21). The New York medical centers are incorporated sepa- 
rately as New York not-for-profit corporations and are 
licensed by the New York State Department of Health as 
“diagnostic and treatment centers” pursuant to N.Y. Pub. 
Health Law § 2805 (McKinney 1993)(JA 98). The centers 
deliver medical services principally to NYSA employed long- 
shore workers, retirees, and dependents (JA 75-76). The cen- 
ters also deliver medical services to ILA members employed 
by non-longshore employers (“outside employers”) (JA 76). 
Finally, the centers deliver medical services to members of 
another union, the Port Police and Guards Union (“PPGU”), 
pursuant to a contract between the PPGU’s ERISA plan and 
the Fund (JA 75-76, 78).

The medical centers’ income is derived from a number of 
sources. NYSA employer contributions are remitted to the 
Fund which in turn transfers them to the medical centers as 
needed. Outside employer contributions are remitted directly 
to the medical centers. Fee-for-service payments are made 
directly to the medical centers by the PPGU fund and from 
workers’ compensation carriers. Employee registration fees 
and co-payments for services are made to the centers. Finally, 
there are miscellaneous sources, such as supplier rebates and 
medical records fees (JA 77-79, 81-82). 


The medical centers filed reports and paid the hospital 
assessment for the period from January 1, 1991 through 
November 30, 1991 (JA 81-82, 84-96). The centers did not 
report as assessable income any monies transferred from the 
Fund, treating such income as a grant which is non-assessable 
income under state law (JA 80-81). The State did not contest 
that exclusion. The centers reported as assessable income out- 
side employer contributions to the centers, fee-for-service 
payments from the PPGU fund and workers’ compensation 
carriers, employee payments for certain services, as well 
as miscellaneous income (JA 80-81). For the period Jan- 
uary 1, 1991 through November 30, 1991, the centers reported 
assessable income of $1,177,670 and paid assessments total- 
ing $7,066.02 (JA 81-82). Thereafter, the centers discontin- 
ued filing reports and paid no further assessments (JA 82). 


B. Prior Proceedings 


On April 16, 1992, the Fund commenced this action against 
the New York State Commissioner of Health and other Health 
Department officials (referred to collectively as the “State”) 
for declaratory and injunctive relief and for a refund of the 
assessments already paid on the ground that the hospital 
assessment is preempted by ERISA to the extent it “imposes 
a levy on contributions and payments for health care benefits 
provided by the Fund” (9a). The parties cross-moved for sum- 
mary judgment pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. 


6 


In an opinion dated February 18, 1993, the district court 
granted the State’s motion, determining that the hospital 
assessment is not preempted by ERISA. The court reasoned 
that the HFA is a law of general application applying to all 
health facilities with only an incidental economic impact on 
ERISA plans (21a). The court concluded that the tax “does 
not interfere with calculation of benefits” or with the “deter- 
mination of an employee’s eligibility for benefits” (21a). 
Pointing to the de minimis amount of the assessment, the 
court distinguished this case from The Travelers Insurance 
Co. v. Cuomo, 813 F. Supp. 996 (S.D.N.Y.), aff'd, 14 F.3d 708 
(2d Cir. 1993), rev'd sub nom. New York State Conference of 
Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co.., 
— U.S. __, 115 S. Ct. 1671 (1995), in which three New 
York State surcharges imposed on hospital bills of varying 
amounts of 9%, 11%, and 13%, depending on the identity of 
the third-party payor, were invalidated by the Second Circuit 
as ERISA-preempted (22a). 


On June 23, 1994, the Second Circuit reversed the district 
court. The court rejected the district court’s conclusion that 
the HFA was a law of general applicability. The court stated 
that because “the HFA targets only the health care industry” 
which is, “by definition, the realm where ERISA welfare 
plans must operate, [the assessment] was bound to affect 
them” (12a). Because the Fund had chosen to provide inedi- 
cal benefits through its own medical centers, the court con- 
cluded that the HFA operated as an immediate tax on 
payments and contributions which were intended to pay for 
medical benefits (JA 16). 


Relying on its decision in The Travelers Insurance Co. v. 
Cuomo, 14 F.3d 708 (2d Cir. 1993), rev'd sub nom. New York 
State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Trav-

The State filed a petition for a writ of certiorari on October 
21, 1994, after this Court granted the State’s request for an 
extension of time within which to file the petition (26a). On 
May 1, 1995, this Court granted the petition, vacated the 
judgment, and remanded the case for further consideration in 
light of its decision in New York State Conference of Blue 
Cross & Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co.. 
—— U.S. __, 115 S. Ct. 1671 (1995) (“Travelers”) (Sa). 


On January 4, 1996, the Second Circuit issued a decision 
adhering to its initial determination that the HFA was pre- 
empted by ERISA insofar as it applies to the income of a 
medical center owned by an ERISA fund. The court noted that 
in Travelers, the impact of the surcharges on ERISA plans 
was indirect because the surcharges were imposed not on 
“ERISA plans themselves, but on insurance carriers provid- 
ing health insurance coverage to plans and their beneficiaries” 
(JA 8). The court held that this Court’s decision in Travelers 
did not control because, unlike Travelers, “there is no third 
party between the plan and the tax imposed pursuant to the 
HFA,” and the statute therefore “depletes the Fund’s assets 
directly” (JA 9). The court concluded that “(nothing in Trav- 
elers undermines our reliance on the HFA’s direct economic 


impact upon the Fund as a basis for finding ERISA pre- 
emption nor precludes a determination that a state law’s direct 
economic impact on ERISA plans is sufficient for a finding of 
pre-emption” (JA 9). 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The HFA is a tax of general applicability imposed upon 
providers of hospital care. Whether characterized as direct or 
indirect, its effect on a plan’s cost of doing business neither 
dictates nor restricts plan benefit structures nor plan admin- 
istration. It, therefore, does not relate to a plan even when 
imposed upon plan-owned medical centers. 


In Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1679, this Court held that State 
laws of general applicability in areas of traditional state reg- 
ulation do not relate to plans, even if they have an economic 
impact upon plan choices, so long as they do not dictate or 
restrict plan benefit structure or plan administration. In Trav- 
elers, the Court held that the surcharges were not preempted, 
even though they affected the cost of benefits and the relative 
costs of competing insurance to provide them, because they 
did not bind plan choice. The HFA, even as applied to plan- 
owned medical centers, does no more. The HFA affects the 
cost of medical care and plans must use additional assets to 
cover the increased costs. This type of economic impact upon 
plans does not, however, relate to plans. 


Travelers involved hospital surcharges on medical bills 
paid by insurers and HMOs which indirectly increased plan 
costs. The HFA, in this case, is imposed “directly” upon plan- 
owned medical centers. That difference is not a legally sig- 
nificant distinction. This Court has never relied upon the 
directness or indirectness of a law's impact upon plans as a 
determinative factor in preemption analysis. Thus, in Mackey 
v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825 
(1988), this Court held a garnishment law of general appli- 


cability did not relate to plans, despite its direct imposition of 


9 


legal obligations upon plans. On the other hand, laws do not 
escape preemption simply because they bear indirectly upon 
plans. Ingersoll-Rand v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133 (1990). 
Instead, this Court has examined the nature of the impact 
upon plans in determining whether a law relates to plans. 


The HFA is also not preempted as applied to plan-owned 
medical centers because operation of a medical facility is not 
a plan function which ERISA preemption was designed to 
protect. Employee benefit plans operate and are subject to 
state laws in many capacities other than as plans. They are 
employers, property owners, purchasers of goods and services 
and in some cases providers of medical care, day care and 
prepaid leyal services. States may regulate plans as they 
engage in these activities so long as that regulation does not 
mandate or restrict the plan functions ERISA preemption was 
designed to protect. 


ERISA preemption protects the plan functions of plan 
administration and benefit structure. The operation of a med- 
ical center is not one of these plan functions protected by 
ERISA preemption. It does not involve the type of plan 
administrative activities that this Court identified in Fort Hal- 
ifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 (1987). The Fund’s 
ownership of the medical centers is not a plan benefit struc- 
ture. The benefit provided by the plan is health care coverage. 
The benefit has been structured to provide coverage only for 
the health services available at certain centers. However, 
ownership of the medical centers is irrelevant to the benefit’s 
structure. Both the NYSA plan, and another unrelated plan, 
the PPGU plan, have been similarily structured to limit cov- 
erage to the medical centers owned by the NYSA Fund. That 
one Fund owns the centers and the other does not, is inci- 
dental to the benefit structure of both plans. 


The State is not precluded, in any event, from regulating 
the centers. Regulation of the plan-owned centers relates, at 
most, to the benefit provided by the plan, not the plan itself. 


10 


In Fort Halifax, 482 U.S. at 11, this Court held that laws that 
related to benefits, but not to plans, were not preempted 
by ERISA. While that case involved regulation of a benefit 
where no plan was involved, the Court's reasoning applies 
equally where a benefit is provided by a plan. States may reg- 
ulate benefits so long as that regulation does not impermis- 
sibly intrude upon the structure or administration of the plan 
that provides the benefit. 


ARGUMENT 


THE HEALTH FACILITY ASSESSMENT DOES 
NOT “RELATE TO” ERISA PLANS BECAUSE IT 
IS A LAW OF GENERAL APPLICABILITY WHICH 
DOES NOT DIRECTLY OR INDIRECTLY 
MANDATE AN EMPLOYEE BENEFIT PLAN’S 
STRUCTURE OR ADMINISTRATION 


Congress enacted ERISA as a “comprehensive statute 
designed to promote the interests of employees and their ben- 
eficiaries in employee benefit plans.” Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, 
Inc., 463 U.S. 85, 90 (1983). However, ERISA’s protection of 
participants in employee benefit plans extends only to the 
administration of plans. ERISA does not require the provision 
of benefits, nor does it substantively regulate any of the ben- 
efits which may be provided by plans. Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 
1674. 


ERISA preempts laws that “relate to any employee benefit 
plan.” 29 U.S.C. § 1144(a). While this preemption provision 
is broad, it does not alter the basic presumption in any pre- 
emption analysis that Congress does not intend to supplant 
state law. See Maryland v. Louisiana, 451 U.S. 725, 746 
(1981). Moreover, where a State is acting in a field of tradi- 
tional state regulation, “the historical police powers of the 
States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless 
that was the clear and manifest intent of Congress.” Travelers, 


115 S. Ct. at 1676 (citing Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 
331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947)). 


Whether characterized as direct or indirect, the economic 
impact of a law of general applicability that does not control 
plan administration or benefit structure is irrelevant to pre- 
emption analysis. The HFA is a provider tax of general appli- 
cability designed to achieve a policy objective in an area of 
traditional state regulation, health care.‘ The law's effect on

B. The Second Circuit Erred in Basing Its Analysis 
on the Directness, Rather Than the Nature, of a 
Law’s Impact Upon Plans 


The Second Circuit's decision on remand rests solely on the 
court’s characterization of the HFA as having a “direct” 
impact upon the Fund. In the court's view, the impact of the 
HFA is direct because “there is no third party between the 
plan and the tax” (JA 9). This Court has never based its pre- 
emption analysis upon whether a law affects plans directly or 
through the regulation of another entity. 


Nothing in Travelers supports the conclusion that a law 
which increases costs to plans “indirectly” through its appli- 
cation to third parties, such as that held not to be preempted 
in Travelers, is transformed into a preempted law when 
applied to a plan that happens to own or operate the third 
party. Indeed, the HFA is very similar to the 9% assessment 
upheld in Travelers, which is imposed upon the in-patient 
hospital costs paid by health maintenance organizations 
(“HMOs”). Under the Second Circuit's rationale, the 9% 
would be preempted if an HMO was owned by an ERISA 
plan. None of the reasons articulated by this Court for finding 
that the 9% assessment is not preempted is affected merely 
because a plan chooses to own an HMO, instead of purchas- 
ing HMO memberships. 


15 


Nor has this Court ever construed ERISA as preempting all 
laws that “directly” operate upon plans. In Mackey, the Court 
upheld a Georgia garnishment statute that operated directly 
upon plans. There was no third party between the law’s obli- 
gations and the plan. Yet, this Court held that the adminis- 
trative burdens and costs resulting from the direct application 
of the statute to plans were not enough to trigger preemption. 
486 U.S. at 831. In Mackey, this Court also noted that run-of- 
the-mill state lawsuits against ERISA plans for unpaid rent, 
failure to pay creditors, and torts committed by plans were not 
preempted by ERISA. 486 U.S. at 833. The legal obligations 
at issue in such lawsuits also are felt directly by ERISA plans. 
On the other hand, this Court has held that laws do not escape 
preemption simply because they affect plans indirectly 
through another entity. Ingersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. at 139 (laws 
which depend upon the existence of ERISA plans are pre- 
empted even though their effect upon plans is indirect). 


This Court instead has examined the nature of the law's 
effect upon the plan in determining whether the law relates 
to the plan. In Alessi v. Raybestos-Manhattan, Inc., 451 
U.S. 504 (1981) and FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52 
(1990), the laws at issue were applied directly to plans. They 
dictated plan structure, therefore they related to the plans. In 
Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724, 
739 (1985), this Court found that the mandated benefit law at 
issue “bears indirectly” on plans since it regulated the content 
of insurance policies which are sold by insurance companies 
to plans. Nevertheless, since it dictated plan benefit structure 
by requiring mental health coverage, the law related to plans. 
In Travelers, the impact of the surcharges was similarly indi- 
rect inasmuch as the law regulated hospitals and not plans. 
However, this Court held that the law imposing those sur- 
charges was not preempted, since the law did not bind plan 
choices. Thus, whether a law's impact upon ERISA plans 
is labeled direct or indirect is not a legally determinative 
distinction.

C. The HFA Is Not Preempted Because It Is Imposed 
upon the Fund In Its Capacity as a Provider of 
Medical Services, Not as a Plan 


The HFA is not preempted for the additional reason that the 
operation of a medical center is not a plan function that 
ERISA preemption was designed to protect. The Fund pays 
the HFA as part of its operation of the medical centers. The 
Fund operates the medical centers not as a plan, but as a 
provider of medical services. The State may regulate this 
activity so long as the regulation does not mandate or restrict 
the plan functions, administration, and benefit structures, 
which ERISA preemption was designed to protect. 


The NYSA plan provides the benefit of health care cover- 
age. This benefit is structured to cover services provided at 
particular medical centers. Regulation of the centers, as hos- 
pitals, does not mandate or restrict the plan’s choice of that 
benefit structure. It therefore cannot be said to relate to the 
plan. Even if the benefit is defined as the provision of med- 
ical services as opposed to health care coverage, preemption 
is not implicated. States may regulate employee benefits pro- 
vided in-kind so long as that regulation does not mandate or 
restrict a plan's administration, or the structure of the bene- 
fits it provides. Therefore, a state tax on hospital income does 
not relate to a plan even when the entity that operates the hos- 
pital also administers an ERISA plan whose participants 
receive medical services at the hospital. 


1. ERISA preemption only protects the plan func- 
tions of plan administration and benefit struc- 
ture and does not extend to other activities in 
which plans engage 


The purpose of ERISA preemption is to permit “nationally 
uniform administration of employee benefit plans.” Travelers, 
115 S. Ct. at 1678; see also FMC Corp., 498 U.S. at 59-60: 
Ingersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. at 142; Fort Halifax, 482 U.S. at 10: 


17 


Shaw, 463 U.S. at 105. Accordingly, state laws that mandate 
plan benefit structures or plan administration relate to plans. 
Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1679. However, the provision of med- 
ical services is not within the scope of these plan functions 
which ERISA preemption is designed to protect, even when 
the services are provided by a plan-owned facility. 


This Court has held that state laws that require plans to 
structure themselves in a certain manner relate to plans. Thus, 
in Alessi, 451 U.S. 524-25, a state law that prohibited plans 
from using a particular method to calculate pension benefits 
related to plans. In FMC Corp., 498 U.S. at 60, an anti-sub- 
rogation statute, which prevented plans from being structured 
SO as to require reimbursement from a third party in the event 
of recovery, related to plans. In Metropolitan Life, 471 U.S. 
at 735, and Shaw, 463 U.S. ai 96, laws that would require 
plans to structure themselves to provide certain benefits, men- 
tal health coverage and pregnancy disability respectively, 
were held to relate to plans. 


In Fort Halifax, 428 U.S. 1, 9, this Court discussed the 
scope of plan administrative practices that Congress intended 
to protect in enacting section 514(a): 


An employer that makes a commitment systematically 
to pay certain benefits undertakes a host of obligations, 
such as determining the eligibility of claimants, calcu- 
lating benefit levels, making disbursements, monitoring 
the availability of funds for benefit payments, and keep- 
ing appropriate records in order to comply with appli- 
cable reporting requirements. The most efficient way to 
meet these responsibilities is to establish a uniform 
administrative scheme, which provides a set of standard 
procedures to guide processing of claims and disburse- 
ment of benefits. 


Thus, ERISA protects from state regulation the adminis- 
trative practices in which plans must engage in order to pro-

vide benefits. However, not every activity in which a plan 
engages has that purpose and is protected by ERISA pre- 
emption. For example, in Mackey, 486 U.S. at 834, this Court 
held that the burden of the administrative practices neces- 
sarily engaged in by plans in order to comply with a general 
garnishment law did not trigger preemption. 


Plans engage in many activities that are not plan functions. 
Plans purchase supplies, own or lease property, enter into 
contracts with banks, accountants, and attorneys, and act as 
employers of their own employees. Since ERISA does not 
preempt state laws that do not relate to plan functions, States 
may require plans to pay real estate, sales, and unemployment 
taxes and may require plans to comply with state contract and 
employment law. Similarily, the “run-of-the-mill state-law 
claims such as unpaid rent, failure to pay creditors, or even 
torts committed by an ERISA plan,” Mackey, 486 U.S. at 834, 
do not relate to plans because they do not affect a plan in its 
capacity as a plan. When plans engage in non-plan activities, 
they may be regulated by the State to the same extent as any 
other commercial entity. 


The distinction between plan functions and plans acting in 
other capacities is reflected in the statute and in this Court's 
decisions. ERISA explicitly allows a single entity to perform 
plan and non-plan functions. It permits, for example, employ- 
ers to act both as plan sponsors, free from ERISA’s fiduciary 
duties, and plan administrators, subject to them. See 29 
U.S.C. § 1108(c)(3). ERISA also provides, as this Court has 
recognized, that any entity, whether an employer or not, that 
performs plan and non-plan administration functions is sub- 
ject to ERISA only when it performs plan functions. See 29 
U.S.C. § 1002(21)(A) (a person is an ERISA fiduciary only to 
the extent that he or she exercises any discretionary authority 
over plan administration or plan assets). Thus, in Curtiss- 
Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen, 5 ere | FOU.

4. A State may regulate an employee benefit to the 
extent that such regulation does not relate to a 
plan 


The State is not precluded, in any event, from regulating 
the centers as hospitals. Regulation of the fund-owned med- 
ical centers relates, at most, to the benefit provided by the 
plan, not to the plan itself. 


Most ERISA benefits are strictly financial. The plan pays 
money to a participant or a beneficiary in the event of some 
contingency such as retirement or death. A few welfare ben- 
efits, such as health care coverage, involve payment for ser- 
vices. ERISA contemplates that plans may provide coverage 
of certain we!fare benefits by delivering services in-kind. 
Thus, plans may operate medical centers, day care centers, 
and prepaid legal plans, instead of providing coverage by pay- 
ing for services rendered by others. See 29 U.S.C. § 1002(1). 
The decision to deliver certain services in-kind, however, 
does not transform the benefit provided by the plan into the 
plan to provide the benefit. 


ERISA defines a welfare benefit plan as follows: 


any plan, fund, or program which was heretofore. . . 
established . . . to the extent that such plan, fund, or 
program was established . . . for the purpose of pro- 
viding for its participants or their beneficiaries, through 
the purchase of insurance or otherwise, (A) medical, sur- 
gical, or hospital care or benefits, or benefits in the event 
of sickness, accident, disability, death or unemployment, 
or vacation benefits, apprenticeship or other training pro- 
grams, or day care centers, scholarship funds, or prepaid 
legal services. .. . 


29 U.S.C. § 1002(1). 


The structure of the statute indicates that the benefits pro- 
vided by plans are distinct from the plan to provide them. A 
“plan, fund, or program” is “established or maintained for the 


24 


purpose of providing” the specified benefits. Benefits, in turn, 
are what plans provide. Moreover, throughout ERISA the 
words “plan” and “benefit” are used separately and are not 
treated as equivalents. Fort Halifax, 482 U.S. at 8. 


ERISA neither requires the provision of benefits nor estab- 
lishes any minimum participation, vesting, or funding require- 
ments for welfare benefits. Instead, “[i]n enacting ERISA, 
Congress's primary concern was with the mismanagement of 
funds accumulated to finance employee benefits and the fail- 
ure to pay employees benefits from accumulated funds.” Mas- 
sachusetts v. Morash, 490 U.S. 107, 115 (1989). ERISA seeks 
to assure that an employee's expectation of benefits is not 
defeated by poor plan administration. /d. at 112. Thus, ERISA 
is concerned with the administrative structure established to 
provide the benefits, not the benefits themselves. 


In Fort Halifax, 482 U.S. at 7, this Court relied on this dis- 
tinction between “benefits” which an employer may provide 
and an “employee benefit plan” in holding that a Maine 
statute which required employers to make a one-time sever- 
ance payment in the event of a plant closing, was not pre- 
empted. While the law mandated an employee benefit, it did 
not relate to a plan because compliance with the law did not 
require the on-going administrative practices which define a 
plan. Thus, this Court held that laws that relate to benefits, 
but not to plans, are not preempted by ERISA. /d. at 11. 
While Fort Halifax involved the regulation of a benefit where 
no plan was involved, the Court's reasoning applies equally 
to situations where the benefit is provided by a plan. There- 
fore, a State may regulate one of the benefits enumerated in 
ERISA even when a benefit is provided through a plan, so 
long as the regulation of the benefit does not impermissibly 
intrude upon the structure or the administration of the plan 
that provides benefits. 


In fact, while ERISA’s legislative history addressing the 
scope of preemption is limited, it contains guidance on pre-

ERISA contemplates plans may provide by delivering services

regulation, such as quality control standards, the only impact 
that state regulation of these benefits has on plans is possibly 
to increase the cost of structuring the benefit as delivery of 
in-kind services. This economic effect is insufficient to result 
in preemption. Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1683.
