MENT ON ERISA PLANS IS INDISTINGUISHABLE 
FROM THE EFFECT FOUND NOT TO BE PRE-

in Travelers applies to all hospital bills; the HMO assess-

involved in this case applies to all hospitals. Because 
ERISA plans may provide hospital care benefits for their

benefits to plans. Contrary to respondents’ claim, how- 
ever, that the assessment in this case “directly assesses 
contributions,” (Resp. Br. at 9), that occurs, for any of 
these laws, only when ERISA plans pay for health care 
services for their participants.

is imposed on hospital income, regardless of source. Thus, 
the effect on the plan here arises only as the result of a 
plan’s payments to the hospital to provide benefits to par- 
ticipants. All hospitals receive a large portion of their

Despite respondent's attempt to suggest otherwise, the 
standard for ERISA preemption of tax laws is no differ- 
ent than the standard for ERISA preemption of any other 
law. ERISA imposes no stricter preemption standards on 
state tax laws than on any other state law. Section 514 
(b)(5)(B)(i) of ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1144(b)(5)(B) 
(i), simply provides that “any tax law relating to [ERISA] 
plans” is not exempt from preemption (emphasis added). 
Thus, the same “relates to” test of preemption which ap- 
plies to state laws generally applies to state tax laws. 
This hospital assessment differs significantly from the laws 
involved in the cases relied upon by respondents (Resp. 
Br. at 5). In all of those cases, unlike the present case, 
the courts preempted state taxes or assessments because 
they referred to, or were directed specifically at, ERISA 
plans. E-Systems, Inc. v. Pogue, 929 F.2d 1100 (Sth 
Cir.), cert. denied, 112 S. Ct. 585 (1991); General 
Motors Corp. v. California Bd. of Equalization, 815 F.2d 
1305 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 485 U.S. 941 (1988); 
Birdsong v. Olson, 708 F. Supp. 792, 798 (W.D. Tex. 
1989); National Carriers Conference Comm. v. Heffernan, 
454 F. Supp. 914 (D. Conn. 1978). These cases fit 
squarely within the Court’s holdings which have pre- 
empted state laws which refer to, are predicated upon, 
or which function only through the existence of, ERISA 
plans. /ngersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133 
(1990); District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Bd. 
of Trade, 113 S. Ct. 580 (1992). The Court has never 
held, however, as respondents urge, that a law of general

application with only an economic impact on plans is 
preempted. 


Finally, respondents attempt to distinguish the hospital 
assessment from other forms of generally applicable reg- 
ulation such as sales tax, or unemployment tax, because 
it “affects ERISA plans qua employee benefit plans” 
(Resp. Br. at 17). This distinction, however, rests on 
a premise accepted by the Second Circuit in the original 
opinion in this case but subsequently rejected by the Su- 
preme Court in Travelers, that health care regulation is 
to be treated differently than any other regulation under 


ERISA’s preemption provision. 
CONCLUSION 


For the foregoing reasons, the Petition for a Writ of 
Certiorari should be granted.
