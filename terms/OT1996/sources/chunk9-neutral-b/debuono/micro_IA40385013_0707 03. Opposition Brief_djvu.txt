Statement of the Case 


This case involves the imposition by the State of New York 
pursuant to N.Y. Pup. HEALTH Law § 2807-d (McKinney 
1993 & Supp. 1996) of a tax upon contributions and payments 
for medical benefits provided to plan beneficiaries, which are 
paid to medical clinics operated in New York by an employee 
welfare benefit plan. The court of appeals previously ruled 
that this New York tax was pre-empted by the Employee Re- 
tirement Income Security Act (“ERISA”). See NYSA-ILA 
Medical & Clinical Serv. Fund v. Axelrod, 27 F.3d 823 (2d 
Cir. 1994) (hereinafter “Axelrod”). On May 1, 1995, this 
Court granted certiorari, vacated the prior Axelrod decision, 
and remanded the case for further consideration in light of the 
Travelers decision. See Chassin v. NYSA-ILA Medical & 
Clinical Serv. Fund, 115 S. Ct. 1819 (1995). 


Travelers 


Travelers involved a challenge by a group of commercial 
insurance carriers and their trade association to invalidate 
New York’s system of imposing surcharges on hospital bills 
paid by commercial insurers and Health Maintenance Organi- 
zations but not on hospital bills paid by Blue Cross/Blue 
Shield insurers. Travelers, 14 F.3d at 712. The sur- 
charge scheme was designed to enhance artificially the 


3 


competitiveness of the latter group of insurers vis-d-vis the 
former. The Second Circuit had concluded that ERISA § 514, 
29 U.S.C.A. § 1144 (West 1985 & Supp. 1996),’ pre-empted 
New York’s hospital rate-setting system because that state 
law (1) purposely interfered with the choices ERISA plans 
make for health care coverage and (2) substantially increased 
the cost to ERISA plans of providing health care benefits to 
their beneficiaries. Jd. at 719-20. The court of appeals found 
that the hospital rate-setting system in Travelers related to 
ERISA plans, the statutory touchstone for pre-emption, be- 
cause it had a connection with ERISA plans. 


‘In 1974 Congress enacted ERISA, a comprehensive regime to protect the 
interests of employees and their beneficiaries in private employee benefit 
plans. Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, 463 U.S. 85, 90 (1983); Ingersoll-Rand 
Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133, 137 (1990); District of Columbia v. 
Greater Wash. Bd. of Trade, 506 U.S. 125, 127 (1992). ERISA’s 
“crowning achievement” was the elimination of state regulation of ERISA 
plans. Shaw, 463 U.S. at 99. Congress fostered this objective by adopting 
a “deliberately expansive” express pre-emption clause, Pilot Life Ins. Co. 
v. Dedeaux, 481 U.S. 41, 46 (1987), that is “conspicuous for its breadth,” 
FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52, 58 (1990). The clause, ERISA 
§ 514(a), provides that ERISA “shall supersede any and all State laws 
insofar as they may now or hereafter relate to any employee benefit plan” 
covered by ERISA. 29 U.S.C.A. § 1144(a) (West 1985). 


* The controlling test for determining whether a state law meets the “relate 
to” standard of ERISA § 514(a) is simple and direct: every state law that 
has a connection with or reference to an ERISA employee benefit plan 
relates to that plan and is pre-empted, whatever the state law’s underlying 
purpose and intent. Greater Wash. Bd of Trade, 506 U.S. at 129-130; 
FMC Corp., 498 U.S. at 58-59; Shaw, 463 U.S. at 96-97. The “relate to” 
standard must be interpreted broadly, see Shaw, 463 U.S. at 98; it must be 
given a “broad common sense meaning,” see /ngersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. at 
139. The “relate to” touchstone does not limit ERISA pre-emption only to 
state laws specifically designed to regulate the terms and conditions of 
employee benefit plans or those dealing with the subjecis of regulatory 
concern covered by ERISA — reporting, disclosure, fiduciary responsi- 
bility, and the like. /ngersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. at 137-39; Shaw, 463 U.S. at 
98. Pre-emption prevails even if the state law is not specifically designed

Analysis of ERISA’s legislative history led the Court to con-

was to avoid a multiplicity of regulation in order to permit the

Id. at 1677-78. So long as the state law does not require

a uniform interstate benefit package,” it does not give rise to

The key analytical tool for determining ERISA pre- 
emption, in the Travelers Court’s view, is the intent of Con- 
gress, as expressed in the language of the pre-emption provi- 
sion and its legislative history. 

While Congress’s extension of pre-emption to all 
“state laws relating to henefit plans” was meant to 
sweep more broadly than “state laws dealing with 
the subject matters covered by ERISA[,] reporting, 
disclosure, fiduciary responsibility, and the like,” 
nothing in the language of the Act or the context of 
its passage indicates that Congress chose to displace 
general health care regulation, which historically 
has been a matter of local concern. 


Id. at 1680 (citations omitted). In reaching this conclusion, the

Against this analytical backdrop the Travelers Court ruled 
that the New York surcharge laws, which have “only an indi- 
rect economic effect on the relative costs of various health 
insurance packages . . . [and] leave plan administrators right 
where they would be in any case, with the responsibility to

that any plan will shop for the best deal it can get, surcharges 
or no surcharges.” Jd. at 1679.

Decision Below 


The answer given was short and to the point: “Upon re- 
consideration in light of the Supreme Court’s holding in 
Travelers, we conclude that the ground upon which our earlier 
decision in this case rested remains undisturbed.” Axelrod, 74 
F.3d at 30. It took the court of appeals less than a page of 
analysis to reason that the Travelers decision of this Court, 
which limited its holding to “a law Operating as an indirect 
source c, merely economic influence on administrative deci- 
sions [by ERISA plans],” see id at 30 (citing Travelers, 115 
S. Ct. at 1680) (emphasis supplied), does not support shield- 
ing from ERISA pre-emption a statute like the New York tax 
at issue in this case which “depletes the Fund’s assets directly, 
and thus has an immediate impact on the operations of an 
ERISA plan.” /d. at 30 (emphasis supplied). Consequently, 
the court of appeals concluded that “[njothing in Travelers 
undermines our reliance on the HFA’s direct economic impact 
upon the Fund as a basis for finding ERISA pre-emption nor 
precludes a determination that a state law’s direct economic 
impact on ERISA plans is sufficient for a finding of pre- 
emption.” /d. at 30. 


Despite the apparent tension between the decision of the 
court of appeals in this case and comments made by other 
panels of the same circuit, neither rehearing nor rehearing en 
banc was sought. Instead, the petition for certiorari now be- 
fore this Court ensued.

REASONS FOR DENYING THE WRIT 


The Decision Below Does Not Deviate From The 
Mainstream of ERISA Pre-emption Law. 
Travelers In No Way Redeems From ERISA Pre- 
emption Direct State Taxation of Contributions 
Or Other Payments For Benefits Provided by An 
ERISA Plan. 


The decision of the court of appeals in this case defines the 
law of ERISA pre-emption when the state law at issue directly 
affects an ERISA plan. The court of appeals determined that 
ERISA pre-empts the New York law in this case because that 
state enactment “imposes ‘an immediate tax on payments and 
contributions wnich were intended to pay for participants’ 
medical benefits . . . [and] thus directly affects the Fund in its 
principal role as an employee welfare benefit plan.’” Axelrod, 
74 F.3d at 30, reaffirming 27 F.3d at 827. Neither this Court 
nor any other court has ever questioned the propriety of con- 
sidering a state law’s direct economic impact upon ERISA 
plans in adjudging whether the state enactment falls within 
the ambit of ERISA pre-emption. It is difficult to conceive 
of a state law that more clearly relates to ERISA plans than 
one which directly assesses contributions and other pay- 
ments that fund the health care benefits provided to plan 
beneficiaries. 


No Conflict With Travelers 


The decision of the court of appeals in this case is perfectly 
compatible with established pre-emption jurisprudence, and 
nothing in the Travelers decision suggests otherwise. Indeed, 
in Travelers this Court expressly declined to abandon the 
principles enunciated in its prior ERISA pre-emption deci- 
sions and intimated its inclination to have found ERISA pre- 
emption if the state law at issue in Travelers had had a direct 
effect upon ERISA plans. See Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1683. 
(“That said, we do not hold today that ERISA pre-empts only 
direct regulation of ERISA plans... .”).

No Conflict With Mackey 


Petitioners also attempt to fabricate a conflict where none 
exists between the decision below and Mackey v. Lanier Col- 
lection Agency & Serv., Inc., 486 U.S. 825 (1988). The pur- 
ported conflict rests upon an inaccurate interpretation of 
Mackey’s holding. Petitioners contend that Mackey sustained 
against a claim of ERISA pre-emption “a generally applicable 
garnishment statute . . . despite its direct imposition of legal 
obligations upon an ERISA plan which resulted in economic 
costs and administrative burdens to the plan.” See Cert. Pet. at 
7 (emphasis supplied); see also id at 10. But this Court in 
Travelers had a completely different understanding of its prior 
Mackey ruling. The Travelers Court noted that because 
Mackey had held that “a law authorizing an indirect source of 
administrative cost is not pre-empted, it should follow that a 
law operating as an indirect source of merely economic influ- 
ence on administrative decisions, as here, should not suffice 
to trigger pre-emption either.” Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1680 
(emphasis supplied). Mackey adds nothing new or different to 
what Travelers supplied to the ERISA pre-emption calculus. 
Neither Travelers nor Mackey contains any principle, holding,

The Congressional treatment of state tax laws within the 
context of ERISA pre-emption exposes the fallacy of the in- 
terpretation of Travelers propounded by petitioners. There 
are two separate and distinct reasons why Travelers does not 
stand for the proposition that state laws having only an eco- 
nomic impact on ERISA plans are not pre-empted. First, there 
is nothing in the 7ravelers decision that supports this reading. 
The ruling of the Travelers Court was limited to indirect eco- 
nomic impacts of an insubstantial nature. See 115 S. Ct. at 
1683. Second, tax laws by their very nature have as their nec- 
essary effect a purely economic impact on the affected tax- 
payer. A state tax by definition, therefore, affects or relates to 
an ERISA plan because the tax imposes an economic exaction 
that the plan must pay. If, as petitioners suggest, economic 
impact alone could never trigger pre-emption, the explicit 
Congressional directive to include state tax laws that relate to 
ERISA plans within the reach of ERISA pre-emption could 
never be fulfilled. Adoption of the suggestion urged by peti- 
tioners would effectively read the directive out of the statute, 
a result that would violate basic principles of statutory con- 
struction. See Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1679-80.

To shield state tax enactments from ERISA pre-emption 
would be inconsistent with the basic “objectives of the ERISA 
statute” — a key guide to defining the scope of ERISA pre- 
emption. /d. at 1677. Subjecting ERISA plans to the taxing 
powers of 50 separate and distinct jurisdictions would frus- 
trate the Congressional objective of allowing ERISA plans to 
adopt nationally uniform administrative practices and inter- 
state benefit packages, which the Travelers Court identified as 
one of the principal goals of ERISA pre-emption. See Travel- 
ers, 115 S. Ct. at 1677-78. State taxation of plan benefits and 
plan contributions would be incompatible with this Congres- 
sional objective. Benefits would vary from state to state de- 
pending upon the magnitude of each state’s tax bite. This 
Congressional objective is implicated in this very case. The 
New York tax at issue in this case has no New Jersey coun- 
terpart, thus requiring the NYSA-ILA Medical & Clinical 
Services Fund to restructure its established method of ad- 
ministration to implement procedures for its New York medi- 
cal clinics that differ from those of its New Jersey clinic. See 
Axelrod, 27 F.3d at 828. “Requirements under state law that 
force plans to change their traditional methods of administra- 
tion or to adopt different methods on a state-by-state basis are 
features of state regulation that ERISA was designed to pre- 
vent.” Jd. at 828 (citations omitted). 


The court of appeals had little difficulty dispelling the wolf 
cry repeated in the petition to this Court, see Cert. Pet. at 14- 
15, that pre-emption of the assessment on contributions would 
shield ERISA plans from numerous other forms of state 
regulation which result in increases in the cost of doing busi- 
ness, such as unemployment taxes, sales taxes, utility costs, 
wage laws, staffing requirements, nursing standards, or sani- 
tary regulations involving the disposal of medical wastes. 
These exactions and regulations affect ERISA plans as they 
do all others in society in their roles as employers, purchasers, 
and regulated businesses. The tax on contributions, however, 
affects ERISA plans qua employee benefit plans; it affects the 
very activities and functions that make employee benefit plans 
what they are. “It does not touch the Fund only slightly on the 
outer limits of its plan activities; it affects the very operations

and functions that make the Fund what it is, a provider of 
medical, surgical, and hospital care to its participants and 
their beneficiaries.” Axelrod, 27 F.3d at 827. It was hardly 
remarkable, then, for the court of appeals to have concluded 
that Congress intended the express pre-emption clause of 
ERISA to proscribe state assessments that sap the lifeblood of 
ERISA plans and directly hinder their essential functions. 


Petitioners’ real quarrel is with Congress’s policy of pre- 
empting state tax exactions. But this disagreement is not a 
justiciable controversy. Congress in its wisdom enacted in 
ERISA a pre-emption provision that deliberately protects 
ERISA plans from rapacious state taxes and other revenue- 
enhancing regulatory schemes. It is not the function of the 
Judicial Branch to override the policy decisions of the Legis- 
lative Branch. If Congressional policy is to be revamped, re- 
course must be sought in the halls of Congress, not in the 
chambers of this court. 


The court of appeals in this case, as did every other tribunal 
that has entertained the same question, applied established 
precedent to invalidate the immediate and direct imposition of 
a state tax levy on contributions to, and benefit payments of, 
an ERISA plan. The decision below is perfectly consistent 
with the analytical methodology adopted by this Court in 
Travelers, since it gives effect to Congressional intent, the 
touchstone for ERISA pre-emption. The decision below rests 
squarely upon a clear and explicit Congressional policy to 
pre-empt state tax impositions on ERISA plan contributions 
and benefits and to discourage any state interference with na- 
tionally uniform administrative procedures and benefit pack- 
ages. The decision below, therefore, is in perfect harmony 
with established principles of ERISA pre-emption law and 
should not be disturbed.

Conclusion 


This case presents no conflict with Supreme Court prece- 
dent, no discord among the circuits, no departure from the 
prevailing law of ERISA pre-emption. Petitioners may quib- 
ble with the underlying Congressional policy that led to the 
enactment of an ERISA pre-emption provision that supplants 
direct state taxation of ERISA plans, but their dissatisfaction 
is not a valid ground for review by this Court of a court of 
appeals decision that complies with the directive embodied in 
established ERISA pre-emption jurisprudence as expressed by 
this Court in a long line of cases culminating in the recent 
Travelers decision — to give effect to the will of Congress in 
ERISA pre-emption cases. The petition for certiorari should 
be denied.
