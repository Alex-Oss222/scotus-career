REPLY BRIEF FOR PETITIONERS 


This brief is submitted by petitioners (referred to collec- 
tively as “the State”) in response to the brief filed by respon- 
dents, trustees of the NYSA-ILA Medical and Clinical 
Services Fund (“the Fund”), and their amici. In support of its 
argument that the Health Facility Assessment (“HFA”) is pre- 


2 


empted, insofar as it is applied to Fund-owned medical cen- 
ters, the Fund attempts to distinguish the application of the 
HFA to its medical centers from the application of the HFA to 
non-ERISA plan-owned hospitals, from other generally appli- 
cable taxes, and from other State regulation of the Fund's 
medical centers. The Fund's effort to distinguish the HFA 
from the surcharges at issue in New York State Conference of 
Blue Cross and Blue Shield Plans v. Travelers Insurance Co., 
514 U.S. __, 115 S. Ct. 1671 (1995) (“Travelers”), and to 
carve out the result it desires in this case, does not withstand 
scrutiny. The Fund cannot distinguish the HFA from the sur- 
charges upheld in Travelers. Nor can it adequately explain 
why even the most narrow application of its theory would not 
require the preemption of all state regulation where an ERISA 
plan opts to provide healthcare benefits in-kind. 


I. GENERALLY APPLICABLE TAXES DO 
NOT RELATE TO AN ERISA PLAN SIMPLY 
BECAUSE THE PLAN PAYS THE TAXES 


The Fund first argues that no extended analysis, such as 
that in which this Court engaged in Travelers, is necessary in 
this case because the connection between the HFA and the 
Fund is “clear and unmistakable.” The Fund pays the tax and 
“unless the tax law relates, the ERISA plan would not have to 
pay the tax” (Resp. Br. 16).' This theory of ERISA preemp- 
tion of state tax laws is far too broad. It would require pre- 
emption of any generally applicable tax that happens to fall 
on any activity in which ERISA plans engage. Sales taxes, 
real estate taxes, unemployment taxes and gasoline taxes all 
“reach into the plan’s coffers to siphon off monies” (Resp. Br. 
16) and would be preempted under this analysis.’ Indeed, the

Fund itself eventually concedes that these types of generally 
applicable taxes do not in fact “relate to” plans that pay them 
(Resp. Br. 31).

The Fund then proffers a narrower theory, claiming that the 
application of a Travelers methodology leads to the conclu- 
sion that the HFA relates to the Fund. The Fund attempts to 
distinguish this case from the surcharges upheld in Travelers 
on two grounds. First, the Fund asserts that there is a clear 
Congressional intent to shield ERISA plans from state taxa- 
tion, as opposed to state healthcare regulation. Second, the 
Fund claims that the HFA directly assesses contributions and 
other benefit payments made by ERISA plans and plan par- 
ticipants and, thus, interferes with the essential purpose of the 
plan. The first argument ignores the statutory language of 
ERISA that applies the same “relates to” test to state tax laws 
as to any other state law. The second argument misconstrues 
the HFA that assesses hospital income, not plan benefits. 


A. State Tax Laws Are Treated As Any 
Other State Law for Purposes of ERISA 
Preemption 


The Fund's attempt to distinguish the HFA from the sur- 
charges in Travelers on the basis that it is a tax is both fac- 
tually and legally unsound. As a factual matter, the HFA can- 
not be distinguished from the surcharges in Travelers.’ Both

The hospital assessment at issue here differs significantly 
from the laws involved in the tax cases relied upon by the 
Fund. Unlike the tax at issue here, in those cases state taxes 
or assessments that were directed specifically at ERISA plans 
were held to be preempted. E-Systems, Inc. v. Pogue, 929 F.2d 
1100 (Sth Cir.), cert. denied, 502 U.S. 981 (1991) (state law 
that expressly imposed a 2.5% tax on claims and benefits paid 
under plans and on plan administrators’ fees is preempted); 
General Motors Corp. v. California Bd. of Equalization, 815 
F.2d 1305 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 485 U.S. 941 (1988) 
(tax computed on the basis of benefits paid by self-insured 
plans relates to plans because reference to plan activities is 
unavoidable); Birdsong v. Olson, 708 F. Supp. 793, 798 (W.D. 
Tex. 1989), appeal dismissed, 901 F.2d 1270 (Sth Cir. 1990) 
(the same law as was at issue in E-Systems is preempted); 
National Carriers’ Conference Comm. v. Heffernan, 454 
F. Supp. 914 (D. Conn. 1978) (law that required ERISA plans 
to pay a tax on amounts paid as benefits is preempted because 
it is specifically directed at plans). These cases fit within the 
Court’s holdings which have preempted state laws which refer 
to, are predicated upon, or which function only through the 
existence of, ERISA plans. District of Columbia v. Greater 
Washington Bd. of Trade, 506 U.S. 125 (1992); Ingersoll- 
Rand Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133 (1990). 


State tax laws of general applicability, like the tax at issue 
here, which happen to catch employee benefit plans within 
their wide sweep, have not been preempted. See Thiokol 
Corp., Morton International, Inc. v. Roberts, 76 F.3d 751,

The Fund mischaracterizes the State's argument concerning 
laws of general applicability. The State does not claim that all 
laws of general applicability are immune from ERISA pre- 
emption. Rather, the State acknowledges that laws of general 
applicability that dictate or restrict plan administration or 
benefit structure may relate to plans. Laws that are specifi- 
cally directed at employee benefit plans, Mackey v. Lanier 
Collection Agency and Serv., Inc., 486 U.S. 825, 830 (1988), 
or that depend upon the existence of employee benefit plans, 
Ingersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. 133, also relate to plans. 


Nor, contrary to the Fund's argument (Resp. Br. 20), does 
the State claim that laws with only an economic impact on 
plans are immune from preemption. A law with only an eco- 
nomic impact on plans nevertheless “relates to” plans when it 
targets ERISA plans, Greater Washington Bd. of Trade, 506 
U.S. 128; Mackey, 486 U.S. 830, when it depends upon the 
existence of ERISA plans, /ngersoll-Rand, 498 U.S. 133, or 
when its economic effect upon a plan is so “acute” that it 
effectively “forces an ERISA plan to adopt a certain scheme 
of substantive coverage or effectively restricts its choice of 
insurers,” Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 1683. 


Many of the Fund's argumenis in support of preemption of 
the HFA under a Travelers analysis are, in fact, identical to 
arguments rejected by this Court in Travelers. Thus, the argu- 


ments that the HFA relates to the Fund because its economic 
impact will deplete plan assets (Resp. Br. 22), will implicate 
interstate uniformity by varying the cost of benefits from state 
to state (Resp. Br. 21), and will provide an economic incen- 
tive to restructure administrative proceedings (Resp. Br. 21), 
have already been rejected by this Court. See 115 U.S. at 
1679-80. 


The Fund’s argument that the application of the HFA to its 
medical centers is in violation of section 302 of the Labor 
Management Relations Act of 1947, 29 U.S.C. § 186, and sec- 
tion 403(c)(1) of ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1103(c)(1), commonly 
known as the exclusive benefit rule, is unavailing. The argu- 
ment would apply equally to all taxes. The suggestion that the 
payment of healthcare taxes is in violation of the exclusive 
benefit rule has been rejected by every Court of Appeals that 
has addressed the issue. See Boyle v. Anderson, 68 F.3d 1094 
(8th Cir. 1995), cert. denied, ___ U.S. ___, 116 S. Ct. 1226 
(1996); New England Health Care Employees Union, District 
1199 v. Mount Sinai Hospital, 65 F.3d 1024, 1032 (2d Cir. 
1995); United Wire, Metal & Machine Health & Welfare Fund 
v. Morristown Memorial Hosp., 996 F.2d 1179, 1196 (3d Cir.), 
cert. denied, 510 U.S. 944 (1993). 


In the end, the Fund's argument that the HFA relates to 
plans because it is a tax begs the question. The Fund's claim 
that the HFA relates to plans, and is distinguishable from the 
surcharges in Travelers, is squarely based upon the fact that 
the HFA is a tax, and not on a claim that the HFA affects 
plans differently from the surcharges upheld in Travelers. 
Taxes are preempted only if they “relate to” plans. Thus, the 
mere fact that a law imposes a tax on plans cannot alone 
establish the claim that the law “relates to” plans. 


B. The HFA Assesses Hospital Income, Not 
ERISA Plan Benefits 


The Fund also argues that the HFA relates to ERISA plans, 
and is distinguishable from the surcharges upheld in Travel- 
ers, because it directly taxes contributions and other payments 
for health care benefits. This characterization misconstrues 
the HFA and its effect on plans. The HFA is not an assessment 
imposed upon employer contributions. Nor is it a tax on ben- 
efits paid by ERISA plans. It is a tax on hospital income. 
Despite the Fund’s characterization, it is unable to articulate 
a coherent theory under which the HFA would be preempted 
as applied to patient care income received by its medical cen- 
ters, but not preempted as applied to patient care income that 
any non-fund owned hospital can trace to an ERISA plan. 


The HFA is an assessment imposed upon hospitals mea- 
sured by hospital receipts “for or on account of hospital and 
health related services,” regardless of source. It was not 
designed to tax ERISA plans or ERISA benefit payments. 
Rather, the HFA covers the Fund’s medical centers only 
because they come within the wide sweep of the covered class 
of “hospitals.” Payments to the medical centers for patient 
care services are subject to the assessment only because they 
fall within the broad definition of “gross receipts.” A payment 
to a hospital from an ERISA plan may constitute a benefit to 
the plan participant on whose behalf the payment is made. To 
the ERISA plan that pays it, the payment may constitute the 
payment of benefits. But to the hospital, the taxed entity, it is 
simply income. 


The laws at issue in Travelers set hospital rates for all 
patients, imposed surcharges on certain rates, and imposed an 
assessment on HMOs. N.Y. Pub. Health Law §§ 2807-c(1)(a), 
(b), (c), 2807-c(2-a). Their effect on ERISA plans is identical 
to that of the HFA. First, none of these laws applies to the 
plans themselves. Second, when ERISA plans provide hos- 
pital care benefits to their participants, the HFA, like the laws 


9 


at issue in Travelers, may increase the cost of providing such 
benefits to plans. 


This analysis is not affected by the fact that an ERISA plan 
may own and operate a medical center subject to the assess- 
ment. If the HFA can be characterized as a tax “on contribu- 
tions and other payments for healthcare benefits provided by 
ERISA plans” (Resp. Br. 10), that characterization is appli- 
cable anytime any ERISA plan pays money to any hospital on 
behalf of a plan participant. Ownership of the hospital is irrel- 
evant to this characterization. The Fund itself does not dif- 
ferentiate between income the medical centers receive from 
employers who participate in its own plan and patient care 
income from other plans. Instead, the Fund’s position is that 
ERISA preempts the HFA even when the assessed income can 
be traced to ERISA plans unrelated to the Fund (Resp. Br. 
7,8). Thus, according to the Fund, payments from the PPGU 
fund, which is unrelated to the NYSA fund, are preempted 
from being included in assessable income. The Fund also 
argues that payments from workers’ compensation carriers, 
who are also third parties to the Fund, cannot be included in 
assessable income. Yet, with regard to these entities, the 
Fund’s medical centers operate no differently than any other 
non-fund owned hospital. They provide medical services for 
which they receive payments that constitute income to the 
centers. 


All hospitals receive a large portion of their income for 
patient care services from ERISA plans. The only difference 
between this case and a case involving a hospital not owned 
by an ERISA plan is the portion of the hospital’s income that 
is derived from ERISA plan payments. In this case, all assess- 
able income from “patient care services” comes from ERISA 
plans, either the PPGU plan, the NYSA plan, or workers’ 
compensation carriers. In the case of a hospital open to the 
general public, income will come from medicaid payments, 
medicare payments, self-insured ERISA plans, insured ERISA 
plans, non-ERISA employee benefit plans, private insurance 


10 


and individuals’ payments. However, since 88% of non- 
elderly Americans get their health insurance through their 
employer,° a very large portion of any hospital’s income could 
be characterized as payments and contributions which are 
intended to pay for ERISA plan participants’ medical 
benefits. 


Moreover, the effect of the HFA on the ERISA plans which 
pay for medical benefits is the same whether or not a hospi- 
tal is owned by an ERISA plan. The PPGU plan, which makes 
payments to the Fund’s medical centers, the NYSA plan 
employers, which send contributions to the medical centers, 
as well as all the ERISA plans that make payments to non- 
fund owned hospitals, are economically affected by the HFA. 


Ill. A GENERALLY APPLICABLE TAX ON HOS- 
PITAL INCOME DOES NOT RELATE TO AN 
ERISA PLAN SIMPLY BECAUSE THE PLAN 
OWNS THE HOSPITAL 


Relying on section 3(1) of ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1002(1), 
which defines a plan as an entity whose purpose is to provide 
healthcare benefits “through the purchase of insurance or oth- 
erwise,” the Fund argues that its ownership of the medical 
centers distinguishes this case from Travelers. According to 
the Fund, “[i)n dispensing care and treatment at its clinics the 
Fund is engaging in the very activity” that defines it as an 
ERISA plan (Resp. Br. 24). Thus, the Fund argues, it does not 
take “Jamesian metaphysics” to see that all state regulation of 
healthcare relates to the plan when an ERISA plan provides 
healthcare benefits in-kind (Resp. Br. 31-32). This theory of 
ERISA preemption would free ERISA plans from state regu- 
lation whenever they opted to provide any benefit in-kind. 
Nor, under this theory, can the Fund distinguish the HFA from

ERISA defines a plan as a program whose purpose is to 
provide certain benefits. By definition, benefits are separate 
and distinct from plans. A benefit is not magically trans- 
formed into a plan when a plan chooses to provide it in-kind. 
For example, when plans provide benefits “through the pur- 
chase of insurance,” not all operations of the insurance com- 
pany become part of the plan. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. 
Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724, 741 (1985). When plans provide 
benefits “otherwise” through HMOs, which also provide med- 
ical care in-kind, not every aspect of the provision of medical 
care implicates ERISA preemption. See Dukes v. U.S. Health- 
care, Inc., 57 F.3d 350 (3d Cir. 1995). 


In Travelers, the HMOs made arguments concerning their

ment in this case. There the HMOs relied upon the statute’s 
“otherwise” language to argue that when a plan contracts with 
an HMO which provides medical care, the HMO is the mech- 
anism through which the plan “otherwise” provides benefits. 
The HMO, therefore, is an integral part of the plan. The 
HMOs argued that they were immune from state regulation 
because there is a “confluence between the employee benefit 
plan and the HMO which is the entity that is actually pro- 
viding for [the] participants . . . the medical, surgical or hos- 
pital care set forth in the statute.” Brief for Respondents New 
York State Health Maintenance Organization Conference er 
al. at 20, submitted in New York State Conference of Blue 
Cross and Blue Shield Plans v. The Travelers Insurance Co.., 
514 U.S.__, 115 S. Ct. 1671 (1995) (Nos. 93-1408, 93-1414, 
93-1415). This Court rejected these arguments sub-silentio. 


The “relate to” standard urged by the Fund, which would 
preempt all laws that affect a plan’s provision of benefits, 
provides no limitation on the scope of ERISA preemption.

Employee benefit plans may not engage in activities unrelated 
to the provision of benefits. They cannot, for example, oper- 
ate amusement parks or shoe stores since ERISA requires 
all assets be used for the exclusive purpose of providing ben- 
efits to participants or deferring reasonable administrative 
expenses. 29 U.S.C. § 1103(c)(1). This is true whether or not 
a plan provides benefits in-kind. Thus, under the Fund's the- 
ory, all plan activities are shielded from state regulation. Fur- 
ther, while the Fund admits that it is subject to state law when 
it is engaging in activities that “are not peculiar to employee 
benefit plans but are carried on by other private entities” 
(Resp. Br. 31), when a plan does provide healthcare, day 
care, or prepaid legal services in-kind, it is in fact engaging 
in activities in which many other private entities engage. 
This contradiction renders the Fund’s proposed standard 
meaningless. 


In order to provide benefits through its medical centers, 
the Fund must hire employees, including nurses, doctors 
and medical records clerks. It must purchase medical supplies 
and pay utility bills for necessary electricity. All of these 
functions are essential to the operation of a hospital and, 
therefore, under the Fund’s theory, affect the plan as a plan 
(Resp. Br. 31). Yet, the Fund concedes that when a plan oper- 
ates as an employer or a purchaser of goods and services it 
may be regulated by the State. State unemployment taxes on 
plan doctors, sales taxes on syringes, gasoline taxes on plan- 
owned ambulances, and minimum wage requirements for 
medical records clerks affect the provision of benefits to the 
same extent as staffing requirements and medical waste reg- 
ulations. Once again, the Fund’s test casts too wide a net and 
is unable to distinguish functions which are peculiar to 
employee benefit plans from those in which many other enti- 
ties engage. 


Perhaps realizing that a test for ERISA preemption that 
invalidates all state laws that affect a plan's in-kind provision

of benefits is untenably broad,’ the Fund finally retreats from 
this position. Instead, the Fund offers a new test of preemp- 
tion: although all regulation “relates to” the in-kind provision 
of benefits provided by ERISA plans, only those laws that do 
not involve the historic police powers of the state are pre- 
empted (Resp. Br. 32). The Fund places tax laws outside this 
arena, but refuses to speculate as to where other state laws 
would fall. The Fund frankly concedes that such a test 
requires a case-by-case determination (Resp. Br. 32). This test 
ignores the statutory language of ERISA, which treats tax 
laws like all other state laws. Instead, it substitutes an inquiry 
tailored to the facts of this case for statutory analysis.

about “unqualified nurses, unprotected children, unethical lawyers and
