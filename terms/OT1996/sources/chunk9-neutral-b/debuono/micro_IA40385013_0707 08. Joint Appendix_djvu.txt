PER CURIAM: 


On June 23, 1994, we entered a judgment and filed an opin- 
ion, NYSA-ILA Medical & Clinical Servs. Fund v. Axelrod, 27 
F.3d 823, which reversed a summary judgment entered 
February 23, 1993 by the United States District Court for the 
Southern District of New York (John S. Martin, Jr., Judge). 
The district court had held that the Employee Retirement 
Income Security Act (“ERISA”), 29 U.S.C. § 1001 et seq., did 
not preempt New York’s Health Facility Assessment (“HFA”), 
N.Y. Pub. Health Law § 2807-d, reasoning that the tax 
imposed by the HFA “is not great enough to pose a serious 
economic threat to the plan.” In reversing, we held that the 
HFA directly affected and was therefore “related to” the Fund 
in its principal role as an employee welfare plan. 


On May 1, *995, the Supreme Court entered an order grant- 
ing certiorari, vacating the judgment of this court, and 
remanding the case to us for further consideration in light of 
New York State Conference of Blue Cross & Blue Shield Plans 
v. Travelers Ins. Co., 115 S. Ct. 1671 (1995), rev'g 14 F.3d 
708 (2d Cir. 1994). See Chassin v. NYSA-ILA Medical & 
Clinical Servs. Fund, 115 S. Ct. 1819 (1995). 


We ordered counsel for the parties to brief the issue of 
whether Travelers requires a different result in this case. 


° Honorable Robert C. Zampano, United States District Judge for 
the District of Connecticut, sitting by designation. Judge Zampano retired 
shortly after oral argument was heard in this case. Accordingly, the 
appeal was decided by the remaining panel members pursuant to Section 
0.14 of the Local Rules of the Second Circuit. 


JA8 


Upon receipt of these briefs, we carefully reconsidered this 
case in light of Travelers. For the reasons that follow, we 
adhere to our original decision reversing the judgment of the 
district court. 


The undisputed facts in this case have been set forth both 
in the district court opinion and in our original opinion, and 
we therefore do not fully restate them here. The Trustees of 
NYSA-ILA Medical & Clinical Services Fund (the “Fund”) 
brought this suit seeking a declaration that ERISA preempts 
New York Public Health Law § 2807-d to the extent that the 
HFA taxes contributions and other payments for health care 
benefits received by two medical centers operated by the 
Fund. On appeal, we found that the HFA directly “relates to” 
the Fund in that “[t)he tax depletes those assets earmarked for 
the provision of health care benefits and, as a result, will 
cause the Fund to reduce benefits provided and/or to charge 
beneficiaries more in the future for benefits received.” 
Axelrod, 27 F.3d at 827. 


Upon reconsideration in light of the Supreme Court’s hold- 
ing in Travelers, we conclude that the ground upon which our 
earlier decision in this case rested remains undisturbed. 
Travelers involved a challenge to New York’s imposition of 
a surcharge on hospital bills paid by commercial insurers and 
Health Maintenance Organizations but not on hospital bills 
paid by Blue Cross/Blue Shield insurers. The statute at issue 
in Travelers did not impose surcharges on ERISA plans them- 
selves, but on insurance carriers providing health insurance 
coverage to the plans and their beneficiaries. The surcharge 
consequently raised costs to the patient, who remained liable 
for the balance of any bill an ERISA plan or insurance carrier 
refuses to pay. The Supreme Court concluded that “a law 
operating as an indirect source of merely economic influence 
_on administrative decisions [by ERISA plans], as here, should 
not suffice to trigger pre-emption.” Travelers, 115 S. Ct. at 
1680. 


JA9 


The facts of the present case distinguish it from Travelers, 
because, unlike in Travelers, there is no third party between 
the plan and the tax imposed pursuant to the HFA. Rather, the 
Statute at issue depletes the Fund’s assets directly, and thus 
has an immediate impact on the operations of an ERISA plan. 
As we observed in our original opinion, the HFA imposes “an 
immediate tax on payments and contributions which were 
intended to pay for participants’ medical benefits. . . [and] 
thus directly affects the Fund in its principal role as an 
employee welfare benefit plan.” Axelrod, 27 F.3d at 827. 


Nothing in Travelers undermines our reliance on the HFA’s 
direct economic impact upon the Fund as a basis for finding 
ERISA pre-emption nor precludes a determination that a state 
law’s direct economic impact on ERISA plans is sufficient for 
a finding of pre-emption. Because our reasoning is not incon- 
sistent with the Supreme Court’s opinion in Travelers, we 
adhere to our original disposition that reversed and remanded 
this case.

WALKER, Circuit Judge: 


The Trustees of NYSA-ILA Medical & Clinical Services 
Fund (the “Fund”) brought this suit seeking a declaration that 
the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (“ERISA”), 29 
U.S.C. § 1001 et seq., preempts New York Public Health Law 
§ 2807-d to the extent the latter provision taxes contributions 
and other payments for health care benefits received by two 
medical centers operated by the Fund. The Fund now appeals 
from a judgment of the United States District Court for the 
Southern District of New York (John S. Martin, Jr., Judge) 
holding that ERISA does not preempt the state tax and grant- 
ing summary judgment to defendant state health care officials. 
We reverse. 


BACKGROUND 


The Fund is an ERISA-regulated multiemployer, labor-man- 
agement trust fund established through collective bargaining to 
administer an employee welfare benefit plan that provides med- 
ical, dental, and other health care services to ERISA plan par- 
ticipants, consisting primarily of longshore workers, and their 
dependents. The Fund provides these health care benefits 
through three medical centers that it owns and operates, two in 


° Honorable Robert C. Zampano, United States District Judge for 
the District of Connecticut, sitting by designation. Judge Zampano retired 
shortly after oral argument was heard in this case. Accordingly, the 
appeal was decided by the remaining panel members pursuant to Section 
0.14 of the Local Rules of the Second Circuit. 


JA 12 


New York and one in New Jersey. The centers provide treat- 
ment exclusively to plan participants and beneficiaries. 


In 1990, the State of New York enacted a Health Facility 
Assessment (“HFA”), which imposed a tax on thé gross 
receipts from patient care services and general operations of all 
hospitals in New York State. N.Y. Pub. Health Law § 2807-d. 
The HFA was enacted to help eliminate the State’s budget 
deficit for the 1990-91 fiscal year. The HFA became effective 
January 1, 1991, and was supposed to expire on April 1, 1992, 
but has subsequently been ameaded to remain in force on and 
after that date. 1992 N.Y. Laws, ch. 41, §§ 54, 163. The HFA 
applies to a wide range of medical facilities, including diag- 
nostic and treatment centers such as the medical centers Oper- 
ated by the Fund. N.Y. Pub. Health Law § 2801(1). The amount 
of the assessment, which varies by type of facility, is six-tenths 
of one percent for the Fund’s medical centers. /d. § 2807- 
d(2)(c). For purposes of the assessment, “gross receipts” are 
defined as “all monies received for or on account of hospital 
and health related services” with only limited exceptions. /d. 
§ 2807-d(3)(c). 


The Fund’s medical centers receive income from a vafiety of 
sources. The bulk of their costs are covered by employér con- 
tributions remitted to the Fund which are then transferred to the 
centers on an as-needed basis to pay for the centers’ payroll 
and other operating expenses. However, the centers obtain 
additional revenue from certain employer payments made 
directly to the centers, employee co-payments, other fee-for- 
service payments, patient registration fees, payments from 
workers’ compensation carriers, and such miscellaneous 
sources as the sale of scrap or waste, fees for reproducing 
medical records, and supplier rebates. For the period from 
January 1, 1991 to November 30, 1991, the Fund’s two New 
York medical centers reported combined assessable income of 
$1,177,670 and paid assessments totalling $7,066. The Fund 
discontinued filing HFA reports with the New York State 


JA 13 


Department of Health after November 1991 and has paid no 
assessments since that time. 


The medical centers did not include in their assessable 
income monies transferred from the Fund to the centers, and 
the State defendants have not contested the exclusion of these 
payments in this case. However, the question of whether 
monies transferred from the Fund to the centers are assessable 
has never, to our knowledge, been litigated or definitively 
resolved. Since early 1992, the Fund has restructured its pay- 
ment procedures so that certain employer contributions and 
registration fees that were formerly paid directly to the medi- 
cal centers are now remitted to the Fund itself. The Fund 
believes that this restructuring avoids the effect of the HFA, but 
is unwilling to redirect to the Fund all payments now received 
by the medical centers until this case has been decided. 


The Fund instituted this suit in April 1992 asserting that 
ERISA preempts the HFA insofar as it imposes a levy on con- 
tributions and payments for health care benefits provided by 
the Fund. The Fund does not challenge imposition of the HFA 
on the centers’ miscellaneous revenue earned from activities 
unrelated to the health care services provided to participants 
and beneficiaries, such as the income derived from the sale of 
scrap, records reproduction fees, and supplier rebates. The 
Fund sought declaratory and injunctive relief as well as resti- 
tution of a substantial portion of the HFA assessments it had 
previously paid. 


On cross-motions for summary judgment, the district court 
ruled that the HFA is a tax of general application that is not 
preempted by ERISA because it has only an incidental impact 
on ERISA plans. The district court reasoned that the minimal 
tax imposed by the HFA “is not great enough to pose a serious 
economic threat to the plan which might trigger preemption.” 
NYSA-ILA Medical & Clinical Servs. Fund v. Axelrod, 92 
Civ. 2279 (JSM), slip op. at 8 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 1992). 
Accordingly, the district court entered judgment dismissing the 
action and this appeal ensued. We review the district court's 


JA 14 


decision de novo. See Westinghouse Elec. Corp. v. New York 
City Transit Auth., 14 F.3d 818, 821 (2d Cir. 1994). 


DISCUSSION 


ERISA was enacted in 1974 “to promote the interests of 
employees and their beneficiaries in employee benefit plans,” 
including both pension and welfare plans. Shaw v. Delta Air 
Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85, 90 (1983). An “employee welfare ben- 
efit plan” is defined in the statute as 


any plan, fund, or program. . . established or maintained 
by an employer or by an employee organization, or by 
both, to the extent that such plan, fund, or program was 
established or is maintained for the purpose of providing 
for its participants or their beneficiaries, through the pur- 
chase of insurance or otherwise, . . . medical, surgical, or 
hospital care or benefits, or benefits in the event of sick- 
ness, accident, disability, death or unemployment. . . . 


29 U.S.C. § 1002(1). The parties do not dispute that the Fund 
qualifies as an employee welfare benefit plan that fulfills its 
institutional mission by providing medical benefits directly to 
plan participants and their beneficiaries through Fund-operated 
medical centers. 


One of ERISA’s main objectives was to eliminate state reg- 
ulation of employee benefit plans. See Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. 
McClendon, 498 U.S. 133, 138 (1990); Shaw, 463 U.S. at 99 
(quoting legislative history). To accomplish this, Congress 
enacted an express preemption clause that is “deliberately 
expansive.” Pilot Life Ins. Co. v. Dedeaux, 481 U.S. 41, 46 
(1987). It provides that ERISA “shall supersede any and al! 
State laws insofar as they may now or hereafter relate to any 
employee benefit plan” covered by the law. 29 U.S.C. 
§ 1144(a). The Supreme Court has observed that “[a] law 
‘relates to’ an employee benefit plan, in the normal sense of the 
phrase, if it has a connection with or reference to such a plan.” 


JA 15 


Shaw, 463 U.S. at 96-97. Preemption thus applies even where 
a state law was not specifically designed to regulate the terms 
and conditions of employee benefits plans or does not relate to 
those subjects covered by ERISA, such as reporting, disclosure, 
and fiduciary responsibilities. See Ingersoll-Rand Co., 498 U.S. 
at 137-39; FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 498 U.S. 52, 58-59 (1990); 
Shaw, 463 U.S. at 98. Indeed, even where a state law has no 
express link to an employee benefit plan, it can be preempted 
“ “insofar as’ the law applies to benefit plans in particular 
cases.” Morgan Guar. Trust Co. v. Tax Appeals Tribunal, 80 
N.Y.2d 44, 49 (1992) (quoting Shaw, 463 U.S. at 97 n.17). 


ERISA preemption is not without limitations, however. The 
Supreme Court has recognized that ERISA preemption will not 
apply to state laws that “affect employee benefit plans in too 
tenuous, remote, or peripheral a manner to warrant a finding 
that the law ‘relates to’ the plan.” Shaw, 463 U.S. at 100 n.21. 
When it announced this limitation, the Court “express[ed] no 
views about where it would be appropriate to draw the line,” 
id., but it has subsequently observed that this limitation will 
apply “with many laws of general applicability,” District of 
Columbia v. Greater Wash. Bd. of Trade, 113 S. Ct. 580, 583 
n.1 (1992). There are a number of cases that bear out this 
observation. See, e.g., Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & 
Serv., Inc., 486 U.S. 825 (1988) (generally applicable gar- 
nishment law not preempted); Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. 
Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 (1987) (state law requiring one-time sev- 
erance payment not preempted); Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Borges, 
869 F.2d 142 (2d Cir.) (state escheat law not preempted when 
applied to recover unclaimed ERISA plan benefits), cert. 
denied, 493 U.S. 811 (1989); see also Matter of Dyke, 943 F.2d 
1435, 1447 (Sth Cir. 1991) (collecting cases). Unfortunately, 
these decisions have provided no sure method for determining 
whether the HFA’s impact on the Fund is too “tenuous, remote, 
or peripheral” to trigger ERISA preemption. 


The State argues that the HFA is a state tax law of general 
application that affects ERISA plans only incidentally. 


JA 16 


According to the State, the HFA is no more directed at ERISA 
plans than laws implementing sales taxes, utility taxes, and 
gasoline taxes, and should thus be seen simply as a “cost of 
doing business” within the state. Relying on Mackey and Aetna 
Life Ins. Co., the State contends that the HFA should not be 
preempted because it covers ERISA plans only by virtue of the 
plans’ inclusion in a broader class of entities subject to regu- 
lation. 


However, we believe that the HFA is distinguishable from 
these laws of general application. The HFA does not apply 
broadly to every sector of society in New York; nor does it 
apply to the Fund simply because it engaged in certain kinds of 
activities which any other member of society might engage in, 
such as purchasing gasoline or paying one’s employees. Rather, 
the HFA targets only the health care industry. Because this 
industry is, by definition, the realm where ERISA welfare plans 
must operate, the HFA was bound to affect them. In a case such 
as the present one where the plan has chosen to provide med- 
ical benefits through self-run medical centers, the HFA oper- 
ates as an immediate tax on payments and contributions which 
were intended to pay for participants’ medical benefits. The 
HFA thus directly affects the Fund in its principal role as an 
employee welfare benefit plan. It does not touch the Fund only 
slightly on the outer limits of its plan activities; it affects the 
very operations and functions that make the Fund what it is, a 
provider of medical, surgical, and hospital care to its partici- 
pants and their beneficiaries. 


We thus conclude that the HFA “relates to” the Fund and 
does so in a very direct way. The tax depletes those assets ear- 
marked for the provision of health care benefits and, as a result, 
will cause the Fund to reduce benefits provided and/or to 
charge beneficiaries more in the future for benefits received. 
Both impacts will detrimentally affect the central mission and 
purpose of the Fund and hence cannot be characterized as 
remote or tenuous. See Travelers Ins. Co. v. Cuomo, 14 F.3d 
708, 720 (2d Cir. 1993) (“[T]he surcharges here force ERISA 


JA 17 


plans to increase either plan costs or reduce plan benefits. 
Therefore, they have the requisite connection to ERISA.”), 
petition for cert. filed, 62 U.S.L.W. 3625 (U.S. Mar. 9, 1994) 
(No. 93-1414); E-Systems, Inc. v. Pogue, 929 F.2d 1100, 1103 
(Sth Cir.) (“The cost of the plan must therefore increase for the 
employer and/or employees or the benefits must be adjusted 
downwards to offset the tax bite. This is the type of impact 
Congress intended to avoid when it enacted the ERISA legis- 
lation.”), cert. denied, 112 S. Ct. 585 (1991). 


Our decision would remain the same even if we assumed that 
the Fund could avoid the HFA by restructuring its finances so 
that funds formerly remitted by plan participants and employ- 
ers directly to the medical centers would be remitted to the 
Fund which would subsequently transfer them to the centers. 
Wholly apart from whether the transmission of assets from the 
Fund to the centers could be taxed without running afoul of 
ERISA preemption, an issue we need not and do not decide, 
such a restructuring would alter the Fund’s established method 
of administration and would require it to implement procedures 
for its New York facilities that differ from those of its New 
Jersey facility. Moreover, these changes would not be insignif- 
icant since they would affect the Fund's primary means of col- 
lecting contributions and paying for benefits. A state law that 
“relates to” an ERISA plan is not saved from ERISA preemp- 
tion by the fact that a plan could restructure its operations to 
avoid the impact of the state law. Requirements under state law 
that force plans to change their traditional methods of admin- 
istration or to adopt different methods on a state-by-state basis 
are features of state regulation that ERISA was designed to pre- 
vent. See FMC Corp., 498 U.S. at 59-60; Alessi v. Raybestos- 
Manhattan, Inc., 451 U.S. 504, 524 (1981); National Carriers’ 
Conference Comm. v. Heffernan, 454 F. Supp. 914, 918 (D. 
Conn. 1978) (Newman, J.). Compare Aetna Life Ins. Co., 869 
F.2d at 147 (discounting Fund’s complaint that it would need to 
make administrative and accounting adjustments in order to 
comply with state escheat law because the impact was not sub- 
stantial enough). 


JA 18 


The district court acknowledged that the HFA will directly 
impact the Fund by leaving less money for benefits, but deter- 
mined that the magnitude of the impact was too insubstantial to 
trigger preemption since the tax rate was only .6%. We do no 
agree with this reasoning. The magnitude of a statute’s eco- 
nomic impact on employee benefit plans may enter the analy- 
sis where the statute’s connection to the ERISA plan is 
otherwise insufficient for preemption. For example, a real 
estate gains tax that has only a peripheral connection to ERISA 
plans may still be subject to preemption if the high rate of tax 
has a substantial economic impact on a plan. See Morgan Guar. 
Trust Co., 80 N.Y.2d at 51-53 (ten percent tax on gain from 
ERISA plan’s sale of real property preempted because of direct 
impact on plan’s administration and investment strategy). 
However, the converse is not true. A statute that “relates to” 
ERISA plans cannot escape preemption simply because the 
magnitude of the impact is thought to be insubstantial. 
Adopting such an approach would contravene the policy that 
underlies the preemption provision of protecting ERISA plans 
from state regulation and would open the door to endless liti- 
gation about what constitutes a “substantial” impact. 


Travelers Insurance Co. v. Cuomo, supra, is not to the con- 
trary. In that case, we held that New York surcharges on hos- 
pital rates were preempted by ERISA to the extent ERISA 
plans must pay surcharges. In the course of our analysis, we 
discussed Judge Martin’s opinion in this case without criti- 
cizing it. However, we cited it only for the proposition that “a 
substantial economic impact, standing alone, could be enough 
to bring ERISA’s preemption clause into play.” 14 F.3d at 721. 
We did not endorse—and now expressly refuse to endorse—the 
converse proposition urged by the State: that a regulation 
which has a de minimis economic impact on ERISA plans may 
withstand preemption regardless of what plan operations it 
affects. 


For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the judgment of the 
district court.

Dear Governor Cuomo: 


We understand that you now have before you for consid- 
eration S.9190/A.12232, the recently negotiated deficit reduc- 
tion proposal for the Medicaid program. Included in this 
proposal is authorization for the Commissioner of Social Ser- 
vices to implement a program of Medicaid Utilization Thresh- 
olds (MUTS). We urge that this provision of the legislation 
not be approved. 

As you are well aware, utilization thresholds for Medicaid 
patients has been a highly controversial proposal for several 
years. While we appreciate that some of our concerns and rec- 
ommendations for improvements to the program have been 
recognized and incorporated into its current form, we remain 
seriously concerned about MUTS’ potential to be detrimen- 
tal to quality of care, further erode physician relationship with 
the Medicaid program, and deter patients from seeking appro- 
priate and much needed health care services. In brief, our 
opposition to MUTS can be summarized as follows: 


* The program imposes an arbitrary maximum limit to 
health care for the poor; 


* The program would create an incentive for shifting 
medical care from the less costly private setting to hos- 
pital emergency rooms; 


JA 45 


* There is a potential for significant implementation 
problems in that the program does not encompass an 
appropriate recourse for the verification and authoriza- 
tion of medical care if the “system” encounters a func- 
tional problem (as it inevitably will, e.g., the computer 
network can malfunction, telephone lines can jam, staff 
determinations can backlog); 


* The aforementioned functional problems would lead to 
an ethical dilemma regarding the treatment of patients 
whose eligibility for care cannot be verified: 


* The addition of onerous eligibility and verification 
processes to an already abysmally low reimbursement 
schedule would discourage current and future providers 
and inhibit access to care; and 


* Finally, the proposal is contradictory to New York 
State’s public policy objectives of expanded access to 
quality health care for all New Yorkers. This program 
would arbitrarily segregate a segment of New York 
State’s Medicaid population and limit services available 
to those persons. For this segment of the Medicaid pop- 
ulation, this program represents nothing less than 
RATIONING OF MEDICAL AND HEALTH CARE 
SERVICES. 


We, of course, recognize that New York State’s fiscal con- 
dition warrants painful choices and that fiscally responsible 
policies are warranted to address the State’s fiscal deficit. To 
this end, we would recommend that, in lieu of MUTS, the 
State could pursue other policy options. These recommen- 
dations include: 


* Meaningful reform of the medical liability system. 
It is unconscienable to limit service to the poor when 
20% of our health care dollar is being squandered on a 


JA 46 


grossly inefficient system that does not appropriately 
meet the needs of any of the system's participants; 


* Strengthen existing fraud and abuse activities with 
regard to providers but with complete and full protection 
of the due process rights of physicians; 


* Strengthen the restricted service program for over- 
utilizing patients; 


* Expand funding for alternate care programs, e.g. man- 
aged care; and 


* Creation of a study commission to examine the total 
restructuring of the Medicaid program. 


We cannot overstate our concern for the serious negative 
impact that this program would have on the Medicaid pro- 
gram and the harm it would cause to the quality and avail- 
ability of medical services to poor New Yorkers. For the 
above-mentioned reasons, we respectfully request that you 
use your veto authority to disapprove this initiative. 


Respectfully,

RUBIN D. FIER, being duly sworn, deposes and says: 


1. I am the treasurer of the NYSA-ILA Medical and Clin- 
ical Services Fund (hereinafter “Fund”), which along with 
its trustees are the plaintiffs in this lawsuit. I make this affi- 
davit in support of plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment 
invalidating on Employee Retirement Income Security Act 
(hereinafter “ERISA”) preemption grounds the imposition by 
the State of New York of an assessment on certain contribu- 
tions received by medical centers operated by the Fund to 
provide healthcare benefits to the Fund’s participants and 
their dependents. I am intimately acquainted with the affairs 
of the Fund and its medical centers and have personal know- 
ledge of the facts set forth in this affidavit. 


Structure of the Fund 


2. The Fund is a jointly administered, multiemployer, 
labor-management trust fund established in accordance with 
§ 302(c)(S) of the Labor Management Relations Act. 29 
U.S.C.A. § 186(c)(5) (West Supp. 1992), and pursuant to an 
Agreement and Declaration of Trust and Plan and collective 
bargaining agreements between the New York Shipping Asso- 
ciation, Inc. (“NYSA”) and the International Longshoremen’s 
Association, AFL-CIO (“ILA”), prescribing the terms and 
conditions of longshore employment in the Port of New York 
and New Jersey. The Fund is a self-insured employee welfare 
benefit plan under ERISA § 3(1), 29 U.S.C.A. § 1002(1) 
(West Supp. 1992), that provides medical, dental and related 
healthcare services on an out-patient ambulatory basis to over 
20,000 eligible beneficiaries consisting of plan participants 
and their dependents, including more than 13,000 in the State 
of New York. 


3. There are four classes of persons receiving healthcare 
services from the Fund. The first and predominant class con- 
sists of longshore workers and retirees and their dependents, 
whose right to Fund benefits stems from longshore employ- 
ment in the Port. The NYSA-ILA labor contract entitles these 


JA 76 


individuals to receive healthcare at medical centers operated 
by the Fund in the states of New York and New Jersey. The 
second class is composed of warehouse and other workers 
represented by non-longshore ILA locals who are eligible for 
treatment at Fund medical centers under the terms of separate 
labor contracts with their employers, who for purposes of dif- 
ferentiating them from longshore employers are classified by 
the Fund as “outside employers.” Private police responsible 
for cargo security at waterfront terminals in the Port 
comprise the third class of persons receiving healthcare ser- 
vices provided by the Fund. These waterfront security per- 
sonnel are represented by the Port Police and Guards Union 
(“PPGU”). The fourth and final group of beneficiaries con- 
sists of local union officers and employees who are eligible to 
use the Fund’s medical centers by virtue of their locals’ 
agreements with the Fund. 


4. The Fund provides healthcare benefits through medical 
centers that it owns and operates. Prior to 1992 the Fund oper- 
ated four medical centers: two in New York and two in New 
Jersey. Only the two medical centers in New York—the 
Brooklyn center and the Manhattan center—are subject to the 
assessment challenged in this case. The Fund’s medical cen- 
ters are not open to the general public but are available only 
to those eligible to receive healthcare services from the Fund. 


5. The Fund and its centers operate exclusively for non- 
profit purposes and conduct no trade, business, or commercial 
activity of any kind. They are not subject to federal, state, or 
local income taxation. They are also exempt from the New 
York City General Corporation and Unincorporated Business 
Taxes, the New York City Commercial Rent or Occupancy 
Tax, and the New York City Real Property Tax. 


6. The Fund is exempt from taxation pursuant to Section 
501(c)(9) of the Internal Revenue Code as a voluntary 
employees’ beneficiary association. Copies of the August 12, 
1963, exemption letter and the July 18, 1979, letter advising 


JA 77 


that the exemption letter continues in effect are attached to 
my affidavit as Exhibit 1. 


7. The 501(c)(9) tax exempt status extends to both the 
Fund and its medical centers. The Fund’s Return of Organi- 
zation Exempt From Income Tax (IRS Form 990) includes the 
operations of both the Fund and the medical centers. A sam- 


ple copy of this tax return for calendar year 1991 is attached 
to my affidavit as Exhibit 2. 


8. The Fund also reports to the Internal Revenue Service 
and the United States Department of Labor, Pension and Wel- 
fare Benefits Administration, on Form 5500, since the Fund 
is covered by ERISA. The Fund’s 5500 report includes the 
operations of both the Fund and its medical centers. A sample 
Form 5500 filed by the Fund for calendar year 1990 is 
attached to my affidavit as Exhibit 3. 


Financing of the Fund 


9. Employer contributions are the principal source of 
monies used to fund the healthcare benefits provided by the 
Fund. Some employer contributions are made to the Fund 
directly and are then funnelled to the centers. Other employer 
contributions are paid directly to the medical centers. 
Employers of longshore workers are obligated by the NYSA- 
ILA collective bargaining agreement to pay assessments that 
fund fringe benefits through an assessment imposed on cargo 
and containers transported through the Port. These assess- 
ments fund ali employee fringe benefits for longshore work- 
ers, including pension benefits, welfare benefits, medical and 
clinical benefits, etc. The portion of the assessments that fund 
medical and clinical benefits is paid to the Fund on a weekly 
basis. The Fund then funnels an amount sufficient to meet the 
weekly expenses for each of the medical centers. The weekly 
amount transferred to the centers is based upon prior expe- 
rience and is adjusted periodically to conform to current fis- 
cal demands. Should the Fund transfer more than is needed 


JA 78 


for a particular week, a lesser amount would be forwarded to 
the medical centers in later weeks. 


10. A second source of employer contributions comes from 
various ILA local unions which pay to the Fund directly man- 
hour contributions on behalf of their officers and employees. 
These contributions in turn form part of the monies trans- 
ferred weekly to the medical centers on an as-need basis. 


11. Outside employers pay man-hour contributions for the 
medical and clinical benefits provided to their employees as 
required by the terms of their separate labor agreements. 
Since many of these outside employers operate single facil- 
ities, their employees are eligible to be treated only at the 
medical center nearest their workplace. Consequently, each 
outside employer has historically paid its contributions 
directly to the medical center at which its employees are eli- 
gible to be treated. Although the outside employers’ contri- 
butions could be paid to the Fund itself, direct payment to the 
medical center simplifies what would otherwise be a cum- 
bersome internal administrative problem of allocating these 
outside contributions to the appropriate medical centers. 


12. Waterfront security guards in the Port are participants 
in their own ERISA welfare plan, the NYSA-PPGU Welfare 
Fund, which provides medical and clinical benefits for the 
guards and their dependents. Excessive costs forced the 
NYSA-PPGU Welfare Fund to close its own medical center in 
July 1988. At a later date it entered into a contractual arrange- 
ment with the Fund to allow port guards and their dependents 
to be treated at the medical centers operated by the Fund. The 
NYSA-PPGU Welfare Fund pays for medical and dental ser- 
vices rendered to its participants on a fee-for-service basis. 


13. The final source of contributions, made only to the 
Brooklyn Medical Center, consists of receipts from workers’ 
compensation insurance carriers. The Brooklyn Medical Cen- 
ter is authorized by N.Y. Work. Comp. Law § 13-c (3) 
(McKinney 1992) to service Fund participants who have been 


JA 79 


injured on the job and are covered by workers’ compensation 
insurance. The Brooklyn Medical Center bills the insurance 
carrier for the medical services it renders to participants, all 
of whom are eligible for treatment at the center. The workers’ 
compensation payments received by the Brooklyn Center 
offset the costs of healthcare services which the Fund must 
provide and which would have had to be financed by contri- 
butions from the same employers paying for the workers’ 
compensation coverage. 


14. In light of the dramatic cost increases experienced in 
recent years, the Fund had to change its traditional method of 
being funded solely by employer contributions. The NYSA- 
ILA labor contract for the three year period from October 1, 
1986, to September 30, 1989, inaugurated a new method of 
employee contributions. To help defray rising costs, the 
NYSA-ILA labor agreement imposed a prescription charge of 
$2.00 per prescription on each beneficiary to whom pre- 
scription drugs are dispensed at any medical center. This was 
the first time that any obligation for medical costs was placed 
directly upon the beneficiaries of the Fund. It produced rev- 
enue of approximately $1 million per annum. 


15. Costs continued to escalate at an alarming rate. As a 
result, the 1990-1994 longshore labor contract imposed addi- 
tional obligations on Fund participants. A $20 per-family, 
annual registration fee was inaugurated, and the prescrip- 
tion charge was increased to $5.00. Even these additional 
employee contributions were insufficient to meet the sky- 
rocketing costs experienced by the Fund. Consequently, the 
Fund was forced to close one of its four medical centers. This 
defunct center was located in Hoboken, New Jersey.

from patient care services of general hospitals, residential 
healthcare facilities, and diagnostic and treatment centers.

N.Y. PuB. HEALTH LAW § 2807-d(2)(c) (McKinney Supp. 
1992).

ment”). The undersigned are also Co-Counsel to the NYSA- 
ILA Medical and Clinical Services Fund (“the Fund”) which 
owns both Medical Centers. 

This statement is submitted in support of the Medical Cen- 
ters’ position that they are not subject to the Assessment since 
they are not-for-profit tax exempt organizations and integral 
parts of an employee welfare benefit plan as defined in 
the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 
(“ERISA”). Accordingly, the Assessment may not be levied 
on the plan since it is pre-empted by ERISA.

Background 


The Medical Centers are not-for-profit corporations incor- 
porated by special acts of the New York Legislature (Chapter 
988, Laws of New York 1957, as amended by Chapter 818, 
Laws of New York 1958, Chapter 807, Laws of New York 
1961 and Chapter 1013, Laws of New York 1974; Chapter 
624, Laws of New York 1958, as amended by Chapter 758, 
Laws of New York 1961 and Chapter 594, Laws of New York 
1963). The Medical Centers render out-patient, ambulatory 
medical, dental and related health care services to approxi- 
mately 17,000 longshore workers employed in the Port of 
New York and New Jersey and their dependents and pen- 
sioners and their dependents. The Medical Centers do not 
operate independently of the Fund. The Fund owns the Med- 
ical Centers and the land on which they are located. More 
importantly, the Fund provides the monies necessary for their 
operation. (The Fund also owns and provides the monies nec- 
essary for the operation of the NYSA-ILA Medical Centers of 
Newark and Hoboken which treat approximately 13,000 long- 
shore workers, pensioners and dependents.) 

The Fund is a joint labor-management trust fund established 
pursuant to a collective bargaining agreement between the 
New York Shipping Association, Inc. (“NYSA”) and the Inter- 
national Longshoremen’s Association, AFL-CIO (“ILA”). A 
copy of the Memorandum of Settlement of the Port of Greater 
New York and New Jersey dated November 28, 1990 and 
effective December 1, 1990 through September 30, 1994 is 
submitted herewith as Exhibit |. Provisions relating to the 
Fund and Medical Centers are on pages 3-5 and page 8. 

The Fund is a Taft-Hartley Trust Fund under Section 302 of 
the Labor-Management Relations Act of 1949, 29 U.S.C. 
§186. It is also exempt from taxation pursuant to Section 
501(c)(9) of the Internal Revenue Code as a voluntary 
employees’ beneficiary association. Copies of the August 12, 
1963 exemption letter and the July 18, 1979 letter advising 
that the exemption letter continues in effect are submitted

herewith as Exhibit 2. As noted previously the Fund is an 
employee welfare benefit plan as defined in ERISA. ERISA 
§ 3(1), 29 U.S.C. § 1002(1). 

The 501(c)(9) tax exempt status of the Fund encompasses 
the Medical Centers. The Fund’s Return of Organization 
Exempt From Income Tax (Form 990) includes the operations 
of the Fund and the Medical Centers. A copy of the Fund’s 
Form 990 for 1989 is submitted herewith as Exhibit 3. 

As an ERISA Fund the Fund also reports to the Internal 
Revenue Service and Department of Labor on Form 5500, 
which also includes the operations of the Fund and the Med- 
ical Centers. A copy of the Fund’s 1989 Form 5500 is sub- 
mitted herewith as Exhibit 4. 

As noted above, the Fund, through the Medical Centers, 
provides medical, dental and related health care services on 
an out-patient ambulatory basis to approximately 12,500 par- 
ticipants and their dependents eligible under the NYSA-ILA 
collective bargaining agreement. The beneficiaries do not pay 
for services rendered to them, except for a $20-per-family 
annual registration fee and a $5-per-prescription charge. *

The Medical Centers Are Not Subject To The 
Assessment Since They Are Tax Exempt, Integral Parts 
of An ERISA Employee Welfare Benefit Plan. Thus, 
The Assessment Is Pre-Empted By ERISA 


Section 514 of ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1144, provides: 
(a) Supersedure; effective date 


Except as provided in subsection (b) of this section, the 
provisions of this subchapter and subchapter III of this 
chapter shall supersede any and all State laws insofar as 
they may now or hereafter relate to any employee benefit 
plan described in section 1003(a) of this title and not 
exempt under section 1003(b) of this title. This section 
shall take effect on January 1, 1975. 


(b) Construction and application 


ad - * 


(5)(A) Except as provided in subparagraph (B), 
subsection (a) of this section shall not apply to the 
Hawaii Prepaid Health Care Act (Haw. Rev. Stat. §§ 393- 
1 through 393-51). 


(B) Nothing in subparagraph (A) shall be construed to 
exempt from subsection (a) of this section— 


(i) any State tax law relating to employee benefit 
plans, or 


(ii) any amendment of the Hawaii Prepaid Health 
Care Act enacted after September 2, 1974, to the

extent it provides for more than the effective 
administration of such Act as in effect on such date. 


* - * 


(c) Definitions 


For purposes of this section: 


(1) The term “State law” includes all laws, decisions. 
rules, regulations, or other State action having the effect 
of law, of any State. A law of the United States appli- 
cable only to the District of Columbia shall be treated as 
a State law rather than a law of the United States. 


(2) The term “State” includes a State, any political sub- 
divisions thereof, or any agency or instrumentality of 
either, which purports to regulate, directly or indirectly, 
the terms and conditions of employee benefit plans cov- 
ered by this subchapter. 


* * - 


Thus, state laws which relate to employee welfare benefit 
plans, including state tax laws, are expressly pre-empted by 
ERISA. 

See Pilot Life Insurance Company v. Dedeaux, 481 U.S. 41 
(1987) (noting the “expansive sweep” of the pre-emption 
clause); Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, 463 U.S. 85 (1983) 
(Congress used the words “related to” in § 514(a) in their 
broad sense); Birdsong v. Olson, 708 F.Supp. 792 (W.D. Texas 
1989) (“Congress has expressly indicated that state tax laws 
related to employee welfare benefit plans are preempted”); 
Northwest Airlines, Inc., et al. v. Roemer, et al., 603 F.Supp. 
7 (D. Minnesota 1984) (“In 1983, when ERISA’s preemption 
language was amended to exclude Hawaii's experimental 
health benefit statute from § 1144(a), Congress specifically 
endorsed the preemption of state tax laws.”); National Car- 
riers’ Conference Committee, et al. v. Heffernan, 454 F.Supp. 
914 (D. Connecticut 1978) (because of the potential use of 


JA 102 


taxation as a means of regulation, preempting state taxation 
of ERISA-covered plans is necessary to effectuate Congres- 
sional objectives). 

Accordingly, to apply the Assessment to the Medical Cen- 
ters would be in violation of the pre-emption provisions of 
ERISA and the cases decided thereunder. 


Conclusion 


The State of New York is pre-empted by ERISA from 
applying the Assessment to the NYSA-ILA Medical Center of 
Brooklyn, Inc. and the NYSA-ILA Medical Center of New 
York, Inc.
