IV. SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Ninth Circuit erred when it held that for 
purposes of maritime products liability, a vessel 
manufacturer’s “product” includes all property on board 
the vessel at the time the vessel is sold by the original 
purchaser to a second purchaser, whether or not that “other 
property” was part of the vessel sold by the manufacturer 
to the original purchaser. The Ninth Circuit erred by 
misreading East River to import a contract (i.e., warranty) 
analysis into product liability law. East River delineated 
the “sphere” of warranty law to include damages caused by 
the product to itself and other business-related damages 
(e.g., lost profits) that might occur where a product breaks 
down or fails to perform as expected (“economic 
damages”). However, where the product defect resulted 
in destruction of property other than the product itself (i.e., 
“other property”), tort liability (i.e., products liability) was 
triggered. By misreading East River and improperly 
importing a warranty analysis into products liability law, 

purchasers 


of the vessel manufacturer’s product from recovering for 
damages to property that was on board the vessel at the 
time of purchase, but that was not part of the vessel (the 
“product”) placed into the stream of commerce and sold by 
the manufacturer.

East River, the spheres of products liability law and 
warranty law were clearly delineated. Under the Ninth 
Circuit’s decision, however, a subsequent purchaser of a 
vessel is left to any warranty he may be able to obtain
