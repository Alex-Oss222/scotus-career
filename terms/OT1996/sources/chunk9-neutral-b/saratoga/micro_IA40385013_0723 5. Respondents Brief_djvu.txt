IV. SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


By looking to the object of Saratoga’s bargain and 
denying tort recovery for direct loss of the physical prop- 
erty acquired as part of Saratoga’s bargain, the Ninth 
Circuit properly applied the economic loss rule adopted 
in East River S.S. Corp. v. Transamerica Delaval, Inc., 476 
U.S. 858 (1986). Adoption of the economic loss rule in 
East River represents a policy-based preference for rules

of contract and risk allocation methods, including insur- 
ance, which have long acceptance in maritime commerce. 
To permit tort recovery for failed contractual expectations 
in a commercial setting would be to extend tort recovery 
beyond its intended purpose, which is to afford a remedy 
to injured consumers, without the ability to negotiate 
product specifications or risk of loss, forced to rely on 
manufacturers for product needs. In product liability par- 
lance, application of tort principles in commercial cases 
where these policies are not furthered, is “misuse.” 


The economic loss rule represents a contract 
approach which sets the boundary on tort recovery in 
commercial cases by excluding direct loss of the physical 
object of a purchaser’s bargain, and indirect consequen- 
tial losses, because the risk of commercial loss is under- 
stood to have been the subject of the parties’ negotiation 
and bargain. These losses must be recovered, if at all, 
under contract law. This contract approach protects and 
enforces the contractual risk allocations of the commer- 
cial parties, as well as all other parties in the commercial 
chain of manufacture, distribution and sale, thereby pre- 
serving uniformity and predictability in maritime com- 
merce. 


The tort approach advanced by Petitioner would 
respect the parties’ contractual risk allocation only to the 
extent of the “product” first sold to a “user.” Any 
components added or associated thereafter would be 
“other property” recoverable in tort, even when these 
additional components are a major part of the plaintiff's 
bargain. Since Petitioner's tort approach does not focus 
on the plaintiff's bargain, its application will necessarily 
nullify voluntary risk allocation methods long employed 


in maritime commerce. In turn, commercial actors will 
find difficulty structuring their business behavior and 
will additionally find themselves exposed to indetermi- 
nate tort liability. In an effort to reign-in such tort lia- 
bility, courts applying maritime law will be forced to 
engage in the difficult line-drawing which the Court 
sought to avoid in East River. 


The economic loss rule simply means that in commer- 
cial settings, where it is presumed that all parties have 
the ability and motivation to bargain for risk allocation, 
“a bargain is a bargain.” Since commercial parties can 
negotiate their rights and remedies, any remedy for loss- 
of-bargain must itself be bargained for. Otherwise, judi- 
cial assistance to the party who failed to bargain for its 
own protection, will result in disruption of the voluntary 
risk allocation achieved by the parties. While the Ninth 
Circuit correctly looked to the object of Petitioner’s com- 
mercial bargain to determine the economic loss, it 
stopped short in its analysis by failing to consider that 
Petitioner's lost tuna catch, a direct result of the failure of 
Petitioner's commercial purchase to perform as expected, 
also represented “insufficient product value.” Two Fifth 
Circuit maritime cases and numerous state court deci- 
sions applying the economic loss rule would deny tort 
recovery for the lost fish catch as a failed contract expec- 
tancy. In order to give effect to the policy choices under- 
lying the economic loss rule, and in the interests of 
predictability and uniformity in maritime law, the deci- 
sion awarding tort damages for loss of the tuna catch 
must be reversed.

V. ARGUMENT 


A. The remedy for loss of the benefit of a commer- 
cial maritime bargain lies only in contract. 


This case involves application of the economic loss 
rule adopted by this Court in East River, supra, 476 U.S. 
858, which states the proposition that loss of a commer- 
cial bargain occasioned by product failure is not compen- 
sable in tort. This includes loss of the product itself and 
consequential economic losses for which contract law 
provide a recovery. The disappointed commercial pur- 
chaser’s remedy arises from the consensual nature of the 
commercial relationship, and has its genesis in the pur- 
chaser’s bargain. “The character of a loss determines the 
appropriate remedies, and, to determine the character of 
the loss, one must look to the product purchased by the 
plaintiff, not the product sold by the defendants.” Pulte 
Home Corp. v. Osmose Wood Preserving, Inc., 60 F.3d 734, 
741 (11th Cir. 1995), citing King v. Hilton-Davis, 855 F.2d 
1047 (3rd Cir. 1988), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 1030 (1989). 
Thus, if the failed “product” was acquired in a commer- 
cial relationship, an upstream seller has no tort liability 
for: (a) loss of the object of the commercial purchaser's 
bargain (often termed “the product”), or for (b) conse- 
quential economic losses susceptible of contract recovery. 


The economic loss rule serves to protect the marine 
industry’s expectation that voluntarily risk allocations 
will be honored. In East River this Court recognized that 
“the main currents of tort law run in different directions

The economic loss rule recognizes the “need to keep 
products liability and contract law in separate spheres 
and to maintain a realistic limitation on damages.” 476 
U.S. at 871. East River affirmed that contract law is 
supreme in commercial intercourse, and that tort does not 
provide an optional legal regime for the disappointed 
commercial purchaser who could have bargained for a 
contractual remedy for the loss suffered. 


Within maritime commerce, the challenge to the body 
of contract law presented by ever-expanding tort liability 
is so great, and the need for appropriate, predictable 
limitations on damages so compelling, that this Court did 
not limit application of the economic loss rule to situa- 
tions where the purchaser actually has a contract remedy 
that would be displaced by a tort remedy. Although the 
disappointed charterers in East River were without a con- 
tract remedy (like Saratoga, they specifically took the 
ship “as is”), the Court saw “no reason to extricate the 
parties from their bargain.” 476 U.S. at 875. With elegant 
simplicity East River teaches: “a bargain is a bargain!”

Direct loss of the physical object of the purchaser's

award of tort damages for the “lost catch,” is at odds with 
the policies underlying the economic loss rule. By permit- 
ting tort recovery in a commercial case for a loss that is 
within warranty protection, a conflict between the Fifth 
and Ninth Circuits has arisen. 


East River recognized that a warranty action allows a 
disappointed purchaser to “seek to recoup the benefit of 
its bargain,” including such indirect losses as forgone 
business opportunities. (476 U.S. at 868, 873.) One of the 
factors favoring the Court’s adoption of the economic loss 
rule was the warranty action’s “built-in limitation on 
liability . . . from the agreement of the parties and the 
requirement that consequential damages, such as lost 
profits, be a foreseeable result of the breach.” 476 U.S. at 
874, citing Hadley v. Baxendale, 9 Exch. 341, 156 Eng. Rep.

Supreme Court explained economic losses can be either direct

encompass damage based on insufficient product value; thus, 
direct economic loss may be ‘out of pocket’ - the difference in

measured by costs of replacement and repair. Consequential

Other courts, including the Fifth Circuit, have given 
the economic loss rule the broad effect needed to fulfill its 
purpose of preserving contract recovery for contract 
losses, by applying the rule to consequential commercial 
losses. Instead of mechanically applying a “product 
itself” versus “other property” analysis, these courts, rep- 
resenting the clear majority view, resolve the issue with 
reference to the basic contract law inquiry whether the 
particular loss represents a commercial purchaser's failed 
contractual expectations. In each of the cases discussed 
below, the “property” which was found by the court to be 
“economic loss” was distinct physical property that was 
not acquired by the purchaser as part of the transaction 
by which the defective product was purchased (which 
seems to be the hallmark of the rule applied by the Ninth 
Circuit). In these cases, the damaged “other property” 
which is deemed to be economic loss was either part of 
an integrated system with the defective product, property 
which the defective product was being used to create or 
process in some way, or property that was naturally used 
or present in conjunction with the defective product. Each 
of these situations is analogous to Saratoga’s lost fish 
catch. Each of these cases seems to apply the lesson that 
consequential contract damages of the type recoverable 
since Hadley v. Baxendale, supra, are not recoverable in 
tort.

system with as was the defective product in Midwhey

The turbines were connected to the steam gener-

Of course, the same logic would apply, not only to the 
lost tuna catch, but equally to the net, skiff, and other 
property not part of the original sale by Martinac to 
Madruga. The point is that damage to commercial prop- 
erty used or present in conjunction with a product that 
turns out to be defective quite often satisfies the require- 
ments of Hadley v. Baxendale, supra, or U.C.C. § 2-715. If 
the seller had reason to know that his product would be 
used in a particular application such that its failure could 
foreseeably damage other property used in conjunction 
therewith, and the defective product proximately causes 
the loss, such loss is “consequential damage” recoverable 
under warranty.

perfectly consistent with the idea that because damage to 
cargo is something naturally to be expected when prod- 
ucts such as a vessel or its systems fail, and because such 
loss is a consequential loss susceptible of warranty recov- 
ery, no tort recovery is available. The Fifth Circuit's com-

erty belonging to plaintiff, and “physically distinct” from 
the floats, it was correctly regarded as economic loss 
because the loss of a helicopter was precisely the kind of 
damage one would expect from failure of the floats. 


Saratoga’s lost tuna catch is qualitatively no different 
than any of the damaged items of property which the 
above cases found to be economic loss. The ability to find, 
catch, preserve, transport, and deliver a catch of tuna was 
the essence of Saratoga’s bargain. Certainly it was within 
the contemplation of Madruga, as seller, and Saratoga, as 
purchaser, that if the vessel failed to perform as antici- 
pated, then Saratoga’s ability to deliver and sell a catch of 
tuna for a profit would be defeated. Without question, 
loss of the catch in this case is a commercial, consequen- 
tial economic loss for which tort recovery is not permit- 
ted. 


Finally, it is undisputed that lost profits constitute 
economic loss for which no tort recovery is allowed.

of lost profits not recoverable in tort. 


VI. CONCLUSION 


The Ninth Circuit correctly applied the “object of the 
bargain” test to determine that everything part and parcel 
to Saratoga’s purchase of the M/V Saratoga was eco- 
nomic loss not recoverable in tort. Saratoga purchased an 
integrated, full outfitted vessel, “as is” and without war- 
ranties. The risk shifting method which Saratoga elected 
to apply was the purchase of insurance. This bargained- 
for allocation of risk, and the bargained-for risk alloca- 
tions of all commercial actors “upstream” from Saratoga 
should be left unmolested. 


The “economic loss” rule applied by the “object-of- 
the-bargain” test serve the goal of preserving contracted- 
for risk allocations using the contract approach of exam- 
ining the plaintiff purchaser’s bargain, and declaring that 
bargain to be off-limits to tort recovery. Saratoga’s pro- 
posed rule will not effectively protect commercial risk 
allocations, because it arbitrarily defines “the product” 


45 


according to tort precepts, without reference to the plain- 
tiff purchaser’s bargain. Thus, Saratoga seeks to have this 
Court announce a rule which involves a tort analysis to 
disregard the contractual allocation of commercial risk 
between commercial entities. 


The Ninth Circuit erred by an incomplete application 
of the economic loss rule in affirming a tort recovery for 
the lost tuna catch. The lost catch is the equivalent of lost 
profits, or work in progress, and clearly represents the 
ultimate failure of the product in this case to fulfill its 
primary mission. Saratoga lost the anticipated perfor- 
ma:.ce of the M/V Saratoga, no more and no less. The 
common law cases that have considered analogous losses 
have correctly precluded tort recovery, as does the Fifth 
Circuit. 


For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm 
the decision of the Ninth Circuit reversing the District 
Court’s award of damages for loss of the net, skiff, fuel 
and other equipment aboard the M/V Saratoga, and 
reverse that portion of the decision of the Ninth Circuit 
which affirmed the award of consequential lost profits 
from the fish aboard the vessel.
