The opinion of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals, filed 
October 26, 1995 (App., 4a), is reported at 69 F.3d 1432 (9th Cir. 
1995). Neither the district court's Findings on Liability (App., 
114a), nor its Oral Findings on damages (App., 173a) are 
reported. The original opinion of the Ninth Circuit Court of 
Appeals, filed April 25, 1995 (App., 80a), and the first revised 
opinion of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals, filed August 4,

STATEMENT OFJURISDICTION 


The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals entered its order 
deaying rehearing of Saratoga’s motion for reconsideration on 
January 30, 1996. App., la. The jurisdiction of this Court is 
invoked under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1). Because these claims arose 
on the high seas, they fall within the admiralty jurisdiction of the 
federal courts under 28 U.S.C. §1333. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Petitioner Saratoga Fishing Co. (“Saratoga”) sued 
Respondents for damages resulting from a conflagration that 
occurred on board the M/V SARATOGAon or about January 16, 
1986, in which M/V SARATOGA burned and sank. M/V 
SARATOGA was originally purchased in or about 1972 from the 
manufacturer, J.M. Martinac & Co. (“Martinac”), by Joseph 
Madruga, a private individual. The hydraulic system on the 
vessel was designed by Marco Seattle Inc. (“Marco”). The 
vessel was completed in 1972, taken to Panama and outfitted 
with a seine and other fishing equipment, and Madruga began 
fishing operations with the vessel, with Manual Vargas as its 
captain. 


2 


In 1974, Joseph Madruga sold M/V SARATOGA to the 
Manual S. Vargas, Jr. Corporation, which later changed its name 
to Saratoga Fishing Co. At the time of that sale, M/V 
SARATOGA was equipped with five speedboats, a purse skiff, 
a seine, and various electronic equipment. On or about January 
16,1986, a hydraulic hose detached from its coupling, spraying 
hydraulic fluid onto the turbocharger. Because Martinanc and 
Marco had failed to provide any effective shielding, the 
hydraulic fluid sprayed onto the adjacent engine surfaces and 
turbocharger and ignited. The resulting conflagration burned 
and sank M/V SARATOGA. The crew just managed to get off the 
ship into a skiff before the vessel sank. 


The district court concluded that Marco and Martinanc were 
strictly liable for the loss of M/V SARATOGA in that the 
hydraulic system was defectively designed. The court awarded 
damages to Saratoga in the amount of $452,326, approximately 
one-third of what it determined were the total of Saratoga’s 
damages. The total damage award was reduced by two thirds for 
comparative negligence. The award included the loss of “other 
property” on board M/V SARATOGA, including the tuna catch, 
seine, skiff, fuel, equipment, payments by Saratoga to the crew 
for their personal property losses, the cost of rescue and certain 
cash. With pre-judgment interest, the total award to Saratoga 
after reduction by two-thirds for comparative negligence came 
to $734,940.38. 


All parties appealed the district court’s decision on various 
grounds, only one of which, the determination of “other 
property,” is at issue in this Petition. In its original opinion, 
issued April 25, 1995 (the “Original Opinion”) (App., 80a), the 
panel correctly recognized that the “product” in products 
liability cases, including maritime products liability cases, is 
determined by what the manufacturer places in the stream of 
commerce. The court therefore agreed with the Fifth Circuit

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 
I. 


THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S RULING PLACES THE 
NINTH CIRCUIT IN DIRECT CONFLICT WITH THIS 
COURT’S DECISION IN EAST RIVER AND WITH THE 
FIFTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION IN NICOR AND OTHER 
CASES. 


The Ninth Circuit is a major maritime law court. With all 
due respect to the Ninth Circuit, the justices in the majority in the 
October 26 Second Revised Opinion issued an opinion that will, 
if given effect, totally and wrongfully disrupt maritime products 
liability law in not only the Ninth Circuit, but in the United States 
as a whole. By misreading and misapplying East River, the 
Ninth Circuit has decided an important question of maritime law 
“in a way that conflicts with relevant decisions of this Court.” 
Rules of the Supreme Court, Rule 10(c). Moreover, the Ninth 
Circuit has, if its decision is given effect, “entered a decision in 
conflict with the decision of another United States court of 
appeals on the same important matter,” namely, the Fifth 
Circuit's ruling in Nicor and other decisions. Rules of the 
Supreme Court, Rule 10(a). 


7 


The Ninth Circuit’s erroneous decision creates an 
intolerable situation that will effectively destroy claims for 
“other property” by second and subsequent purchasers of a 
vessel, even though the damage to other property was caused by 
the exact same defect that existed at the time the manufacturer 
sold the vessel to the first purchaser. Effectively, once a vessel 
is sold or transferred by the first buyer, the second vessel buyer 
has no remedy for product defects that cause damage to “other 
property” added to the vessel by the first buyer. Resales or 
transfers of vessels to another owner or operator are, however, 
commonplace, and often occur within a short time of the useful 
life of the vessel. It is also common that, as in this case, after a 
vessel is purchased, other items are added to outfit it for its 
particular purpose. These additional items, as can be seen in this 
case, Can amount to as much or more than one-third of the total 
value of the vessel. 


THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN DECIDING HOW 
“OTHER PROPERTY” IS DETERMINED IN MARITIME 
PRODUCT LIABILITY CASES. 


Under its Original Opinion, the panel correctly recognized 
that the “product” is delineated by that which a manufacturer 
places in the stream of commerce. The Original Opinion 
correctly held that the distinction between the “product” and 
“other property” in a products liability claim turned on whether 
the property was “‘separate from the product that the 
manufacturer originally sold” to the first buyer; in this case, 
what Martinac sold to Joseph Madruga, the original buyer of 
M/V SARATOGA. App., 108a (emphasis added, italics in the 
original), citing Nicor, 876 F.2d at 505. The panel’s Second 
Revised Opinion (and its First Revised Opinion) misread East 
River as holding that “product” is defined for strict products 


liability as the object that was last transferred from any seller to 
a buyer, even if the seller, as in this case, is an occasional seller, 
nota“seller. .. whois engaged in the business of selling sucha 
product” as defined by the Restatement (Second) of Torts, 
Section 402(1)(a). App., 33a-35a. 


The Second Revised Opinion abrogates fundamental 
products liability principles that (1) only commercial sellers 
‘e.g., manufacturers and distributors), not end-users of a product 
can be held liable for a product's defects; (2) the “product” at 
issue in strict products liability analysis is the product the 
manufacturer or distributor placed into the stream of commerce, 
(3) product liability cannot be disclaimed by warranty; and (4) 
“(t]he consumer’s cause of action does not depend upon the 
validity of his contract with the person from whom he acquires 
the product. . . .” Restatement (Second) of Torts, §402A, cmt. m 
(1977). The Ninth Circuit erred by deviating from first principles 
of product liability law, then misreading East River as 
supporting its deviation. The Ninth Circuit's error is profound 
and should be corrected. 


III. 


THE NINTH CIRCUIT MISREAD EAST RIVER TO 
IMPORT CONTRACT ANALYSIS INTO PRODUCTS 
LIABILITY TORT LAW. 


The Ninth Circuit erroneously misread East River as 
holding that a downstream sales contract between consumers 
determines “the product” at issue for purposes of distinguishing 
“the product” from “other property.” App., 33a-35a. The Ninth 
Circuit was wrong. Looking to a downstream contract to define 
a manufacturer’s “product” for strict products liability purposes 
ignores East River's determination that the recovery of property 
damages under maritime products liability law, including strict 


9 


products liability, is controlled by Section 402A of the 
Restatement (Second) of Torts. East River, 476 U.S. at 875, 
citing with approval Pan-Alaska Fisheries, Inc, 565 F.2d 1129, 
1135 (9th Cir. 1977) (adopting Restatement (Second) of Torts 
Sec. 402A (1965)). 


A. Strict Products Liability Law Applies Only to Sales by 
Commercial Manufacturers, Distributors or Retailers to 
Consumers. 


The Ninth Circuit initially erred when it framed the question 
before it as “whether the ‘product’ changed when Madruga 
added various components, including a skiff, seine and other 
miscellaneous parts, to the vessel and resold it to Saratoga 
Fishing.” App., 33a-34a (emphasis added). By answering “yes,” 
the Ninth Circuit placed Joseph Madruga, a consumer, in the 
shoes of Martinac, the manufacturer of M/V SARATOGA, for 
purposes of determining what Martinac s product was. The 
Ninth Circuit was wrong. 


Section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts, cited 
with approval by this Court in East River and adopted by the 
Ninth Circuit in Pan-Alaska Fisheries, Inc., 565 F.2d at 1134- 
1135, provides as follows: 


§ 402 A. Special Liability of Seller of 
Product for Physical Harm to User or 
Consumer. 


(1) One who sells any product in a defective 
condition unreasonably dangerous fo the 
user or consumer or to his property is subject 
to liability for physical harm thereby caused 
to the ultimate user or consumer, or to his 
property, if 


10 


(a) the seller is engaged in the 
business of selling such a product, and 


(d) itis expected to and does reach the 
user or consumer without substantial 
change : 1 the condition in which it is 
sold. 


(2) The rule stated in Subsection (I) applies 
although 


(a) the seller has exercised all 
possible care in the preparation and 
sale of his product, and 


(b) the user or consumer has not 
bought the product from or entered 
into any contractual relation with the 
seller. 


Restatement (Second) of Torts, § 402A (1977) (emphasis added). 


A “seller” of a product under Section 402A is 
unequivocally not a consumer reselling the product, but a 
commercial seller (e.g., a manufacturer, distributor) of that 
product: 


The rule stated in this Section applies to any 
person engaged in the business of selling 
products for use or consumption. It therefore 
applies to any manufacturer of such product, 
to any wholesale or retail dealer or 
distributor, and to the operator of a 
restaurant. 


Restatement (Second) of Torts, § 402A, cmt. f (1977) (emphasis 
added). A “product,” as defined by the most recent Tentative 
Draft of the Restatement (Third) is not an item resold by one 
consumer to another consumer but, 


something distributed commercially for use 
or consumption. Most but not necessarily all 
products are tangible personal property; 
most have been subjected to processing and 
fabricating prior to entering the stream of 
commerce; and most pass through a 
commercial chain of distribution before 
ultimate use and consumption. 


Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability, § 4 (Tentative 
Draft No. 2, March 13, 1995) (emphasis added). 


Joseph Madruga indisputably was not in the business of 
selling tuna boats. Madruga was a small shipowner who sold his 
boat to his former captain. App., 6a. He was not a commercial 
seller when he sold M/V SARATOGA. He therefore could not, 
by definition, have placed Martinac 's product into the stream of 
commerce as a seller under Section 402A of the Restatement, and 
he could not change the manufacturers’ product for product 
liability purposes. 


B. The Ninth Circuit’s Second Revised Opinion Undermines 
the Rationale for Applying Strict Products Liability Only to 
Commercial Manufacturers and Distributors. 


The rationale for strict products liability, fully recognized 
by East River, the Restatement, and the Ninth Circuit itself, 
centers on the manufacturer's ability to spread the risk of defects, 
and to correct defects in the product: 


12 


been said to be that the seller, by marketing 
his product for use and consumption, has 
undertaken and assumed à special 
responsibility toward any member of the 
consuming public who may be injured by it; 
that the public has the right to and does 
expect, in the case of products which it needs 
and for which it is forced to rely upon the 
seller, that reputable sellers will stand behind 
their goods; that public policy demands that 
the burden of accidental injuries caused by 
products intended for consumption be placed 
upon those who market them, and be treated 
as a cost of production against which liability 
insurance can be obtained; and that the 
consumer of such products is entitled to the 
maximum of protection at the hands of 
someone, and the proper person to afford it 
are those who market the products. 


Restatement (Second) of Torts, § 402A, cmt. c (1977) (emphasis 
added). This Court in East River likewise understood that this 
rationale applied to the manufacturer, not the occasional 
downstream seller: 


The manufacturer is liable whether or not it is 
negligent because ‘public policy demands 
that responsibility be fixed wherever it will 
most effectively reduce the hazards to life 
and health inherent in defective products that 
reach the market.’ Escola v. Coca Cola 
Bottling Co. of Fresno, 24 Cal.2d, at 462, 150 
P.2d, at 441 (opinion ccncurring in 
judgment). 


East River, 476 U.S. at 866-867 (emphasis added). 


13 


East River understood that a manufacturer's strict liability 
for defective products stands separate and apart from contract 
based warranty law and is based on a wholly separate rationale: 


Products liability grew out of a public policy 
judgment that people need more protection 
from dangerous products than is afforded by 
the law of warranty. See Seely v. White Motor 
Co., 63 Cal.249, 15,45 Cal. Rptr. 17,21, 403 
P.2d 145, 149 (1965). 


East River, 476 U.S. at 866.' 


Under the Ninth Circuit's reasoning, an occasional seller 
like Joseph Madruga cannot be held liable under products 
liability law for a products’ defects, and none of the rationales 
upon which strict product liability rests apply to him. Yet the 
Ninth Circuit's bizarre conclusion is that this same occasional 
seller can nonetheless “change” the manufacturer's 
“product” for products liability purposes. App. 34a-35a. The 
focus of the Ninth Circuit's inquiry was not on what the 
manufacturer placed into the stream of commerce, but what the 
downstream consumer, Madruga, resold. App., 34a-35a. The 
district court (and the dissent) correctly rejected this awkward 
result: “I conclude that the definition of the vessel . . would 
change with each successive sale of the boat, including and then 
excluding the new or removed items on board. This rule of law 
would be awkward and illogical.” App. 176a; see also App. 
41a. Noonan, J., concurring and dissenting.

strict products liability law did not apply. 


Thus, in the first part of its decision, East River held that the 
plaintiffs had no products liability claims because only 
“economic damage,” that is, damage to the product itself, was at 
issue. East River, 476 U.S. at 867. It made this determination 
only after it had decided what “the product” at issue was. To do 
this, the Court looked to what had been “supplied by Delaval,” 
the manufacturer, to the owners of the ships at the time of 
delivery to the owners. Because “each turbine was supplied by 
Delaval { the manufacturer] as an integrated package,” each 
turbine was “properly regarded as a single unit.” East River, 476 
U.S. at 867. As a result, because the “defectively designed 
turbine components [had] damaged only the turbine itself,” 
“there was no damage to other property. East River,476U.S. 
at 867. 


Once the Court had determined that the turbines were “the 
product” sold by Delaval to the owners, and that the turbines had 
damaged only themselves, the Court considered whether 
damage caused by the product to itself was recoverable in tort 
under a products liability theory. The Court examined the 
dispute between those courts that held that damage to the product 
itself was recoverable, and those that did not. East River, 476 
U.S. at 868-871. Ultimately, the Court sided with California's 
Supreme Court decision in Seely, and held that damage to the 
product itself, in that case the turbine supplied by Delaval, was 
“economic damage” that could not be recovered in tort under a 
products liability theory. Therefore, such damage could not be 
recovered in tort by either the owners or the bareboat charterers. 
East River, 476 U.S. at 867, 875. Products liability law was 
simply the wrong instrument. The Court therefore proceeded to 
its analysis under warranty law.

The Ninth Circuit opinion will create chaos in maritime law 
if given effect. Forexample, if a first purchaser keeps a vessel for 
20 years, adding many pieces of equipment to the ship during that 
period, the manufacturer clearly can be held liable in tort should 
this “other property” be damaged or destroyed by a defect in the 
ship. Thus, the manufacturer's liability would increase every 
time the vessel is modified, so long as it is owned by the first 
purchaser. Yet if the first purchaser sells the ship and all its 
additions to purchaser two after owning it for 20 years and one 
day, the manufacturer's liability for the “other property” 
immediately springs back to zero. As a result, the manufacturers 
liability for precisely the same damage caused by precisely the

day before the defect manifested itself. See App., 41a, Noonan, 
J., concurring and dissenting.“

Admiralty courts use two methods to determine whether a 
product is defectively designed. The first method, drawn from 
Restatement section 402A comment i, provides that a product 
is defective if it is “dangerous to an extent beyond that which 
would be contemplated by the ordinary consumer who pur- 
chases it, with the ordinary knowledge common to the com- 
munity as to its characteristics.” This is the consumer 
expectations test. See Wonacott, Shipbuilder Liability, at 469, 
Prosser & Keeton § 99, at 698. 


Although utilized by many courts, this test has come under 
criticism because of several shortcomings. First, it allows a 
manufacturer or seller to escape liability when the unreason- 
able danger is open and obvious. See Prosser & Keeton § 99, 
at 698-99. M. Stuart Madden, Products Liability § 6.7, at 
213-14 (1988). Second, it is unpredictable in application. See 
Prosser & Keeton § 99, at 699. Because of the weaknesses 
of this test, some courts have sought an altemative way to 
determine whether a product was defectively designed. 


A second test evolved from Judge Learned Hand's balanc- 
ing test in a negligence case, United States v. Carroll Towing 
Co., 159 F.2d 169, 173 (2d Cir. 1947). The risk-utility balanc- 
ing test, in the context of a strict products liability action, 
weighs the utility of the product against the gravity of the 
danger. If, after considering the utility of the product, the eco- 
nomic and practical feasibility of alternative designs, and the 
gravity and likelihood of the potential harm, the factfinder 
determines that the risk outweighs the utility, the product is 
deemed defective or unreasonably dangerous. See Paulides v.

CONCLUSIONS ON COMPARATIVE FAULT 


(144) Strict products liability was originally brought into 
admiralty suits within the Ninth Circuit in Pan Alaska Fisheries 
vs. Marine Construction, 565 F.2d 1129 (9th Cir. 1977). In that 
case, the crab fishing vessel, Enterprise was returning to Dutch 
Harbor, Alaska after a major rebuilding of the vessel in Seattle. 
The rebuilding included the installation of a new D343 
Caterpillar marine diesel engine. 


While off Nanaimo, British Columbia, fire broke [64] out in 
the engine room resulting in the sinking and total loss of the 
vessel. At trial, the district judge rejected the doctrine of strict 
liability. The Ninth Circuit reversed, adopting the Restatement 
of Torts, § 402A as the law of products liability in the circuit for 
cases arising in admiralty. 


(145) Under the doctrine of strict liability, manufacturers, 


165a 
Appendix E 


dealers and retailers are all subject to liability because they are 
an integral part of the overall producing and marketing 
enterprise that should bear the cost of injuries arising from 
defective products. 


(146) In Pan Alaska, the product defect was an oil filter that 
Caterpillar knew could crack and spray fuel on a hot engine and 
that this created an extreme fire hazard. Caterpillar had issued a 
warning notice to its dealers, but this was held insufficient to 
insulate Caterpillar from liability in view of the extremely 
dangerous condition to both life and property from a fuel fire on 
vessels sailing the high seas. 


(147) The Ninth Circuit adopted the rule of comparative 
negligence to strict product liability citing with approval the rule 
of Butaud vs. Suburban Marine, 555 (65) P.2d 42 (Alaska 1976). 
Quote: 


“We feel that the public policy reasons for 
strict product liability do not seem to be 
incompatible with comparative negligence. 
The manufacturer is still accountable for all 
the harm from a defective product except that 
part caused by the consumer s own conduct.” 
Butaud, 555 P.2d at 46. 


(148) Having adopted the rule of comparative negligence 
to strict products liability, the Ninth Circuit addressed the 
question of what conduct of the plaintiff should be compared. 
The court answered that question, saying, quote, 


“In short, we hold that all of the Plaintiff's 
conduct contributing to his loss or injury can 


166a 


Appendix E 


be compared to the Defendant's liability 
regardless of the label attached to that 
conduct.” 


This ruling marks a significant departure from that part of § 402A 
of the Restatement which would bar recovery by a plaintiff who 
voluntarily and unreasonably proceeds to encounter a known 
risk amounting to assumption of the risk. 


In specifically rejecting that provision of Restatement § 
402A, the Ninth Circuit ruled, quote: 


“Such ‘all or nothing’ defenses are 
inequitable in their operation because they 
fail to distribute responsibility in proportion 
to fault and place upon one party the entire 
[66] burden of a loss for which two are, by 


hypotheses, responsible.” 


(149) I conclude the rationale of the Ninth Circuit in Pan 
Alaska is particularly appropriate to the controlling issues in the 
present case.
