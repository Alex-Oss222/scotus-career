1. RESPONDENTS INCORRECTLY 
ASSUME THAT THE EXISTENCE 
OF PRE-LOSS RISK ALLOCATION 
IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE 
APPLICATION OF ESTABLISHED 
TORT PRINCIPLES 


Although Respondents begrudgingly acknowledge 
the existence of a “basis for extending tort remedy into the 
realm of commercial contract,” they deem it 
incomprehensible that fostering the goals of strict products 
liability cam rise to the level of an “overriding public 
policy.” See Respondents’ Brief, p. 13. 


Focusing solely on the existence of pre-loss risk 
allocation among those in the distributive chain, 
Respondents characterize the rule proposed by Saratoga as 
a giant step backward or, in other words, a retreat from the 
principles espoused in East River SS. Corp. v. 
/ransamerica Delaval Inc., 476 U.S. 858 (1986). 


Respondents’ position reflects a basic 
misunderstanding of what this Court sought to accomplish 
in East River and in doing so suggests a false choice 
between pre-loss risk allocation and the application of 
established tort principles. East River sought harmony 
between contract and tort, not the elimination of tort law. 
Indeed, the regime envisioned by Respondents would all 
but nullify strict products liability as it has developed. 
Clearly, this Court never intended-through its affirmation 
of the economic loss rule-to arm manufacturers with an 
impenetrable shield against public accountability for the 
dangers present when their defective products enter the 
marketplace and destroy property never sold or warranted

Respondents’ pitch for exclusive reliance upon

this case: Should the fortuity of an intermediate sale allow 
manufacturers of unreasonably dangerous products to

to property they never sold or warranted? The mere 
existence of pre-loss contractual risk allocation to which 
the Respondents were not a party does not provide a basis 
for such an unjustified windfall. 


Saratoga does not seek an ex post facto transfer of 
purely contractual losses into the realm of tort as 
Respondents suggest. The rule of Nicor Supply Ships 
Associates v. General Motors, 876 F.2d 501 (5* Cir. 1989) 
is workable and straightforward. The economic loss

Those products never sold or warranted by the 
manufacturer, however, were never part of “the product” 
and therefore are “other property” and recoverable in tort!

Respondents fail to acknowledge in any way the 
Fifth Circuit's more recent decision in Nicor. In fact, the 
only mention of Nicor is in a footnote wherein the 
decision is cited for a proposition which it does not 
support. In Nicor the court allowed the plaintiff to recover 
for damage to its “other property” in tort although at the 
center of the controversy was a “commercial” transaction. 


Rather than citing to and/or attempting to 
distinguish Nicor or otherwise explain away its 
significance, Respondents choose to ignore it, discussing 
instead at length the Shipco decision which preceded 
Nicor. As discussed below, the facts of Shipco are readily 
distinguishable from the present case and support 
Saratoga’s position. 


Shipco stands for the proposition that a component 
part manufacturer may escape tort liability where its 
contribution to the product originally sold fails, damaging 
the other components. The Shipco court looked to the 
“object of the transaction” to define “the product” rather

IV. CONCLUSION 


Maritime products liability law prior to the Ninth 
Circuit’s Final Opinion properly recognized the boundary 
between tort and contract principles elucidated by Fas 
River. Manufacturers are not subject to tort liability for 
the self-destruction of the product they placed into the 
stream of commerce. Shipco clarified and reinforced that 
boundary, defining that product as the integrated subject 
of the original bargain. The Ninth Circuit’s Final Opinion 
has now disturbed the harmony envisioned by East River. 
Should the Ninth Circuit’s Final Opinion be given effect, 
manufacturer liability for placing unreasonably dangerous 
products into the stream of commerce will depend upon 
the fortuity of an intermediate sale. Such a windfall has 
no relation to the original bargain between buyer and 
manufacturer and does not serve the goals of public safety 
and predictability which animate products liability law.
