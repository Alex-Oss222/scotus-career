STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION 


Petitioners seek review of the opinion of the United 
States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit and its 
judgment of October 17, 1995. That judgment reversed 
and remanded the District Court’s determination with 
respect to the constitutionality of the municipal Seal of 
the City of Edmond, Oklahoma. A stay of mandate was 
issued by the Teftth Circuit Court of Appeals on Novem- 
ber 6, 1995, pending petition to this Court for writ of

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


At issue in this case is the constitutionality of the 
official Seal of the City of Edmond, Oklahoma, and, by 
application, of the governmental seals of hundreds of 
cities and counties throughout the United States. The Seal 
of Edmond, Oklahoma, is a circular design divided into 
four sections. Each of these quadrants contains a repre- 
sentation of an aspect of Edmond’s history and heritage 
that significantly contributed to the City’s historical 
development. The Seal depicts a covered wagon com- 
memorating the Land Run of April 22, 1889, the day 
Edmond was settled. A train and oil well are depicted 
which represent the importance of Santa Fe Railroad's 
“Edmond Station” and the oil industry in early Edmond. 
The left quadrant of the Seal depicts the Old North Tower 


3 


on the campus of the University of Central Oklahoma in 
Edmond, where the first classes of higher education in the 
Oklahoma Territory were conducted. The right quadrant 
contains a cross, representing the historical significance 
of early churches in Edmond (all of which happen to have 
been Christian). St. John the Baptist Catholic Church in 
Edmond was the first church established in the Oklahoma 
Territory after the Land Run. In the center of the Seal 
there are two clasped hands. The Seal also contains the 
motto “A Great Place to Grow.” A copy of Edmond’s Seal 
is found at p. 19a in the Appendix to this Petition. 


At trial, the undisputed evidence showed that in 1965 
the City Officials who selected the design of the Seal did 
so with the express and specific intent of symbolizing the 
unique history and heritage of Edmond. The historical 
collage would be incomplete if any of its elements were 
removed. There was absolutely no evidence presented at 
trial that the Seal was adopted for the purpose of promot- 
ing religion or proselytizing the citizens of Edmond. 


Respondents each lived or worked in Edmond, Okla- 
homa, at the time the action was filed. The action was 
filed under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, invoking the District Court's 
federal question jurisdiction conferred at 28 U.S.C. § 1331. 
Mr. Feldman is a member of the Jewish faith. The remair. 
ing Respondents are members of Respondent Wayne Rob- 
inson’s Unitarian congregation. The evidence at trial 
showed the Edmond City Seal was used without incident 
or question from 1965 until 1992 when Respondents and 
the ACLU demanded that the cross be removed from the 
Seal. Respondents’ testimony at trial showed, and they 


for the most part admitted, that they exhibit unique sensi- 
tivities with respect to the personal offense they claim to 
experience at the sight of the cross on the City Seal. 


The District Court relied on this Court’s three-prong 
test articulated in Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 91 
S.Ct. 2105, 29 L.Ed.2d 745 (1971). The lower court found 
that the Seal was adopted for the proper purpose of 
identifying City property and that there was no entangle- 
ment of government with religion. The lower court deter- 
mined, based on the evidence at trial, that the average 
observer of the City Seal perceives it to be a representa- 
tion of the City’s historical foundations and does not 
perceive it as conveying a message of governmental 
endorsement or advancement of religion. From 1965 until 
the ACLU targeted Edmond’s Seal in 1992, there was no 
controversy regarding the Seal. The evidence showed that 
whatever controversy now exists regarding the Seal was 
ccused by Respondents and the ACLU. 


The only witnesses who testified that they perceived 
the City Seal to endorse Christianity were Respondents. 
These individuals, whose demeanor and appearance was 
observed by the trial court, expressed their very strong 
feelings that any religious symbol, including the “Eye of 
God” that appears on one dollar bills and the phrase “In 
God We Trust” that appears on U.S. coins, have no place 
on any government property and violate the Establish- 
ment Clause. Respondents also admitted that they had 
lived and/or worked in Edmond for a number of years 
without even noticing that the Seal included among its 
representations a depiction of a cross. 


The District Court, after a two-day trial, found that 
the evidence showed that an average observer would not 
perceive that the Seal had the effect of advancing or 
inhibiting any religion. The District Court determined 
that the average observer would recognize the Seal for 
just what it is ~- a celebration of the history and heritage 
of Edmond, Oklahoma. See Memorandum Opinion at p. 
24a in the Appendix to this Petition. The lower court 
found, “Religion is a part of Edmond’s history as are 
railroads, covered wagons, schools and the land run. The 
First Amendment prohibits the establishment of religion 
- it does not mandate that government deny or ignore its 
existence.” Id. 


The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed the Dis- 
trict Court’s judgment, finding that the presence on the 
Seal of a “visible and clearly defined religious image” 
inescapably leads to the conclusion that the Establish- 
ment Clause of the First Amendment to the United States 
Constitution has been violated. 


Petitioners now seek this Court's review.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE WRIT 
I. 


CERTIORARI SHOULD BE GRANTED BECAUSE THE 
OPINION OF THE TENTH CIRCUIT COURT OF 
APPEALS ESTABLISHES AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL 
BRIGHT-LINE TEST IN FIRST AMENDMENT CASES 
THAT HAS BEEN REJECTED BY THIS COURT. 


This case involves important questions having broad 
ramifications to myriad governmental bodies whose seals 


include visible and clearly defined religious symbology. 
According to the Tenth Circuit's opinion, visible and 
clearly defined religious symbols simply cannot with- 
stand constitutional scrutiny and therefore must be 
removed from governmental seals. However, this Court 
has never been willing to establish such a bright-line test. 
In fact, throughout Establishment Clause jurisprudence, 
one dominant theme has emerged — that is, in cases 
regarding the interpretation of the Establishment Clause, 
no bright-line test can or should be applied. Although this 
Court has wrestled with the interpretation of the Clause 
in various applications, this Court has consistently held 
that the question of whether a governmental action vio- 
lates the Establishment Clause depends on all of the 
circumstances of the particular case. As this Court stated 
in Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668, 678-79, 104 S.Ct. 1355, 
1362, 79 L.Ed.2d 604 (1984): 


[The inquiry in each Establishment Clause case] 
calls for line-drawing; no fixed, per se rule can 
be framed .. . . The line between permissible 
relationships [between government and reli- 
gion] and those barred by the Clause can [not] 
be straight and unwavering . . . . The Clause 
erects a “blurred, indistinct and variable barrier 
depending on all the circumstances of a particu- 
lar relationship.” Lemon, 403 U.S. at 614, 91 S.Ct. 
at 2112. 


While the construction of this variable barrier may be 
difficult, this Court has emphasized that governmental 
actions must be considered in the context of the “particu- 
lar physical setting” of the challenged action. See County 
of Allegheny v. American Civil Liberties Union, 492 U.S. 573, 
595, 109 S.Ct. 3086, 3102, 106 L.Ed.2d 472 (1989). The 


inquiry is “what viewers may fairly understand to be the 
purpose of the display.” Id. This inquiry turns on the 
context in which the contested object appears. County of 
Allegheny, 492 U.S. 573, 595, 109 S.Ct. 3086, 3102. “Every 
government practice must be judged in its unique circum- 
stances to determine whether it [endorses] religion.” 


Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668, 694, 104 S.Ct. 1355, 1370 
(1984). 


The decision of the Tenth Circuit in this case in 
reality tacitly rejects this Court’s requirement that the 
challenged activity be viewed in context. Instead, the 
Tenth Circuit states: 


While we acknowledge that each case must turn 
on its own facts, and that the particular context 
and setting of a particular governmental seal or 
logo is relevant to its fate when challenged 
under the Establishment Clause, we decline 
defendants’ invitation to carefully and minutely 
distinguish the Edmond seal from the Bernalillo 
County seal based upon the particular dimen- 
sions of the crosses in the two seals or the 
secular or non-secular nature of the other ele- 
ments of the seal . . . . We too decline to hold that 
some visible and clearly defined religious images are 
permissible while other identically visible religious 
images are not. 


See Opinion at pp. 16a-17a (emphasis added). While ini- 
tially appearing to acknowledge that there is a require- 
ment that each case be determined on its own facts, the 
Tenth Circuit went on to create a new test for constitu- 
tionality of governmental seals that calls for no such 
factual determination. That is, according to the Tenth 
Circuit, if the average observer can recognize the seal as 


containing a symbol which has religious meaning, the 
Establishment Clause is violated.' 


The effects test in Lemon requires that the Court 
determine whether the challenged activity has the effect 
of endorsing or advancing religion. No such determina- 
tion is required by the Tenth Circuit's holding. Under the 
Tenth Circuit’s decision, the only determination neces- 
sary is whether the religious symbol is a familiar symbol 
that is visible and clearly defined. No inquiry is necessary 
into the effect of the seal, the perception of the average 
observer, or whether the seal endorses or advances reli- 
gion. Rather, the Tenth Circuit’s opinion requires that all 
familiar religious symbols be excised from governmental 
seals, regardless of the particular physical setting. 


In other words, the Tenth Circuit holds that govern- 
mental seals may celebrate history and heritage as long as 
the history and heritage do not involve religion. This 
rigid position has been expressly rejected by this Court. 
In Lynch, this Court provided a long list of references to 
religious symbology included in our nation’s founda- 
tional institutions. Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 676-77, 104 


! The Tenth Circuit distinguished the Latin cross, a “very 
familiar religious symbol,” from the LDS (Mormon) church at 
issue in its opinion in Foremaster v. City of St. George, 882 F.2d 
1485 (10th Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 495 U.S. 910, 110 S.Ct. 1937, 109 
L.Ed.2d 300 (1990), on the ground that the LDS temple is a less 
familiar religious symbol than is the Latin cross. Therefore, the 
Circuit Court explained that it properly remanded Foremaster 
for trial on the issue of the average observer's perception. Opin- 
ion at p. 12a n.9. The implication is that no trial is necessary 
when the religious image is “very familiar.” 


S.Ct. at 1360-61. This Court noted, for example, that refer- 
ences to our religious heritage are found in the national 
motto (“In God We Trust”) as set forth on this nation’s 
currency as well as in the language of the Pledge of 
Allegiance to the American flag. Id. To allow cities to 
commemorate all aspects of their unique histories except 
those aspects related to religion represents hostility 
toward religion that runs directly counter to Establish- 
ment Clause jurisprudence. See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 
577, 112 S.Ct. 2649, 120 L.Ed.2d 467 (1992); Zorach v. 
Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314, 72 S.Ct. 679, 684, 96 L.Ed. 954 
(1952); Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 
U.S. 203, 211, 68 S.Ct. 461, 465, 92 L.Ed. 649 (1948). Justice 
O’Connor pointed out in her concurring opinion in 
County of Allegheny v. American Civil Liberties Union, 492 
U.S. 573, 623, 109 S.Ct. 3086, 3117 (1989), that the reason 
this Court has avoided bright-line tests such as that 
adopted by the Tenth Circuit is because to draw lines 
“which entirely sweep away all government recognition 
and acknowledgement of the role of religion in the lives 
of our citizens [would] exhibit not neutrality but hostility 
to religion.” Similarly, Justice O’Connor opined in Board 
of Ed. of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, __ 
U.S. __, 114 S.Ct. 2481, 2499, 129 L.Ed.2d 546 (1993), 
“Experience proves that the Establishment Clause, like 
the Free Speech Clause, cannot easily be reduced to a 
single test.” 


Further, had the rationale of the Tenth Circuit been 
applied in County of Allegheny, neither the creche nor the 
menorah at issue in that case could have withstood judi- 
cial scrutiny. The menorah is a visible and clearly defined 
symbol of the Jewish faith. Under the Tenth Circuit's 


10 


rationale, its presence in the City’s display would have 
violated the Establishment Clause. 


This Court, however, did not find that the menorah 
violated the Constitution. Rather, the Court examined the 
setting, including the secular symbols in the display as a 
whole and the written message accompanying the dis- 
play, to determine that the average observer would 
appreciate that the display was an acknowledgement of 
the cultural diversity of the city and not an endorsement 
of the Jewish faith. 


It is apparent that the primary effect of Edmond’s 
Seal is to commemorate the history of Edmond, rather 
than to promote or advance any religion. This is espe- 
cially true when the cross on the Seal is viewed in the 
context of the Seal’s other clearly secular symbols and in 
the context of the particular physical settings where the 
Seal is displayed by the City, all of which are also purely 
secular. 


The trial court carefully considered all the evidence 
at trial and determined that the average observer would 
conclude that the Seal depicts the history and heritage of 
Edmond. The trial court found, based on the evidence 
presented, that the vast majority of Edmond’s citizenry, 
both the Christian and the non-Christian citizens, see the 
Seal for what it is - a collage of historical symbols cele- 
brating Edmond’s unique history and heritage. And that 
is the exact theme the Seal was designed in 1965 to 
represent. 


The Tenth Circuit’s decision has drairatic conse- 
quences not just for the City of Edmond but also for all 
other cities, counties and governmental bodies whose 


11 


seals contain any identifiable religious symbology. With- 
out inquiring whether these seals as a whole have the 
effect of endorsing religion, these governmental bodies 
must immediately change their seals or risk liability for 
damages to any willing plaintiffs who might choose to 
bring suit. As a result, the Tenth Circuit’s decision requires 
hostility toward religion. Under the Tenth Circuit's deci- 
sion, it is only religious symbols that must be excised from 
the seals. The impact of such a decision is far-reaching 
and unconstitutional. 


THE TENTH CIRCUIT COURT OF APPEALS HAS 
ESTABLISHED A TEST THAT IS CONTRARY TO 
THE TEST ESTABLISHED BY THE FIFTH CIRCUIT 
AS WELL AS BY THIS COURT AND ONE THAT 
IS HOSTILE TOWARD RELIGION. 


The opinion of the Tenth Circuit in this case is in 
direct conflict with the decision of the Fifth Circuit in 
Murray v. City of Austin, 947 F.2d 147 (5th Cir. 1991), cert. 
denied, _. US. __, 112 S.Ct. 3028, 120 L.Ed.2d 899 
(1992). In Murray, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals 
upheld the use of a Latin cross on the municipal seal of 
rhe City of Austin, Texas. Because the plaintiff (like 
Respondents here) conceded that the first and third 
prongs of the Lemon test were met, the case turned on the 


2 The Seventh Circuit has also held similarly to the Tenth 
Circuit's holding. In Harris v. City of Zion, 927 F.2d 1401 (7th Cir. 
1991), cert. denied, US. __, 112 S.Ct. 3025, 120 L.Ed.2d 897 
(1992), the Court held “that sectarian religious imagery simply 
has no place on municipal seals.” 927 F.2d at 1402. The seal 
found unconstitutional in Harris had no relationship to history. 


12 


application of Lemon's “effect” test. In applying the 
“effect” test, the Murray court stated: 


If we focus exclusively on the inclusion of the 
religious symbol, display, or practice, then every 
use of religious symbolism - and prayer - 
would fail. The piuin wording of the First 
Amendment does not require this; nor does the 
Supreme Court. 
Id. at 154. Thus, the Fifth Circuit held that the focus is 
whether the seal, taken as a whole and in the particular 
physical setting in which it is displayed, has the principal 
or primary effect of advancing or inhibiting religion. Id. 
at 155. 


In Murray, the court found that, taken as a whole, the 
City of Austin’s seal did not have the primary effect of 
advancing religion. Instead, the seal had the principal or 
primary effect of identifying city activity and property 
and promoting the city’s unique role and history. Id. at 
155. In fact, the court held that any perceived preference 
of religion by use of Austin’s seal was even more remote 
than the Nativity Scene held constitutional in Lynch, 
supra, the menorah held constitutional in County of Alle- 
gheny, supra, and the legislative prayer held constitutional 
in Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783, 103 S.Ct. 330, 77 
L.Ed.2d 1019 (1983). However, if the Fifth Circuit had 
applied the rule announced by the Tenth Circuit in the 
instant action, the Austin seal would have violated the 
First Amendment. The visible and clearly defined Latin 
cross in the Austin seal would have been unconstitu- 
tional. 


Contrary to the Tenth Circuit’s holding, the test set 
out by this Court is not whether the average observer 


A 


13 


would recognize the symbol as one that has religious 
significance. Before the Court can find an Establishment 
Clause violation, the Court must determine that an aver- 
age observer would primarily perceive the seal as a whole 
as conveying a message of government endorsement or 
advancement of religion. See Lemon v. Kurtzman, supra. 


In the case at bar, the trial court heard the evidence 
regarding the perceptions of the average observer. As set 
forth in the Statement of Facts above, it is clear that the 
evidence strongly suggested that the average observer, 
unlike Respondents, views Edmond’s Seal as a historical 
representation of the City of Edmond. When viewed as a 
whole, the Seal presents a collage of the history and 
heritage of a community that traces its roots to the Land 
Run of 1889 and to the triumphs and tragedies of pioneer 
living. Only Respondents, who view all religious symbol- 
ogy as offensive and inappropriate, testified that they 
viewed the Seal as an endorsement of the Christian reli- 
gion. The fact of the matter is that when Respondents see 
the Seal, they see nothing but the cross. They are unable 
to view the entire collage. No doubt, because of their 
unique sensitivities, if they viewed the Lynch creche in its 
setting, they would see nothing but the manger and 
would be similarly offended. However, the test of consti- 
tutionality is not based on the perceptions of the city’s 
most hypersensitive citizens but that of an average 
observer. 


The quadrant depicting the cross on Edmond’s Seal 
cannot be viewed in isolation. Rather, the four quadrants 
of the Seal must be viewed as a whole and in the context 
for which they were intended. See Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 
U.S. 668, 679, 104 S.Ct. 1355, 1362, 79 L.Ed.2d 604 (1984). 


14 


As the Lynch Court noted, “Focus exclusively on the 
religious component of any activity would inevitably lead 
to its invalidation under the Establishment Clause.” Id. 
The City Seal, when viewed as a whole, presents a histor- 
ical picture of Edmond, Oklahoma, that would be incom- 
plete and inaccurate without the inclusion of the cross. 


This Court has not yet taken the opportunity to 
decide a case involving the constitutionality of a govern- 
mental seal. Yet it is clear that such cases beg for guid- 
ance from this Court. The Circuits are divided in their 
interpretations of existing law. The lower courts have 
frequently been reversed because it has been so difficult 
to decipher the conflicting Circuit Court decisions. The 
Tenth Circuit has now attempted to make the determina- 
tion easy by singling out all visible and clearly defined 
religious symbols as unconstitutional. However, this test 
itself violates the United States Constitution by requiring 
hostility toward religion. This Court should grant cer- 
tiorari in this case to resolve the conflict among the 
circuits and to prevent the far-reaching application of the 
Tenth Circuit's recently announced rule. 


¢ 


CONCLUSION 


The decision of the Tenth Circuit not only conflicts 
with the Fifth Circuit’s holding but also conflicts with the 
previous rules announced by this Court. The issue of the 
constitutionality of governmental seals is one affecting 
hundreds of communities throughout this nation. 
Although creation of a bright-line test such as that offered 


— ee 


15 


by the Tenth Circuit might serve to make the determina- 
tion of constitutionality a simple one, it is contrary to 
whai this Court has held, and such a test has constitu- 
tional ramifications of its own. 


For these reasons, Petitioners request that this Court 
grant certiorari in this case.

DISCUSSION 


The First Amendment provides that “Congress shall 
make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or 
prohibiting the free exercise thereof... . “ U.S. Const. 
amend. I. The Supreme Court generally applies the three- 
part test of Lemon when evaluating claimed violations of 
the Establishment Clause.5 Thus, we continue to apply it 
to such cases, while recognizing that “[ajlthough the 
Supreme Court has been unwilling to endorse Lemon as 
the ‘be-all’ and ‘end-all’ in Establishment Clause cases, it 
has continued to apply it almost exclusively.” Friedman v. 
Board of County Comm'rs, 781 F.2d 777, 780 (10th Cir. 1985) 
(en banc), cert. denied, 476 U.S. 1169 (1986). 


Government action violates the Establishment Clause 
under Lemon if it fails to meet any of the following 
conditions: (1) it must have a secular purpose; (2) its 
principal or primary effect must be one that neither 
advances nor inhibits religion; and (3) it must not foster 
excessive government entanglement with religion. Lemon, 
403 U.S. at 612-13. The first and second parts of the test 
have been modified, or at least recast, to ask whether the 
challenged government action was intended to endorse, 
or has the effect of endorsing, religion. County of Alle- 
gheny v. American Civil Liberties Union, 492 U.S. 573, 
592-93 (1989); Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668, 687-94 


5 While the Lemon test has been criticized by many, includ- 
ing some members of the Supreme Court, and has been declared

Free Sch. Dist., 113 S.Ct. 2141, 2149-50 (1993), the Supreme Court 
has specifically declined to overrule it. See id. at 2148 n.7; see also 
Lee v. Weisman, 112 S.Ct. 2649, 2655 (1992).

Plaintiffs concede that the main issue in this case is 
whether the City seal violates the second part of the 
Lemon test: whether its primary effect is to advance or 
inhibit religion, or, in endorsement test terms, whether it 
conveys or attempts to convey the message “that religion 
or a particular religious belief is favored or preferred.” 
Allegheny, 492 U.S. at 593 (quoting Wallace v. Jaffree, 472 
U.S. 38, 70 (1985) (O’Connor, J., concurring in the judg- 
ment)).° In applying this “effects” test, we must consider 
the “ ‘particular physical setting’” of the challenged 
action. Foremaster, 882 F.2d at 1491 (quoting Allegheny, 492 
U.S. at 597). Further, we evaluate the effect “by an objec- 
tive standard, which looks only to the reaction of the 
average receiver of the government communication or 
average observer of the government action.” Friedman, 
781 F.2d at 781; see also, Foremaster, 882 F.2d at 1491 (“We 
inquire what an average observer would perceive when 
viewing the action of the City.”).

In 1965 the City Council for the City of Edmond 
decided that it wanted a seal to identify city property and 
personnel. Through a local newspaper, a contest was run 
for persons to submit drawings for the proposed seal. 
According to a newspaper article at the time “contest 
entries should reflect the factors which make Edmond the 
finest small city in Oklahoma - her birth in the Run ot 
‘89, the college and the culture, commerce, agriculture 
and downright friendliness of the city.” A local resident, 
Francis Bryan, submitted two entries which won the first 
and third prizes in the contest. The City Council was not 
entirely satisfied with the first place entry and asked Mrs. 
Bryan to combine her two submissions into a seal which 
reflected the history of Edmond. She did so and ulti- 
mately came up with the design which became the 
Edmond Seal. See Appendix A. The seal is used on city 
property, stationery and uniforms. 


The seal was used without incident or question from 
1965 until 1992 when several members of a local Uni- 
tarian Church including its pastor protested to the City 
the inclusion of the Christian cross in the seal. Three of 
the Plaintiffs are Unitarian and one is Jewish. Ultimately, 
the City refused to remove the cross from the seal and 
this case ensued. 


The Edmond seal contains four quadrants. One quad- 
rant depicts a steam engine and oil derrick, another Old 
North Tower, another a covered wagon and the number 
1889 and one quadrant depicts the Christian cross. 


22a 


As indicated, the Plaintiffs contend that the inclusion 
of the cross in the official seal constitutes such promotion 
of the Christian faith that it violates the Establishment 
Clause of the Constitution. 


There have been a number of cases in several circuits 
which have dealt with the use of religious symbols in 
official city seals. As the seals have varied, so have the 
results. See Gonzalez v. North Township of Lake County, 4 
F.3d 1412 (7th Cir. 1993); Murray v. City of Austin, 947 F.2d 
147 (5th Cir. 1991), cert. denied, __ US. __, 112 S.Ct. 
3028, 120 L.Ed.2d 899 (1992); Harris v. City of Lion, 927 
F.2d 1401 (7th Cir. 1991), rehearing en banc denied, 934 F.2d 
141 (7th Cir. 1991), cert. denied, _._ US. __, 112 S.Ct. 
3025, 120 L.Ed.2d 897 (1992); Friedman v. Board of County 
Commissioners of Bernadillo County, 781 F.2d 777 (10th Cir. 
1985), cert. denied, 476 U.S. 1169, 106 S.Ct. 2890, 90 
L.Ed.2d 978 (1990). 


In Foremaster v. City of St. George, 882 F.2d 1485 (10th 
Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 495 U.S. 910, 110 S.Ct. 1937, 109 
L.Ed.2d 300 (1990), the Tenth Circuit last considered the 
inclusion of religious symbols in a city seal. In Foremaster, 
the Court applied the First Amendment analysis set out 
in Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 91 S.Ct. 2105, 29 
L.Ed.2d 745 (1971). In order to survive an Establishment 
Clause challenge, Lemon requires that government action 
meet these conditions: (1) it must have a secular purpose; 
(2) its primary effect must be one that neither advances 
nor inhibits religion; and (3) it must not foster an exces- 
sive government entanglement with religion. 403 U.S. at 
612, 91 S.Ct. at __, 29 L.Ed.2d at 755. 


23a 


Applying the Lemon analysis to the present case, the 
Court finds that the City of Edmond seal meets all three 
conditions. First, the seal’s purpose was and is secular. It 
was created for the purpose of identifying city property 
and personnel. The items included were for the purpose 
of showing Edmond’s history and heritage. 


Second, the Court is satisfied that the primary effect 
of the seal neither advances nor inhibits religion. In Fore- 
master, the Court said that the finder of fact must deter- 
mine whether the depiction (the temple in the context of 
the St. George logo) conveys primarily a message of 
government endorsement and advancement of religion. 
882 F.2d at 1491-92. In this regard, the Court must deter- 
mine what an average observer would perceive when 
viewing the city seal. 882 F.2d at 1491. Although the 
testimony was in conflict and perception is in the eye of 
the beholder, the Court finds that the average observer 
would conclude that the seal taken as a whole primarily 
depicts the history and heritage of Edmond as opposed to 
conveying primarily a message of endorsement or 
enhancement of a religion.? Obviously, not everyone who 
views the seal will be completely aware of the history the 
seal is intended to depict. Perhaps only a historian would 
realize that Old North Tower is the building where the 
first classes of higher education were conducted in the

In Lynch v. Donnelly, 465 U.S. 668, 104 S.Ct. 1355, 79 
L.Ed.2d 604 (1984), in permitting the display of a creche 
at Christmas paid for with city funds, the Supreme Court 
with approval recognized that “[t}here are count- 
less . . . illustrations of the Government's acknowledg- 
ment of our religious heritage and governmental 
sponsorship of graphic manifestations of that heritage.” 
465 U.S. at 677, 104 S.Ct. at ___, 79 L.Ed.2d at 612. Like- 
wise, in County of Allegheny v. American Civil Liberties 
Union, Greater Pittsburgh Chapter, 492 U.S. 573, 109 S.Ct.

The final prong of the Lynch test is whether the 
depiction fosters excessive entanglement of the Govern- 
ment with religion. There is no evidence of entanglement 
of the City with religion other than the fact of the cross 
being in the seal. There is no evidence that any church 
played any role in its inclusion or its continuation. 


The Plaintiffs also contend that the seal violates Arti- 
cle II Section 5 of the Oklahoma Constitution. Without 
going into extensive analytical discussion, suffice it to 
say, based on the Oklahoma Supreme Court's interpreta- 
tion of that provision, the seal does not violate Oklahoma 
law. See Meyer v. Oklahoma City, 496 P.2d 789 (1972), cert. 
denied, 409 U.S. 980, 93 S.Ct. 314, 34 L.Ed.2d 244 (1972).

ment should be entered in behalf of the Defendants and 
against each Plaintiff.
