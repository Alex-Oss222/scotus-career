                    QUESTIONS PRESENTED
      1. Do the governmental interests promoted by the
blanket (bright-line) rule reaffirmed in State v. Richards,
201 Wis. 2d 839, 549 N.W.2d 218 (1996), outweigh the
individual interests protected by the rule of announcement
so that the bright-line rule passes the Fourth Amendment
test of reasonableness applied in Maryland v. Wilson, 65
U.S.L.W. 4124 (U.S. Feb. 19, 1997)?
     2. Did the police have a particularized and objective
basis for suspecting that further announcement would
result in a threat to safety, the flight of the suspect or the
destruction of evidence so that their entry into the
defendant’s room prior to announcing the purpose of the
entry passes the Fourth Amendment test for
reasonableness applied in Ohio v. Robinette, 117 S. Ct. 417
(1996)?
     3. Was the police entry into the defendant’s room prior
to announcing the purpose of the entry reasonable because
further announcement would have been a useless gesture
once the defendant closed and locked the door after looking
in the direction of a uniformed police officer?
     4. If the police entry into the defendant’s room was
unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, should the
exclusionary rule be applied to prevent the state from
introducing at trial the evidence seized in the execution of
the search warrant?

                 SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

     In Wilson v. Arkansas, 115 S. Ct. 1914, 1915 (1995),
this Court held that the “common-law ‘knock and
announce’ principle forms a part of the reasonableness
inquiry under the Fourth Amendment." The common law
knock and announce principle recognized a law
enforcement officer’s authority to break open the doors of
a dwelling, but generally indicated that he first ought to
announce his presence and authority. Id.
     In Statev.Richards, 201 Wis2d.839, 549 N.W.2d 218
(1996), the Wisconsin Supreme Court reaffirmed a rule it
had adopted prior to Wilson v. Arkansas and held that the
police are not required to adhere to the rule of
announcement whenever they execute a search warrant for
felonious drug dealing.

     The defendant contends that the bright-line rule
reaffirmed in Richards cannot comply with the
reasonableness inquiry under the Fourth Amendment
because it does not look to the particular facts of each
search to determine whether the entry into the residence
was reasonable.

     Contrary to the defendant’s claim, the rule reaffirmed
in Richards is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment.
The legitimate governmental interests in police safety,
crime detection and prevention and the prevention of flight
by the suspect outweigh the occupant’s interest in privacy
and property rights. Thertehefbro ightr-liene ,rule
reaffirmed in Richards satisfies the Fourth Amendment
reason tesa t b appl liee d in n Me ars ylas nd v. Wilson,65
U.S.L.'W. 4124 (U.S. Feb. 19, 1997).

     The entry also satisfied the Fourth Amendment test

where the Court emphasized the fact-specific nature of the
reasonableness inquiry. Bala thensamc e i goven rnmg ental

     In Maryland v. Wilson, 65 U.S.L.W. at 4125 n.1, this

     In Stevens, 181 Wis. 2d at 424-25, 511 N.W.2d at 595,

at 4126.

    that a warrant to search for contraband founded on

     Explaining the test for reasonableness under the

Fourth Amendment, the Court said in Pennsylvania v.
Mimms, 434 U.S. 106, 108-09 (1977):

     The touchstone of our analysis under the Fourth
     Amendment is always “the reasonableness in all
     the circumstances of the particular governmental
     invasion of a citizen’s personal security." Terry v.

     Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 19 (1968). Reasonableness, of

     interest and the individual’s right to personal
     security free from arbitrary interference by law
     officers." United States v. Brignoni-Ponce, 422 U.S.
     873, 878 (1975).

     In Maryland v. Wilson, 65 U.S.L.W. at 4125, the Court
balanced the public’s interest in officer safety with the
passengers’ liberty interest. The Court concluded that the
danger to the traffic officer during the traffic stop
outweighed the minimal intrusion imposed on the
passengers by ordering them out of the car. Id. at 4126.

the no knock entry reduces the chances that the officer will
be confronted by violence, the no knock entry also means
the occupant will be less likely to start the violence. By
the no knock entry, the police can exercise command of the
situation as soon as possible and, thereby, minimize the
risk to themselves and to the occupants.

exploitation of an illegality; second, because the warrant

illegal entry; and, third, because the evidence was seized
pursuant to a valid warrant, application of the
exclusionary rule would put the police in a worse position
than if they had complied with the rule of announcement.

     In this case, even if the police entry was unreasonable,
the evidence should not be suppressed since the seizure of
the evidence was not an exploitation of the entry. The
evidence was seized under the authority of the search
warrant that had been issued by the neutral and detached
magistrate. Because the evidence was seized under the
authority of the warrant, the police entry did not taint the
seizure of that evidence. The only event that would have
prevented the police from seizing the evidence would have
been its destruction; and the defendant does not have a
constitutional right to destroy the evidence. Ker v.
California, 374 U.S. at 39, quoting People v. Maddox, 46
Cal. 2d 301, 306, 294 P.2d 6, 9 (1956).

     In a dissenting opinion in New York v. Harris, Justice
Marshall explained that the principal purpose of the
exclusionary rule is to eliminate incentives for police
officers to violate the Fourth Amendment and he noted: "A
police officer who violates the Constitution usually does so
to obtain evidence that he could not secure lawfully."
Harris, 495 U.S. at 22 (Marshall, J., dissenting).

     In United States v. Crews, 445 U.S. 463, 471 (1980), the
Court also considered when suppression of evidence is
propandesraid: “Thus most cases begin with the premise
that the challenged evidence is in some sense the product
of illegal governmental activity."

     Again, under that standard, suppression is not proper
in the instant case because the evidence was not the
product of illegal governmental activity. The police did not
have to act unlawfully to seize the evidence because its
seizure was the product of the valid search warrant, not
the mode or manner of entry.

     In a case where the police searched a residence under
the authority of a valid warrant after they had illegally
entered the residence, this Court refused to suppress the
evidence seized under the authority of the valid warrant.

In Segura v. United States, 468 U.S. 796 (1984), the police

illegally entered the defendant’s apartment without a
warrant and arrested the occupants. Police remained in
the apartment for nearly twenty-four hours while other
officers obtained a search warrant that was based on
information the police possessed before they entered the
apartment. The defendant sought to suppress evidence
seized in the execution of the search warrant. The
defendant argued that the evidence seized under the
warrant should be excluded as “fruit” derived from the
illegal entry. Segura, 468 U.S. at 813.

     In rejecting the defendant’s argument, the Court said
that the legality of the entry was irrelevant where the
source of the information on which the warrant was
secured was unconnected with the entry and was known to
the police before the entry. Segura, 468 U.S. at 813-14.

     The Court later explained: "Suppression is not justified
unless ‘the challenged evidence is in some sense the
product of illegal governmental activity.’ United States v.
Crews, 445 U.S., at 471." Segura, 468 U.S. at 815. The
Court said suppression was not appropriate since the
illegal entry into the apartment did not contribute in any
way to the discovery of the evidence seized under the
warrant.

     The Court rejected the dissent’s argument that had the
police not entered the apartment the occupants could have
removed or destroyed the evidence before the warrant
issued. The majority of the Court refused to extend the
exclusionary rule to protect criminal activity such as the
destruction of evidence. Segura, 468 U.S. at 816.
                                     44

     The Court added: "The essence of the dissent is that
there is some ‘constitutional right’ to destroy evidence.
This concept defies both logic and common sense.” Segura,
468 U.S. at 816.

     The decision in Segura dictates that the exclusionary
rule not be applied to evidence seized in the execution of
the search warrant in this case even if this Court holds
that the entry was unreasonable under the Fourth
Amendment. As in Segura, the warrant was based on
information obtained by the police before entering the
residence. The warrant and the evidence seized under it
were obtained wholly independent from the entry. The
entry only prevented the occupants from destroying the
drugs; and, as the Court made clear in Segura, the
occupants did not have a constitutional right to destroy the
evidence. Therefore, the exclusionary rule cannot be
applied to protect their chance to destroy evidence. As
made clear in Segura, because the evidence in this case
was seized on a warrant independent from the entry, the
exclusionary rule cannot be applied to the evidence
obtained in the execution of the warrant.

     If the exclusionary rule were applied where the police
made a no knock entry to execute a valid search warrant,
the lesson for the police, in light of Segura, would be to
make the entry before obtaining the warrant, ther get the
warrant and return to the premises that are under the
protection of the police. Such would be an anomalous
result because the police would find it more to their
advantage to plan an illegal entry prior to obtaining the

warrant.

     The exclusionary rule should not be applied when to do
so would put the poliin acwe orse position than they would
have been if they complied with the constitutional
requirements. Murray v. United States, 487 U.S. 533, 541
(1988). One reason for the exclusionary rule is to protect
against the prosecution being put in a better position than
                                      45

it would have been if no illegality had transpired. Nix v.
Williams, 467 U.S. 431, 443 (1984). Under the
independent source doctrine, evidence is not suppressed if
it would have been discovered wholly independent of any
constitutional violation.

     In this case, even if the police entered the room
illegally, the evidence was discovered and seized by a
means wholly independent from the entry because it was
discovered and seized in the execution of a valid search
warrant. Had the police entered legaily, the evidence
would have been discovered and seized pursuant to the
warrant. If the exclusionary rule were applied in this case,
the police would be put in a worse position than if they had
complied with the rule of announcement. To avoid putting
the police in a worse position than if they complied with
the rule, the evidence must be admitted at trial.

entry in this case was unreasonable under the Fourth
Amendment, suppression of the evidence is not the proper
remedy because the evidence was seized under the
authority of the valid search warrant, not out of
