STATEMENT OF THE CASE

         This case revolves around police entry into the
petitioner’s hotel room in Madison, Wisconsin, at 3:40
a.m. on December 31, 1991.

         The police did not comply with the announcement rule
in effecting the entry. The magistrate who issued the
warrant specifically determined that the particular
circumstances did not justify a no- knock warrant, and
he struck those portions of the warrant that would have
authorized such execution.

The Wisconsin supreme court opinion relates,

The dispositive facts for purposes of this

appeal can be stated succinctly: on December 31,

1991, police executed a search warrant for the

motel room of the defendant seeking evidence of the

felonious crime of Possession with Intent to

Deliver a Controlled Substance in violation of Wis.

Stat. § 161.41 (In) (1991-92). They did not knock

and announce prior to their entry.            Drugs were

seized.

The circuit court denied Richards’[s]} motion

to suppress. Richards subsequently entered pleas

of no contest to the felony of possession of

cocaine base with the intent to deliver, Wis. Stat.

$161.41(1m), and a tax stamp violation, Wis. Stat.

$139.95(2).      The court found him guilty and
sentenced him to 13 years imprisonment on the
possession with intent count and three years
concurrent imprisonment on the tax stamp count.
Richards appealed. The court of appeals upheld the
circuit court’s ruling, relying on (State v. ]}

              „  181 Wis.  2d 410, 511 N.W.2d 591 (1994)).
Richards         filed a   petition for review which we
granted.

201 Wis. 2d at 843.

For the purposes of this petition, Richards does not

contest this recitation of facts. The Wisconsin Supreme

Court held that the particular circumstances of this case

did not matter in light of the blanket exception to the

announcement rule. If the United States Supreme Court

finds the Wisconsin blanket rule repugnant to the Fourth

Amendment, then and   only then   will further  factual

analysis be required   on remand  to determine  whether

specific exigent circumstances were present.

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION

THE SUPREME COURT SHOULD GRANT CERTIORARI TO
DETERMINE WHETHER THE FOURTH AMENDMENT PERMITS
BLANKET EXCEPTIONS TO THE RULE OF ANNOUNCEMENT.

Review is requested under Supreme Court Rule 10(c)

in that the Wisconsin supreme court has decided an

important question of federal law that has not been, but

should be, settled by this Court, and the Wisconsin court

has also decided an important federal question in a way

that conflicts with Wilvs . Ao rkan nsas, 514 ub. 8. 115

S.Ct. 1914, 1919, 131 L.Ed.2d 976 (1995).

In sum, the Wisconsin supreme court has ruled in the

case at bar that in drug cases, police need never comply

with knock and announce“ despite the holding of Wilson

that compliance with announcement forms a part of the

reasonableness analysis courts must conduct under the

Fourth Amendment.  The importance of this federal

question is beyond dispute. It affects every execution

of a search warrant in the United States involving

allegations of drug-dealing. This Court should subject

the Wisconsin opinion to testing because to fail to

review this decision invites other courts to join the

Wisconsin court in refusing to balance the interests of

states against neutral objective criteria in "knock and

announce" cases.   When probable cause exists, it is

usually not necessary to perform a balancing test, but in

the case of unannounced entry into a home, due to the

unusual harmfulness to an individual’s privacy and
physical interests, such a balancing must take place.

See United States v. Whren, 116 S.Ct. 1769, 1776 (1996).

          Nowhere is the protective force of the Fourth
Amendment more powerful than when the sanctity of the
home is involved, and the rule of announcement protects

people in their homes. In Mil v. Ul nite ed r States, 357

U.S. 301, 313 (1958), this Court stated,
                    {Tjhe requirement of prior notice of

          authorization and purpose before forcing entry into
          a home is deeply rooted in our heritage and should
          not be given a grudging application.
          Despite such a long-standing doctrine of recognition
of the importance of the announcement rule in Anglo-
American law, the opinion below mocks this statement and
encourages other state courts to bandy with the rule.
          The broad precedential effect of the instant case is
demonstrated by events in the Sixth Circuit Court of
Appeals following the issuance of the decision. Ina
letter to counsel for the defendant in United States v.
Bates, 84 F.3d 790 (6th Cir. 1996), dated July 17, 1996,
Clerk Leonard Green of the Sixth Circuit advised that the
court would

                    appreciate your filing a response to the
          government’s petition for rehearing...In connection
          with your response to the first section of the
          government’s argument, the court will find
          particularly helpful your comment on the recent

v. Richards, 549 N.W.2d 218 (1996).

(See letter in Appendix C).

The Sixth Circuit had earlier rejected a blanket

ruie in Bates. In fact, the court found after examining

the specific circumstances of the case that an

informant’s report of the presence of a handgun, the fear

that cocaine would be destroyed, and the barricading of

the apartment door all together did not constitute

exigent circumstances. No decision has yet issued on the

government’s petition for rehearing.

The Bates situation is simply a foretelling example

of the floodgates of non-balancing that could open up if

this Court remains silent about Richards.  Courts in

Wisconsin are already not engaging in case-by-case

analysis of home entries in drug cases as a result of the

lower court ruling. For example, in Sta of t Wisce onsin

          The issue to be resolved is not whether the state
court can form an exception to the rule; it is whether
such exception can be blanket in nature. Wilson v.

Arkansas, 514 U.S. __, 115 S.Ct. 1914, 1919, 131 L.Ed.2d

976 (1995), stated that

          For now, we leave to the lower courts the task
of determining the circumstances under which an
unannounced entry is reasonable under the Fourth
Amendment.

According to the Wisconsin supreme court, this gives

license to the lower courts to establish a blanket rule

in drug cases. See State v. Richards, 201 Wis. 2d 839,

849, 549 N.W.2d 218 (1996):

         Wilson does not forbid blanket rules; rather,
it requires such rules to be supported by the
standard of "reasonableness." The Court left it to
the lower courts to determine "the circumstances
under which an unannounced entry is reasonable

under the Fourth Amendment." Wilson, 115 S. Ct. at

1919.

Thus, the Supreme Court should grant certiorari to

determine whether the Wisconsin court properly

interpreted, applied or extended Wilson.  See Oregon v.

                  SUNDBY, J. In this warranted drug search and seizure case,
defendant Steiney J. Richards relies on our decision in State v. Stevens, 173 Wis.2d
290, 496 N.W.2d 201 (Ct. App. 1992). Because the Wisconsin Supreme Court

                                                                                                                          No. 93-0391-CR

reversed our decision, State v. Stevens, No. 92-1557-CR (Wis. Feb. 8, 1994), and
created a “drug house exception to the “knock and announce” rule which applies
here, we affirm the judgment of conviction and the order denying Richards’ motion
to suppress evidence of drugs seized in the search of his motel room.

                  At 3:40 a.m. on December 31, 1991, police officers executed a warrant
to search Richards’ motel room. Richards does not claim that the police had failed
to establish probable cause to obtain a warrant to search his room for drugs. The sole
issue he raises is whether, when their attempt to gain entry through a ruse failed, the
police were required to comply with the “knock and announce” rule.

                  In Stevens, the Wisconsin Supreme Court adopted a “blanket” drug
house rule. Stevens, slip op. at 15. The court concluded that, “where the police
have a warrant to search the property, the residents retain only a very limited interest
in privacy. The warrant authorizes the police to enter the residence and search for
evidence pursuant to the search warrant.” Id. at 17. The court further explained that
“the societal interest in stopping the drug trade, combined with the police officers’
safety interest, outweigh{s] the occupants’ limited privacy interests. Id. at 18. The
Stevens court concluded that that privacy interest is protected by the probable cause
requirement which must be met before a warrant maybe issued to search a residence
for evidence of delivery of drugs or evidence of possession with intent to deliver
drugs. See id. at 9-10. When that probable cause requirement is met, the police may

                                               2.

                                                                                                                          No. 93-0391-CR

execute a warrant to search a private residence for drugs without first knocking and
announcing their presence and purpose.

                  Under the drug house exception, the officers’ search of Richards’
motel room was reasonable and did not violate his limited right of privacy.
