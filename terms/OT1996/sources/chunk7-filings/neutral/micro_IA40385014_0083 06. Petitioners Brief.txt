         QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW

     I. Does the Fourth Amendment, as interpreted by

Wilson v. Arkansas, 115 S.Ct. 1914 (1995), permit a court to

uphold police noncompliance with the rule of announce-
ment without looking to the particular circumstances of
the entry if the police have a warrant to search for drugs?

     II. Does the Fourth Amendment, as interpreted by

Wilson v. Arkansas, 115 S.Ct. 1914 (1995), permit a blanket

exception to “knock-and-announce” if drugs are the
object of a search warrant?

                        STATEMENT OF THE CASE
         A. Introduction

       The case involves Wisconsin’s drug case blanket

 exception to the Fourth Amendment’s knock-and-
 announce requirement of Wilson v. Arkansas, 115 S.Ct.
 1914 (1995).

       Prior to Wilson, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in

 State v. Stevens, 181 Wis. 2d 410, 511 N.W.2d 591 (1994),

 cert. denied, 115 S.Ct. 2245 (1995),) that the nature of the

 “drug culture” permitted Wisconsin police to dispense
 with the knock-and-announce requirement in drug cases.

 Id. at 425, 511 N.W.2d at 596. The Wisconsin court stated,
 in summary, id. at 424-25, 511 N.W.2d at 595:

            We hold that when the police have a search
      warrant, supported by probable cause, to search
      a residence for evidence of delivery of drugs or
      evidence of possession with intent to deliver
      drugs (evidence of drug dealing), they neces-
      sarily have reasonable cause to believe exigent

       circumstances exist. Thus, in all such searches

      the police are justified in dispensing with the
      rule of announcement and making a no-knock
      entry. (footnote omitted)
     Richards’ appeal arrived first at the Wisconsin Court
of Appeals where his conviction was affirmed based on
Stevens. (J.A. 73-75) This decision is unreported and
appears in the table at 183 Wis. 2d 429, 516 N.W.2d 19
(1994).

      ' Stevens was pending before this Court for several months,
and the Court denied certiorari eight days after Wilson was
decided.

      Petitioner requested review in the Wisconsin
 Supreme Court to determine whether Wilson affected its
 ruling in Stevens. The Wisconsin Supreme Court granted
 review and affirmed June 12, 1996, finding that the
 knock-and-announce exception for evidence of drug deal-

 ing was constitutional under Wilson. State v. Richards, 201

 Wis. 2d 839, 549 N.W.2d 218 (1996). (J.A. 76-109)

              SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

     I. “Physical entry into the home is the chief evil
against which the wording of the Fourth Amendment is
directed.” Therefore, the Fourth Amendment imposes the
highest scrutiny.

     Drug raids are significant events. Officers break
down the door with battering rams, and they sometimes
even throw in stun grenades. A multitude of police
swarm in wearing bulletproof vests, their weapons drawn
and pointed.

     The Wisconsin Supreme Court's view is based on a de
minimis view of this invasion of privacy, and this is fun-
damentally and constitutionally wrong. The privacy
interests in the knock-and-announce rule are much more
than slightly advanced by announcement, contrary to the
Wisconsin court's view.

      Il. Wilson v. Arkansas, 115 S.Ct. 1914 (1995), held

that the common law rule of announcement is a part of
the Fourth Amendment. The “underlying command of
the Fourth Amendment is always that searches and sei-
zures be reasonable.” Id. at 1916.

      The Wisconsin Supreme Court’s reasoning is based
on false assumptions of fact. Drug dealers are not better

                                          15

armed and trained for assault than officers. The lower
court relied on meaningless statistics regarding officer
deaths. In fact, in the past 14 years, only 23 officers died
in drug raids, none of whom died in Wisconsin. As many
citizens died from gunfire in the course of such raids.
Innocent persons suffer mistaken raids constantly, and
sometimes they die. There are no real remedies for such
police mistakes.

     A. There is a higher standard of privacy in the
           home.

     The resolution of the question in this case starts from
the premise that this case involves a dwelling, and dwell-
ings are entitled to the highest scrutiny under the Fourth
Amendment, which provides that “[t]he right of the peo-
ple to be secure in their . . . houses . . . shall not be
violated.” “(T]he sanctity of private dwellings [is] ordi-
narily afforded the most stringent Fourth Amendment
protection.” United States v. Martinez-Fuerte, 428 U.S. 543,
561 (1976).

     The maxim that “every man’s home is his castle” is
part of the common law and the Fourth Amendment.
“From earliest days, the common law drastically limited
the authority of law officers to break the door of a house
to effect an arrest.” Miller v. United States, 357 U.S. 301,

                                         16

306-7 (1957). See Wilson v. Arkansas, 115 S.Ct. 1914,
1916-17 (1995). The “right to maintain control over [one’s]
home, and to be free from governmental interference, is a
private interest of historic and continuing importance.”
United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, 510 U.S.
43, 53-54 (1993) (bracketed material added).

     Also, the Fourth Amendment protects more than just
privacy. As Justice Story stated in his commentaries, the
Fourth Amendment was designed to protect “personal
security, personal liberty and private property.” J. Story,
Commentaries On The Constitution Of The United States,
§ 1902 (2d ed.1851). See Soldal v. Cook County, 506 U.S. 56,
62 (1992) (the Fourth Amendment “protects property as
well as privacy”).

     It matters not that the place searched here was
merely a motel room; the Fourth Amendment protects
Room 138 at the Ramada Inn in Madison as much as it
protects a fashionable home in Georgetown or Beverly
Hills, or on Park Avenue in New York City. United States
v. Johnson, 333 U.S. 10 (1948); United States v. Jeffers, 342
U.S. 48 (1951); Stoner v. California, 376 U.S. 483 (1964). If it
has walls and people can stay there,'! it is protected by
the Fourth Amendment.

            6. Protection against mistakes.

     The knock-and-announce rule is intended to protect
against mistakes. In Ker v. California, 374 U.S. 23, 57-58
(1963), Justice Brennan, writing for four Justices concur-
ring and dissenting, said:

            Beyond these constitutional considerations,
      practical hazards of law enforcement militate
      strongly against any relaxation of the require-
      ment of awareness. First, cases of mistaken
      identity are surely not novel in the investigation
      of crime. The possibility is very real that the
      police may be misinformed as to the name or
     address of a suspect, or as to other material
      information. That possibility is itself a good rea-
      son for holding a tight rein against judicial

                                      34

      approval of unannounced police entries into pri-
     vate homes. Innocent citizens should not suffer
     the shock, fright or embarrassment attendant
     upon an unannounced police intrusion. Second,
     the requirement of awareness also serves to
     minimize the hazards of the officers’ dangerous
     calling. We expressly recognized in Miller v.
      United States, supra, 357 U.S., at 313, n. 12, that
     compliance with the federal notice statute “is
     also a safeguard for the police themselves who
     might be mistaken for prowlers and be shot
     down by a fearful householder.” Indeed, one of
     the principal objectives of the English require-
     ment of announcement of authority and purpose
     was to protect the arresting officers from being
     shot as trespassers,” *** for if no previous
     demand is made, how is it possible for a party
     to know what the object of the person breaking
     open the door may be? He has a right to con-
      sider it as an aggression on his private property,
      which he will be justified in resisting to the
      utmost.” Launock v. Brown, 2 B. & Ald. 592, 594,
      106 Eng. Rep. 482, 483 (1819).

           These compelling considerations underlie
      the constitutional barrier against recognition of
     exceptions not predicated on knowledge or
     awareness of the officers’ presence.

     The Wisconsin rule has conducted balancing of indi-
vidual liberty and the public need in advance, a priori,
without regard to the potential facts, and it has left indi-

vidual liberty wanting. Under the blanket exception, mis-
takes of the police are tolerable, no matter how grievous
they may be. Deference to the police cannot be this abso-

lute under our form of government.

Ill. DEFERENCE TO “SPECIAL NEEDS” OR “OPER-
       ATIONAL NECESSITIES” IN THIS AREA OF
       LAW IS BOOTSTRAPPING. THE ENTIRE PREM-
       ISE OF JUDICIAL REVIEW OF SEARCH AND
       SEIZURE QUESTIONS REQUIRES TRIAL AND
       APPELLATE ANALYSIS OF REASONABLENESS.

       A. Neither “special needs” nor “operational
             necessities” support the blanket rule.

             1. The holdings from this Court on “special
                   needs.”

     The court below did not mention this topic, but this
Court likely will, so we address it now: If the Court
adopts the blanket exception to knock-and-announce in
drug cases, it would also have to create a new exception
to the “special needs” exception to the probable cause
and warrant requirement for what amounts to a question
of execution of a search warrant.

     The Court first addressed the “special needs” excep-
tion in Fourth Amendment cases in New Jersey v. T.L.O.,
469 U.S. 340, 351 (1985) (Blackmun, J., concurring), where
the Court held that neither a warrant nor probable cause
was necessary to conduct a search of a student where
there was reasonable suspicion to believe that the student
was in possession of contraband, considering the unique
status that a school has with the children attending it.

           Only in those exceptional circumstances in
      which special needs, beyond the normal need
      for law enforcement, make the warrant and
      probable-cause requirement impracticable, is a
      court entitled to substitute its balancing of inter-
      ests for that of the Framers.

                                      36

     The Court has applied Justice Blackmun’s language
repeatedly in finding exceptions to the warrant require-
ment. See, e.g., Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868, 873 (1987)
(probationer searches); Skinner v. Railway Labor Executive's
Association, 489 U.S. 602, 620 (1989) (urine testing of rail-
road employees involved in accidents); Vernonia School

Dist. 47] v. Acton, 115 S.Ct. 2385, 2391 (1995) (urine testing
of student athletes).

     Only Griffin involved a dwelling, and the Court held
there that an informant’s tip that the probationer was in
possession of a weapon was a sufficient basis to authorize
a warrantless search under probation regulations requir-
ing reasonable grounds to believe that the probationer
was violating the law. Griffin, supra, at 875.

     In two other cases, the Court has mentioned “opera-
tional necessities” as a justification for a police seizure on
less than probable cause. See Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S.
321, 327 (1987):

           We do not say, of course, that a seizure can
      never be justified on less than probable cause.
      We have held that it can —- where, for example,
      the seizure is minimally intrusive and opera-
      tional necessities render it the only practicable
      means of detecting certain types of crime. See,

      e.g., United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411 (1981)

      (investigative detention of vehicle suspected to
      be transporting illegal aliens); United States v.

       Brignoni-Ponce, 422 U.S. 873 (1975) (same);
      United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696, 709, and n. 9
       (1983) (dictum) (seizure of suspected

                                      37

      drug dealer’s luggage at airport to permit expo-
     sure to specially trained dog). No special opera-
     tional necessities are relied on here, however -
     but rather the mere fact that the items in ques-
     tion came lawfully within the officer’s plain
      view. That alone cannot supplant the require-
     ment of probable cause.

             2. How do they apply? Can they apply?

     Here, the police obtained a warrant after satisfying a
judge that there was probable cause for a search. So, on
the one hand, probable cause has been shown. But, does
that still permit them to nullify a basic constitutional
protection and the requirement that all searches be rea-
sonable without showing any specific articulable facts
that they have a need to dispense with announcement?
(The phrases “operational necessities” and “special
needs” are seductive in this situation; but the Court
should not be tempted.)

     Remember that here a dwelling was involved, and
only one case permitted a search of a dwelling on less
than probable cause and with “special needs”: Griffin v.
Wisconsin where the probationer was already on the street
by the grace of the executive branch of state government
and under its complete control. These historical facts of
life of probationers made the use of a lesser standard of
probable cause permissible.

      For the “special needs” exception to apply, there has
to be some existing relationship between the person con-
ducting the search and the person to be searched. The
“special needs” exception has never been extended to a

                                      38

purely criminal search. If the Court does so here, “rea-
sonableness” will be a concept that will forever defy
definition. And, if “reasonableness” can no longer be
defined, then the courts will never be able to determine
whether particular police conduct is lawful. And, if that
happens, the “reasonableness” requirement of all
searches and police activity will have been virtually read
out of the law.

       B. Deference to the police is a dangerous propo-

                sition when knock-and-announce is at issue.

     The Wisconsin rule defers to the police the question
of reasonableness in drug searches. This is a dangerous
proposition that goes far beyond anything this Court has
ever approved. As the Court said in 1964, “If subjective
good faith alone were the test, the protections of the
Fourth Amendment would evaporate, and the people
would be ‘secure in their persons, houses, papers, and
effects’ only in the discretion of the police.” Beck v. Ohio,

379 U.S. 89, 97 (1964) (quoted in United States v. Leon, 468
U.S. 897, 915 n. 13 ((1984)) as a function belonging to a
neutral and detached magistrate). What the state has

done here gives the police absolute discretion to deter-
mine how a householder will give up his or her privacy
to a search warrant.

       C. Deference to the police and “blanket rules”
              are contrary to established law.

      The Wisconsin blanket rule is contrary to established
law and the genius of the reasonableness requirement.

                                       39

      This court has never sanctioned this kind of defer-
ence in Fourth Amendment cases. In United States v. Leon,
468 U.S. 897, 914 (1984), the Court held that even defer-
ence to magistrates is not boundless. Deference to police
is inappropriate because they are “ ‘engaged in the often
competitive enterprise of ferreting out crime,’ ” id. (quot-
ing United States v. Johnson, 333 U.S. 10, 14 (1948)).

            Even if the warrant application was sup-
      ported by more than a “bare bones” affidavit, a
      reviewing court may properly conclude that,
      notwithstanding the deference that magistrates
      deserve, the warrant was invalid because the
      magistrate’s probable cause determination
      reflected an improper analysis of the totality of
      the circumstances.

Id. at 915. In adopting the “totality of circumstances” test
for probable cause in Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 229

(1983), the Court held:

           Sufficient information must be presented to
      the magistrate to allow that official to determine
      probable cause; his action cannot be a bare rat-
      ification of the bare conclusions of others. In
      order to ensure that such an abdication of the
      magistrate’s duty does not occur, courts must
      continue to conscientiously review the suffi-
      ciency of affidavits on which warrants are
      issued.

See Malley v. Briggs, 475 U.S. 335 (1986) (officer may be
civilly liable for executing a warrant that could not justi-
fiably be relied on).

     As to the veracity of the officer applying for a war-
rant, the Court has said that the officer’s statement of

                                      40

probable cause is open to challenge for knowing or reck-
less falsity, holding that “a flat ban on impeachment of
veracity could denude the probable cause requirement of
all! meaning.” Franks v. Delaware, 438 U.S. 154, 155-56
(1978).

     Similarly, the Court has never held that the method
of conducting a search is beyond judicial review, either.
In Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 15 (1968), the Court held that

     courts still retain their traditional responsibility
      to guard against police conduct which is over-
      bearing or harassing, or which entrenches upon
      personal security without the objective evidenti-
      ary justification which the Constitution requires.
      When such conduct is identified, it must be
      condemned by the judiciary and its fruits must
      be excluded from evidence in criminal trials.

“[A] search which is reasonable at its inception may

violate the Fourth Amendment by virtue of its intolerable
intensity and scope . . . The scope of the search must be
‘strictly tied to and justified by’ the circumstances which
rendered its initiation permissible.” Id. at 17-19. The
Court sanctioned a limited patdown for weapons where a
reasonably prudent officer would be warranted in the
belief, based on “specific and articulable facts,” id. at 21,
and not on a mere “inchoate and unparticularized suspi-
cion or ‘hunch,’ ” id. at 27, “that he is dealing with an
armed and dangerous individual.” Id.

      Finally, “[t}he manner in which a warrant is executed
is subject to later judicial review as to its reasonableness.”

Dalia v. United States, 441 U.S. 238, 258 (1979). See also
Arizona v. Hicks, supra, reviewing an overintensive search.

                                   41

     Perhaps the closest case of all is Maryland v. Buie, 494
U.S. 325 (1990), the protective sweep case. There, the state
argued for a blanket rule, which this Court rejected. Id. at
334 n. 2. The Buie Court held that “specific and articulable
facts” demonstrating a potential danger to the officer
were required to be shown before officers could conduct
a protective sweep. Id. at 334, 336, 337. Buie rejected
essentially the same justification made here:

           The State’s argument that no level of objec-
      tive justification should be required because of
      “the danger that inheres in the in-home arrest
      for a violent crime,” Brief for Petitioner 23, is
      rebutted by Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), itself.
     The State argues that “[o]fficers facing the life
      threatening situation of arresting a violent crim-
     inal in the home should not be forced to pause
     and ponder the legal subtleties associated with a
     quantum of proof analysis,” Brief for Petitioner
     23. But despite the danger that inheres in on-
     the-street encounters and the need for police to
     act quickly for their own safety, the Court in
     Terry did not adopt a bright-line rule authoriz-
     ing frisks for weapons in all confrontational
      encounters. Even in high crime areas, where the
      possibility that any given individual is armed is
     significant, Terry requires reasonable, individu-
     alized suspicion before a frisk for weapons can
     be conducted. That approach is applied to the
     protective sweep of a house.

           We reject the State’s attempts to analogize
      this case to Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106

      (1977) (per curiam), and Michigan v. Summers,
      452 U.S. 692 (1981). The intrusion in Mimms -

     requiring the driver of a lawfully stopped vehi-

      cle to exit the car — was “de minimis,” 434 U.S., at

                                      42

      111. Summers held that a search warrant for a
     house carries with it the authority to detain its
     occupants until the search is completed. The
     State contends that this case is the “mirror
     image” of Summers and that the arrest warrant
     carried with it the authority to search for per-
     sons who could interfere with the arrest. In that
     case, however, the search warrant implied a
     judicial determination that police had probable
     cause to believe that someone in the home was
     committing a crime. Here, the existence of the
     arrest warrant implies nothing about whether
      dangerous third parties will be found in the
      arrestee’s house. Moreover, the intrusion in
     Summers was less severe and much less suscept-
     ible to exploitation than a protective sweep. A
      more analogous case is Ybarra v. Illinois, 444 U.S.
      85 (1979), in which we held that, although
     armed with a warrant to search a bar and bar-
     tender, the police could not frisk the bar’s
     patrons absent individualized, reasonable suspi-
     cion that the person to be frisked was armed
      and presently dangerous. Here, too, the reason-
     able suspicion standard - “one of the relatively
     simple concepts embodied in the Fourth
     Amendment,” United States v. Sokolow, 490 U.S. 1
      (1989) — strikes the proper balance between offi-
     cer safety and citizen privacy.

     The leading case?° on the so-called blanket rule in

knock-and-announce is People v. Gastelo, 67 Cal. 2d 586,
588-89, 63 Cal. Rptr. 10, 12, 432 P.2d 706, 708 (1967), where
Justice Traynor held that the particularity clause was also

violated by a blanket rule:

      20 2 Wayne R. LaFave, Search and Seizure § 4.8(d), at 611 (3rd
ed. 1996).

             Under the Fourth Amendment, a specific
       showing must always be made to justify any
      kind of police action tending to disturb the secu-
      rity of the people in their homes. Unannounced
       forcible entry is in itself a serious disturbance of
       that security and cannot be justified on a blanket
      basis. Otherwise the constitutional test of rea-
      sonableness would turn only on practical expe-
      diency, and the amendment’s primary safeguard
      - the requirement of particularity - would be
      lost. Just as the police must have sufficiently
      particular reason to enter at all, so must they
      have some particular reason to enter in the man-
      ner chosen.

(Illinois recently rejected a blanket rule under Wilson and
applicable state law. People v. Krueger, 1996 WL 732035, 65
U.S.L.W. 2443 (Ill. Dec. 19, 1996)).

     Wisconsin initially rejected the blanket rule in State v.
Cleveland, 118 Wis. 2d 615, 631 n. 16, 348 N.W.2d 512, 521

n. 16 (1984). The court said

           The officer must have knowledge of specific
      facts which indicate that this particular person
      will conduct himself or herself in this manner
      when confronted by police. In this case, how-
      ever, there was no information that the defen-
      dant was armed or had taken other security

       precautions.

Neither the Richards nor Stevens decisions explained the

legal principles upon which the blanket rule can comport

with the Fourth Amendment. Indeed, in Richards, supra,

at 849 n. 6, 549 N.W.2d at 521 n. 6, (J.A. 85) the court

states that its “holding is based on the assumption that
drug dealers will have weapons and pose a danger to

officers ... ” The Richards court also stated that “[p]olice

                                         44

officers face an unquantifiable risk of violence every time
they go into a house to execute a search warrant.” Id. at
850, 549 N.W.2d at 223 (J.A. 86). Therefore, the Wisconsin
Supreme Court has abandoned the knock-and-announce
rule based on “assumption[s]” that are in turn based on
risks that are “unquantifiable.”

       D. The Wisconsin rule subverts judicial review
             of reasonableness.

     It is the function of trial and appellate courts to
determine reasonableness in every case, and no case has
dispensed with it as this blanket rule does. Leon and Gates
show that deference to the magistrate is not boundless;
rather, judicial review enables the courts to determine
whether probable cause existed for the search. It is the
fundamental function of the courts to review executive
action under the separation of powers principle. Accord-
ingly, reasonableness is always subject to review. Wilson
v. Arkansas, supra, at 1919; Ornelas v. United States, 116
S.Ct. 1657, 1662 (1996).

     If this Court adopts Wisconsin’s position on the blan-
ket rule, Wilson and Buie become meaningless abstrac-
tions.

       E. The standard should be reasonable suspicion
              under Buie and “totality of the circumstances”
              on review

      The rules of Buie and Gates should apply: Officers
must be able to point to specific and articulable facts
which justify their entering without announcement under

                 45

any of the recognized exceptions to kiiock-and-announce.
The court can look to the “totality of the circumstances”
to see whether the police action was justified.

                      STATUTES INVOLVED

Wis. Stats. § 968.10 (1989-90):

       Searches and seizures; when authorized

            A search of a person, object or place may be
      made and things may be seized when the search
      is made:

             (1) Incident to a lawful arrest;

             (2) With consent;

             (3) Pursuant to a valid search warrant;

            (4) With the authority and within the
     scope of a right of lawful inspection;

             (5) Pursuant to a search during an autho-

      rized temporary questioning as provided in s.

      968.25; or

            (6) As otherwise authorized by law.

Wis. Stats. § 968.12 (1989-90):

       Search warrant

           (1) Description and issuance. A search
      warrant is an order signed by a judge directing a
      law enforcement officer to conduct a search of a
      designated person, a designated object or a des-
      ignated place for the purpose of seizing desig-
      nated property or kinds of property. A judge
      shall issue a search warrant if probable cause is
      shown.

            (2) Warrant upon affidavit. A search war-
      rant may be based upon sworn complaint or
      affidavit, or testimony recorded by a phono-
       graphic reporter or under sub. (3)(d), showing

                                  A2

probable cause therefor. The complaint, affidavit
or testimony may be upon information and
belief.

     (3) Warrant upon oral testimony.

      (c) Issuance. If the judge determines that
there is probable cause for the warrant, the

judge shall order the issuance of a warrant by
directing the person requesting the warrant to
sign the judge’s name on the duplicate original
warrant. In addition, the person shall sign his or
her own name on the duplicate original warrant.
The judge shall immediately sign the original
warrant and enter on the face of the original
warrant the exact time when the warrant was
ordered to be issued. The finding of probable
cause for a warrant upon oral testimony shall be
based on the same kind of evidence as is suffi-
cient for a warrant upon affidavit.

      (e) Contents. The contents of a warrant

upon oral testimony shall be the same as the
contents of a warrant upon affidavit.

      (f) Entry of time of execution. The person
who executes the warrant shall enter the exact
time of execution on the face of the duplicate
original warrant.

      (4) Location of search. A search warrant

may authorize a search to be conducted any-
where in the state and may be executed pur-
suant to its terms anywhere in the state.

Wis. Stats. § 968.13 (1989-90):

      Search warrant; property subject to seizure

             (1) A search warrant may authorize the
      seizure of the following:

             (a) Contraband, which includes without
      limitation because of enumeration lottery

     tickets, gambling machines or other gambling
      devices, lewd, obscene or indecent written mat-
      ter, pictures, sound recordings or motion picture
      films, forged money or written instruments and
      the tools, dies, machines or materials for making
     them, and controlled substances, as defined in s.
     961.01(4), and controlled substance analogs, as

      defined in s. 961.01(4m), and the implements for
      smoking or injecting them. Gambling machines

     or other gambling devices possessed by a ship-
     building business that complies with s. 945.095
     are not subject to this section.

           (b) Anything which is the fruit of or has
      been used in the commission of any crime.

            (c) Anything other than documents which
      may constitute evidence of any crime.

            (d) Documents which may constitute evi-

     dence of any crime, if probable cause is shown

      that the documents sre under the control of a

     person who is reasonably suspected to be con-

      cerned in the commission of that crime under s.
      939.05(2).

            (2) In this section, “documents” includes,

     but is not limited to, books, papers, records,
      recordings, tapes, photographs, films or com-
     puter or electronic data.

Wis. Stats. § 968.22 (1989-90):

      Effect of technical irregularities

            No evidence seized under a search warrant
      shall be suppressed because of technical irregu-
      larities not affecting the substantial rig of the
     defendant.

                              APPENDIX B
     U.S. Department of Justice, Uniform Crime Reports:
Law Enforcement Officers Killed and Assaulted (UCR
(year)), analysis of annual summaries of officer killings in
drug raids:

     In 1994, one officer died during a drug raid at a Los
Angeles Hotel. The officers knocked and announced, and
an assailant hiding under the bed shot the officer in the
course of the search. The SWAT team killed the assailant.
UCR (1994) 45.

     In 1993, two officers died in drug raids and one died
in a “buy-bust” situation. The drug raid killings were in
Stockton, California and Shreveport, Louisiana. In both
descriptions, the element of surprise had nothing to do
with the killing; the officers were killed well after
announcement by persons coming from inside rooms
away from the room where entry occurred. UCR (1993) at
41, 47. The “buy-bust” was in New York City, and the
summary says nothing about when or where the shooting
occurred in relation to a drug raid. Id. 49.

     In 1992, three officers died in drug raids. In Palm-
dale, CA. an officer was killed inside the house after
announcement. In E] Paso County, Colorado, the officer
was shot on entry. An officer in Shreveport was killed in a
drug raid where he was shot by a person hidden in a
bedroom. UCR (1992) 42, 47.

      In 1991, no officer died during a drug raid. UCR
(1991).

      In 1990, two officers were killed. In West Sacramento,
California an officer was shot pursuing a drug suspect to

                                         A6é

a bedroom after entry. An FBI agent was killed in St.
Louis, Missouri after opening a bedroom door inside; the
officer and his assailant shot simultaneously. UCR (1990)
23 & 30.

     In 1989, there were two deaths. In San Joaquin
County, California, the slain officer, standing at the back
window trying to divert the attention of the occupants,
was shot from inside. A Titusville, Florida officer was
shot entering a bedroom after the initial entry. UCR
(1989) 26, 27

     In 1988, there were three deaths during drug raids.
An officer in Los Angeles County, California was killed,
apparently on entry. An officer in Wichita, Kansas, was
killed after entry by a suspect hidden in kitchen. A Bos-
ton, Massachusetts officer was killed by a suspect shoot-
ing through the door after forcible entry was attempted

and failed (whether announcement occurred is not men-

tioned). UCR (1988) 24, 29, 31.

     In 1987, one officer died during a drug raid in Ft.
Pierce, Florida. He was killed after entry when he fired at
a suspect first and his gun jammed; the suspect returned
fire. UCR (1987) 25.

     In 1986, there were no officer deaths during drug

raids. UCR (1986).

     In 1985, there was one officer death during a drug
raid in Westville, New Jersey: an officer was shot dead by
a person hidden inside after police handcuffed the first

arrestee. UCR (1985) 33.

     In 1984, one officer was killed in a drug raid. The
attempted forcible entry in Longview, Texas failed

                                         A7

because the door was held by a chain. The assailant shot
through the opening. UCR (1984) 24.

      In 1983, five officers perished in drug raids. An offi-
cer in Townsend, Georgia, was stabbed in the back. A
Pine Lawn, Missouri officer announced his presence,
entered, and was shot. An Alameda, California officer
was shot by a suspect who was leaving through a win-
dow trying to escape. A Los Angeles, California, officer
was shot while entering through a window. In Pittsburgh,
Pennsylvania, an officer was shot during a raid where the
first officer entered by ruse and lost her gun to the
occupants. When the others tried to enter to get her, one
got shot. UCR (1983) 23, 24, 26, 27, 32.

     In 1982, one officer was killed during a drug raid in
San Joaquin County, California. There was announcement
and refusal, but the forced entry failed. The assailant

opened the door and shot him. UCR (1982) 26.

     In 1981, one officer was killed during a drug raid in
Tampa, Florida. After announcement and entry, there was

gunfire from the bedroom. UCR (1981) 28.

      The UCR (1995) will be published shortly.
