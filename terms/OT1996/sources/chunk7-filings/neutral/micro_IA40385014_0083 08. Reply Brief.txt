                              ARGUMENT

I. THE QUESTION OF REASONABLENESS OF AN
     UNANNOUNCED FORCIBLE ENTRY INTO THE
     HOME REQUIRES A PARTICULARIZED EVALUA-
     TION OF THE FACTS OF EACH CASE, NOT JUST
     PIGEONHOLING IT AS A DRUG DELIVERY
     CASE.

      A. Determining whether announcement is
            required requires a particularized evaluation of
            the circumstances in each case.

      Whether or not an announcement is required is a
reasonableness inquiry. Wilson v. Arkansas, 115 S.Ct. 1914,
1918, 1919 (1995). How a search is conducted is always a
reasonableness inquiry. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 18-19
(1968); Dalia v. United States, 441 U.S. 238, 258 (1979). In
the protective sweep case, Maryland v. Buie, 494 U.S. 325,
334, 336, 337 (1990), the Court held that specific and
articulable facts must be shown to authorize the police to
look for others present in a home. Buie “offers guidance
by analogy here” because it is the closest case on the law
and facts. Cf. Maryland v. Wilson, No. 95-1268 (Feb. 19,
1997) slip op. 4.

     In Whren v. United States, 116 S.Ct. 1769, 1776-77
(1996), the Court recognized balancing in the manner a
probable cause-based seizure of a vehicle was conducted.
A traffic stop is not “extraordinary” or “extreme” but an
unannounced entry in the home is:

      Where probable cause has existed, the only
      cases in which we have found it necessary actu-
     ally to perform the “balancing” analysis
     involved searches or seizures conducted in an
      extraordinary manner, unusually harmful to an
      individual's privacy or even physical interests -

       such as, for example, seizure by means of
      deadly force, see Tennessee v. Garner, 471 U.S. 1
       (1985), unannounced entry into a home, see
       Wilson v. Arkansas, 514 U.S. ___ (1995), entry into
      a home without a warrant, see Welsh v. Wiscon-
      sin, 466 U.S. 740 (1984), or physical penetration
      of the body, see Winston v. Lee, 470 U.S. 753
      (1985).

      This Term, in Ohio v. Robinette, 117 S.Ct. 417, 421

(1996), the Court reiterated that, when Fourth amend-

ment reasonableness is at issue, it

      is measured in objective terms by examining the
      totality of the circumstances.

            In applying this test we have consistently
      eschewed bright-line rules, instead emphasizing
      the fact-specific nature of the reasonableness
      inquiry. Thus, in Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491
      (1983), we expressly disavowed any “litmus
      paper test” or single “sentence or . . . paragraph

          . rule,” in recognition of the Candloas varia-
      tions in the facts and circumstances” implicating
      the Fourth amendment. Id., at 506. Then, in

      Michigan v. Chesternut, 486 U.S. 567 (1988), when

     both parties urged “bright-line rules applicable
      to all investigatory pursuits,” we rejected both
      proposed rules as contrary to our “traditional
     contextual approach.” Id., at 572-73. And again,

       in Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), when

      the Florida Supreme court adopted a per se rule
     that questioning aboard a bus always constitutes
      a seizure, we reversed, reiterating that the
     proper inquiry necessitates a consideration of
      “all the circumstances surrounding the encoun-
      ter” Id. at 493.

     Just five weeks before our argument, the Court
applied this principle in Maryland v. Wilson, supra, slip op.

3. The court engaged in a balancing of interests to deter-
mine whether ordering passengers out of a car during a
traffic stop was reasonable, and it concluded that it was.
Id. at 4-6. See point 1.B., infra.

     The fact remains, however, both sides can marshall
empirical evidence that supports their position. Police get
shot, and innocent people get shot. This probably will
continue no matter how this case comes out because
police and citizens are killed right now both with and
without announcement. But isn’t that the point? We are
dealing with a search that happens, in Justice Scalia’s
words from Whren, “in an extraordinary manner, unusu-
ally harmful to an individual’s privacy or even physical
interests.” Petitioner believes that his proposed rule will
save lives.

     Because guns are “tools of the trade,” Respondent
and amici assume that drug dealers with guns have them
just to shoot the police. Common sense suggests other-
wise; the police are not the victims of violence in the
cases they cite. Rather, the cases usually show that drug
dealers carry guns to protect their valuable stash from
those who would steal it or rob them of it.’ See, e.g.,

United States v. Sanchez-Barreto, 93 F.3d 17, 19 (Ist
Cir.1996); United States v. Singer, 943 F.2d 758, 762 (7th

Cir.1991) (inference of drug dealing found from posses-
sion of firearm and past threats); United States v. Golter,

880 F.2d 91, 93 (8th Cir.1989); United States v. Alvarez, 860
F.2d 801, 830 (7th Cir.1988) (gun “raised the inference that

the cocaine was valuable to Ms. Rivera and, therefore,

      1 Petitioner did not have a gun, and there is no suggestion
that officers thought would find one when they searched.

that it was worth protecting”); United States v. Sarda-Villa,
760 F.2d 1232, 1234 n. 1 (11th Cir.1985); State v. Williams,
168 Wis.2d 970, 977, 485 N.W.2d 42, 44 (1992) (“According
to the informant, Williams carried the gun because he had
previously been threatened by another drug dealer.”).? If
the police announce their presence, then it is always
going to be against the best interest of a drug dealer to
engage in a shoot-out, just as it is in the best interest of
police to avoid a shoot-out. The drug dealer foolish
enough to resist will almost invariably be shot or killed.
Balancing is mandated; too much is at stake for either
side to have a blanket or per se rule.

     The practical reality is that modern day police prac-
tices are such that the police bring overwhelming force to
virtually every search warrant scene and use it whether
or not announcement occurs. Here, there were apparently
eight to ten officers for an unarmed man in a motel room.
It is not surprising to see twenty or more officers show up
for a drug raid along with the SWAT Team; sometimes
there are a lot more. See, e.g., United States v. Ramirez, 91
F.3d 1297, 1299 (9th Cir.1996) (“45 armed officers”
showed up at 6:15 a.m. raid while occupants were
believed to be asleep). After all, the show of force is the
whole point.

     The Respondent suggests that Michigan v. Summers,
452 U.S. 692 (1981), is another example of a bright line
rule which ought to persuade this Court to ratify the
blanket exception adopted by the Wisconsin Supreme

      B. Maryland v. Wilson which authorizes the police
            to have passengers get out of a car during a
            traffic stop does not authorize a blanket rule in
            an unannounced entry of the home.

     The Respondent argues that Maryland v. Wilson
stands for the proposition that a bright line rule allowing
the police to enter a home with a search warrant and
without announcement is permissible under the Fourth
Amendment. Maryland v. Wilson permits a lone police
officer making a traffic stop to order passengers out of a
vehicle in order to protect the officer’s safety during the
stop. This Court determined that there is only one limited
change in circumstance occasioned by allowing police
officers this authority: The passengers “will be outside of,
rather than inside, the stopped car.” Id., slip op. 4. It is
this extremely limited intrusion which, when balanced
against the need for officer safety, permits this type of

      When the police en masse forcibly and unannounced
enter a home, the level of intrusion is overwhelmingly
and undeniably greater: Property is destroyed, the occu-
pants, including children, spouses and guests, are inten-
tionally frightened and intimidated by strange people
pointing guns at them and threatening to shoot them if
they even move.* Sometimes even stun grenades are
lobbed in before they enter in this paramilitary operation.
There are occupants who may be engaged in private
intimate activities who are suddenly confronted by
strangers waiving guns and shouting threats. Hall v. Ship-
ley, 932 F.2d 1147 (6th Cir.1991) (Hall was engaged in
intercourse with his girlfriend, and the officers in the
raiding party mistook the sounds of their lovemaking for
Hall attempting to flush drugs down the toilet; the drug
offense he was allegedly guilty of was so minor compared
to what he went through that he was not prosecuted).
What greater search of the home can be envisioned by
anyone?

     All too often, the police or the occupants feel threat-
ened enough to respond with violence. It is for this
reason that the Fourth Amendment has always provided
that the manner of entry is subject to special scrutiny.
Simply put, it is wrong, dead wrong, to equate a momen-
tary, public, non-violent detention at a roadside with a
violent forcible entry by the government into one’s home.

     * One innocent man, a preacher, died of a heart attack after

As stated in Petr.Br. at 15-17, the Fourth Amendment has
always provided greater protection inside the home than
anywhere else. No Fourth Amendment case has ever
drawn such a parallel, and this is not the place to start.®

     C. The government's alternative blanket rule does
            not comport with the Fourth Amendment. It is
            not workable.

     The United States agrees that an absolute blanket
rule does not comport with the Fourth Amendment's
reasonableness test. Its position is that where the police
possess specific knowledge that indicates there is no exi-
gency, an unannounced entry is not permitted. Where,
however, such knowledge is lacking, the “general experi-
ence” of law enforcement in drug cases militates in favor
of an unannounced entry. The government refers to this
as a “workable rule” (U.S.Br. 25). It is anything but, and
here is why:

      1. The police have no incentive to acquire or docu-
ment information which could be used to later demon-
strate that they illegally entered a home.

     2. Even where the police possess such information,
is the officer who has it required to communicate it to the
home invasion team executing the warrant? If such a
duty exists, and it is breached, what is the remedy?

     5 The position advocated by the Respondent prevents
citizens from obtaining judicial review of government's choice
regarding the manner in which homes are entered. Because the
home, unlike the roadside, is entitled the greatest amount of
Fourth Amendment protection there is no justification for
abandoning an aggrieved citizen’s right to such review.

     3. It is unclear what level of knowledge is required
before the police are required to knock and announce. It
is reasonable suspicion, probable cause, or some other
standard?

     4. Often search warrant information is developed
through the use of a confidential informant. There may be
no ability for the defense to learn the identity of such an
informant, and all the information he possesses, because
of the informer identity privilege. See, e.g., § 905.10, Wis.
Stats.; Unif.R.Evid. 509.

     5. The government’s position does not clarify
whether there is a requirement that the police acquire
information regarding the need for announcement -
assuming such information is available. If there is a duty,
what is the remedy if it is breached?

     6. Who has the burden of going forward and bur-
den of proof at a suppression hearing when lack of
announcement during execution of a warrant is at issue?

     D. The law enforcement community is not in
            agreement that a blanket rule is appropriate or
            necessary.

     Amicus Attorney General of Ohio, speaking for the
attorneys general of 26 states, Guam, and Puerto Rico
has, like the United States, opposed an absolute blanket
rule. The attorneys general state (Ohio Br. 14):

      The harder question is whether the proper result
      here — noncompliance with the announcement
      rule — invariably applies when police officers
      execute felony drug search warrants. The
      answer is an unqualified no. Normally the gen-
      eralized peril to officers conducting such

                                       9

      searches together with the risk of destruction of
      evidence will give them discretion to abandon
      the niceties of knocking and announcing their
      presence. But, because easily imagined situa-
      tions exist in which objective evidence will sub-
      stantially (if not entirely) diminish these
      dangers, it is not appropriate to establish a cate-
      gorical no-knock rule.

      Even more significant, we submit, are the states that
joined Ohio in its brief. The Attorney General of Mary-
land who advocated for a blanket rule in Maryland v.
Wilson joined in Ohio’s opposition to a blanket rule
involving no-knock execution of search warrants of the
home. The Attorney General of Arkansas, who wanted a
blanket rule two years ago in Wilson v. Arkansas, now has
decided against asking for one. It should also be noted
that Nebraska, which has had a blanket rule for many
years; see State v. Meyer, 311 N.W.2d 520, 522 (Neb.1981);
now opposes such a rule.

      Although the Attorneys General of 26 states and the
Solicitor General acknowledged that the execution of
search warrants in drug cases poses some threat to the
safety of law enforcement officers and citizens, citing
statistics (Ohio Br. 10-12; U.S.Br. 15-17), they do not make
the quantum leap to the blanket rule that the Respondent
does.

     There are many examples of situations where the
need to knock and announce will always be present. For
example: a residence where only children are known to
be home; a home not occupied by anyone suspected of
drug trafficking because the suspected drug dealer is

already incarcerated®; a search warrant executed at the

side (as was attempted here); or a controlled delivery by
Federal Express, U.P.S., or the post office delivered by
subterfuge to an unsuspecting home owner.

 IV. THE BROAD VIEW.

       What is the potential cost of Respondent's position in
 constitutional and human terms?

      In constitutional terms, the sanctity of the home will
 have been subordinated to this overwhelming need of
 Respondent to get drugs off the street. The “war on
drugs,” however, should not degenerate into a war on
individual liberty. When common law and the Fourth
Amendment rule of announcement can be classified as
mere ceremony that can cost law enforcement officers
their lives, individual liberty suffers.

      In human terms, Respondent's fear of police shoot-
ings, however, never even considers the equally weighty
risk that citizens will get shot, too. We agree that losing

just one police officer’s life is one too many; we just want

the Court to recognize that losing one innocent citizen's

life is one too many, too.

      Dispensing with announcement will cost lives when
homeowners defend themselves from the heavily-armed
unknown intruders. Gunfire will erupt, bullets will fly,
and there is nothing we can do to stop it. All we can do is

pray that police officers will make the proper decision to

announce or not before they go in based on the facts
known to them at the time and not simply based on the
class of crime involved. When they stand there at the
threshold, they should pause and think and balance the
facts they have with the need for announcement. Their
lives and the lives of those inside could well depend on

it.

     Drugs have severely harmed our national fabric, but
not just in the lives wasted from ingesting them or prison

                            20

sentences meted out. Drugs should not become a thumb
on the scales of justice that exalts law enforcement inter-
ests over individual liberty and takes balancing out of the
constitutional equation. That is just what this blanket rule
does, and that is why it must be rejected.
