of the overall system of free, local broadcast television 
(nationally or in particular markets), the First Amend- 
ment allows Congress to impose on cable operators a 
requirement of mandatory carriage of local broadcast 
Stations in preference to all other programmers.

_ STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


The history of this case is explained comprehensively 
in Justice Kennedy’s opinion for the Court in Turner

Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 114 S. Ct. 2445, 
2451-56 (1994) (“Turner”). Appellants represented on 
this brief are providers of cable programming services— 
commonly known as cable program networks—who dis- 
seminate a diverse array of video speech almost exclusively 
through means of cable television systems.’ Appellants 
initiated this action in 1992, challenging the constitution- 
ality of the “must-carry” provisions of Sections 4 and 5 
of the Cable Act, 47 U.S.C. §§ 534 & 535. The must- 
carry provisions reduce the opportunities for cable pro- 
grammers to speak by requiring cable operators to devote 
up to one third or more of their systems’ channel capacity 
to carriage of local television broadcast stations. A three- 
judge panel of the district court, by a 2-1 vote, upheld 
the challenged provisions.* Upon review, this Court va- 
cated that judgment and remanded the case for further 
proceedings. On remand, the district court, by a split 
decision, again sustained the provisions.’ 


By a 5-4 vote, this Court found in Turner that the 
must-carry provisions burden the fully protected speech 
rights of cable programmers and cable operators, and 
held that the appropriate standard by which to assess the 
provisions is the “intermediate” level of First Amendment 
scrutiny set forth in United States v. O’Brien, 391 U.S.

A. The Evidence on Remand. 


As directed by the plurality opinion, the record now 
contains detailed information regarding the viability of 
broadcast television in the absence of must-carry; the 
actual effects of must-carry on the speech of cable pro- 
grammers and cable operators; and the availability and 
efficacy of less restrictive alternatives to the existing must- 
carry regime. 

1. The Health of Local Broadcasting. 


The record shows that there was no “disease to be 
cured” by Congressional intervention. Even without 
must-carry, cable operators voluntarily carry virtually all 
broadcast stations with any measurable viewing in non- 
cable households. The broadcast system was thriving and 
grew markedly during the period without must-carry. In 
fact, commercial broadcasters were so confident of their 
ability to secure voluntary cable carriage that most opted 
to demand compensation for retransmission of their sig- 
nals rather than utilize must-carry.

2. The Harm to Cable Programmers and Cable Opera- 
tors Inflicted by Must-Carry. 


The evidence developed on remand shows that channel 
capacity on cable systems remains scarce; that must-carry 
Stations occupy a significant portion of that capacity, 
thereby reducing the number of channels available for 
carriage of cable network programming; and that numer- 
ous cable programmers have been and are continuing to 
be displaced from carriage and foreclosed from dissem- 
inating speech to their intended audiences on thousands

4. The Decision Below. 


The three-judge district court upheld the must-carry 
provisions. Judge Sporkin concluded that defendants were 
entitled to summary judgment. Circuit Judge Williams 
dissented, concluding that summary judgment should have 
been entered for plaintiffs. Judge Jackson, in a separate 
concurrence, opined that a trial was recessary, but joined 
in the result reached by Judge Sporkin to avoid “prevail- 
[ing] by stalemate.” ” 


Notwithstanding this Court’s instruction to ascertain 
whether “the must-carry rules are necessary to protect the 
viability of broadcast television” (Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 
2471), Judge Sporkin concluded that “{t}he relevant in- 
quiry is whether the health of those broudcasters protected 
by the must-carry provisions would be in jeopardy with- 
out the provisions.”™ After thus recasting Congress’ in- 
terest, Judge Sporkin found, based largely on evidence 
already found deficient by the plurality in Turner, that a 
“ ‘significant’ number of broadcast stations would be re- 
fused carriage” absent the must-carry provisions, id. at 
13a, and that broadcast stations denied cable carriage 
would be harmed. /d. at 15-18a. In his evaluation of 
the “actual effects” of must-carry, Judge Sporkin dis- 
missed the evidence that must-carry has foreclosed pro- 
grammer speech from millions of viewers, because he 
found that technological advances will, at some point, 
increase the channel capacity of cable systems, “ ‘thereby 
rendering must-carry a problem of the past.’” /d. at 18- 
19a, 21-22a (quoting Turner, 819 F. Supp. at 54 n.5). 
Finally, Judge Sporkin summarily dismissed as “less effec- 
tive” the more narrowly tailored alternatives offered as 
evidence of the “availability and efficacy of ‘constitution- 
ally acceptable less restrictive means’ ” (Turner, 114 S. Ct. 
at 2472) (internal citation omitted ).

A. Unlike the first time this case was before this 
Court, the Court is now able to assess “the actual effects 
of must-carry on the speech of cable operators and cable 
programmers.” Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 2472. And, as the 
post-implementation evidence dramatically shows, con- 
cerns prompting challenges to this legislation were not 
merely theoretical. The must-carry provisions have, in 
fact, significantly constrained the editorial choices of cable 
operators and shut down avenues of communication for 
cable programmers. In many major markets, where there 
are typically more than a dozen broadcast television 
stations, the effect of must-carry has been particularly 
severe. In virtually all markets, the challenged provisions

CONCLUSION 


At bottom, we think that this remains a simple case 
that turns on a handful of facts. Congress’ recited harms 
remain merely conjectural, not real. See Edenfield, 113 
S. Ct. at 1800. The remedy chosen to solve this imaginary 
problem is horribly overbroad. If there is a problem here, 
or one later develops, other more tailored solutions remain 
available. These must-carry provisions represent special 
interest legislation, not public interest legislation, and 
should be struck down.
