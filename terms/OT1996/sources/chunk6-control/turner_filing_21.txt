SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The must-carry provisions of the Act require cable op- 
erators to set aside a limited portion of their channel 
capacity to ensure access for local television broadcasters. 
In affirming that these provisions were content-neutral 
laws subject to only intermediate scrutiny under United 
States v. O’Brien, 391 U.S. 367 (1968), this Court held 
that “Congress’ overriding objective in enacting must- 
carry was not to favor programming of a particular subject 
matter, viewpoint or format, but rather to preserve access 
to free television programming for the 40 percent of 
Americans without cable.” Turner Broadcasting System, 
Inc. v. FCC, 114 S. Ct. 2445, 2461 (1994) (“Turner 
I”). As the Court held, Congress imposed these require- 
ments based on its assessment that cable had achieved 
bottleneck control over video programming access to most 
American households, and was likely to exploit that con- 
trol in ways inimical to broadcasters, who depended on 
cable for access to most of their potential viewers. /d. at 
2454-55, 2466. Without must-carry, Congress concluded, 
significant numbers oi stations would likely be denied 
carriage, Act at §2(a)(15), and stations so affected 
would likely deteriorate or fail, to the substantial detri- 
ment of the over 90 million Americans who depend on 
free over-the-air broadcasting. 


The Court remanded for the limited purposes of deter- 
mining whether Congress’ predictive judgments were based 
on reasonable inferences drawn from substantial evidence, 
and assessing whether in actual operation must-carry was 
narrowly tailored. The Court made clear that review on 
remand was to be deferential. The district court was not 
to reweigh the evidence de novo or replace congressional 
predictions with judicial ones. Turner ], 114 S. Ct. at 
2471. The district court faithfully followed this Court's 
remand instructions, determined there was “substantial 
evidence” supporting Congress’ “predictive judgments” re- 
garding the need for must-carry, and found the “actual 


3 


effect” of must-carry on the cable industry was so de 
minimis that “if the burden to the cable industry was 
much smaller, then the First Amendment would not even 
be implicated.” Jurisdictional Statement Appendix 7a, 
13a n. 22, and 22a (December 21, 1995 (“J.S. App.”)). 


The evidence on these points is not only substantial, 
but overwhelming. The record in the district court in- 
cluded: 


* More than 30,000 pages of the record before 
Congress, which showed Congress’ careful exami- 
nation of the cable and broadcast industries, and 
contained ample evidence supporting Congress’ 
conclusions; 

* The testimony of distinguished economists and ex- 
perts, who supported Congress’ factual conclusions 
and concluded that Congress’ judgments and pre- 
dictions were entirely reasonable; and 

* Testimony and documents from the cable and 
broadcasting industries that supplemented the Con- 
gressional record and demonstrated the complete 
lack of any significant harm to cable systems and 
programmers from must-carry. 

The Harm to Broadcasting. Because any significant 
decrease in, or harm to, existing broadcast sources would 
threaten Congress’ “overriding” objective, this Court asked 
whether there was substantial evidence supporting Con- 
gress’ predictive judgments that significant numbers of 
Stations would be denied carriage absent must-carry, and 
that broadcasters so affected would suffer financial diffi- 
culties as a result. The district court correctly concluded 
that the answer to these questions was yes. Even cable’s 
expert concluded that by 1992, cable operators were 
already refusing to carry 19-31% of all broadcast sta- 
tions, including network affiliates, and were refusing to 
carry 39% of all UHF independents. Moreover, Congress 
found. and the evidence on remand confirmed, that the 
cable industry had increasing incentives to deny carriage 


4 


to broadcast stations. And there is no serious dispute 
that stations denied carriage suffer financial difficulties. 
Indeed, cable industry documents candidly acknowledge 
that cable carriage is crucial to the viability of broadcast 
stations. 


Appellants and the dissent below contend that this 
massive record can be read differently to support their 
argument that must-carry was unnecessary. That evidence 
about complex and rapidly evolving industries is subject 
to differing interpretations is not surprising, but for pur- 
poses of this case, is irrelevant. As the court below 
correctly observed (J.S. App. 6a), even if appellants’ 
interpretation of the record were the better one (and most 
emphatically it is not), the question is not whether Con- 
gress was right or wrong, but whether its judgment was 
supported by “substantial” evidence and whether it made 
“reasonable” predictions based on that evidence. Any 
more searching inquiry would violate this Court’s remand 
instructions and would substitute judicial judgments for 
Congress’ economic and social policy judgments. 


Appellants argue that cable operators would volun- 
tarily carry perhaps 70% of the existing broadcast sta- 
tions, including the popular, “mainstream” broadcast 
stations that attract the great majority of viewers. That 
argument is factually wrong, but the more important 
point is that it is irrelevant once the federal interest in 
maintaining broadcast diversity is squarely acknowledged. 
Plainly, a significant reduction in the number of broad- 
cast sources available to noncable homes would threaten 
the federal interest in ensuring that noncable homes have 
access to a “multiplicity” of “diverse” broadcast sources, 
even if the broadcast industry, as a whole, would survive. 


Actual Effects. This Court also asked the lower court to 
determine the “actual effects” of must-carry on cable 
operators and programmers. Although appellants in 
Turner I made extravagant claims that must-carry in fact 
occupied 30-40% of cable channel capacity (claims on 
which the dissenting Justices relied), only 7.19% of 


5 


cable capacity was used for must-carry, and only 1.18% 
was used to add stations cable systems were not already 
carrying. Moreover, because cable systems were able to 
use previously unused capacity to add those stations, only 
5.5% of the nation’s cable systems had to drop even a 
single programmer for any period of time. Nationwide, 
cable systems were able to continue carrying 99.8% of the 
programming they were carrying before must-carry was 
enacted. Cable programmers likewise have faced minimal 
burdens. The appellant programmers each alleged injury 
on less than 1% of the nation’s cable systems as a result 
of must-carry. All of the appellant cable programmers 
substantially increased their subscribership since enact- 
ment of must-carry. Even these de minimis effects are 
rapidly vanishing as cable channel capacity continues to 
increase. Every six months, aggregate cable capacity in- 
creases by an amount greater than the fotal capacity 
presently used for must-carry stations. Plainly, these iso- 
lated and de minimis effects are insufficient to sustain 
appellants’ nationwide facial challenge. 


Furthermore, the regulatory alternatives appellants pro- 
pose would not maintain a multiplicity of broadcast 
sources nearly as effectively as the means Congress chose. 
Thus, they provide no basis for invalidating must-carry 
under O’Brien. Leased access, in particular, is not a viable 
alternative. Requiring broadcasters to pay for access 
would affirmatively obstruct Congress’ “overriding” ob- 
jective of preserving a multiplicity of diverse broadcast 
programming. Indeed, Congress considered and rejected 
the option that broadcasters pay for access, and did so 
precisely for this reason. 


Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed.

ARGUMENT 


I. THE QUESTION IN THIS APPEAL IS A NARROW 
ONE: WHETHER MUST-CARRY REASONABLY 
FURTHERS THE CONTENT-NEUTRAL GOAL OF 
PRESERVING THE EXISTING STRUCTURE OF 
BROADCASTING. 


At the outset, it is important to make clear what is and 
is not at issue in this appeal. All that remains at issue is 
whether must-carry is a reasonably tailored means to 
achieve a goal this Court already determined to be both 
substantial and content-neutral: “preserv[ing] the existing 
structure of the Nation’s broadcast television medium.” 
Turner I, 114 S. Ct. at 2464. This Court already held 
that this goal is “unrelated to the content of speech” 
because Congress aimed at “preserv[ing] access to free 
television programming for the 40 percent of American 
households without cable,” irrespective of the content of 
that programming. /d. at 2461. This Court also held that 
Congress’ interest in preserving the broadcast structure 
for noncable households is “not only a permissible govern- 
mental justification, but an important and substantial 
federal interest.” /d. at 2461 (quotation omitted). Ac- 
cordingly, it is settled that “the appropriate standard by 
which to evaluate the constitutionality of must-carry is 
the intermediate level of scrutiny applicable to content- 
neutral restrictions that impose an incidental burden on 
speech.” Id. at 2469.° 


Despite these clear holdings, appellants seek to reliti- 
gate the applicable standard of scrutiny and to rewrite 
Congress’ goals in enacting must-carry. Employing the 
entirely inapposite language of strict scrutiny, appellant 
NCTA argues that must-carry is “not necessary to assure

the economic viability of the broadcast system as a whole,”

governs this case. 


Likewise, appellant NCTA asserts that must-carry’s 
“discriminat[ion’ in favor of local broadcast stations at 
the expense of cable programmers is presumptively un- 
constitutional.” NCTA Br. at 15 (emphasis added). But 
the Court already flatly rejected this assertion when it was 
couched as a direct plea for strict scrutiny. Turner I, 114 
S. Ct. at 2460, 2461 (must-carry distinguishes between 
cable and broadcast speakers based “upon the manner in 
which speakers transmit their messages to viewers, and 
not upon the messages they carry”; “speaker distinctions

Amendment”) (emphasis added). As the Court held 
in Leathers v. Medlock, “a differential burden on speak- 
ers is insufficient by itself to raise First Amendment con- 
cerns.” 499 U.S. 439, 452 (1991). Must-carry does not 
seek to enhance the persuasive power of broadcasters or 
diminish that of cable programmers.* It regulates tech- 
nological and economic relationships that have nothing to 
do with the content of speech. 


For these reasons, appellants’ barely concealed efforts 
to substitute full-bore strict scrutiny for this Court’s clear 
instructions in Turner I are unavailing. The issue is not

“Congress has drawn reasonable inferences based on sub-

the substantial and content-neutral goal of preserving the 
structure of the broadcasting system. 114 S. Ct. at 2471 
(emphasis added). This standard “is not a license to re- 
weigh the evidence de novo, or to replace Congress’ 
factual predictions.” Id. 


Appellants also seek to redefine the government interest 
underlying must-carry. This Court held that “Congress’ 
overriding objective” was “maintaining the local broad- 
casting structure” in order to preserve the level of broad- 
casting diversity established through the “system of alloca- 
tion” created by Section 307(b) of the Communications 
Act of 1934. Turner I, 114 S. Ct. at 2461, 2469. That 
“system of allocation,” Congress noted, was designed to 
provide a “fair, efficient and equitable” distribution of 
broadcast services, and, as the Court recognized, must- 
carry was enacted in order to “maintain[]” it. Act, § 2(a) 
(9); 114 S. Ct. at 2469. By “preserv[ing] the existing 
structure of the Nation’s broadcast television medium,” 
Congress sought to ensure that the 90 million Americans 
without cable would continue to have access to “a multi- 
plicity of information sources.” Id. at 2464, 2470. The 
point of must-carry, therefore, is to prevent any material 
erosion of the multiplicity of broadcast programming 
sources available to noncable households. 


Rather than confront this government interest directly, 
appellants (following the lead of the dissent below) seek 
to set up straw interests they find easier to challenge.

First, they suggest that must-carry is justifiable only if 
the entire broadcast television industry would have col- 
lapsed without it. To the extent this is an argument that 
Congress would not have acted to prevent any lesser 
threat than the collapse of broadcasting, the argument is 
simply wrong. Along with must-carry Congress enacted a 
“retransmission consent” provision, which allows broad- 
casters to forego mandatory access in exchange for the 
right to compensation for their signals. See 47 U.S.C. 
§ 325(b). Because only broadcasters confident of cable 
carriage will opt for retransmission consent, and because 
Congress expected many broadcasters to pursue this op- 
tion, it cannot be that Congress expected the entire broad- 
cast industry to collapse without must-carry. Congress 
obviously knew that many broadcasters would survive 
without must-carry, but enacted must-carry to protect the 
many who might not. 


Appellants do ultimately acknowledge that Congress 
“justified must-carry as vital to the preservation of the 
allocation system created by Congress and administered 
by the FCC for the benefit of non-cable viewers.” TW 
Br. at 28. They argue, however, that the system of allo- 
cation seeks to protect only “access to a minimum amount 
of television broadcast services,” and cable operators would 
carry that amount voluntarily. TW Br. at 28 (emphasis 
in original). That argument misapprehends a half-century 
of national communications policy. The system of alloca- 
tion, created in the 1927 Radio Act, continued in the 
Communications Act of 1934, and embodied since 1952 
in the FCC’s Table of Allotments, seeks to ensure that 
every community has at least one broadcast outlet for 
local programming, and that every community receives as 
many diverse programming sources as the broadcast spec- 
trum will permit and economic conditions will sustain.* 


*The FCC’s Table of Allotments assigns television frequencies

Second, appellants (again tracking the dissent) alter- 
natively posit the straw argument that Congress’ over- 
riding objective was a mini-Sherman Antitrust Act for the 
cable industry. They then argue at length that cable’s 
conduct toward broadcasters is not predatory in the Sher- 
man Act sense, and that Congress misjudged cable’s 
economic incentives. Again, this extended foray is simply 
beside the point. Even if appellants were correct that 
cable carriage decisions have not generally risen to the 
level of Sherman Act violations, O’Brien surely does not 
justify judicial second-guessing of Congress’ judgment 
about what economic behavior to categorize as anti- 
competitive. The First Amendment does not limit Con- 
gress to Sherman Act standards of allocative efficiency 
when it regulates marketplace activity. 


Furthermore, this is not and has never been merely an 
antitrust case. The Communications Act of 1934 did not 
provide only for allocative efficiency in broadcasting; to 
the contrary, it seeks a “fair, efficient and equitable dis- 
tribution of broadcast services,” as Congress expressly 
noted in the 1992 Act. §$2(a)(9) (emphasis added). 


12 


The equitable distribution of broadcast services has always 
been at the heart of federal communications policy. Con- 
gress’ overriding concern in enacting must-carry was a 
threat to “the allocation system . . . established by Con- 
gress and the FCC.” H.R. Rep. No. 628, 102d Cong., 
2nd Sess. 52 (June 29, 1992); CR VOL. LA., Exh. 4, 
CR 00431 (emphasis added). Congress sought not only 
to ensure broadcasters a fair opportunity to compete for 
cable viewers, but, more importantly, to “ensur[e] that 
[the] local broadcast system will continue to thrive and 
that a broad array of views and programs from diverse, 
competitive and antagonistic sources is available to all 
television households, whether or not they subscribe to 
cable service.” /d. at 67. That interest is threatened 
whether or not cable’s noncarriage of local broadcasters 
would violate the Sherman Act. Even if a cable operator’s 
decision to carry a cable programmer rather than a broad- 
caster could be said to promote allocative efficiency in 
the market for video information as a whole, Congress 
was still entitled to conclude that such decisions impaired 
the “fair” and “equitable” distribution of broadcasting to 
the 40% of (generally less affluent) noncable households 
who depend on free television. 


Once appellants’ diversionary tactics and the dissent’s 
elaborate excursion into inapposite antitrust economics 
are properly put to one side, the critical issue in this case 
and the facts that bear on its resolution are remarkably 
simple: Did Congress draw reasonable inferences, sup- 
ported by substantial evidence, that must-carry would 
directly advance its goal of preserving the broadcast allo- 
cation system? The record elaborated on remand—con- 
sisting of the voluminous evidence before Congress (which 
alone is sufficient), substantial additional testimony by 
distinguished experts, and other evidence—makes clear 
that the district court properly answered this question yes.

Il. THE RECORD CONTAINS SUBSTANTIAL EVI- 
DENCE SUPPORTING CONGRESS’ REASONABLE 
JUDGMENT THAT MUST-CARRY WAS NEEDED 
TO MAINTAIN THE MULTIPLICITY OF BROAD-

HOUSEHOLDS. 


There is no dispute that cable operators exercise bottle- 
neck “control over most (if not all) of the television pro- 
gramming that is channeled into the subscriber’s home. 
. . . A cable operator, unlike speakers in other media, 
can thus silence the voice of competing speakers with a 
mere flick of the switch.” Turner I, 114 S. Ct. at 2466- 
67. See also Hurley v. Irish-American Gay Group of 
Boston, 115 §. Ct. 2338, 2349 (1995). 


Because cable now serves at least 60 percent of Ameri- 
can households, cable operators indisputably have the 
power to deny local television broadcasters access to most 
of their potential audience. Similarly, it is beyond dispute 
that must-carry would constitute an effective response to 
cable’s exploitation of that power against broadcasters, 
for it addresses the problem at its source. The remaining 
question, therefore, is limited to whether Congress reason- 
ably inferred that cable operators would exploit their 
bottleneck control with sufficient frequency to threaten 
the existing multiplicity of broadcast sources achieved 
through the “system of allocation.” 


Congress’ conclusion that there was such a threat rests 
on economic policy judgments and empirical assessments 
that warrant substantial deference. As the Court recog- 
nized, Congress “is far better equipped than the judiciary 
to amass and evaluate the vast amounts of data bearing 
upon an issue as complex and dynamic as that presented 
here.” Turner I, 114 S. Ct. at 2471 (quotations omit- 
ted); accord Walters v. National Ass'n of Radiation Sur- 
vivors, 473 U.S. 305, 331 n.12 (1985); see also Lopez v. 
United States, 115 S. Ct. 1624, 1628 (1995) (Breyer, J., 
dissenting). Deference is particularly warranted because 
Congress was making predictive judgments regarding the 


14 


complex relationships between rapidly changing indus- 
tries. See FCC v. National Citizens Comm. for Broad- 
casting, 436 U.S. 775, 813 (1978); Columbia Broad- 
casting Sys., Inc. v. Democratic Nat'l Comm., 412 US. 
94, 102-03 (1973). Thus, this Court need only satisfy 
itself that “Congress has drawn reasonable inferences 
based on substantial evidence.” Turner I, 114 S. Ct. at 
2471. Substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence as 
a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a 
conclusion.” Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 
U.S. 197, 229 (1938). So long as that standard is satis- 
fied, summary judgment is appropriate whether or not the 
evidence is in conflict or experts disagree. 


To test the reasonableness of Congress’ judgments, the 
Court directed deferential inquiry into the following ques- 
tions (114 S. Ct. at 2469, 2471): 


* whether absent must-carry “significant numbers of 
broadcast stations will be refused carriage on cable 
systems” 

* whether “broadcasters so affected would suffer 
financial difficulties as a result,” or “would be at 
serious risk of financial difficulty,” or would “either 
pare to a substantial degree or fail alto- 
gether” 


As will be shown, the district court correctly held there 
was substantial evidence supporting Congress’ judgments 
on these questions. Appellants are thus forced to take 
issue with the Court’s prior conclusion that evidence of 
“significant numbers” of carriage refusals would ade- 
quately support Congress’ decision to impose must-carry. 
First, they argue that the overall health of broadcasting 
from 1985 through 1991, when no must-carry require- 
ments were in effect, precludes any inference that must- 
carry was needed to sustain the broadcast system “as a 
whole.” Second, they argue that it does not matter whether 
“significant numbers” of stations would be denied car-

riage, because the stations denied carriage are not “sig- 
nificant stations.” 

In making these arguments, appellants frankly invite 
this Court to reweigh the evidence, to substitute judicial 
predictions for those made by Congress. and to make 
substantive policy choices about how much and what 
kind of broadcast programming noncable households 
should receive—in short, to engage in precisely the kind 
of Lochner-style review this Court previously disavowed in 
Turner I.

Fox, 492 U.S. 469, 478 (1989). Judicial disagreement

A. Must-Carry Does Not Substantially Burden the 
Programming Choices of Cable Operators, Or the 
Nationwide Speech Opportunities of Cable Pro- 
grammers. 


Must-carry self-evidently has the potential to “reduce 
the number of channels over which cable operators exer- 
cise unfettered control” and to “render it more difficult 
for cable programmers to compete for carriage on the 
limited channels remaining.” Turner I, 114 S. Ct. at 
2456. But this hypothetical burden is not determinative. 
Rather, must-carry is to be evaluated on the basis of a 
practical assessment of its “actual effects”; i.e., “the extent 
to which cable operators will, in fact, be forced to make 
changes in their current or anticipated program selec- 
tions; the degree to which cable programmers will be 
dropped from cable systems to make room for local broad- 
casters; and the extent to which cable operators can satisfy 
their must-carry obligations by devoting previously unused 
channel capacity to the carriage of local broadcasters.’ 
114S. Ct. at 2472 (emphasis added). 


In actual operation, must-carry occupies only a small 
fraction of cable’s overall capacity, much less than appel- 
lants claimed in their first appeal to this Court. The 
“actual effect” of must-carry on cable programmers is 
likewise de minimis. And even this minimal burden is 
vanishing as cable channel capacity continues to increase.

B. There Are No “Constitutionally Acceptable” Less 
Restrictive Alternatives That Advance Congress’ 
Objectives Nearly As Effectively As Must-Carry. 


“An incidental burden on speech is . . . permissible 
under O’Brien,” if it “promotes a substantial government 
interest that would be achieved less effectively absent the 
regulation.” Albertini, 472 U.S. at 689 (emphasis 
added); accord Ward, 491 U.S. at 801. This Court 
directed the district court to apply that standard on 
remand, 114 S. Ct. at 2469, and it did. See J.S. App. 
22a.” None of the regulatory alternatives appellants have 
advanced—the A/B switch, leased access, the Century 
Rules, or subsidies—would advance Congress’ interest in 
maintaining a multiplicity of diverse broadcast sources 
nearly as effectively as must-carry. Moreover, the doc- 
trie of less restrictive alternatives is designed for situa- 
tions where the means Congress chose will have a sub- 
stantial impact on speech; it does not authorize judicial 
fine-tuning within a range of already de minimis burdens.”

Subsidies. Providing direct subsidies is a simplistic 
alternative that raises far more questions than it answers. 
As a practical matter, a subsidy scheme risks entangling 
the government in content-based judgments. Unlike must- 
carry—which distinguishes between speakers based on 
technology, deciding which broadcast speakers to fund 
would require the government to use other criteria. and 
to establish an elaborate administrative mechanism to do 
so. If Congress determined that a broadcast station was 
not attracting enough viewers to be self-sufficient, Con- 
gress would have to decide whether to subsidize the sta- 
tion anyway, or to direct the station to make changes,
