STATEMENT 


The “must-carry” provisions of the Cable Television 
Consumer Protection and Competition Act of 1992 (1992 
Cable Act), 47 U.S.C. 534 and 535, require cable operators 
to set aside a limited number of channels for the carriage 
of qualified broadcast television stations. Pub. L. No. 
102-385, 106 Stat. 1460. In 1998, a three-judge district 
court granted summary judgment upholding the provi- 
sions against First Amendment challenges. 819 F. Supp. 
32. On appeal, this Court concluded that genuine issues of 
material fact were in dispute and remanded the case for 
development of a fuller factual record. 114 S. Ct. 2445 
(1994). On remand, the district court again granted sum- 
mary judgment and sustained the challenged provisions in 
light of the legislative record and additional factual evi- 
dence submitted by the parties. 

1. In its prior decision, this Court rejected appellants’ 
contention that the must-carry provisions require strict 
scrutiny. The Court concluded that must-carry does not 
“distinguish favored speech from disfavored speech on the 


(1) 


2 


basis of the ideas or views expressed,” and does not give a 
preference to “broadcasters over cable programmers based 
on the content of programming each group offers.” 
Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 1148. Ct. 2445, 
2459, 2467 (1994). Rather, the Court emphasized, the 
provisions are designed “to prevent cable operators from 
exploiting their economic power to the detriment of 
broadcasters,” and “to ensure that all Americans, espe- 
cially those unable to subscribe to cable, have access to 
free television programming—whatever its content.” Id. 
at 2462. 

Because the must-carry provisions are content-neutral 
and do not pose any “inherent danger] to free expression,” 
the Court held that they must be sustained if they satisfy 
an intermediate level of First Amendment scrutiny. 114 8. 
Ct. at 2469. Under that standard, the Court reaffirmed, a 
content-neutral regulation will be sustained if “it furthers 
an important or substantial governmental interest; if the 
governmental interest is unrelated to the suppression of 
free expression; and if the incidental restriction on alleged 
First Amendment freedoms is no greater than is essential 
to the furtherance of that interest.” Ibid. 

Based on the record then before the Court, a four- 
Justice plurality could not conclude that the government 
had demonstrated the absence of any genuine issue of 
material fact as to two parts of the constitutional test of 
intermediate scrutiny. The plurality therefore found it 
necessary to remand the case to the district court for 
further factual development. 114 S. Ct. at 2472. Justice 
Stevens, although concluding that the government had 
sustained its burden under intermediate scrutiny, con- 
curred in the remand to ensure a disposition of the case. 
Id. at 2475.

(O'Connor, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part); id. at 2481

2. On remand, the district court again granted sum- 
mary judgment to appellees. J.S. App. la-32a, 153a-154a. 

a. (i) On the need for must-carry to advance the gov- 
ernment’s important interests, the court found substantial 
evidence “from which Congress could reasonably conclude 
that cable systems had significant and increasing 
incentives to deny carriage or reposition local broad- 
casting stations in order to: 1) increase their advertising 
revenues, the ‘cable industry’s hottest and brightest area 
of growth;’ and 2) favor cable programming in which they 
had an equity interest.” J.S. App. 9a (citation to record 
evidence omitted). The court noted that cable operators 
are becoming capable of selling advertising to an extent 
comparable to sales by local broadcast competitors, and 
that “the cable industry [is] increasingly looking to ad- 
vertising as a source of revenue.” bid. It also found 
evidence that “cable operators with equity interests in 
cable programmers would have every reason to discrimi- 
nate against broadcast stations competing with their own 
programmers.” Id. at 10a. 

On the question of harm to broadcasters in the absence 
of must-carry, the court rejected appellants’ argument 
that must-carry could be sustained only if the entire 
broadcast industry (including the network affiliates)

The court instead focused on what Turner had identified 
as the government’s “two essential propositions” in sup- 
port of mandatory carriage rules: first, that “significant 
numbers of broadcast stations will be refused carriage on 
cable systems” absent must-carry protection, and second, 
“that the broadcast stations denied carriage will either 
deteriorate to a substantial degree or fail altogether.” Id. 
at 3a (quoting 114 S. Ct. at 2471).

The court first concluded that Congress had received

It also noted that channel capacity is expected to increase

21a-22a.

regarding “the extent to which cable operators can satisfy

INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


In Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 114 S. Ct. 
2445 (1994), this Court resolved the principal issues of 
First Amendment doctrine raised by the must-carry law. 
It held, first, that must-carry does not require strict 
scrutiny because it does not regulate any speech because 
of the message it conveys (id. at 2460-2464), does not grant 
cable access to broadcasters in order to counteract the 
speech of others (id. at 2464-2465), does not force the cable 
operator to associate with broadcasters’ messages (id. at 
2465-2466), and does not single out speakers or part of the 
press in a way that suggests a danger to free expression 
(id. at 2466-2469). It therefore ruled that must-carry must 


8 


be evaluated under an intermediate level of scrutiny, 
which requires that the regulation further an important 
governmental interest in a narrowly tailored way. /d. at 
2469. 

The Court had “no difficulty concluding” that each of 
the “three interrelated interests” declared by Congress to 
be served by must-carry— (I) preserving the benefits of 
free, over-the-air, local broadcast television, (2) promot- 
ing the widespread dissemination of information from a 
multiplicity of sources, and (3) promoting fair competition 
in the market for television programming“ —is “impor- 
tant,” under the test for intermediate scrutiny. 114 S. Ct. 
at 2469. It also necessarily rejected the rationale under 
which three dissenting Justices would have held that 
must-carry fails intermediate scrutiny (see id. at 2479 
(O’Connor, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part)). 
All those matters are now settled as the law of the case. 

But because “the standard for summary judgment is 
high,” and because “there remainled] a few unresolved 
issues of material fact” (114 S. Ct. at 2472 (Blackmun, J., 
concurring)), the Court remanded the case “to permit the 
parties to develop a more thorough factual record, and to 
allow the District Court to resolve any factual disputes 
remaining.” Id. at 2472. The remand emphasized that 
“courts must accord deference to the predictive judgments 
of Congress,” and must not “replace Congress’ factual 
predictions with [their] own”; rather, it stated, the courts’ 
task “is to assure that, in formulating its judgments, 
Congress has drawn reasonable inferences based on 
substantial evidence.” Id. at 2471. Those instructions too 
are the settled law of the case. 

The district court on remand compiled a vast record, 
comprising materials available to Congress before enact- 
ment of the 1992 Cable Act, as well as facts and expert 
opinion adduced since its passage. Based on those sub- 
missions, the district court concluded that Congress had 
indeed reasonably concluded that must-carry is necessary 


9 


to further the “three interrelated interests” already 
accepted by this Court to be substantial. That conclusion 
was correct. It was reasonable for Congress—“far better 
equipped than the judiciary” to evaluate the need for 
economic regulation of the cable industry (114 S. Ct. at 
2471)—to conclude that the harms addressed by must- 
carry are real, that must-carry would alleviate those 
harms, and that no alternative to must-carry would be 
sufficiently effective. 

I.A. Must-carry serves substantial interests, even if 
it is not necessary to prevent the collapse of the en- 
tire broadcast industry. The rule guards against anti- 
competitive conduct by cable operators against vulnerable 
broadcasters, a governmental interest that is “always 
substantial.” It prevents erosion in the financial health 
and programming quality of broadcasters, by affording 
them an opportunity to compete fairly for the entire cable 
audience. And in doing so, it advances the substantial, 
indeed, compelling governmental interest in multiplicity 
and diversity of communications sources available to the 
public. 

B. Absent must-carry, cable operators would have the 
incentive and means to discriminate against a substantial 
number of broadcasters by denying them access to cable; 
and in fact, when must-carry was not in existence, they 
did so discriminate. Cable operators affiliated with cable 
programmers have an incentive to run their own pro- 
gramming instead of local independent and public broad- 
casters, with whom they compete directly for audience 
share; that is true regardless of local audience preference. 
Whether or not vertically integrated, cable operators also 
have an incentive to replace broadcasters with cable net- 
works, because operators can sell their own local adver- 
tising on the latter but not the former. Cable operators 
compete directly with local independent commercial broad- 
casters in the advertising market. 


10 


The experience before must-carry was enacted shows 
that cable operators did indeed drop substantial numbers of 
the broadcasters with whom they compete. The typical 
cable system denied access to more than a quarter of local 
broadcasters, principally independent commercial stations 
and secondary public broadcasters. As a result, dropped 
commercial stations were unable to reach almost half of 
their potential cable audiences. 

C. The effect of loss of carriage on broadcasters is 
severe. Loss of access to viewers causes a decline in ad- 
vertising revenues and viewer contributions. Without 
assured access, new stations have difficulty in obtaining 
the investment capital necessary to start operations. In- 
versely, independent broadcasters that gain cable access 
benefit substantially, which proves their value to the 
public. The increase in broadcasting outlets in the 1980s 
was due largely to increased cable access, and after the 
demise of must-carry in 1987, that increase fell sharply— 
a reasonable basis for Congress’s conclusion that must- 
carry is necessary to the financial health and program- 
ming quality of many broadcasters. 

II. A. Whereas, in the absence of must-carry, there 
would be a serious impairment of the financial health and 
programming quality of local broadcasters at the hands 
of cable operators, and substantial erosion of broadcast 
diversity as well, the presence of must-carry has had a 
negligible effect on the editorial discretion of cable 
operators and on the ability of cable programmers to gain 
access to the cable. Although they often constitute a 
substantial percentage of the free broadcast options 
available, must-carry stations by contrast occupy only a 
tiny percentage of cable channel capacity. Most cable 
operators have been able to meet their obligations with 
unused capacity, and have not had to drop any cable 
channels. Channel capacity has increased and continues 
to increase; that growth further diminishes the slight 
burden of mandatory carriage. Cable networks continue 


11 


to gain access to cable systems and to increase their sub- 
scribership. 

B. There is no effective alternative to must-carry as a 
means of preventing anti-competitive conduct by cable 
operators, the erosion of free broadcast television, and 
the resulting impairment of media diversity. Consumers 
will not use A/B switches and outdoor antennas to watch 
over-the-air signals on their cable-wired sets, because of 
expense, complexity, and inconvenience. More limited 
carriage rules, featuring minimum viewership standards, 
would fail to protect the very broadcast stations that are 
most vulnerable to predation by cable operators, and would 
allow those operators to discriminate in favor of cable 
programmers against broadcasters without regard to the 
ratings of either. A leased-access regime open to cable 
programmers as well as broadcasters would drain re- 
sources from broadcasters’ programming and would not 
enable broadcasters to gain the necessary access to their 
potential cable audiences. Direct subsidies would keep 
broadcasters afloat regardless of their popularity, rather 
than give them the opportunity to prove their value to the 
entire audience, which is the objective of must-carry. 
Antitrust enforcement and administrative oversight are 
too cumbersome or expensive to protect broadcasters vul- 
nerable to discrimination. 


ARGUMENT 


I. THE MUST-CARRY RULE DIRECTLY ADVANCES SUB- 

STANTIAL GOVERNMEYTAL INTERESTS 

Cable television plainly carries the potential for a great 
expansion in the diversity of communications outlets 
available to the American public, but it also has the 
potential seriously to impair that diversity as well. The 
technology of the delivery of the signal to the home, the de 
facto monopoly position of most cable operators, and the 
increasing vertical integration and horizontal con- 
centration of the industry place cable systems in a 


position of great economic power over programmers who 


12 


need to secure a place on the cable to survive. Cable 
operators’ incentives for discriminatory self-preference 
place the voices of many of those programmers at risk of 
being silenced or severely weakened. 

Those concerns, Congress found, are acute for free local 
broadcast programming, the leading source of news and 
entertainment for the public. As most of the courtry has 
obtained cable service, cable carriage has become essential 
for broadcasters to reach much of their audience. But 
cable operators, increasingly affiliated with programmers 
and increasingly oriented to advertising as a revenue 
source, compete directly with local broadcasters and 
therefore have incentives to deny them carriage. Without 
cable carriage, broadcasters would lose much of their 
audience and, with it, the revenues (from advertising or 
viewer contributions) essential to creating programming 
or even staying on the air. The remaining audience, less 
affluent than those who can afford cable or less inclined 
to watch television, would also be less attractive to 
advertisers. 

The threat is not just to individual broadcasters but also 
to the First Amendment interest of the viewers, “which is 
paramount.” Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC, 395 U.S. 
367, 390 (1969). The Nation’s fundamental communications 
policy has been to increase the number of speakers, to 
promote a multiplicity and diversity of outlets. If 
broadcasters unfairly lose access to much of their cable 
audience, not just those stations, but also the diversity of 
voices in the media may be impoverished. Viewers with 
and without cable service could lose valuable options in 
broadcast television. These are the dangers to which 
must-carry is addressed, and Congress’s remedy for them 
advances the values protected by the First Amendment. 


13 


A. Must-Carry Prevents Erosion Of Media Diversity And 
Broadcasters’ Quality And Financial Health, And 
Also Prevents Anti-Competitive Conduct 


1. Appellants contend, at bottom, that must-carry can- 
not be sustained unless it is shown that the entire broad- 
cast industry would collapse in its absence. But Congress 
did not predicate must-carry on any such assumption, and 
this Court did not suggest in Turner that must-carry 
could be upheld only in that extreme situation. Nor did 
we argue in Turner that must-carry was based on a 
prediction that broadcast television is likely to be totally 
eliminated in its absence.’ 

“Congress declared that the must-carry provisions

II. THE MUST-CARRY RULE IS NARROWLY TAILORED 


For a content-neutral regulation to satisfy the require- 

ment of narrow tailoring, it “need not be the least speech- 
restrictive means of advancing the Government’s inter- 
ests”; the standard is met “so long as the . . . regulation 
promotes a substantial government interest that would be 
achieved less effectively absent the regulation,” without 
“burden{ing] substantially more speech than is necessary 
to further” that interest. Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 2469. The 
record on remand estabished two crucial points with re- 
spect to the question of narrow tailoring. 
First, must-carry’s actual effect on the cable industry 
is negligible. The rule has had a minimal impact on cable 
operators’ editorial discretion and cable programmers’ 
ability to gain access to cable systems. That minimal ef- 
fect must be contrasted with the severe impact on broad- 
casters caused by denial of carriage on a cable system 
(reducing their cable audience to zero), and with the 
resulting significant impairment of viewers’ choices in the 
broadcast medium. 

Second, no effective alternative to must-carry has been 
identified. The principal proposals mooted by appellants 
and Judge Williams—the antenna/switch mechanism and a 
leased-access regime—cannot ensure the vitality of broad- 


36 


casting that Congress believed was of fundamental impor- 
tance, and cannot protect more vulnerable stations from 
anti-competitive conduct by cable operators. The evidence 
confirms that Congress reasonably concluded that must- 
carry is indeed necessary to prevent anti-competitive con- 
duct, protect vulnerable broadcasters, and preserve diver- 
sity in broadcasting.

2. The Century rules 


Appellants suggest that Congress could have adopted 
the more limited “Century rules” promulgated by the 
FCC after the D.C. Circuit struck down its first set of 
must-carry rules in 1985. See Century Communications 
Corp. v. FCC, 835 F.2d 292 (D.C. Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 
486 U.S. 1032 (1988). The Century rules included a 
minimum viewership standard for must-carry eligibility, 
restricted eligible stations to cable systems within 50

broadcast stations that any cable system was required to

alleged burden on cable operators’ editorial discretion.

4. Subsidies 


A system of direct governmental subsidies to local 
broadcasters cannot substitute for must-carry, for it 
would further materially different goals. Must-carry’s 
purpose is not to guarantee the fortunes of financially 
vulnerable broadcasters, but to afford them the opportu- 
nity to compete fairly for a share of the viewing public 
over the cable. A subsidy system would guarantee the 
survival even of broadcasters that do not engage the 
attention of the viewing public (which must-carry does 
not) and therefore would not be a means commensurate to 
Congress’s ends. Congress is entitled to take full action 
to “eliminat[e] restraints on fair competition” (Turner, 
114 S. Ct. at 2470), not just compensate the victims.

5. Antitrust and FCC enforcement 


The district court correctly understood that antitrust 
enforcement and administrative complaint proceedings are 
inadequate substitutes for must-carry protection. The 
inordinate time and expense of antitrust litigation, 
especially for a nascent or financially precarious station 
seeking cable carriage from a large vertically integrated 
cable operator-programmer such as Time Warner, renders 
such litigation a manifestly less effective alternative to 
the must-carry approach. An administrative complaint 
mechanism, requiring the FCC to examine many thou- 
sands of cable operators’ business decisions to carry or not 
to carry individual programmers, would be highly cumber- 
some, and would involve long delays during which broad- 
cast stations would continue to suffer the adverse eco- 
nomie consequences of discriminatory non-carriage. 


Ultimately, appellants demonstrate only that they dis- 
agree with Congress over how much carriage protection 
broadcast stations need, and how an acceptable level of 
protection is to be attained. Whether or not Congress 
could have struck a different balance among the various 
interests affected by must-carry, that disagreement with 
the regulatory regime carefully chosen by Congress pro- 
vides no basis for second-guessing its policy judgments 
and reasonable factual determinations in this highly com- 
plex field. Appellants of course engage in protected 
speech; but that provides no cause to turn the First 
Amendment into a shield against economic regulation.“
