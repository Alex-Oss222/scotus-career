CONSTITUTIONAL AND 
STATUTORY PROVISIONS 


The First Amendment to the United States Constitution 
provides in part: “Congress shall make no law . . . abridging 
the freedom of speech, or of the press”. The Fifth Amendment 
to the United States Constitution provides in part: “(Nor shall 
private property be taken for public use without just 
compensation”. Sections 4 and 5 of the Cable Television 
Consumer Protection and Competition Act of 1992 (the “1992 
Cable Act” or “Act”), Pub. L. No. 102-385, 106 Stat. 1460 
(1992), 47 U.S.C. §§ 534, 535, are reprinted at J.S. 113a- 
143a. 


STATEMENT 
A. Introduction. 


This case presents a First and Fifth Amendment challenge 
to Sections 4 and 5 of the 1992 Cable Act—the so-called 
“must-carry” provisions. Those provisions impose the most

far-reaching must-carry regime known to date.* Section 4 of 
the Act requires virtually every cable operator in the Nation

B. The First Turner Opinion. 


In 1994, when first reviewing Plaintiffs’ First Amendment 
challenge to the must-carry provisions, a unanimous Supreme 
Court held that cable operators and programmers are full- 
fledged First Amendment speakers and that must-carry 
restricts their speech. Turner Broadcasting Sys., Inc v. FCC, 
114.S. Ct. 2445, 2456 (1994). After reviewing the Act and its 
various findings, a five-Justice majority held that must-carry 
was content-neutral because “Congress’ overriding objective 
was not to favor programming of a particular subject matter,

Summary Of The Argument 


The central question directed by this Court to the district 
court—“‘whether the Government has adequately shown that 
the economic health of local broadcasting is in genuine 
jeopardy and in need of the protections afforded by must- 
carry’——must be answered with a definitive “no”. Turner, 114 
S. Ct. at 2470. The undisputed record below establishes that 
at the time must-carry was enacted (1) the local broadcasting 
system was in excellent financial condition and had 
experienced impressive growth in the absence of must-carry; 
and (2) cable operators voluntarily carried virtually all 
broadcast stations that had any measurable viewership. 
Moreover, post enactment, most broadcast stations actually 
demanded compensation from cable operators for the right to 
carry the stations’ programming rather than exercise their 
must-carry rights. Faced with that indisputable evidence, 
Defendants and their own experts simply conceded below that 
there was no threat to the well-being of the free, local 
broadcasting system. Thus, the Government failed to show 
what this Court had asked it to prove and must-carry must be 
struck down under intermediate First Amendment scrutiny. 

Prior to the remand proceeding, which demonstrated that 
there was no threat to the broadcasting system, Defendants 
agreed that the interest purportedly served by must-carry was 
the protection of the broadcasting system and that they bore 
the burden of showing that the system was at risk. In 1992, 


9

industry”. Brief for the Fed. Appellees at 39 (Dec. 7, 1993) 
(“Gov't App. Br.”).? The Government told this Court that 
Congress intended to prevent the “devastation [that would be]

After four years of litigation and three judicial decisions,

at stake. Congress claimed to have enacted must-carry to 
protect the local broadcasting system for the benefit of the 
40% of Americans who cannot afford or do not want cable 
television, not to protect marginal broadcasters who do not 
attract viewership because of their poor programming and 
related lack of adequate financial resources. Accordingly, 
Judge Sporkin erred as a matter of law in crediting the new 
interest manufactured by Defendants below.

unlike the interest recognized by this Court, is fundamentally 
illegitimate under the First Amendment and properly subject

The must-carry provisions are also constitutionally infirm 
because they are overbroad. They grant each broadcast station. 
including financially strong ones, the right to force its way 
onto cable systems. The record establishes that a substantial 
number of stations (including powerful and highly profitable 
network affiliates) have exercised must-carry rights, many to 
expand greatly the reach of their over-the-air television signal. 
Likewise, the must-carry provisions apply irrespective of 
whether a cable system has engaged in anticompetitive 
behavior or whether a broadcast station has ever been the 
object of anticompetitive conduct with respect to cable 
carriage. There simply is no “fit” between the must-carry 
provisions and the interests that have been asserted. 


The undisputed facts below more than substantiate that 
must-carry imposes a substantial burden on the First 
Amendment rights of both cable operators and programmers. 
The number of programming sources seeking carriage far 
outstrips the number of available channels on virtually every 
cable system in the Nation. Very few cable operators have 
unused channel capacity, and when such capacity exists, it is 
filled quickly. Thus, cable operators must choose from among 
a wide variety of programming sources to fill their finite 
channel capacity and create the most desirable package of 
programming for their viewers. The must-carry provisions 
directly override those editorial choices and foreclose limited 


12 


channel capacity that could otherwise go to cable 
programmers. This governmental restriction on cable 
operators’ and programmers’ speech is true First Amendment 
injury. As such, it raises grave constitutional concerns, unlike 
the economic harm allegedly suffered by a few marginal 
broadcasters. 


Faced with overwhelming evidence that must-carry neither 
furthers a substantial governmental interest ner is narrowly 
tailored to advance any such interest, Defendants argued 
below for undue deference to Congress, claiming that as long 
as Congress can find some selection of facts—even anecdotal, 
unreliable facts—to support its “findings”, then an Article III 
court need look no further. That is at best “rational basis 
review”—a standard this Court has already unanimously 
rejected. Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 2456-58. This Court has plainly 
held that Federal courts must exercise “independent judgment” 
of the facts and be the ultimate arbiter of constitutionality 
itself. See Sable Communications of Cal., Inc. v. FCC, 492 
U.S. 115, 129 (1989); Landmark Communications, Inc. v. 
Virginia, 435 U.S. 829, 843-44 (1978). Thus, the facts relied 
upon by Judge Sporkin below—unfounded speculation and 
conjecture by an FCC Commissioner, industry insiders, and 
a “slew of notable experts”—cannot justify the burden placed 
by must-carry on the First Amendment activities of cable 
operators and programmers.

D. Judge Sporkin Grossly Undervalued The First

Judge Sporkin’s view that First Amendment injury is 
substantial only if cable speakers are no longer “healthy and

Burns, 427 U.S. 347, 373 (1976) (plurality) (“{t}he loss of

unquestionably constitutes irreparable injury”). Judge Sporkin 
also erred in limiting his inquiry to the number of must-carry

inquiry, he further erred in concluding that the must-carry

rights of cable operators and programmers. Whether the must-

Amendment injury inflicted directly by Congress. That injury 
cannot be weighed against the alleged nonconstitutional, 
economic harm suffered by broadcasters in the private 
marketplace for speech. The core principle underlying the 
First Amendment is that public power—the State—poses the 
greatest threat to speech.

obscures the harm caused by must-carry to particular cable 
operators and programmers. As Judge Williams noted, the 
percentages offered by the Defendants lump together the few 
cable operators who may have dozens of vacant channels and 
the vast majority of cable operators who do not. Yet, “dozens 
of vacant channels on a handful of several-hundred-channel 
systems are obviously of no use to a constrained system of

cable operator and programmer.

VI. THE LOW POWER CARRIAGE OBLIGATIONS 
AND MARKET MODIFICATION PROVISIONS 
ARE CONTENT-BASED 


This Court recognized in its earlier decision in this case 
that two provisions within Section 4 of the Act may be 
content-based, but reserved judgment so that the district court 
could consider the issue first. 114 S. Ct. at 2460 n.6. The first 
provision allows a low power broadcast station to qualify for 
must-Carry in certain circumstances if the FCC determines, 
among other things, that it “would address local news and 
informational needs which are not being adequately served by 
full power television broadcast stations”. 47 U.S.C. 
§ 534(h)(2)(B). The second provision allows the FCC to alter 
a television market to include a station, particularly where the 
station “provides news coverage and issues of concern to such 
community ... or coverage of sporting or other events of 
interest to the community”. Jd. § 534(h)(1)(C)(ii)(III). On 
remand, Plaintiffs argued that both provisions were content- 
based restrictions on speech. Judge Sporkin upheld the first 
on the ground that it was “viewpoint-neutral” and thus not 
subject to strict scrutiny. J.S. 30a. He did not even address the 
second provision. 


Judge Sporkin was wrong to insist that strict scrutiny did 
not apply (and wrong to ignore the market modification 
provision entirely). As this Court’s precedents make clear, a 
statute is content-based if it grants special rights to speakers 
based on the type of speech they offer, regardless of the 
viewpoint of that speech. Accordingly, the provisions at issue 
here trigger strict scrutiny because they favor a particular type 
of speech—for instance, local news and sports programming. 
See, e.g., Arkansas Writers’ Project, Inc. v. Ragland, 481 U.S. 


(E.D.N.Y. 1994); Sunbelt Television, Inc. v. Jones Intercable, Inc., 795 
F. Supp. 333 (C.D. Cal. 1992); Aurora Cable Communications, Inc. v. 
Jones Intercable, Inc., 720 F. Supp. 600 (W.D. Mich. 1989). 


48 


221, 229 (1987) (tax exemption of religious, professional, 
trade and sports publications held content-based). Indeed, the 
express terms of the statute plainly call for the FCC to make 
content-related decisions when considering the eligibility of 
low power stations for must-carry status and adjudicating 
market modification petitions. The Government has never 
articulated a compelling interest supporting either provision. 
They must be stuck down.
