CONSTITUTIONAL AND STATUTORY PROVISIONS 


The First Amendment to the United States Constitution 
provides in pertinent part: “Congress shall make no law 
* * * abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press.” 


2 


Relevant provisions of the Cable Act are reprinted at

STATEMENT 


Appellants brought this action in 1992 before a three- 
judge district court seeking (among other things) to en- 
join enforcement of the so-called “must-carry” provisions 
of the Cable Act, 47 U.S.C. §§ 534 & 535, which require 
that cable operators carry the programming of commercial 
and noncommercial television broadcasters. The district 
court, by a 2-1 vote, upheld the provisions, but this Court 
vacated the judgment and remanded the case for further 
proceedings. Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC, 
114 S. Ct. 2445 (1994), rev’g 819 F. Supp. 32 (1993). 
On remand, the district court by a 2-1 vote again upheld 
the challenged statutory provisions, granting appellees’ 
motions for summary judgment and denying appellants’ 
motions. 


1. The background of this dispute is comprehensively 
explained in Justice Kennedy’s opinion for the Court in 
Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 2451-56. The Court unanimously 
rejected the Government's claim for review under the 
rational-basis standard or the deferential standard appli- 
cable to broadcast regulation. Jd. at 2456-58. By a 5-4 
vote, this Court then held that the constitutionality of the 
Cable Act’s must-carry regime is to be analyzed under 
the two-part First Amendment test for intermediate scru- 
tiny outlined in United States v. O’Brien, 391 U.S. 367 
(1968). See Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 US. 
781 (1989). The Court thus demanded a showing by 
the Government that the law (1) serves a substantial 
speech-neutral government interest and (2) is narrowly 
tailored—i.e., burdens freedom of speech no more than 
necessary—to serve that interest. 114 S. Ct. at 2469. 


On the basis of the record then before the Court, seven 
Justices in Turner concluded that the Government had 
failed to meet its burden of justifying the must-carry law 
under intermediate scrutiny. The four-Justice plurality 
found itself “unable to conclude that the Government has 


3 


satisfied either inquiry” mandated by O’Brien (114 S. Ct. 
at 2470) and concluded that the case should be remanded 
for development of “a more thorough factual record” 
concerning both parts of the O’Brien analysis. 114 S. Ct. 
at 2472. Four Justices would have invalidated the statute 
under strict scrutiny (id. at 2475 (O’Connor, J., dissent- 
ing); id. at 2481 (Ginsburg, J., dissenting) ); three of 
those Justices also found the law to fail O’Brien scrutiny 
(id. at 2479-80 (O'Connor, J., dissenting)). Justice 
Stevens would have affirmed the district court but, lest no 
disposition command a majority, concurred in the judg- 
ment remanding for further proceedings. Jd. at 2475. 


On the “substantial governmental interest” branch of 
O’Brien, the Court concluded that the Government's as- 
serted interest—preserving the economic viability of free, 
over-the-air, local broadcast television—is “important in 
the abstract.” 114 S. Ct. at 2470 (plurality). But, the 
plurality explained, the Government, when regulating 
speech, “must do more than simply ‘posit the existence of 
the disease sought to be cured.’” J/bid. (citation omitted). 
Rather, the Government “must demonstrate that the re- 
cited harms are real, not merely conjectural.” Ibid. The 
plurality thus defined as follows the proper inquiry in this 
case under the first branch of O’Brien: “whether the Gov- 
ernment has adequately shown that the economic health of 
local broadcasting is in genuine jeopardy and in need of 
the protections afforded by must-carry.” 114 S. Ct. at 
2470. 


Even if the Government could meet its burden on this 
first question, the plurality explained that “the Govern- 
ment still bears the burden of showing that the remedy it 
has adopted does not ‘burden substantially more speech 
than is necessary to further the government's legitimate 
interests.” 114 S. Ct. at 2470 (citation omitted). This 
“narrow tailoring” step of the O’Brien analysis involves 
two subsidiary inquiries, as to both of which the record 
was deficient. First, the plurality cited the lack of “any 
findings concerning the actual effects of must-carry on the 


4 


speech of cable operators and cable programmers,” i.e., 
“the extent to which the must-carry provisions in fact inter- 
fere with protected speech.” Jd. at 2472. Second, the 
plurality noted the absence of “any judicial findings con- 
cerning the availability and efficacy of ‘constitutionally 
acceptable less restrictive means’ of achieving the Govern- 
ment’s asserted interests.” /bid. (citation omitted). 


2. On remand, a sharply divided district court sus- 
tained the must-carry regime. Judge Sporkin concluded 
that appellees were entitled to summary judgment uphold- 
ing the statute. J.S. App. la-32a. Judge Williams con- 
cluded that appellants were entitled to summary judgment 
invalidating the statute. J.S. App. 38a-112a. Judge 
Jackson believed that a trial was necessary but joined the 
result reached by Judge Sporkin to avoid “prevail[ing] by 
stalemate.” J.S. App. 33a-37a. 


a. Judge Sporkin concluded that evidence introduced 
by appellees in Congress and on remand amounted to 
“substantial evidence” from which Congress could draw 
a “reasonable inference” that the must-carry law was nec- 
essary. J.S. App. 6a. The first question posed by this 
Court’s remand was “whether the Government has ade- 
quately shown that the economic health of local broad- 
casting is in genuine jeopardy and in need of the protec- 
tions afforded by must-carry.” Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 2470. 
Although this Court, in remanding the case, instructed the 
district court that the proper inquiry was whether “the 
must-carry rules are necessary to protect the viability of 
broadcast television” (id. at 2471), Judge Sporkin never- 
theless rejected the idea that “the Court should examine 
the broadcasting industry as a whole.” J.S. App. 8a-9a 
n.11 (emphasis in original). Instead, he concluded, “[t]he 
relevant inquiry is whether the health of those broadcasters 
protected by the must-carry provisions would be in jeop- 
ardy without the provisions.” /bid. (emphasis added). Ac- 
cording to Judge Sporkin, “[t]he Supreme Court never 
really questioned Congress’ decision that the group of 
broadcasters covered by the must-carry provisions are en- 


5 


titled to the protections afforded by those provisions if 
they are in genuine jeopardy, so long as the regulations 
meet the narrow tailoring requirements.” Ibid. 


Having defined the “harm to broadcasting” interest in 
that way, Judge Sporkin, citing broadcasters’ congres- 
sional testimony and the 1988 FCC Carriage Survey 
previously deemed inconclusive by the plurality opinion 
in this Court (114 S. Ct. at 2471-72), then concluded that 
Congress reasonably inferred that “ ‘significant’ numbers 
of broadcast stations would be refused carriage” absent 
must-carry rules. J.S. App. 13a; see id. at 9a-l3a. Judge 
Sporkin noted some economic incentives of cable opera- 
tors to deny carriage to broadcasters (id. at 9a-10a, 14a) 
and explained: “Obviously, this case itself confirms that 
the cable industry would drop broadcast stations if the 
must-carry rules were not in place.” /d. at 13a. Judge 
Sporkin also discerned substantial evidence that stations 
denied carriage would thereby be harmed (id. at 15a-18a), 
stating: “That a broadcast station that was denied access 
to the cable system in its local market would suffer finan- 
cial harm and possible ruin seems to be an obvious con- 
clusion.” Jd. at 15a. Judge Sporkin made no mention 
of the numbers showing that a broadcast station, on aver- 
age, has roughly 50 cable systems in its local market (the 
so-called “Area of Dominant Influence,” the area in which 
its signal can generally be viewed over the air). J.S. App. 
43a n.1 (Williams, J., dissenting). 


In addressing the “actual effects” of the must-carry law 
on cable speakers (Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 2472), Judge 
Sporkin ignored the fact that, in the face of undisputed 
limited capacity of cable systems (i.e., capacity is far less 
than programmers’ demand), there is a one-for-one corre- 
spondence between forced carriage of broadcasters and 
foreclosed opportunities for cable programmers: each 
channel occupied by a broadcast station as a result of com- 
pelled carriage, whether added in 1992 or frozen in place 
at that time, is thereby closed off to cable programmers. 
Instead, Judge Sporkin focused on the particular additions 
and drops of stations and programmers that occurred when 


6 


the 1992 must-carry law took effect. J.S. App. 18a-22a. 
The evidence on remand revealed at least 5,880 instances 
in which broadcasters were added to cable systems as a 
result of the law, forcing cable systems to drop 780 pro- 
gramming services and reposition 1,385 cable program- 
mers. /d. at 20a-2la. According to Judge Sporkin, these 
data showed that “the burden due to the must-carry rules 
has been small” and would likely “diminish as [cable sys- 
tems’] channel capacity increases.” Jd. at 21a. 


Turning to the “availability and efficacy of ‘constitu- 
tionally acceptable less restrictive means’” (Turner, 114 
S. Ct. at 2472), Judge Sporkin assumed arguendo that the 
five alternatives suggested by appellants were less restric- 
tive but found that Congress could reasonably have found 
none of them to be “as effective.” J.S. App. 23a. In this 
analysis, Judge Sporkin did not find the must-carry law 
narrowly tailored to any interest in preventing anticom- 
petitive conduct or in preserving the health of the overall 
broadcast system. Rather, with respect to each suggested 
alternative, Judge Sporkin concluded simply that it was 
not as effective at serving the identified interest in adding 
to the revenues of each broadcaster claiming the law’s pro- 
tection. J.S. App. 23a-27a. 


Judge Sporkin thus characterized a leased-access regime 
—affording nondiscriminatory cable-carriage rights to 
cable and broadcast programmers alike—as not “an 
equally effective alternative” because it would require 
broadcasters to pay a fee for carriage, and thus possibly 
to “reduce * * * expenditures on their programming.” /d. 
at 26a. Judge Sporkin similarly rejected “A/B” switches 
and an earlier set of FCC must-carry rules on the simple 
ground that they would not aid broadcasters as much as 
the 1992 Act’s requirements. /d. at 23a-26a.' And he

Among other matters, Judge Sporkin rejected appellants’ free- 
speech challenge to low-power stations’ must-carry eligibility, con-

First, he stated that “|p]resent and future budget restraints

a law requiring forced carriage of essentially a// stations

b. Judge Jackson concurred in the result reached by 
Judge Sporkin, though only after stating that the disposi- 
tion he actually thought proper was to “deny both cross- 
motions for summary judgment and set this case for trial.” 
J.S. App. 33a. Reluctant, however, to “prevail by stale- 
mate” as “a minority of one,” Judge Jackson “elect[ed] 
to concur” with Judge Sporkin. /bid. Unlike Judge 
Sporkin, Judge Jackson focused on the interest originally 
pressed by the Government—the prevention of harm to the 
health of broadcasting as a whole. He professed himself 
“satisfied that, taken at face value, the evidence before 
Congress in 1992 of an impending demise of local broad- 
casting was at least ‘substantial’ enough to warrant its 
prediction” by Congress. Ibid. (emphasis added). He was 
“also prepared to accept as minimal the burden must-carry 
imposes on the cable industry’s First Amendment rights.” 
Ibid. He did not address the efficacy of less restrictive 
means. 


c. Judge Williams dissented. J.S. App. 38a-112a. In 
determining whether the Government had demonstrated 
“genuine jeopardy” to local broadcasting (Turner, 114 
S. Ct. at 2470), Judge Williams interpreted this Court's 
mandate to require inquiry into whether the must-carry 
law is actually needed to maintain the health of the broad- 
cast system as a whole, that is, to “preserve access to free 


cluding that these provisions of the Cable Act “are very close to 
content-based legislation triggering strict scrutiny” but “do not 
* * * cross the line.” Jd. at 29a.

television programming for the 40 percent of Americans 
without cable” (id. at 2461). See J.S. App. 42a-43a. On 
this point, Judge Williams found the evidence undisputed: 
“The parties agree that there is no threat to the continued 
viability of broadcast television, either now in existence 
or looming on the horizon.” /d. at 40a. “In fact,” Judge 
Williams noted, “the defendants unequivocally concede, in 
the briefs and in the depositions of their experts, that the 
evidence they have introduced on remand does not show 
that the health of the broadcast industry as a whole is in 
danger.” /d. at 48a-49a. The accuracy of appellees’ con- 
cession was confirmed, in Judge Williams’ view, by undis- 
puted evidence concerning growth in broadcast advertising 
revenues (id. at 50a-55a), the number of broadcast sta- 
tions (id. at 55a-59a), and broadcast profitability (id. at 
59a-61a). Judge Williams accordingly discerned “insuffi- 
cient evidence from which Congress could conclude that 
‘broadcast television is in jeopardy,’ Turner, 114 S. Ct. at 
2472, under any interpretation of that phrase consistent 
with the Court’s analysis.” J.S. App. 72a. Only if the 
Government’s interest were redefined to focus, not on the 
broadcast system’s overall health, but on “the maximum 
growth of broadcast, or enhancement of precisely the sta- 
tions that would elect must-carry against the will of cable 
operators—interpretations of Turner's mandate that are 
manifestly implausible—can the posited harms be viewed 
as real rather than conjectural.” Jd. at 72a-73a.° 


Judge Williams took it as obvious—what appellants 
have of course never disputed—that, without the must- 
carry law, some stations have been (and would be) denied 
carriage, resulting in some potential lost audience and 
revenues. J.S. App. 6la-63a. He observed, however, that 
the evidence produced by both appellants and appellees

Id. at 72a. 


While finding the facts undisputed concerning the over- 
all health of the broadcast system, Judge Wiiliams ac- 
knowledged a distinct governmental interest in preventing 
even isolated instances of “unfair competition” by cable 
operators. J.S. App. 73a. He explained the numerous 
reasons casting doubt (or worse) on the Government's 
theories in this regard. /d. at 73a-88a. But if the “possi- 
bility of a risk of anticompetitive behavior” could not, 
“at least without trial,” be precluded, Judge Williams con- 
cluded that appellants were nevertheless entitled to sum- 
mary judgment because “must-carry is plainly not a remedy 
narrowly tailored to any such risk.” /d. at 73a. 


Exhaustively analyzing undisputed record evidence con- 
cerning “the actual effects of must-carry on the speech of 
cable operators and cable programmers” (Turner, 1i4 
S. Ct. at 2472), Judge Williams first determined that “the 
harm from must-carry is substantial.” J.S. App. 93a; id. 
at 90a-96a. According to appellees’ evidence, “in 1993 
must-carry stations added as a result of the Cable Act 
accounted for between 1.4% and 6% of the channels 
offered by sampled systems, and total must-carry stations 
accounted for between 7% and 13% of channels.” /d. 
at 91a. Appellant Time Warner, for example, “added 240 
broadcast stations to its systems because of must-carry and 
had to drop programming on 75 channels as a result.” /d. 


10 


at 93a. Even the evidence focusing only on the 1992/ 
1993 forced changes in channel occupation and availabil- 
ity, Judge Williams concluded, demonstrated “an obvious 
and direct burden on the First Amendment rights of both 
the programmers affected and the cable operator, who had 
freely contracted with the programmers to air their 
speech.” Ibid. 


With the impact on freedom of speech plainly substan- 
tial, Judge Williams concluded that undisputed evidence 
showed the existence of at least two “less restrictive alter- 
natives” that would address the Government's legitimate 
concerns yet impose lesser burdens on other speakers’ First 
Amendment rights—“genuinely non-discriminatory entitle- 
ments to carriage at regulated rates [and] the ‘A/B’ or 
input selector switch.” J.S. App. 96a. Appellees’ main 
objection to the A/B switch on a television (which allows 
viewers to switch from cable signals to signals from an 
over-the-air antenna) was that those consumers who lacked 
such a switch on their remote controls “ ‘had to get up’ to 
change them” and thus found the switch “inconvenient.” 
Id. at 106a. In Judge Williams’ view, “government- 
mandated carriage seems a rather astonishing solution to 
a problem now redefined as consumers’ occasional need 
to ‘get up.’” Ibid. 


Appellees opposed a requirement of nondiscriminatory 
access principally because broadcasters, like other pro- 
grammers, would have to pay for such access. J.S. App. 
100a. But if cable carriage “generates for broadcasters 
the enormous incremental revenues that defendants claim,” 
and if the government interest is truly in creating the 
“standards of a free market,” Judge Williams judged it 
“odd to find [broadcasters] disparaging the idea that they 
should pay any of the costs of access to this bonanza.” 
Id. at 100a, 101a. And it was particularly so, given that 
“the salient difference between independent programmers 
and broadcasters is that the latter enjoy use of a valuable 
asset given them by the government—a broadcast license 
—that the former lack.” /d. at 10la. Judge Williams 


il 


accordingly concluded that “must-carry violates the First 
Amendment requirement to use the least restrictive alter- 
native, as that has been understood since Ward v. Rock 
Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781 (1989), by adopting a 
mandate of completely uncompensated carriage in favor of 
a privileged class of users, at the expense of other speakers 
whose access to viewers is subject to more severe limits.” 
Id. at 101a-102a (emphasis in original). 


SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


I. At the heart of this case lies a basic First Amend- 
ment principle: that, as a general rule, the government 
may not decree that certain speakers be heard and other 
speakers be silenced. See, e.g., Buckley v. Valeo, 424 
U.S. 1, 48-49 (1976); First National Bank of Boston vy. 
Bellotti, 435 U.S. 765, 780-81 (1978); Meyer v. Grant, 
486 U.S. 414, 426 n.7 (1988). Targeted regulation that 
discriminates among speakers is presumptively unconstitu- 
tional, with the weight of the presumption depending upon 
whether the discrimination is content-based (subject to 
strict scrutiny) or, as here, content-neutral (subject to 
intermediate scrutiny). In either case, however, the gov- 
ernment bears the burden of justifying its discriminatory 
regulation. The critical question in this case, therefore, 
is whether the Government has shown something about the 
need to discriminate between these particular speakers— 
broatcast stations (favored) and cable programmers (dis- 
favo.ed)—that would allow it to do what it ordinarily 
cannot. To that end, the Government has in the course 
of this case offered three principal reasons to support the 
discrimination of the must-carry law. None stands up to 
close scrutiny. 


A. The first purported ground for discrimination is 
that forced carriage of broadcast stations is necessary to 
preserve the viability of the broadcast system as a whole. 
But, as the D.C. Circuit noted a decade ago, it is irra- 
tional to think that protection of each broadcast station 
is essential to protect the broadcast system. See Quincy 
Cable TV, Inc. v. FCC, 768 F.2d 1434 (D.C. Cir. 1985), 


12 


cert. denied, 476 U.S. 1169 (1986). Given the record 
in this case, the Government has now acknowledged, and 
Judge Sporkin himself recognized, that there is no basis 
in the evidence for concluding that the system as a whole 
is in any meaningful sense endangered. To the contrary, 
the facts demonstrate that the stations making up the 
vast bulk of the system are voluntarily carried anyway 
(often, today, pursuant to the retransmission consent pro- 
visions), and that the must-carry law serves primarily to 
assist little-watched stations in markets with unusually 
large numbers of broadcast stations. Enforced protection 
of these stations, however, has little to do with preserva- 
tion of the historical broadcast system: it is simply an 
effort to insist that, regardless of interest, viewers in some 
communities must be offered many more broadcast sta- 
tions than viewers in most communities can even get. 


B. The second claimed ground for discrimination is 
that forced carriage of broadcast stations is necessary to 
overcome anticompetitive treatment by cable systems. But 
this argument—whether based on concern about vertical 
integration between cable operators and programmers or 
on concern about cable operators’ supposedly dominant 
incentive to drop broadcast stations to injure them as 
competitors for advertising revenues (see Findings @€ 15- 
16 (J.S. App. 116a-17a))—founders on an obvious in- 
ability to fit the law to the claimed interest. Thus, even 
if concern about vertical integration were actually a basis 
for the must-carry law, which it is not, the law is not 
remotely designed to address that particular concern. 
Other provisions of the Act explicitly address any prob- 
lems arising from vertical integration, see 47 U.S.C. 
$$ 533(f)(1)(B), 536, 548(b) & (c), and the must- 
carry law, in any event, would be both a wildly over- 
inclusive and underinclusive solution to those problems. 
It applies, on its face, to every cable system (regardless 
of whether it is vertically integrated) and to every deci- 
sion about carriage of a broadcast station (regardless of 
whether it would favor a vertically integrated program- 
mer). And the law provides mandatory access only to 


13 


unaffiliated broadcast stations, not to unaffiliated cable 
programmers. 

The supposed concern about diversion of advertising 
revenues fares no better. Most notably, of course, the 
must-carry law is not directed at non-carriage for that 
reason: it prohibits non-carriage for any reason, including 
a desire to provide viewers with more attractive program- 
ming. Moreover, taking the argument on its own terms, 
the record again shows clearly that cable systems, regard- 
less of any incentive to capture advertising revenues, will- 
ingly carry the vast majority of broadcast stations, includ- 
ing those that account for some 97-98 percent of viewer- 
ship (and hence virtually all of the advertising revenues 
attracted by broadcast stations). Although the Govern- 
ment below tried to trim its theory, to argue that broad- 
cast stations with the /east advertising revenues are most 
at risk of anticompetitive actions, it utterly failed to show 
that this new-found theory is correct, even though its orig- 
inal theory (the theory set out in the congressional find- 
ings) has proved wrong. The record, in fact, provides no 
reasonable basis whatsoever for concluding that a desire 
to injure little-watched stations (or otherwise to divert 
advertising revenues), rather than a desire to provide at- 
tractive programming to subscribers, is behind most (or 
even many) carriage decisions made by cable operators. 


C. The third and final interest—and the interest ulti- 
mately relied on by Judge Sporkin in upholding the law— 
is nothing more than an unadorned governmental desire 
to aid broadcast stations. But this is not at all what Con- 
gress said it was doing in 1992 or what this Court (or 
the Government previously) has understood the law to 
do. Furthermore, this interest—unless viewed as simply 
advancing a “more is better” principle—lacks internal 
coherence. Given that the number of local broadcast 
signals varies from community to community, the effect 
of the must-carry law is to require carriage of twelve 
broadcast stations in some areas, but only six broadcast 
stations in other areas. Yet, the Government has never 
explained why it is essential to some important govern- 


14 


mental interest that residents of one community must get 
twice as many stations as residents of a second commu- 
nity can get. Whatever might be the importance of assur- 
ing that viewers everywhere have access to a minimum 
number of broadcast outlets, there is no apparent reason 
why viewers in each community must have access to the 
maximum number of possible outlets, with that number 
changing from place to place. 


The fragility of this interest does not mean that the 
Government is absolutely barred from making broadcast- 
ers a privileged class of speakers. It does mean, however, 
that the Government must choose different means more 
appropriate to such an end. If the Government wishes to 
provide benefits to certain broadcast stations, it may, for 
example, do what governments traditionally do: it may 
expend its own funds, thereby assuring that any stations 
in need remain available over-the-air to viewers in their 
community. What it may not do, however, is to impose 
its preference for certain speakers on the private market- 
place by shutting out other speakers. Although the Gov- 
ernment admittedly may prefer not to commit its own 
resources to assist broadcasters, the First Amendment does 
not allow it to say that it is willing to aid broadcast sta- 


tions at the expense of cable operators and programmers, 
but not at its own expense. 


II. The other principal argument pressed by the Gov- 
ernment is that the law, in the end, really does not sup- 
press much speech. The Court need not reach this ques- 
tion because the Government, by failing to fit the law to 
a genuine, important interest, has already failed the ap- 
plicable intermediate-scrutiny test. A law that unneces- 
arily burdens speech cannot be rescued by asserting that 
the unnecessary burden is not all that burdensome. But, 
in any case, the assertion by the Government is wrong. 
Most subscribers are served by cable systems with no 
available capacity—a fact wholly ignored by Judge 
Sporkin—and the forced carriage of even one of two 
broadcast stations forecloses a significant amount of 
speech. On those channels, subscribers are completely 


15 


shut off from programming that, without government in- 
terference, they would be able to receive, while cable pro- 
grammers, in turn, are completely shut off from reaching 
potential viewers. And First Amendment principles bar 
the Government from dismissing this obstruction merely 
by sayings that subscribers get enough speech from other 
programmers or that programmers have access to enough 
viewers in other places. See, e.g., Schneider v. New 
Jersey, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939). 


ARGUMENT 


The central issue in this case is whether the federal 
government, consistent with the First Amendment, may 
order cable systems to discriminate in favor of local 
broadcast stations at the expense of cable programmers. 
It is thus essential, at the outset, to recognize that this 
kind of forced discrimination is presumptively unconstitu- 
tional. As this Court has frequently observed, “the con- 
cept that government may restrict the speech of some 
elements of our society in order to enhance the relative 
voice of others is wholly foreign to the First Amendment.” 
Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. at 48-49. See First National 
Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, 435 U.S. at 780-81 (same); 
Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. at 426 n.7 (same); Citizens 
Against Rent Control/Coalition for Fair Housing v. City 
of Berkeley, 454 US. 290, 295-96 (1981) (same); 
Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Public Utilities Commission 
of California, 475 U.S. 1, 14 (1986) (plurality opinion) 
(same); id. at 24 (Marshall, J., concurring in judgment) 
(same). See also San Francisco County Democratic 
Central Committee v. Eu, 792 F.2d 802, 818 (9th Cir. 
1986) (“[w]e must reject the argument that one person’s 
First Amendment rights may be restricted in order to 
enhance the rights of another” ).*

Although Judge Sporkin suggested that cable systems 
would refuse to carry broadcast stations for anticompeti- 
tive reasons (J.S. App. 8a-14a), the must-carry law can- 
not be sustained on that basis. Whether the concern is 
over vertical integration of cable operators and cable pro-

desire to carry cable programming of greater interest to 
its subscribers—is an impermissible means of addressing 
any such interest. 


A. The lack of fit between the law and any interest in 
protecting against anticompetitive activity is readily seen 
with respect to any concern about vertical integration 
between cable operators and programmers. Judge Sporkin 
noted the fact that some vertical integration exists (J.S. 
App. 8a), but he made no effort to assess whether the law 
was tailored to that interest. Had he made such an in- 
quiry, he would first have had to come to grips with the 
fact that vertical integration is dealt with in separate pro- 
visions of the 1992 Cable Act. See 47 U.S.C. §§ 533 
(f)(1)(B), 536, 548(b) & (c). Those provisions, with 
their implementing regulations, limit operators to specified 
capacity that may be filled with programming in which 
they have an interest, bar certain exclusive distribution 
agreements and discrimination against non-integrated pro- 
grammers, and prohibit various unfair competitive practices 
by integrated cable programmers and operators. See Sec- 
ond Annual Report, Jn re Annual Assessment of the Status 
of Competition in the Market for the Delivery of Video 
Programming, CS Docket No. 95-61, 44 157-172 (Dec. 
11, 1995). Judge Sporkin utterly failed to explain why, 
despite those targeted restrictions, Congress would have 
thought that the must-carry law—which, of course, makes 


25 


no reference, by its terms, to vertical integration at all— 
was needed to address some (undefined) problem still to 
be solved. Cf. McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm'n, 115 
S. Ct. 1511, 1520-21 & n.12 (1995) (government inter- 
est in challenged statute is weakened by fact that the same 
interest is already directly attacked by another statute). 
It is also self-evident that the must-carry law is not in 
any way tailored to a problem with vertical integration. 
Although Congress expressed concern that “cable opera-

[t}his could make it more difficult for noncable-affiliated

B. The assertion of an interest in promoting individ- 
ual stations for their own sake also raises additional prob- 
lems under the First Amendment. Whatever the asserted

does not critically harm another, longstanding medium. 
As an end in itself, by contrast, the discrimination does 
nothing other than declare that the speech of broadcast 
Stations is—in the eyes of the Government—more impor- 
tant than the speech of cable programmers. It is that sort 


of judgment that government is generally not free to 
make.” 


A law that favors certain speakers as an end in itself

motivation for the discrimination is a preference for what 
the favored speakers will say. Although discrimination by 
class reduces that risk somewhat (and thus allows for less 
strict, though still heightened, review), see Turner, 114 
S. Ct. at 2467-69, it is not implausible to think that gov- 
ernment officials, in deciding to prefer one category of 
speakers (broadcast stations) over another (cable pro-

programming that they offer. And, even if government

IV. THE MUST-CARRY LAW IS A SUBSTANTIAL RE- 
STRICTION ON FREEDOM OF SPEECH 


The Government has repeatedly argued, during the 
course of this case, that the must-carry law really does 
not burden a great deal of speech. But this argument is 
unavailing for several reasons. In the first place, even if 
it were correct, it is irrelevant: because there is no genu- 
ine, substantial interest to support the sweeping reach of 
the must-carry law, the Government cannot excuse the un- 
necessary intrusion on speech by saying that it is only 
a modest burden. And, in any event, the argument is in- 
correct. The must-carry law restricts the programming 
available to two out of every three cable subscribers across 
the country.

at 2469, 2470. Here, as we have discussed, there is no

done so in this law. It has required every cable system to 
carry every qualifying local broadcast station (up to the 
cap), even if the result is to exclude other desired pro-

possible legitimate response to the problem, and it must 
be struck down.
