CONSTITUTIONAL AND STATUTORY PROVISIONS 


The First Amendment to the United States Constitution 
provides in pertinent part: “Congress shall make no law * * * 
abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press.” Relevant 
provisions of the Cable Television Consumer Protection and 
Competition Act of 1992 (“the 1992 Cable Act” or “the Act”), 
are reprinted at J.S. App. 113a-143a. 


STATEMENT 


Discovery Communications, Inc., and The Learning Channel 
concur in the other appellants’ statement of the case. 


INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Appellants submit this separate brief to emphasize two 
points. First, section 5 of the 1992 Cable Act, which affords a 


must-carry preference to public broadcasters, is 
unconstitutional under any level of First Amendment scrutiny. 
Second, sections 4 and 5 are both unconstitutional under the 
“narrow tailoring” prong of the O’Brien test because switch- 
and-antenna capacity represents a workable less restrictive 
alternative to must-carry. 


A. In enacting must-carry, Congress has mandated that 
cable operators prefer one set of speakers (television 
broadcasters) over another set of speakers (cable programmers), 
without regard to the viewing preferences of cable subscribers 
or the editorial decisions of the operators themselves. In the 
parties’ initial appearance before this Court, defendants sought 
to justify this discrimination on a ground that was, at least in 
theory, substantial: preserving the existing system of free over- 
the-air television for the 40% of TV viewers who do not 
subscribe to cable. 

On remand, defendants conceded that the local broadcast- 
ing system is in no jeopardy from cable and that the public’s 
access to “free TV” is not at risk. Defendants accordingly 


3 


urged, and Judge Sporkin adopted, a new rationale for must- 
carry: enhancing the financial well-being of the relatively small 
subset of broadcasters — generally, little-watched UHF and 
third-tier public stations in heavily-saturated markets — that 
actually elect must-carry. As Judge Williams has shown, this 
new rationale is not the objective Congress posited, and it is 
both circular and fundamentally illegitimate. 


The public broadcasters made the same strategic retreat as 
the Government and the commercial broadcasters, although 
their new rationale has a slightly different spin. The evidence 
on remand indisputably established that (1) the public 
broadcasting system is in excellent financial health and grew at 
a strong rate in the absence of must-carry; and (2) the vast 
majority of public stations would be carried by cable operators 
without government intervention. Indeed, the public 
broadcasters could not identify a single station that has failed, 
gone dark, or suffered cognizable harm from an adverse 
Carriage decision. 

Faced with this evidence, the public broadcasters 
abandoned any pretense of proving systemic harm, asserting 
instead a congressional objective to promote “universal access” 
to public television. By “universal access,” they mean carriage 
of each and every, present or future, public station on all cable 
systems, at the expense of competing cable networks (like 
Discovery and TLC) that offer similar documentary and 
educational programming. This theory is a more candid 
version of the “more is better” rationale that infects defendants’ 
submission generally. But as articulated by the public 
broadcasters, it is essentially content-based, for it rests on the 
assertion that public programming is “different” or “better” and 
thus worthy of maximum public support. 

At the end of the day, the public broadcasters have not 


shown, and do not contend, that must-carry is necessary to 
preserve the health of the public broadcast system — that is, the 


4 


ability of public stations to present a diverse mix of 
programming to over-the-air viewers nationwide. Rather, they 
seek mandatory carriage on cable systems in order to expand 
their reach to viewers who are unable to receive their signals, 
or who receive them poorly, thus realizing more fully their goal 
of “universal access.” In demanding “universal access” to 
cable viewers at the expense of competing cable programmers, 
the public broadcasters are seeking a subsidy that Congress 
cannot grant consistently with the First Amendment. 


B. Even if the defendants could identify a legitimate and 
substantial governmental interest, a means of achieving it 
without discriminating against cable programmers is readily 
available — education of cable subscribers in the acquisition 
and use of an inexpensive input-selector (“A/B”) switch. 
Together with a receiving antenna, this switch enables cable 
subscribers to receive broadcast stations not carried on their 
cable system to the same extent such stations are available to 
their neighbors who do not subscribe to cable. 


The availability of switch-and-antenna capability as a 
preferable alternative to must-carry rules was recognized by the 
D.C. Circuit in Quincy Cable TV, Inc. v. FCC, 768 F.2d 1434 
(1985), cert. denied, 476 U.S. 1169 (1986), adopted by the FCC 
in its 1986 and 1987 Reports reinstating a more limited set of 
“interim” must-carry rules, and embraced again by the D.C. 
Circuit when rejecting those interim rules in Century 
Communications Corp. v. FCC, 835 F.2d 292 (1987), cert. 
denied, 486 U.S. 1032 (1988). Prior approval of switch-and- 
antenna capability by the FCC and a unanimous D.C. Circuit 
surely creates a prima facie case that it is an acceptable 
alternative to any must-carry scheme. 


As Judge Williams explains, the evidence on remand over- 
whelmingly confirms this conclusion. Switching from cable to 
off-air reception is a simple exercise entailing no significant 
cost, in time or money, for the viewer. Thousands of cable

subscribers successfully used A/B switches in 1993 to watch 
broadcasters dropped from cable during a “retransmission 
consent” dispute. And A/B switches are used effortlessly by 
millions of subscribers to DBS and other distribution services. 


Defendants oppose switch-and-antenna capacity as an 
alternative to must-carry for two main reasons. They contend 
that some cable subscribers may be too lazy or disinterested to 
acquire A/B switches; and they urge that, even with an A/B 
switch, some subscribers may be unable to receive a broadcast 
signal because of distance or terrain obstacles. However, as 
Judge Williams points out, possible consumer lethargy cannot 
be a legitimate basis for impairing cable programmers’ First 
Amendment rights. And while broadcasters may have a fair 
claim to Government assistance to ensure their access to 
viewers they can reach over-the-air (which is all that their 
broadcast license entitles them to), commandeering cable 
channels to extend broadcast signals to viewers they could not 
otherwise reach stands on a wholly different footing. This 
latter objective, which underlies all the defendants’ submis- 
sions below, is a fundamentally illegitimate preference for the 
financial interests of broadcasters over the First Amendment 
rights of competing cable speakers. 

ARGUMENT 


I. THE MUST-CARRY PROVISIONS HAVE 
ADVERSELY AFFECTED PLAINTIFFS’ 
EXERCISE OF THEIR FIRST AMENDMENT 
RIGHTS 


Discovery Communications, Inc. (“DCT”) is a cable pro- 
grammer: it creates video programming and distributes that 
material through cable systems nationwide. It currently 
operates two major programming networks: The Discovery 
Channel and The Learning Channel. Both are dedicated to the 
provision of high-quality documentary and educational 


6 


programming aimed at people who watch television to broaden 
their understanding of the world. 


The Discovery Channel (“Discovery”) was founded in 
1985 and is now a well-established basic cable network with 
more than 63 million subscribers. In a November 1994 survey, 
Discovery ranked first among basic cable networks in terms of 
perceived value and importance to viewers. J.A. 984. DCI 
acquired The Learning Channel (“TLC”) in early 1991, 
invested more than $40 million to upgrade its programming, 
and relaunched it in October 1991. J.A. 984-985. In a Novem- 
ber 1994 survey, TLC ranked first among mid-sized cable 
networks in terms of perceived value and importance to 
viewers. Similarly, in a December 1994 survey of cable 
executives, TLC ranked first among emerging cable networks 
in terms of the overall appeal of its programming. J.A. 985. 


TLC incurred operating losses in 1991, 1992, 1993, and 
1994. It is now carried on about 20% of U.S. cable systems 
and has about 32.4 million paying subscribers. As a start-up 
network, TLC usually competes for one of the last available 
channels (if any) on a cable system. In order to achieve 
financial viability, it is essential that TLC increase its 
subscribership by obtaining carriage on additional cable 
systems. J.A. 985. 


The must-carry provisions have harmed TLC, and to a 
lesser extent Discovery, in a variety of ways. TLC and 
Discovery suffer First Amendment injury whenever a cable 
system not now carrying them is forced to add a must-carry 
broadcaster. Whether or not Discovery and TLC would enjoy 
a certainty of carriage absent the must-carry law, the addition 
of each marginal broadcaster reduces the number of slots for 
which Discovery and TLC may compete. This discriminatory 
impairment of cable programmers’ ability to reach an audience 
constitutes First Amendment injury per se.

Il. DEFENDANTS HAVE FAILED TO PROVE A 
SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST 


A. The Government’s Actual Interest, Far From 
Being “Substantial,” Is Illegitimate 


When initially before this Court, the Government’s defense 
of must-carry rested on the submission that, absent the statute, 
“the economic viability of free local broadcast television * * * 
will be seriously jeopardized” (Act § 2(a)(16)). This Court 
took defendants at their word. “[I]n applying O’Brien 
scrutiny,” Justice Kennedy stated, “we must first ask whether 
the Government has adequately shown that the economic health 
of local broadcasting is in genuine jeopardy and in need of the 
protections afforded by must-carry.” Turner Broadcasting 
System, Inc. v. FCC, 114 S.Ct. 2445, 2470 (1994). 


Put to the proof, defendants on remand completely 
changed their characterization of the “governmental interest” 
alleged to support must-carry. They explicitly abandoned any 
contention that the system of local broadcasting is at risk, or 
that must-carry is needed to ensure the existence of “free TV” 
for viewers who do not subscribe to cable. Instead, defendants 
posited a more modest objective, newly coined to match their 
evidentiary showing: “protection of financially weak stations” 
with a view to ensuring “the widest possible dissemination of 
information from diverse and antagonistic sources.” Govt. 
Opp. Br. 6, 13; NAB Opp. Br. 61, 63-64, 69; Pub. Opp. Br. 7, 
24. 


Defendants on remand offered varied formulations of this 
new theme. The public broadcasters said that Congress aimed 
to promote “universal access” to all public stations. Pub. Opp. 
Br. 24. At times, NAB contended that Congress legislated “to 


9 


prevent a material reduction in the diversity of [broadcast] 
programming” or in “the quality of broadcast service.” NAB 
Opp. Br. 18-19, 69. At other times, NAB asserted that 
Congress could permissibly legislate “to maintain, or even 
enhance, the quantity and quality of local broadcasting 
— Id. at 61, 64 (emphasis add- 


Missing from all these formulations was any contention 
that the system of local broadcasting is at risk. Indeed, 
defendants could concede the system’s perpetual health and 
still advance the argument they made below — that must-carry 
encourages entry of more stations, helps those stations offer 
more programming, enables that programming to be of higher 
quality, and thus promotes “the widest possible dissemination 
of information.” Govt. Opp. Br. 13 (emphasis original). 
Reduced to its essentials, defendants’ justification for must- 
carry was simply that “more is better.” 


Judge Sporkin largely accepted defendants’ eleventh-hour 
submissions concerning this supposed “governmental interest.” 
He deemed it irrelevant that “the economic health of the broad- 
casting industry as a whole is not in genuine jeopardy.” J.S. 
App. 8a-9a n.11 (emphasis added). “The relevant inquiry,” he 
said, concerns the health “of those broadcasters protected by 
the must-carry provisions,” that is, the relatively small number 
of stations that actually elect must-carry instead of demanding 
payment for retransmission consent. 


Defendants have retreated to an indefensible position, and 
Judge Sporkin followed them there. The governmental interest 
that defendants now assert is not the interest that Congress 
identified, and it is certainly not the interest whose genuineness 
and substantiality this Court required the Government to 
demonstrate on remand. Under defendants’ revisionist view, 
must-carry seeks merely to enhance the prosperity of some 
individual broadcasters — existing stations with minimal 


10 


viewership, and potential new entrants that find guaranteed 
cable carriage advantageous in securing investment financing. 
But it cannot plausibly be contended that maximizing the 
fortunes of every would-be broadcaster is necessary to preserve 
the health of the /ocal broadcast system. By many measures, 
this country’s economic “system” is now in good health. But 
the system’s strength does not hinge on the permanent financial 
viability »* every single entrepreneur. 

As compared with the interest that the Government 
advance is trivial or worse. The Government theoretically has 
a substantial interest in preserving the system of free, over-the- 
air broadcasting from destructive, anticompetitive action. But 
that interest is not implicated here because the systemic risk 
that Congress hypothesized is “merely conjectural,” not real. 
Turner, 114 S.Ct. at 2470. In effect, must-carry is a mere form 
of wealth redistribution — a subsidy for marginal individual 
broadcasters, funded by a tax on cable speakers and their 
subscribers. The desire to effect such a wealth transfer does not 
constitute a “substantial government interest” justifying 
infringement of First Amendment rights. “Indeed,” as the D.C. 
Circuit has previously held, “were the individual broadcasters 
themselves the object of [must-carry’s] favors, the objective 
itself would be fundamentally illegitimate.” Quincy Cable TV, 
supra, 768 F.2d at 1460. 


In any event, this Court should hold the Government to the 
standard that Congress enunciated. The must-carry law is 
unconstitutional because the system of local broadcasting is not 
in jeopardy. Defendants’ new-found “governmental interest” 
is a transparent attempt to turn the effect of must-carry into its 
purpose. This Court has previously refused to allow parties to 
evade their burden under First Amendment scrutiny by equating 
the effects of the statute with the governmental interest at stake. 
Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. New York State Criminal Victims Bd., 


ll 


502 U.S. 105, 120 (1991) (disapproving attempt to “take[] the 
effect of the statute and posit{] that effect as the State’s 
interest”). 
B. The Public Broadcasters Have Not Shown A 
Substantial Governmental Interest 


In this brief, we rely largely on our co-plaintiffs’ submis- 
sions to show the error of defendants’ assertions concerning the 
health of broadcasting generally. We will, however, address 
certain allegations of harm advanced by the public broad- 
casters. Discovery and TLC produce educational and 
documentary programming that is highly valued by viewers. 
Public stations pride themselves on offering similar fare, and 
they compete directly with Discovery and TLC for viewers. 

1. The Public Broadcasting System Thrived 
In The Absence Of Must-Carry 

Public broadcasting has enjoyed continuous growth since 
its inception, with the number of public stations increasing 
every year since 1953. J.A. 834-835, 851-853. From 1985 to 
1992 — a period without must-carry rules — the number of 
public TV stations increased from 316 to 368. J.A. 329, 852- 
853. Public broadcasters’ member revenues during these years 
grew faster than rate of inflation.’ Thus, the public 
broadcasting industry was generally healthy and growing. See 
also Public Television Service and Structure Analysis (prepared 
for APTS) (May 1991), J.A. 2112-2113.

II. “SWITCH-AND-ANTENNA” CAPABILITY IS A 
CONSTITUTIONALLY ACCEPTABLE, LESS 
RESTRICTIVE ALTERNATIVE TO ANY 
MUST-CARRY REGIME 


Broadcast television, by its nature, requires that viewers 
install antennas to receive signals over-the-air. Americans for 
decades have used receiving antennas to watch television 
without the benefit of cable service, and approximately 38% of 
the public today does not subscribe to cable. J.A. 763-764: see 
H.R. Rep. 102-628, 102d Cong., 2d Sess. 30 (1992) (noting that 
cable passed 95% of households in 1992, while only 60% sub- 
scribed). At least 36 million U.S. households are currently 
using antennas to view television off-the-air. J.A. 764. 


The advent of cable created the potential for impairing 
subscribers’ off-air reception capacity, since antennas were 
often disconnected from the back of the TV set when cable was 
installed. It has always been recognized, however, that a 
simple switch — called an “input selector” or “A/B switch” — 
permits the antenna to remain in place and thus enables a cable 
subscriber to alternate at will between cable and off-air 
reception of broadcast signals. By use of this switch, a cable 
subscriber is free to choose “between cable’s offerings and all 


18 


the television programming that would have been available to 
the viewer if cable had never been installed.” J.S. App. 103a 
(Williams, J., dissenting). 

We submit that switch-and-antenna capability — and any 
measures that Congress reasonably believes would encourage 
consumers’ convenient use of it — represents a constitutionally 
permissible, less restrictive alternative to any must-carry 
regime. There is no justification for impairing cable speakers’ 
First Amendment rights if subscribers can readily view off-air 
any broadcasters that cable systems decline to carry. 

In the pages that follow, we will first show that the FCC 
and the D.C. Circuit have previously approved switch-and- 
antenna capability as a constitutionally-preferable alternative to 
must-carry. Second, we will show that the evidence on remand 
— including expert testimony, practical experience in the cable 
industry, and successful use of A/B switches in such alternative 
distribution technologies as DBS and MMDS — confirms that 
the Commission and the D.C. Circuit were correct in embracing 
this option. Third, we will show that Judge Sporkin erred in 
discerning “substantial evidence” to support Congress’s rejec- 
tion of A/B switches in the 1992 Act. As Judge Williams 
correctly concluded in his dissent (J.S. App. 41a), the evidence 
overwhelmingly demonstrates that 


Every cable household can secure as good access to 
over-the-air television as it had before adopting cable 
simply by (1) retaining or acquiring the sort of 
antennae that the uncabled 40% of households 
necessarily use, and (2) operating either an input 
selector switch on their “remote” or an “A/B” switch 
that can be acquired at a local hardware store for a 
trivial sum.

A. The FCC And The D.C. Circuit Have 
Previously Approved Switch-And-Antenna 
Capability As A Less Restrictive Alternative 


1. The Quincy Decision. In an opinion written by Judge 
Wright and joined by Judge (now Justice) Ginsburg and Judge 
Bork, the court of appeals unanimously held the FCC’s original 
must-carry rules unconstitutional. Quincy Cable TV, supra, 
768 F.2d 1434. The FCC’s defense of those rules rested on the 
assumption that “cable subscribers would cease to view local 
television signals unless such signals were carried by the cable 
system” (768 F.2d at 1441). But “connection of the cable,” the 
court noted, “typically has no direct effect at all on receipt of 
UHF signals.” And while the cable was usually attached to the 
receiver's VHF input terminal, the FCC acknowledged that “an 
inexpensive switch (the ‘A/B switch’) would enable a viewer 
to alternate between cable and off-air VHF signals.” The court 
thus observed that, “in principle, a cable subscriber with little 
or no effort could still view local broadcasts even without the 
benefit of the must-carry rules” (ibid.). 


When first issuing must-carry rules in 1965, the FCC had 
cited possible impediments to successful use of A/B switches. 
See 768 F.2d at 1441 (citations omitted). But “[ijn the two 
decades since the rules’ original promulgation,” the FCC had 
never reexamined what the court described as “the admittedly 
speculative links in [its] chain of reasoning” (id. at 1457). The 
court thus held that must-carry could survive First Amendment 
scrutiny only if the FCC provided support for the two assump- 
tions that were “the linchpins of its analysis” (ibid.): 


(1) that without protective regulations cable 
subscribers would cease to view locally available 
over-the-air television either because they would 
disconnect their antennas or because the inconve- 
nience of switching would deter them; and (2) that 
even if some cable subscribers did abandon local 


20 


television, they would do so in sufficient numbers to 
affect the economic vitality of local broadcasting. 


2. The FCC’s Response To Quincy. Following notice- 
and-comment rulemaking, the FCC reconsidered its earlier 
pronouncements and adopted a “two-part regulatory program 
that eventually will eliminate the need for cable television 
mandatory signal carriage regulation.” See Amendment of Part 
76 of the Commission's Rules Concerning Carriage of 
Television Broadcast Signals by Cable Television Systems, | 
FCC Red. 864 (1986), ¥ 1 (“1986 Report”), reconsidered in 
part, 2 FCC Red. 3593 (1987) (“1987 Report”). The first part 
of this program “require[d] cable systems to offer subscribers 
input selector switches for use with antennas and to conduct a 
consumer education program concerning * * * the need for 
maintaining off-the-air reception capability.” The second part 
consisted of interim must-carry rules designed to “provide an 
orderly transition to a new environment.” 1986 Report { 1. 


The FCC concluded that use of antennas, coupled where 
necessary with A/B switches, did indeed offer the best long- 
term method of ensuring viewers’ access to local broadcasters 
that cable systems do not voluntarily carry. It found that an 
AJB switch is a “low-cost item” whose “installation costs will 
be minimal” (1986 Report § 215). It also noted the “relatively 
low cost and simple installation of indoor antennas,” available 
in set-top and more sensitive attic designs; such antennas

prohibited (J 166). The FCC considered a variety of technical 
objections to A/B switches, such as possible signal leakage or 
“ghosting” caused by improper switch installation or by 
inadequate isolation between components. But the 
Commission found these objections “overstated” and conclud- 
ed that such problems could easily be solved by equipment 
redesigns pursuant to FCC-mandated standards (f¥J 142, 165).

B. Expert Testimony And Practical Experience 
Prove The Feasibility Of The Switch-And- 
Antenna Alternative 

Prior approval of switch-and-antenna capability by both the 

FCC and a unanimous D.C. Circuit surely creates a prima facie 
case that it is a constitutionally acceptable, less restrictive alter- 
native to a must-carry scheme. The evidence introduced on 
remand overwhelmingly confirms that conclusion. The 
evidence shows that switching from cable to off-air reception 
of TV signals is a simple exercise that entails no significant 
cost, in time or money, for the viewer. With a minimum of 
effort, cable subscribers can watch — in precisely the same 
manner as they did before cable existed — any broadcast sta- 
tions that their cable system chooses not to carry. 


1. Switching To Off-Air Reception. Although a cable 
subscriber can switch to antenna reception by manually 
reconnecting wires on the back of the TV set, the most 
convenient way to do so is by activating an “input selector” or 
“A/B” switch. “The record shows that most cable-ready TV 
receivers now on the market have built-in input selector 
switches operable by remote control.” J.S. App. 104a 
(Williams, J., dissenting). As a rule, therefore, cable 
subscribers would have to acquire stand-alone A/B switches for 
use only with older or less-expensive TV sets. 

When needed, stand-alone A/B switches are readily 
available at modest cost from a wide variety of electronics and 
hardware stores, including such national retail chains as Radio

Shack and Circuit City. J.A. 1221. All A/B switches currently 
marketed are high-isolation switches intended specifically for 
use with cable television. The packaging usually bears the 
following label: “This device is verified to comply with FCC 
rule Part 15 for use with cable television service.” J.A. 1221. 

Stand-alone A/B switches come in various shapes and 
sizes and offer a variety of features. The simplest A/B switch 
is a mechanical device, operated manually by pushing a button 
on the switch. J.A. 1222-1223. High-quality switches of this

2. Reception Of Off-Air Signals Via Antenna. A 
receiving antenna is always needed to receive television signals

off-the-air, whether or not the viewer subscribes to cable. J.A.

There are three main types of antenna: “master” antennas, 
“indoor” antennas, and “outdoor” antennas. J.A. 764. “Mas-

condominium complexes to feed broadcast signals to individual 
dwelling units. They are used where, for convenience or 
aesthetic reasons, it is impractical for residents to install their 
own outdoor antennas. J.A. 764. 

“Indoor” antennas typically consist of a wire loop for the 
UHF band and familiar “rabbit ears” for the VHF band. J.A. 
764. These set-top antennas enable most households within a 
station’s Grade A contour — typically, within 30-45 miles of 
the transmitter — to receive high-quality broadcast signals. 
J.A. 762-764; 1986 Report § 166. NAB itself recently noted 
that “more than 50% of all TV sets in the U.S. depend on set- 
top antennas to receive TV programming.” See J.A. 767-768. 


“Outdoor” antennas may be mounted within an attic, for 
convenience or aesthetic reasons, or attached to a mast beside 
a dwelling. J.A. 765; 1986 Report 4 166 (attic antennas “give 
additional off-the-air reception capability”). “Outdoor” 
antennas enable most households within a station’s Grade B 
contour — typically, within 55-60 miles of the transmitter — 
to receive high-quality broadcast signals. J.A. 762-763, 765. 

Antenna reception provides much better picture quality 
now than it did 30 years ago, owing to improvements in TV 
receivers and antenna design. Because of increased tuner 
sensitivity, modern receivers can present high-quality pictures 
— both in the UHF and VHF ranges — despite relatively low

signal inputs from the antenna. J.A. 766. Amplifiers are now 
available to boost the antenna signal, permitting use of indoor 
antennas where outdoor antennas would once have been 
required. J.A. 766. The efficiency or “gain” of outdoor

use of better transformers. J.A. 765. And further improve- 
ments in antenna design can be expected in the future. Noting

tively captured a 70% share of the total cable audience; after a 
month off cable (and with distribution of A/B switches), they 
had a share of 67%-69% of the total cable audience.” J.S. App

young people,” said one station owner. “They didn’t know 
there were other ways to watch TV than through the cable.” 
Warren's Cable Regulation Monitor, Nov. 29, 1993, at 7; 
Cable World, Nov. 8, 1993, at 12. The Corpus Christi experi- 
ence shows that substantial numbers of A/B switches can be 
installed properly in a short period of time, without meaningful 
damage to broadcast stations’ viewership. 

The fact that other media outlets depend heavily on 
antenna-selector switches to provide access to local broadcast 
programming provides further evidence of the switches’ 
practical utility. One such industry is the direct broadcast 
satellite (“DBS”) industry, which currently has three major 
providers: Primestar, DirecTV and USSB. See FCC, First 
Annual Report to Congress Assessing the Status of Competition

Subscribers to DBS service “must use an A/B switch to

customers’ earth-station antennas; because they offer a single, 
nationwide programming line-up, they cannot carry broadcast 
stations in each local market. For that reason, many DBS 
subscribers maintain antennas and switch as desired between 
satellite and local broadcast reception. Depending on the 
service provider, this switching capacity may be furnished as 
part of the satellite receiver or through a stand-alone A/B

customers in 1994, and there were projected to be 2.26 million

New Media, Feb. 20, 1995, at 4. 


Another industry that relies heavily on A/B switches is 
terrestrial point-to-multi-point microwave (“MMDS” or 
“wireless cable”). Because MMDS service has limited channel 
capacity — 24 to 33 channels are typical — operators usually 
carry few, if any, local broadcast stations. As is true for DBS 
customers, MMDS subscribers commonly maintain their own

microwave or off-air reception as they wish. J.A. 1241-1242. 
MMDS providers served 430,000 subscribers in 1994 and were

Associates, Marketing New Media, Feb. 20, 1995, at 4.

homes to obtain access to local broadcasts, and each of these

switch-and-antenna capacity. And if the need for switches and

technologies, there is no reason to believe it cannot be 
accommodated by the broadcasting industry as well.°

In sum, the evidence establishes that switch-and-antenna

C. Congress’s Dismissal Of The Switch-And- 
Antenna Alternative Was Not Supported By 
“Substantial Evidence” 


Judge Sporkin’s discussion of A/B switches, occupying 
two pages in his opinion (J.S. App. 23a-25a), is cursory at best. 
He catalogs six items of evidence introduced by defendants, 
wholly ignoring contrary findings by the FCC and the D.C. 

- Circuit, as well as voluminous evidence introduced by plaintiffs 
which showed defendants’ submissions to be misleading, 
outdated, and unreliable. With no analysis whatever, Judge 
Sporkin asserts that there was “substantial evidence” from 
which Congress “could reasonably conclude that these switches 
do not represent an equally effective alternative.” J.S. App. 
23a. 

As our co-plaintiffs explain in greater detail, Judge 
Sporkin’s “equally effective” test is legally erroneous. Judge 
Sporkin was able to find switch-and-antenna capacity “less 
effective” only because he had already posited, as the 


33 


governmental interest underlying must-carry, the conferral of 
maximum economic benefits on the particular broadcasters 
electing it. Judge Sporkin’s approach, moreover, looks only to 
one side of the equation — the effectiveness of the means 
chosen by the Government — ignoring the fact that the option 
deemed “more effective” imposes dramatically more severe 
restraints on protected speech. 


_ Apart from legal error, Judge Sporkin’s discussion of A/B 
switches fails on its own terms. The evidence on remand over- 
whelmingly demonstrates that A/B switches “work” in a 
technical sense and that switch-and-antenna capability is easily 
within the means of viewers who can afford cable. As Judge 
Williams explains (J.S. App. 104a-109a), defendants’ argument 
on remand — reduced to its essentials — consisted of two 
propositions. First, defendants contended that some unquan- 
tified number of cable subscribers would be too ignorant or too 
lazy to acquire and use A/B switches and antennas to view 
broadcast stations not carried on their cable system. Second, 
defendants urged that an additional unquantified number of 
cable subscribers may either lose access to, or receive a poorer 
quality picture from, dropped broadcasters because of terrain or 
man-made obstacles that affect cable subscribers in the same 
manner that these obstacles affect their neighbors who do not 
subscribe to cable. 


These arguments cannot possibly satisfy defendants’ 
burden of proof, for three main reasons. First, the 
constitutional rights of cable speakers cannot legitimately be 
impaired because of the possibility that some cable subscribers 
may be unwilling to make the modest effort needed to view 
broadcast programming off-the-air. By use of A/B switches, 
cable subscribers have the ability to receive such programming 
at a cost not significantly greater than the cost non-cable 
viewers must incur to see the same programming. If cable 
subscribers demur to this modest effort, it can only be because 


34 


they have no desire to watch the broadcasters that their cable 
systems decline to carry. If that is so, it is hard to understand 
how noncarriage of such stations could jeopardize the health of 
the local broadcast system. And in any event, “government- 
mandated carriage seems a rather astonishing solution to a 
problem now redefined as consumers’ occasional need to ‘get 
up”” from their chairs to flick a switch. J.S. App. 106a 
(Williams, J., dissenting). 

Second, protecting the public’s interest in “free TV” — by 
definition — means preserving the ability of TV households to 
view broadcasts they can receive over-the-air. It is undeniably 
true, as Congress found and defendants urged below, that 
mountains, skyscrapers, and other obstacles impair off-air 
reception in certain locations by blocking transmission of 
broadcast signals. But possible obstruction of over-the-air 
signals by natural and man-made impediments is an immutable 
characteristic of the broadcast medium; it has been a fact of life 
for broadcasting since its birth. The existence of these 
obstacles does not constitute a “harm” to broadcasting that 
Congress could legitimately seek to cure by impairing First 
Amendment rights: the health of the broadcast industry, as a 
matter of law, is simply not affected by viewers’ inability to 
view inherently non-viewable signals. Commandeering cable 
channels to extend broadcast stations’ reach to viewers who 
cannot otherwise receive their signals, or who receive them 
poorly, is a fundamentally illegitimate preference for the 
financial interests of broadcasters over the First Amendment 
rights of cable speakers. 

Third, even if one were to assume the legitimacy of the 
preceding two propositions, defendants below made absolutely 
no showing about the magnitude of these problems. 
Defendants bear the burden of proving that switch-and-antenna 
capability — which does not impair anyone’s First Amendment 
rights — fails adequately to serve the interests Congress 


35 


legitimately seeks to protect. Defendants below made no effort 
to show that substantial numbers of consumers would be af- 
flicted by supposed technological inertia, especially if Congress 
and the FCC took active steps to encourage use of A/B Switch- 
es. Nor did defendants quantify the proportion of cable house- 
holds that would suffer from reception problems despite use of 
an appropriate antenna; “from all that appears, [weak-signal 
areas] may represent a very small fraction of the country.” J.S. 
App. 109a (Williams, J., dissenting). In short, while it may be 
assumed that broadcasters would lose some viewers after being 
dropped from cable, defendants have not proven that they 
would lose a sufficient number of viewers to endanger their 
own survival, much less to jeopardize the health of the entire 
broadcast system.
