SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


CPA, et al., support the position of the United States 
that the decision of the court below be affirmed. CFA, et ai. , 
file this separate brief solely to address one specific issue that 
has arisen in this litigation: whether providing cable access 
for local commercial television stations based on a leasing 
mechanism would be a constitutionally acceptable less restric- 
tive alternative to must-carry. 


Turner Broadcasting System, Inc., et a/. ("Turner") 
argue that the existing "leased access" mandate in Section 612 
of the 1992 Cable Act, or perhaps an expanded variant, 
would be an effective, yet less restrictive, alternative to must- 
carry. Brief for Turner Broadcasting System at 57-58. Thus, 
Turner claims, must-carry cannot withstand constitutional 
scrutiny under the test established by this Court in Turner J. 
In his dissent below, Judge Williams acknowledges there may 
be deficiencies in the current statutory scheme, but contends 
that a hypothetical leased access or similar scheme could be 
enacted to afford unaffiliated programmers a non-discrimina- 
tory entitlement to access at a price set by an administrative 
agency. The availability of such a legislative option, he 
Suggests, renders must-carry unconstitutional. Turner Broad- 


casting Sys., Inc. v. FCC, 910 F. Supp. 734, 784 (D.D.C. 
1995). 


Under leased access, operators must make a certain 


3 


proportion of their capacity available to unaffiliated program- 
mers. Section 612 delineates the number of channels that 
must be set aside and establishes the terms and conditions 
under which they may be offered. 47 U.S.C. § 532. CFA, 
et al. show here that neither existing nor hypothetically im- 
proved versions of leased access can provide a workable, 
equally effective alternative to must-carry. 


Because it requires payment for leased access, Section 
612 is inherently incapable of achieving one of the critical 
congressional objectives of must-carry: preserving free, over- 
the-air local broadcast television.' This point is addressed 
in the government’s brief and is not further addressed here. 


That aside, under the cable industry's own interpreta- 
tion of Section 612, leased access could not be relied on to 
achieve any of the congressional goals underlying leased 
access: preserving free broadcast television, encouraging 
widespread dissemination of a multiplicity of programming, 
and promoting fair competition in programming. And, tell- 
ingly, while the cable industry enthusiastically advances 
leased access as an alternative to must carry in this case, it 
also has aggressively pursued a constitutional challenge 
elsewhere in the courts to any leased access responsibility. 


'The payment requirement for leased access would pose 
an especially insurmountable barrier for non-commercial 
television stations, which also are covered by must-carry 
under Section 615. 47 U.S.C. § 535. Neither Turner nor 
Judge Williams mention, much less explain, how leased 
access could possibly serve the special needs of public broad- 
casting. 


4 


Judge Williams’ proposal of a theoretical version of 
leased access that would provide a non-discriminatory entitle- 
ment to access at a government-established price also fails. 
What he envisions might well expand the number and, per- 
haps, the variety, of independent programming voices, but it 
is hardly a less restrictive alternative. Whatever the validity 
of cable industry objections to Section 612, such complaints 
would be much stronger as against the hypothetical regime 
which Judge Williams depicts. 


ARGUMENT 


I. UNDER CABLE OPERATORS’ OWN INTER- 
PRETATION OF LEASED ACCESS, IT 
CANNOT ACCOMPLISH ANY OF THE 
CONGRESSIONALLY ESTABLISHED 
OBJECTIVES OF MUST-CARRY. 


Congress declared that the must-carry provisions 
serve, inter alia, three interrelated interests: (1) preserving 
the benefits of free, over-the-air local broadcast television, (2) 
promoting the widespread dissemination of information from 
a multiplicity of sources, and (3) promoting fair competition 
in the market for television programming. Turner Broadcast- 
ing Sys., Inc. v. FCC, 114 §. Ct. 2445, 2469 (1994). To 
achieve these goals, local broadcasters must be carried on 
cable channels without incurring costs that would threaten 
their very viability. This programming must be made avail- 
able to such a substantial audience that it affords broadcasters 
the opportunity to attract advertising. And access must be 
beyond the manipulation of cable operators. 


As the government appellees show in their brief, must- 


5 


carry meets these criteria by requiring cable operators to 
carry local broadcasters for no fee, by requiring cable opera- 
tors to provide these channels to the entire cable audience, 
and by generally denying cable operators the right to decline 
to carry access programming. 


The cable industry's interpretation of leased access 
raises serious questions about its potential as an alternative 
to must-carry. In an ongoing proceeding before the FCC, 
cable operators have maintained that Section 612 permits 
them to provide leased access programming to the least 
popular program tiers with the narrowest of audiences; grants 
authority to manipulate fees to make access unaffordable to 
many classes of programmers; allows them to influence 
program selection by setting discriminatory costs, rates and 
terms; and permits them even to refuse to carry programming 
under certain circumstances. Thus the cable industry, while 
trumpeting leased access under Section 612 as an alternative 
to must-carry, interprets Section 612 in a way that would 
make it incapable of accomplishing any of must-carry’s 
objectives. 


A. Cable Operators Interpret Section 612 As 
Allowing Dissemination Of Leased Access 
Programming To The Narrowest Of Cable 
Audiences. 


To accomplish the fundamental congressional goals of 
must-carry, local commercial broadcast programming must 
be available to a broad cable audience for no extra fee. 
Without access to a substantial cable audience, local broad- 
casters may be unfairly disadvantaged from competing with 
cable and from attracting the advertising needed to maintain 


6 


their economic viability. Failure to reach the full cable 
audience also would frustrate the congressional goal of pro- 
moting the widespread dissemination of information from a 
multiplicity of sources. Must-carry achieves these goals by 
requiring cable operators to carry local commercial broad- 
caster channels as part of their basic service. 


The cable industry's claims that leased access can be 
an adequate substitute for must carry are belied by the posi- 
tions they continue to advance before the FCC with respect 
to the implementation of Section 612. Before that agency, the 
industry claims that it cannot be required under Section 612 
to provide leased access programming as part of the widely- 
seen basic tiers. See Reply Comments of Time Warner Cable, 
In Re Implementation of Sections of the Cable Television 
Consumer Protection and Competition Act of 1992: Rate 
Regulation, Leased Commercial Access (FCC Docket Nos. 
92-266, 96-60), filed May 31, 1996, at 26 ("Time Warner 
Reply Comments"); Comments of Tele-Communications, Inc. 
and Request for Further Reconsideration, In Re Implementa- 
tion of Sections of the Cable Television Consumer Protection 
and Competition Act of 1992: Rate Regulation, Leased Com- 
mercial Access (FCC Docket Nos. 92-266, 96-60), filed May 
15, 1996, at 21 ("TCI Comments"). Cable operators main- 
tain that leased access programmers have no statutory right 
to have a "genuine outlet" for their programming, and that 
Section 612 imposes "no obligation on operators...to ensure 
the delivery of leased programming to all or most of its 
subscribers...." Comments of the National Cable Television 
Association, In Re Implementation of Sections of the Cable 
Television Consumer Protection and Competition Act of 
1992: Rate Regulation, Leased Commercial Access (FCC 
Docket Nos. 92-266, 96-60), filed May 15, 1996, at 28-89 
("NCTA Comments"). Any mandatory tier placement, they 


7 


maintain, would interfere with a cable operator's program- 
ming editorial function, in violation of the First Amendment. 
Time Warner Reply Comments at 26. Cable operators main- 
tain that under Section 612 they could make leased-access 
programming available only as a special premium service, 
provided for an extra fee, a fee not subject to FCC regula- 
tion.?, NCTA Comments at 28. 


Particularly under such a narrow construction (which 
is still under consideration by the FCC), leased access could 
not be an equally effective means as must-carry of achieving 
Congress’ stated goals of preserving free broadcast television, 
promoting fair competition, and encouraging widespread 
dissemination of programming. 


B. Cable Operators Insist That Leased Access 
Allows Them To Discriminate Among And Even 


Reject Programming. 


Any alternative to must-carry would also have to 
prohibit cable operators from discriminating among or against 
local broadcast programming and from rejecting certain 
programming. Prohibiting discrimination is critical to the 
fundamental goals of must-carry, i.e., promoting fair compe- 
tition in the market for television programming and promoting 


“In addition, while basic tier charges are regulated by the 
FCC, premium charges are unregulated. 47 U.S.C. §623. 
The potential for cable operators to charge discriminatory 
premium fees for local commercial broadcasting under cable 
leased access further undermines its potential as an effective 
alternative to must-carry. 


8 


programming from a multiplicity of sources. 


To this end, the must-carry provisions require cable 
operators to allocate a certain number of channels on the 
system to local commercial and non-commercial broadcasters. 
47 U.S.C. §§ 534, 535. Cable operators may exercise discre- 
tion to choose among local commercial broadcast stations only 
when the number of qualified must-carry channels exceeds the 
allocation. 47 U.S.C. § 534(b)(2). 


Leased access, therefore, could only achieve even part 
of the goals of must-carry if it similarly requires non-discrim- 
inatory access by all local programmers who request to be 
carried. Cable operators argue, however, that leased access 
allows operators to discriminate among programmers and to 
reject programmers of certain classes. 


Both TCI and NCTA, for example, assert that the 
FCC cannot impose a first-come, first-served leasing require- 
ment on cable operators under Section 612 of the Act. NCTA 
Comments at 31; TCI Comments at 36. NCTA maintains that 
Congress did not intend to require operators to make leased 
access available on a non-discriminatory basis. NCTA Com- 
ments at 31. To the contrary, NCTA argues that Congress 
meant to "allow operators to establish price, terms and condi- 
tions that are discriminatory." /d. And while it is Section 
612’s similarity to regulatory schemes for common carriers 
that underlies Judge Williams suggestion that it could serve 
as an alternative to must-carry, TCI asserts that the legislative 
history of leased access specifically indicates that Section 612 
is not intended to operate as a common carrier regulation. 
TCI Comments at 36. 


9 


In fact, cable operators have specifically proposed that 
they be allowed to deny leased access to certain part-time 
programmers. See, e.g., Time-Warner Reply Comments at 
19 (arguing cable operators should not have to provide leased- 
access to programmers who request access for less than eight 
hours a day, seven days a week); TC] Comments at 33 (opera- 
tor should be able to deny leased access to a part-time pro- 
grammer entirely if it would require preempting a channel 
being used by an operator's affiliate). 


Under cable operators’ proposed interpretation, there- 
fore, leased access would allow operators to deny access to 
certain programmers and would authorize discrimination 
among programs. It thus could not be as effective as must- 
carry in achieving the congressional objectives of promoting 
a multiplicity of programming and fair competition. 


C. The Cable Industry, While Offering Leased 
Access As A Constitutionally Acceptable 
Alternative To Must Carry In This Case, Is 
Challenging Its Constitutionality Before The 
FCC And In The Courts. 


While Turner suggests to this Court that leased access 
provides a reasonable alternative to the allegedly constitution- 
ally defective must-carry provisions, wief of Turner Broad- 
casting System at 58, the cable industry is contending before 
the FCC and the courts that leased access itself is unconstitu- 
tional. In fact, in litigation involving most of the same 
industry parties now before this Court, the cable industry has 
challenged the constitutionality of any leased access provision. 
See Brief for Appellants at 63-70, Time Warner Entertainment 
Co. v. FCC, (D.C. Cir. 1995) (No. 93-5349). 


10 


Cable operators contend that leased access, like must- 
carry, violates the First Amendment. /d.; see also TCI 
Comments at 40-43. TCI, in fact, contends that the leased 
access provisions of the Cable Act have even more serious 
problems than the must-carry provisions because they seek to 
ensure access to a multiplicity of diverse sources, and thus 
are subject to the highest degree of First Amendment scruti- 
ny.’ TCI Comments at 41. 


il. A HYPOTHETICAL VERSION OF LEASED 
ACCESS THAT WOULD PROVIDE NON- 
DISCRIMINATORY ACCESS AT A SET 
ADMINISTRATIVE PRICE WOULD FACE 
EVEN STIFFER CONSTITUTIONAL 
CHALLENGES FROM CABLE OPERATORS 
THAN HAVE CURRENT LEASED ACCESS 
PROVISIONS AND IS NOT A LESS 
RESTRICTIVE ALTERNATIVE TO MUST- 
CARRY. 


Giving tacit recognition to the shortcomings of the 
existing leased access scheme enacted by Congress in Section 
612, Judge Williams suggests that a hypothetical, and truly 
non-discriminatory, leased access mechanism could be a 
suitable and less restrictive alternative to must carry. Turner, 
910 F. Supp. at 784. 


‘Cable operators also maintain that if an administrative 
agency sets fees at a level that does not provide full compen- 
sation for the value of the channels, such a fee results in a 
taking without just compensation in violation of the Fifth 
Amendment. See Time Warner Reply Comments at 24, n. 46; 
TCI Comments at 39.

Leaving aside the speculative nature of the claim that 
such a "pure" system realistically could be devised and enact- 
ed,‘ the very characteristics required for a truly non-discrimi- 
natory access scheme would make the system even more 
burdensome for cable operators to administer, and even more 
intrusive into their programming activities. Making an analo- 
gy to common-carrier type price and access regulations used 
for public utilities, Judge Williams contends that Congress 
could craft a scheme that presumably would serve all pro- 
grammers on a first-come first-served basis at regulated 
prices. But selecting programmers would pose hitherto 
unimagined problems, especially in the context of the First 
Amendment. Since, by definition, auctions could not be 
employed to select programmers, it would be necessary to 
devise an extremely complicated selection mechanism, and 
one which would likely be susceptible to manipulation as 
well. And the notion of devising common carrier or cost- 
based price regulation as a /ess-restrictive alternative is oxy- 
moronic. Indeed, in recently rejecting the cable industry's 
First Amendment challenge to the far less intrusive price 
regulations established under the 1992 Act, Judge Randolph 
contrasted them with the kind of regulations which would be 
needed under Judge Williams hypothetical regime. Judge 
Randolph said that 


The obvious alternative - holding a cust of 
service proceeding for each regulated cable 
system - would also cause operators to incur 


“Unlike Congress, Judge Williams did not confront the 
political, practical and technical obstacles to creating a truly 
non-discriminatory, price regulated service. He simply 
assumed it could be done.

expenses and would in any event be unwork- 
able in light of administrative burdens such a 
scheme would entail. 


Time Warner Entertainment Co, L.P v. FCC, 56 F.3d 151, 
186 (D.C. Cir. 1995). Certainly from the perspective of the 
cable industry, as well as for the reasons discussed above and 
in the government’s brief, a common-carrier-type provision 
would not be a constitutionally acceptable, less restrictive 
alternative. 


CONCLUSION 


Cable leased access cannot provide a constitutionally 
acceptable less restrictive alternative to must-carry. The 
judgment of the D.C. Circuit Court should be affirmed.*
