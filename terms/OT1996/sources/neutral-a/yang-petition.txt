STATEMENT 


1. Respondent and his wife are natives of the 
People’s Republic of China and citizens of Taiwan. 
App., infra, 13a. They were married in China in 1940 
(id. at 16a), emigrated to Taiwan in 1945 (see id. at 8a- 
9a), and had six children together (id. at 8a). 

In 1977, while in the United States as a visitor, 
respondent’s wife was introduced by a friend to an 
individual who told her that if she were single and 
willing to pay or “invest” $60,000, she could obtain 
United States citizenship or permanent residency. 
App., infra, 6a, 15a. The couple accordingly obtained a 
divorce in Taiwan, and in 1978 respondent’s wife 
returned to the United States and paid $60,000 (appar- 
ently obtained from respondent or from joint funds) to 
purchase a fraudulent United States birth certificate 
and passport in the name of Mary Wong. /d. at 6a-7a, 
16a, 18a. Respondent then married “Mary Wong” in 
Taiwan, and subsequently sought and obtained entry 
to the United States as a lawful permanent resident 
on the ground that he was married to a United States 
citizen. Id. at 7a, 16a-17a. 

Respondent and his wife thereafter successfully 
petitioned for immigrant status for four of their six 
adult children. App., infra, 16a-17a. One son pur- 
ported to marry one of the daughters and attempted to 
bring her into the United States as his wife; both 
were later prosecuted for that and other immigration 
fraud. See id. at 17a. Another daughter obtained 


6 


United States citizenship through her own marriage 
to a citizen. Ibid. The family resided together in 
California in a house purchased by respondent. Id. at 
19a. 

In 1982, respondent applied for naturalization as a 
United States citizen. App., infra, 7a. In his applica- 
tion, respondent claimed that his wife, “Mary Wong,” 
was a United States citizen by birth. In May 1984, 
respondent’s wife began to worry about immigration 
fraud. She therefore abandoned her identity as “Mary 
Wong,” and had her daughter file a petition to allow 
her to immigrate again, this time under her true 
name, as the parent of a United States citizen. In 
1985, respondent and “Mary Wong” obtained a divorce 
in California. Later, after the Immigration and Na- 
turalization Service (INS) commenced deportation 
proceedings against both respondent and his wife, 
they again remarried, this time using her true name. 
Id. at 7a-Sa, 17a-18a. 

2. In January 1992, the INS initiated deportation 
proceedings against respondent, partly on the ground 
that he had procured his initial entry into the United 
States by fraud or willful misrepresentation. App., 
infra, 13a; see 8 U.S.C. 1182(aX(6)(C)G) (formerly 8 
U.S.C. 1182(a)(19) (1988), 1251(a)1(A). Respondent 
conceded that he was deportable, but he sought a 
waiver of deportation under Section 241(a)(1)(H) of the 
Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), 8 U.S.C. 
1251 0a ICH). App., infra, 18a. Section 241(a)(1)(H) 
provides that the provisions of the INA relating to the 
deportation of aliens who sought to or did procure 
entry through fraud or willful misrepresentation of a 
material fact “may, in the discretion of the Attorney 
General, be waived” for an alien who (i) is the spouse, 
parent, or child of a United States citizen or lawful 


7 


permanent resident, and (ii) possessed an immigrant 
visa or equivalent documentation and was “otherwise 
admissible” to the United States at the time of entry. 

At the deportation hearing, respondent “claimed 
that [he] had believed in the propriety of [his] method 
of entering the United States,” and that he did not 
find it “unusual that [his] wife would adopt a different 
name, present another’s birth certificate in order to 
obtain immigration benefits, or be required to obtain a 
sham divorce.” App., infra, 8a. Respondent also 
claimed that he was indigent, and that he was not 
aware of his family’s other acts of immigration fraud, 
including the “marriage” between two of his children 
(both of whom resided with him), until after they had 
been discovered by the INS. Ibid. 

In October 1992, the immigration judge denied re- 
spondent’s request for a waiver. App., infra, 12a-25a. 
Relying on judicial precedent that had, in turn, relied 
on the Attorney General’s position concerning what 
factors should be considered in determining whether 
to waive deportation based on an initial fraud com- 
mitted on entry into the United States, the judge 
noted that he did] not take into account the original 
fraud perpetrated against the United States Govern- 
ment by [respondent]; that is, * * * respondent’s 
coming to this country and making claims falsely to 
be a spouse of a United States citizen.” Id. at 14a 
(citing Liwanag v. INS, 872 F.2d 685, 687 (5th Cir. 
1989)); see also App., infra, 2la. The immigration 
judge pointed out, however, that a waiver under 
Section 241(a)(1)(H) could “appropriately be denied if 
an alien continues in his fraud or in other subsequent 
acts from the original fraud or misrepresentations.” 
App., infra, 14a-15a (emphasis added). The immi- 
gration judge accordingly based his decision in part 


8 


on respondent’s acts of fraud after his initial entry. 
Id. at 20a, 23a, 24a-25a. In addition, the immigration 
judge relied on his conclusion that respondent’s 
testimony was “not * * * candid,” particularly in so 
far as he originally claimed indigency and a need for 
familial support, while after persistent questioning 
he admitted that he had “over $3 million in property 
and real estatel,] and liquid assets in cash, stocks, etc. 
of $700,000.” Id. at 23a-24a. 

The Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) affirmed 
the immigration judge’s decision. App., infra, 5a-11a. 
The BIA observed that, Hals with other discretionary 
applications for a waiver of deportability,” Section 
241(a)(1)(H) “necessitates a balancing of the alien’s 
undesirability as a permanent resident [against] the 
social and humane considerations present to 
determine whether according him such relief is in the 
best interest of this country.” App., infra, 10a (citing 
In re Marin, 16 I. & N. Dec. 581 (BIA 1978) 
(discussing waiver of deportation under INA § 212(c), 
8 U.S.C. 1182(c)). In this case, the BIA based its 
denial of relief on respondent’s “acts of immigration 
fraud before and after his 1978 entry into the United 
States” (including participation in his wife’s illegal 
immigration, two sham divorces, and intervening 
remarriage under a false name), and on his “apparent 
lack of candor during the deportation hearing regard- 
ing his knowledge of the significance of his fraud.” 
App., infra, 10a. Although the BIA recognized (id. at 
lla) that respondent had family and business ties to 
the United States that might have supported a waiv- 
er, it concluded (id. at 10a-1la) that those equities 
were outweighed by respondent’s “pattern of violating 
this country’s immigration laws through fraud.” Id. 
at 10a-Ila. 


9 


3. The court of appeals granted respondent's 
petition for review, vacated the BIA’s decision, and 
remanded the case for further proceedings. App., 
infra, la-4a. In so doing, the court applied its own 
version of the rule applied by the immigration judge 
(App., infra, 14a-15a) and the BIA (id. at 10a), that the 
initial fraud at entry should be disregarded in 
balancing the equities with respect to a waiver under 
Section 241(a)(1)(H). The court held that, in balanc- 
ing those equities in this case, the Board should not 
have considered respondent’s participation in his 
wife’s illegal entry into this country, including his 
sham divorces and marriages, because those acts 
were “inextricably intertwined with [respondent’s| 
own efforts to secure entry into the country and must 
be considered part of [his] initial fraud.” App., infra, 
2a. Furthermore, the court held that the Board 
should not have considered respondent’s fraudulent 
application for naturalization, which it held “must 
[also] be considered an extension of the initial fraud.” 
Id. at 2a-3a. The Court remanded the case, however, 
because it concluded that “being untruthful with the 
immigration court is an adverse factor which may be 
properly considered by the BIA.” /d. at 3a. 

The court denied the government’s petition for 
rehearing, and rejected a suggestion of rehearing en 
bane. App., infra, 26a. 


REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


The court of appeals has held that the Attorney 
General abused her discretion by refusing to waive 
the deportation of an alien who not only entered this 
country through fraud and misrepresentation, but 
also engaged in a pattern of serious immigration 
fraud both before and after that entry—culminating 


10 


in a fraudulent application for naturalization as a 
citizen of the United States. The Ninth Circuit’s 
decision ignores the text and history of the relevant 
provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act 
(INA), and it conflicts with the decisions of other 
courts of appeals. If uncorrected, the decision below 
will impede the proper and uniform administration of 
the immigration laws, thereby creating unfairness 
and imposing a serious practical burden on immi- 
gration authorities. It will also require the retention 
in this country of individuals whom Congress has 
declared to be deportable, and who the Attorney 
General believes are unworthy of a favorable exercise 
of her statutory discretion to waive deportation. The 
court of appeals’ error thus warrants review and 
correction by this Court. 

1. An alien who procured his initial entry into the 
United States “by fraud or [by] willfully misrepre- 
senting a material fact,” 8 U.S.C. 1182(a)(6)(C)(ji), is 
normally subject to deportation once the deception is 
discovered. 8 U.S.C. 1251(a)(1)(A). If the alien is the 
spouse, parent, or child of a citizen or lawful resident 
alien, however, and if, at the time of entry, he would 
have been eligible for admission to the United States 
but for the fraud or misrepresentation, then depor- 
tation may be waived, “in the discretion of the 
Attorney General.” 8 U.S.C. 1251(a)(1)(H). A decision 
not to grant such a waiver, like other discretionary 
determinations made during administrative deporta- 
tion proceedings, may be reviewed by a court of 
appeals under 8 U.S.C. 1105a only for abuse of discre- 
tion. Foti v. INS, 375 U.S. 217, 226-232 (1963). 

The waiver provision at issue here was originally 
enacted in 1957, and was reenacted as Section 241(f) of 
the INA in 1961. Act of Sept. 11, 1957, Pub L. No. 85- 


11 


316, § 7, 71 Stat. 640; Act of Sept. 26, 1961, Pub L. No. 
87-301, § 16, 75 Stat. 655; see IVS v. Errico, 385 U.S. 
214, 220-223 (1966). In each instance, Congress made 
the waiver mandatory when the ground for deporta- 
tion was that an alien had previously entered the 
United States through fraud or misrepresentation. 
See 8 U.S.C. 1251(f) (1964); see also Errico, 385 U.S. 
at 224-225; Reid v. INS, 420 U.S. 619, 622-631 (1975). 
Over the ensuing 20 years, the mandatory waiver

litigation.” H.R. Rep. No. 264, 97th Cong., Ist Sess. 25 
(1981). Although Congress had originallly] inten{d-

for deportation” for aliens with close citizen or 
permanent resident relatives, the scope of the waiver

expand the provision into “a charter of amnesty, 
waiving all restrictions for those who had entered the 
United States through fraud.” /bid. By 1975, this 
Court was moved to observe that decisions of the 
lower courts under Section 241(f) might be “sum- 
marized in the language of Macduff: ‘Confusion now 
hath made his masterpiece.” Reid, 420 U.S. at 628. 
Four years later, the Acting Commissioner of the 
INS echoed that assessment when he pointed out in 
con, essional testimony that questions concerning 
the waiver provision had reached this Court twice (in 
Errico and Reid) with “no clear resolution, and

241(a\1)(H) of the INA, 8 U.S.C. 1251(a)(1)(H), which 
provides that deportation “may, in the discretion of 
the Attorney General, be waived for any alien” who

Alonso and Da Silva were both exclusion cases, so that the 
waiver provision at issue was that set out in INA § 212(i), 8 
U.S.C. 1182(i). See note 1, supra. Since 1981, when Congress 
made the parallel waiver-of-deportation provision in Section 
241(f) (now Section 241(a)(1)(H)) discretionary, the BIA and 
the courts have generally held or assumed that the standards 
for discretionary relief under the two provisions should be the 
same. See, ¢.g., Delmundo v. INS, 43 F.3d 436, 440 & nA (9th 
Cir. 1994). We make the same assumption for purposes of this 


petition. 


14 


Attorney General has not, however, chosen to extend 
that leniency to any fraud or misrepresentation oc- 
curring before or after the alien’s initial entry. The 
BIA accordingly weighs such separate acts of dishon- 
esty in the balance in determining whether or not a 
particular alien merits a discretionary waiver of de- 
portation. See, e.g., Delmundo v. INS, 43 F.3d 436 
(9th Cir. 1994) (reversing BIA decision that relied on 
post-entry fraud). 

The BIA followed that approach in this case, taking 
into account acts that both preceded and followed 
respondent’s fraudulent entry. The Board found that 
respondent “engaged in a pattern of violating this 
country’s immigration laws through fraud” by, among 
other things, (i) participating in his wife’s fraudulent 
entry through a sham divorce and remarriage, (ii) 
entering the country fraudulently himself, (iii) 
applying for naturalization under false pretenses, and 
(iv) entering into another sham divorce in order to 
“rectify” his wife’s initial fraud. App., infra, 10a. In 
accordance with the rule of Alonzo and Da Silva, the 
Board put to one side the fraud involved in. respon- 
dent’s own initial entry into the United States. Ibid. 
The Board weighed respondent’s various other acts of 
fraud against him, however, in concluding that he did 
not merit a discretionary waiver of deportation. App., 
infra, 10a-lla. Neither the Board’s procedure nor its 
conclusion remotely suggests any abuse of the broad 
discretion conferred on the Attorney General by 
Section 241(a)(1)(H). 

2. The court of appeals nonetheless summarily 
reversed the Board’s decision in this case. App., infra, 
la-4a. The court concluded that respondent’s assis- 
tance in his wife’s various frauds was “inextricably 
intertwined with [his] own efforts to secure entry 


15 


into the country,” and that his own fraudulent appli- 
cation for citizenship was merely “an extension of 
[his] initial fraud.“ Id. at 2a-8a. Thus, although the 
court purported to apply the Board’s own “initial 
fraud” rule, it substituted its own view for the 
Board’s in determining how that rule should be 
interpreted and applied. In so doing, it relied in part 
on its prior decision in Delmundo v. INS, 43 F.3d 436 
(9th Cir. 1994). Indeed, this case is one of a series of 
five recent decisions in which the court below has 
reversed BIA decisions denying discretionary relief 
from deportation under Section 241(a)1)H) or its 
predecessor, Section 241(f) (or under the parallel 
provision in Section 212(i) that is applicable to exclu- 
sion proceedings; see note 3, supra). 

That series began with Brawn v. INS, 992 F.2d 1016 
(9th Cir. 1993). In Brawn, the court of appeals noted 
the Board’s practice of not considering the alien’s 
initial-entry fraud as an adverse factor (id. at 1020- 
1021), and incorrectly suggested that that practice 
was required by the INA itself (id. at 1019-1020). The 
court then imposed its own view of how the initial 
fraud rule should be applied to the facts of that case. 
Id. at 1020-1021. Over the past two years, the court of 
appeals has followed Braun in Casem v. INS, 8 F.3d 
700, 703 n.1 (majority opinion), 704 (Rymer, J., 
concurring) (1993); in Delmundo, supra; in this case; 
and in Alquisalas v. INS, 61 F.3d 722 (1995). In 
Delmundo, for example, the court held that any post- 
entry fraud undertaken to preserve the immigrant 
status obtained through an alien’s initial fraud is 
immune from consideration, so that “misrepresen- 
tations made to obtain items necessary to live and 
work“ —in that case, a driver's license, social 
security card, and voter identification card—“may not 


16 


be held against” the waiver applicant. Delmundo, 48 
F. gd at 441. In this case, the court extended the same 
construction of the “initial fraud” rule to respon- 
dent’s continuing course of serious immigration 
fraud—culminating in his fraudulent application for 
naturalization as a United States citizen, which the 
court held likewise “must be considered an extension 
of the initial fraud.” App., infra, 2a-3a." 

As another panel of the Ninth Circuit has observed, 
the erroneous course eeisions that began with 
Braun has thus led the court of appeals to “substitute 
[its] discretion for that of the Attorney General” 
in evaluating requests for waivers under Section 
241(aX(1)(H). Alquisalas v. INS, 61 F.3d at 724.“ 


Both in this case and in Delmundo, the court of appeals 
remanded to the BIA for a reweighing of the equities, taking 
into account only the fact that in each case the alien apparently 
lied to the immigration judge in the course of the deportation 
proceedings under review. App., infra, 3a-4a; Delmundo, 43 
F.3d at 442. The court did not explain why lying to an immi- 
gration judge may be counted against an alien in balancing the 
equities under the court’s version of the “initial fraud” rule, 
whereas lying to the INS or to other governmental authorities 
(such as motor vehicle or Social Security officials) about the 
same matters may not be. Moreover, in this case, respondent 
could have obtained his 1985 diverce only by committing fraud

his wife’s identity and their marital history); yet the Ninth 
Circuit's decision would force the Attorney General to coun-

respondent's “apparent lack of candor” (App., infra, 3a) before 
the immigration judge. There is no apparent reason for that 
distinction.

4. If allowed to stand, the Ninth Circuit’s approach 
to review of discretionary waiver decisions under 
Section 241(a)(1)(H), and the conflict that that ap- 
proach has created with the BIA and with other 
courts of appeals, will present a serious obstacle to 
the appropriate and even-handed implementation of 
the Attorney General’s statutory waiver authority. 
The resulting disparity in applicable rules is unfair 
both to the INS, which must administer Section 
241(aX(1)(H) throughout the country (see, e. g., 
Rios-Pineda, 471 U.S. at 451), and to potentially eligi-

ble aliens, who are entitled to expect uniformity in 
the application of the waiver provision, regardless of 
the region in which their cases arise. Indeed, the lack 
of uniformity caused by the Ninth Circuit’s decisions 
will prevent the Attorney General and her delegates 
from achieving precisely those goals that Congress 
identified when, at the NS's request, it provided for 
discretionary implementation of the waiver authority, 
rather than repealing it entirely: to “obviate the need 
for further litigation, and [to] promote the uniform 
administration of” the provision. H.R. Rep. No. 264, 
supra, at 25. 

Even within the Ninth Circuit itself, application of 
the Braun-Delmundo rule will continue to produce 
anomalous results. For example, the rule piaces an 
alien who obtains his initial entry by fraud, and then 
commits a series of further fraudulent acts to 
preserve his illicit position, in a better position than 
an alien who enters the country properly, but later 
embarks on a similar series of fraudulent acts in an 
attempt to remain here. The former will, in the Ninth 
Circuit, have all of his frauds ignored in evaluating 
his request for a waiver of deportation under Section 
241(a)(1)(H); the latter will have them all weighed in 
the balance against him if he seeks some other form of 
discretionary relief, such as that available under 
Section 212(c) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. 1182(c). That 
cannot be the result that Congress intended in 
providing for a limited, discretionary waiver of fraud 
in the initial entry itself. 

The gravest anomaly caused by the Ninth Circuit’s

noted, the decision below requires the Attorney

the Attorney General to ignore such fraud completely

McKAY, Circuit Judge: 


Petitioner, Mr. Yang, claims that the Board of 
Immigration Appeals abused its discretion in refusing 
to grant him a discretionary waiver under Section 
241(a)(1)(H) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 
U.S.C.A. S 1251(a(1)(H) (West Supp. 1994). Mr. Yang 
concedes that he is deportable, but argues that the 
BIA improperly considered impermissible factors in 
denying the waiver. 

The BIA considered several adverse factors in 
declining to grant the waiver. First, the BIA seems 
to have considered Mr. Yang’s participation in his 
wife’s fraudulent entry into the country, including 
his taking part in sham divorces and remarriages. 
These activities were clearly inextricably inter- 
twined with Mr. Yang’s own efforts to secure entry 
into the country and must be considered part of the 
initial fraud. Thus, the BIA improperly considered 
these acts as adverse factors. 

The BIA also considered as an adverse factor the 
fact that Mr. Yang has submitted a fraudulent appli- 
cation for citizenship. The BIA apparently viewed 
this as a separate fraud from the initial series of 
frauds that allowed Mr. Yang to gain access to the 
country and become a lawful resident in the first 
place. However, in the recent case of Delmundo v. 
INS, 43 F.3d 436, 440-41 (9th Cir.1994), we held that 
the fraudulent acts committed in applying for perman- 
ent resident status and obtaining a driver’s license, 
social security card, and voter’s identification card 
were an extension of the initial fraudulent entry and 
could not be considered by the BIA in denying a 
discretionary waiver of excludability. We think that 
applying for citizenship is similar in kind to the acts 


3a 


at issue in Delmundo and also must be considered an 
extension of the initial fraud. Thus, the BIA improp- 
erly considered this fraudulent act. 

Finally, the BIA considered as an adverse factor 
Mr. Yang’s “apparent lack of candor” before the immi- 
gration judge. A review of the initial decision by the 
immigration judge discloses that theve were two com- 
ponents to this “apparent lack of candor.” First, the 
immigration judge did not find Mr. and Mrs. Yang’s 
testimony to be credible with respect to how they 
came to be in the country in the first place. Mr. Yang 
claimed to be an innocent and ignorant pawn rather 
than an active and knowing participant in a con- 
tinuing plan involving false birth certificates, phony 
names, and sham divorces and remarriages. The 
immigration judge did not take all of the frauds 
themselves into account, but did take into account Mr. 
Yang’s implausible continuing assertions that he was 
not involved in any intentional wrongdoing. 

The other aspect of Mr. Yang’s “apparent lack of 
candor” was his claim of indigency. Mr. Yang ini- 
tially claimed to the court that he needed the econ- 
omic support of his children here in the United States 
because of his poor financial situation. Upon further 
questioning, he eventually admitted to having very 
substantial assets, including “over $3 million in prop- 
erty and real estate and liquid assets in cash, stocks, 
etc. of $700,000.” Being untruthful with the immigra- 
tion court is an adverse factor which may be properly 
considered by the BIA. Delmundo, 43 F.3d at 443. 

Because the BIA considered impermissible factors, 
namely, Mr. Yang’s collaboration with his wife and 
his fraudulent application for citizenship, the decision 
of the BIA is vacated, and this matter is remanded for 
a reweighing of the equities, considering solely as an 


da 


adverse factor Mr. Yang’s allegedly dishonest testi- 
mony before the immigration court.

through fraud. The respondent initially participated 
in his wife’s scheme to enter the United States under 
an assumed identity by going through a sham divorce 
and remarriage. He then entered the country himself 
as the purported beneficiary of a United States 
citizen. He next applied for naturalization in 1982 and 
perpetuated the fraud of his wife’s identity as a 
United States citizen. Finally, he entered into anoth- 
er sham divorce in order to attempt to rectify his 
wife’s initial fraud and allow her to resume her true 
identity. Although the respondent’s fraud in entering 
the United States in 1978 can be waived, his remain- 
ing acts involving fraud cannot. See Start v. INS, 803 
F. 2d 539 (9th Cir. 1986). 


lla 


In his favor, the respondent has resided in this 
country for 15 years and has invested a substantial 
amount of money here, which has resulted in sig- 
nificant property and business ties. In addition, three 
of his children live in the United States. M con- 
clude, however, as did the immigration judge, that the 
respondent does not merit relief in the exercise of 
discretion. 

First, the respondent claims that he no longer 
takes an active part in his businesses. Second, while 
three of his children reside here on a permanent 
basis, his eldest daughter lives in Taiwan, two of his 
children appear to spend a large part of their time 
outside of the United States, and his wife has 
returned to Taiwan. Third, although the respondent 
suffers from hypertension, there is no evidence to 
suggest that his condition could not be treated 
effectively in Taiwan. Finally, although the respon- 
dent has lived in the United States for 15 years, he 
previously resided in Taiwan as an adult for more 
than 30 years. 

Because we find that the serious nature of 
respondent’s continuing course of immigration viola- 
tions, both before and after his entry in 1978, out- 
weighs the factors in his favor, the appeal will be 
dismissed. 


ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.

The respondents are a wife and husband, citizens of

were issued Orders to Show Cause by Government 
officials on January 28, 1992, charging them with 
being deportable under Sections 241(a)(1)(A) as it re- 
lates to 212(a)(19) and (20) in regard to the female 
respondent, and 241(a)(1)(A) as it relates to 212(a)(14), 
(19), and (20) as it relates to the male respondent. 

These respondents first appeared before this Court 
at a hearing on March 19, 1992. At that time, the 
respondents, through their counsel, admitted to the 
allegations and charge of deportability originally 
contained on [sic] their Orders to Show Cause. 

Subsequently, at the hearing on October 26, 1992, 
the Government withdrew the 241(a)(1)(B) charge of 
entry without inspection in allegation 5 on the 
original Order to Show Cause of the female respon- 
dent and, at that time, also lodged an additional charge 
of 241(a)(1)(A) as it relates to 212(a)(19) of the Act in 
relationship to the male applicant. The respond- 
ent, through counsel, admitted the additional lodged 
charge filed against the male applicant at the hearing 
on October 26, 1992. 

Deportability, therefore, is not in issue in this case 
as the respondents, through their counsel have 
conceded the allegations and charges of deportability 
filed against them and presently active in this case. 

The respondents sought relief from deportation in 
the form of a Section 241(a)(1)(H) waiver. That appli- 


14a 


cation places the burden upon the respondents to 
prove that they meet both the statutory and disere- 
tionary eligibility factors that warrant a favorable 
exercise of discretion. The respondents must show 
that they are persons who are a spouse, parent, son, 
or daughter of a United States citizen. In this 
specific case, the respondents make that qualification 
and claim based upon relationship as parents to a 
naturalized U.S. citizen, one of their daughters, Lily 
Liu, also known as Qui-Li Yang. 

The respondents also must present evidence and 
verify that they are in possession of immigrant visas 
or equivalent documents and are not [sic] otherwise 
admissable to the United States at the time of such 
entry, except for the grounds of 212(a)(14), (19), and 
(20), now known as Sections 212(a)(5)(A), 212(a)(6)(A), 
and 212(a)(7)(A). 

The respondents, from the evidence and record 
presented, clearly are eligible to apply for the relief 
they are seeking. It is now the Court’s responsibility 
to review the record to see whether they warrant as a 
matter of discretion that relief. The Court, in making 
this determination, notes that it does not take into 
account the original fraud perpetrated against the 
United States Government by the respondents; that 
is, the original fraud of the female respondent filing 
papers claiming to a United States citizen and obtain-

take into account the male respondent’s coming to 
this country and making claims falsely to be a spouse 
of a United States citizen. See Liwanag v. INS, 872

stated in that case, a Section 241(f) waiver (now 
Section 241(a)(1)(H)) can be appropriately denied if an
