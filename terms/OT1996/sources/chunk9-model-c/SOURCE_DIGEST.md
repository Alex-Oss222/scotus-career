## Pembaur

**Fracture.** Brennan wrote I, II-A, and II-C for the Court, with White, Marshall, Blackmun, and Stevens joining; O’Connor joined only I and II-A. II-B was Brennan/White/Marshall/Blackmun only. White concurred separately. Stevens and O’Connor separately concurred in part and in the judgment. Powell dissented with Burger and Rehnquist. Kennedy and Scalia were not members of this Court. Judgment: reversed and remanded, six to three. See 471, 485–502.

**Court propositions.** Monell’s official-policy requirement distinguishes acts of the municipality from acts of its employees; municipal liability concerns acts officially sanctioned or ordered, not employment alone. A single authorized decision can constitute municipal policy even if it neither repeats an earlier act nor is intended to govern later cases. Formal legislative rules are not the only source of policy; executive policymakers can bind the entity. **477–481 (II-A).** A single application of an established policy suffices; repetition is not a separate prerequisite. **478 n.6.**

**Plurality formulation, not itself a five-vote II-B holding.** Final authority must concern the challenged subject matter. Discretion to act is not equivalent to authority to establish final policy. The authority may originate in legislation or be delegated by an official possessing it; its location is a state-law question. The deliberate choice must be made by the official responsible for final policy on the subject. **481–484.** The sheriff hypothetical separates discretion over hiring/firing from final employment policy: a sheriff may have the former but not the latter, although law-enforcement policy may independently be his responsibility. Genuine delegation of employment policymaking changes the attribution. **483 n.12.** Widespread custom is an independent potential route without an affirmative policymaker decision; no custom claim was presented there. **481–482 n.10.** Tuttle rejected inferring policy from a single nonpolicymaking officer’s act; it did not bar a policymaker’s single decision. **482 n.11.**

**Actual attribution and limits.** The Court accepted the Sixth Circuit’s reading of Ohio law that sheriff and prosecutor could make county policy in appropriate circumstances. The sheriff’s office routinely referred matters to the prosecutor, instructed deputies to obey him, and followed that practice here. The prosecutor’s command was not merely legal advice. Thus his particular order was county action. **484–485 and n.13.** The Court did not announce that every sheriff/prosecutor everywhere acts for a county; attribution rested on Ohio authority and delegation. The constitutional violation and retroactive application of Steagald were conceded. **477 n.5.**

**O’Connor, 491.** Agreed for White’s reasons that these officers acted as policymakers. Her express combination was an inferred established practice of forcible entry, law then permitting that practice, and state-law authority to approve or disapprove the entry. She refused II-B and II-C because the reasoning exceeded what the case required and risked liability beyond Monell. Her concurrence is narrower than a wholesale endorsement of Brennan’s II-B formula.

**White, 485–487 (important to O’Connor’s stated ground).** Distinguished authority to choose the then-lawful practice from violating controlling restrictions. He thought final arrest authority did not let a sheriff set the probable-cause standard; contrary individual decisions were not municipal policy merely because the decisionmaker was senior. This is his separate reasoning, not a rule immunizing every unconstitutional municipal act.

**Stevens, 487–491.** Viewed the remedial purpose and common-law background of §1983 as supporting municipal liability for agents acting in the course of employment. He rejected Monell’s exclusion of respondeat superior and therefore declined II-B, while joining II-A and the actual II-C attribution. Compensation, deterrence, resources, and governmental responsibility reinforced his reading. **489–491; 491 n.6.**

**Strong contrary argument.** Powell argued that possessing authority to make policy does not establish that an official actually exercised it; an ad hoc telephone answer lacks a general rule or a deliberative formal process. **497–502.** He also disputed retroactivity, **492–496**; the Court disposed of that point by the respondent’s concession, not a fresh merits holding.

## Praprotnik

**Fracture.** O’Connor plurality joined by Rehnquist, White, and Scalia. Brennan concurred in the judgment with Marshall and Blackmun. Stevens dissented. Kennedy took no part. Reversal and remand: seven to one among eight participants. **113–114, 132.**

**O’Connor/Scalia position.** Official policy and established custom remain distinct routes; employment alone does not suffice. **121–123.** Final-policymaker identification is a question of state/local law, including valid ordinances and regulations, rather than an independent federal ranking of officials or an open-ended jury assessment of who is sufficiently powerful. States have broad authority to structure local government. **124–126.** Crucially, the plurality expressly includes a custom or usage having legal force as a source of policymaking authority. **124 n.1.** More than one body may possess final authority in the same field. **126.**

**Delegation, review, ratification, custom.** A municipality cannot evade liability merely by genuinely delegating policy authority. Where lawful policies constrain an official, a discretionary departure does not itself become city policy. Where authorized policymakers retain review, the reviewing officials retain authority to enforce their policies; approving the decision **and its basis** constitutes attributable ratification. **126–127.** Mere acquiescence in a discretionary decision or failure to investigate an unstated retaliatory motive does not establish delegation. Express approval of a subordinate’s policy statement, or an established series of decisions showing custom known to the supervisor, differs. **130.** Written rules do not automatically defeat liability: actual refusals to enforce them can establish a different actual municipal policy. **130–131.**

**Application and limits.** The charter allocated personnel policy to mayor/aldermen and the Civil Service Commission. The record showed neither their retaliatory policy nor a broader retaliatory custom; supervisors’ transfer/layoff discretion and deferential review did not prove delegation. **128–130.** The plurality expressly did not decide whether the First Amendment prohibited the retaliation, whether retaliation and compensable injury were proved, or whether exonerating individual defendants conflicted with liability against the city. **127–128.** Further review on remand remained possible under the proper standard. **131–132.**

**Brennan’s strongest objection, joined by Marshall/Blackmun.** State law is the starting point, but actual allocations of power, testimonial proof of delegation, and whether review is meaningful matter. An otherwise final policymaker’s act is not rendered nonfinal merely by nominal or severely limited review. The factfinder should resolve relevant predicate facts. **142–147; especially 143–145.** A custom of delegating authority can be lawful while the delegate’s isolated act is unconstitutional, so insisting on a widespread *unconstitutional* practice would miss this distinction. **144.** He nevertheless agreed this official lacked policymaking authority: no authority to prescribe binding transfer criteria, no actual assertion of it, and only implementation of superiors’ budget policy. **139–142.** His opinion distinguished an unconstitutional policy from a facially lawful policy causing constitutional injury and expressly left the latter category open. **147.**

**Stevens.** Reaffirmed his respondeat-superior position but reasoned within the Court’s precedents. Federal statutory purposes should guide the attribution boundary rather than a presumption favoring local preferences. **148 n.1.** Liability follows rules of general application, sufficiently authoritative formal actions, and decisions by officials authorized in the relevant governmental area; top officials’ actions also communicate a pattern for subordinates and affect future conduct. **167–172.** His agency-law analogy distinguishes general agents with broad perceived authority from agents known to have limited authority. **172 n.22.** Secret or informal coordinated retaliation should not receive better treatment than formal action. **172–174.** He specifically criticized rigid area-by-area attribution where several high officials can orchestrate retaliation without possessing formal personnel-policy authority. **174 n.23.** He separately argued procedural forfeiture and adequate evidence connecting mayoral officials to the injury. **158–167.** The contrary plurality objection was that influence/perception tests lack a stable legal boundary and approach respondeat superior. **125 n.2.**

## Jett

**Controlling coalition.** O’Connor wrote I and IV for Rehnquist, White, Scalia, and Kennedy. The same five supported III, with Scalia excluding its legislative-history reliance. II’s extended legislative history had only O’Connor/Rehnquist/White/Kennedy. Brennan dissented with Marshall, Blackmun, and Stevens; Stevens added a short dissent. **704, 738–739, 753.**

**Attribution holding — the most direct five-Justice authority here.** Policymaker identification is a legal question for the trial judge before submission to the jury. The judge reviews state/local positive law **and custom or usage with legal force**, and identifies who speaks with final policymaking authority for the governmental entity concerning the particular allegedly injurious action. The jury then determines whether those officials’ decisions caused injury through affirmative commands or acquiescence in a longstanding practice constituting the entity’s standard procedure. **737 (IV).** Thus Kennedy expressly joined this formulation; Scalia and O’Connor did too. This source supplies more than their stance in the earlier fragmented cases.

**Application and remedy.** A jury instruction treating administrative officials’ wrongful acts as automatically binding the school district was manifest error: it either assumed policymaker status or imposed vicarious liability. **736.** The Court did **not** itself decide the superintendent’s Texas-law authority; it remanded for the court of appeals, with greater state-law expertise, to identify final transfer authority and decide whether a new trial was needed. **738.** The record’s failure to determine attribution was not filled by the superintendent’s ability to make an individual personnel decision.

**Related but distinct holding.** In this 1989 decision, §1983 supplied the exclusive federal damages remedy against state actors for violations of §1981 rights; a plaintiff suing a local government had to establish Monell policy/custom rather than respondeat superior. **731–736.** Its use of “state actor” in this remedial discussion includes local governmental defendants. It does **not** classify the school district as the State, establish Eleventh Amendment immunity, or answer which government an official represents.

**Scalia.** Fully joined IV’s state-law/custom identification and judge/jury division. His separate disagreement concerned reliance on legislative history for the §1981/§1983 remedy question; he used specific-over-general and harmonious-construction principles instead. **738–739.** Do not extend that methodological reservation to a dissent from IV.

**Stevens.** Joined Brennan’s dissent advocating a separate §1981 remedy and respondeat-superior liability under that statute. Brennan argued common-law remedial background, anti-implied-repeal principles, lack of §1983-style causation wording in §1981, and a distinction between liability for employees and liability for private mobs. **739–753, especially 746–752.** Stevens separately relied on his earlier Sea Clammers and Tuttle writings. **753.** He did not join IV; his short separate opinion offers no new affirmative final-policymaker test.

## Attribution cautions for use of these sources

1. Distinguish **federal statutory responsibility** from the **state-law allocation of governmental power** used to implement it. Jett makes the latter a judicial legal inquiry; it includes legally effective custom, not only formal labels.
2. Distinguish **authority to decide a particular act** from **final policymaking authority on that subject**, and both from **which governmental entity the official represents**. The three cases primarily resolve the first distinction. None directly resolves a contested State-versus-county classification of a sheriff.
3. Pembaur’s Ohio attribution is concrete and function-specific; it is not an all-States status rule. Praprotnik’s state-structuring language supports respect for actual legal allocation, while its custom/delegation qualifications prevent reducing the inquiry to an official’s title.
4. Do not describe Kennedy as participating in Pembaur or Praprotnik. His affirmative source support here is his Jett join. Do not call all of Pembaur or Praprotnik a majority opinion. Jett IV supplies the five-Justice holding on identification and the judge/jury roles.
