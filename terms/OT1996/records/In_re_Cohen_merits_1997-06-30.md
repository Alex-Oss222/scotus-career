## Event, posture and participation

In re Cohen. Argued and decided June 30, 1997. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to review the Third Circuit's February 6, 1997 judgment, 106 F.3d 52, lower appellate No. 96-5155. ## Controlling holdings

### I. Federal fraud remains a necessary predicate

**Holding and operative rule:** On the actual-fraud theory presented under 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A), a qualifying acquisition requires actual and justifiable reliance proved by a preponderance of the evidence. The sustained federal fraud findings permit decision of this debt's extent; a State consumer-fraud label or treble award alone would not establish that predicate.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common nine-Justice coalition controls all four holdings and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 523(a)(2) addresses debts for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by its specified conduct. Subparagraph (A) covers false pretenses, false representation or actual fraud, but excludes statements respecting the debtor's or an insider's financial condition. Subparagraph (B) separately requires a materially false writing respecting that financial condition, reasonable reliance, and the debtor's causing its making or publication with intent to deceive. This is an actual-fraud acquisition of rents, not a financial-statement case or a decision of every representation theory.

Field v. Mans supplies actual and individualized justifiable reliance; Grogan supplies the preponderance burden. The recipient's knowledge, capacities and circumstances matter. There is no universal duty to investigate the ordinance. A recipient cannot rely on falsity personally known or obvious to that recipient; an actually encountered warning may require inquiry under that recipient-specific standard. Objective imprudence may bear on whether reliance actually occurred, without becoming a prudent-person test. Limited tenant knowledge of the ordinance therefore does not itself defeat reliance. The bankruptcy findings, sustained below, include Cohen's selective knowledge of favorable provisions and deliberate avoidance of rent limits. The Court makes no new findings and identifies no supported material federal-fraud error requiring remand. A material preserved defect would receive its ordinary disposition under Field; the possibility of additional facts supplies no basis for an exploratory proceeding.

### II. The exception covers valid liability for the fraudulent acquisition

**Holding and operative rule:** Once money or property is obtained through the fraud covered by §523(a)(2)(A), the nondischargeable debt includes the legally enforceable liability arising from that acquisition, not merely the value the debtor received. A qualifying acquisition and a legal connection between it and the liability remain necessary; lawful receipts and unrelated debts are outside the rule.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part II in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The Code defines debt as liability on a claim. The acquisition language identifies which receipts give rise to the excepted liability; it does not redefine that liability as restitution alone. The overcharged rents supply the qualifying acquisition here, while lawfully received rents do not. State law establishes enforceable liability, and federal law independently determines its discharge treatment.

Cohen's strongest reading accepts that the modifier attaches to the acquired money or property but argues that unreceived enhancements cannot represent what was obtained. That reading gives the acquisition words a role but too little work to debt. Liability can arise from the qualifying acquisition without every remedial consequence being another acquisition. Sections 523(a)(4) and (6) address distinct wrongs; their different language does not impose a restitution cap on (a)(2)(A) after its acquisition condition is met. Their independent elements are not decided. Neither the placement of the acquisition phrase nor a general fresh-start policy replaces the Code's liability definition. Text and structure suffice; legislative history is not essential to the result.

Field expressly left debt extent and the obtained-by relationship open. Noland's adherence to statutory categories supports disciplined construction, but its priority and equitable-subordination holding does not resolve this discharge question. The Court creates no State cause of action or federal damages remedy and does not except every obligation of a dishonest debtor.

### III. The existing treble judgment is nondischargeable even assuming a wholly punitive enhancement

**Holding and operative rule:** The existing $94,147.50 treble judgment for $31,382.50 in fraudulently acquired rent is nondischargeable in full. Even if the enhancement is assumed wholly punitive, that character does not remove valid liability for this qualifying acquisition from §523(a)(2)(A).

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part III in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The enhancement is an enforceable consequence of the same fraudulent receipts. A court cannot add a general equitable exception for punitive consequences to the category Congress enacted. Noland reinforces respect for statutory categories, without making every penalty nondischargeable. An unrelated penalty, or an obligation outside this qualifying acquisition, remains outside this holding. The Court does not decide that New Jersey's enhancement is wholly punitive: the lower court assumed that characterization, while Cox v. Sears recognized a partly compensatory purpose. The federal result is the same under the assumption, so settling that State-law question is unnecessary. The $94,147.50 figure is the existing total, not a new addition to the $31,382.50 acquisition.

### IV. Existing attributable fees and costs share the debt's treatment; no new award is made

**Holding and operative rule:** Valid State-law attorney's fees and costs imposed because of, and forming part of liability for, this qualifying fraudulent acquisition are also nondischargeable. The Court affirms the existing connected fee-and-cost award with the treble judgment, without adding an amount, awarding new relief or directing further factfinding.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part IV and the judgment in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The same liability construction governs the existing fees and costs attributable to establishing or recovering this debt. A valid underlying entitlement and the fraud connection are necessary; federal discharge law supplies neither a new fee entitlement nor coverage for unrelated claims. No separate fee-and-cost amount is specified here, and none is calculated. The decision awards no Supreme Court fees, new appellate fees, future costs, interest or enlarged enhancement. Ordinary enforcement remains in the proper proceedings. The Court sets no distribution priority, collection terms or payment schedule, assesses no liability against another person and orders no recalculation or additional hearing.

## Precedent and resulting law

- Field v. Mans preserves actual and justifiable reliance for the actual-fraud theory and its individualized limits. Its express reservation of debt extent is resolved here for this qualifying acquisition; its distinct financial-condition rules remain intact.
- Grogan v. Garner supplies the preponderance burden for the federal discharge exception.
- United States v. Noland reinforces adherence to the Code's enacted categories. Its priority and equitable-subordination rule is not extended into a direct holding on fraud discharge or every punitive debt.
- Cox v. Sears identifies a partly compensatory purpose in New Jersey's treble remedy; the Court leaves the exact State-law characterization undecided because even the wholly punitive assumption does not change the result. No earlier decision is overruled.

The actual-fraud acquisition and federal reliance requirements remain necessary. Once satisfied, §523(a)(2)(A) reaches valid liability for that acquisition, including its enforceable enhancement and attributable fees and costs. It is not an exception for every debt associated with dishonesty or every penalty. State law supplies the existing liability; the Code determines its discharge status.

### Competing constructions and supported alternatives

**Acquired-value ceiling.** The strongest rival concedes that the modifier concerning what was obtained attaches to the acquired money/property rather than directly to the word debt, yet argues that the resulting exception still reaches only an obligation representing what the debtor actually obtained. An enhancement or litigation expense was not received by the debtor; the acquisition words must impose a limit different from the broader debt-for-wrong language in §523(a)(4) and (6). Fresh-start policy favors confining an exception where the text reasonably permits it. This is the Greenberg construction preserved in the neutral packet, not a caricature based solely on a modifier's nearest noun. It would affirm nondischargeability of the qualifying acquired value and reverse the larger extent determination, with any necessary confined allocation of the existing award; it would not itself erase the underlying State liability.

The eight reject that construction because it turns the fraudulent-acquisition limitation into a second definition of debt as restitution, although debt remains the liability legally arising from the claim. The difference from (a)(4) and (6) is that (a)(2)(A) requires this kind of acquisition. A liability can be for that acquisition without each consequence being a second acquisition. The phrase concerning extent still prevents treatment of lawful rent or unrelated liabilities as fraud debt. The 1984 placement of the phrase supplies no clear direction to discharge the nonrestitution consequences of the very fraudulent acquisition Congress excepts; the conclusion does not depend on a policy-based expansion of every fraud-related claim. Legislative history may confirm that conclusion but is unnecessary to the common sufficient textual ground.

**Automatic punitive-debt exclusion.** Noland's insistence on enacted categories and the precise discharge text defeat a general judicial choice to discharge an enhancement simply because it is noncompensatory. That does not turn Noland into a holding on fraud discharge or decide the enhancement's State-law character. The assumed wholly punitive enhancement still belongs to the existing debt for the qualifying acquisition.

**A federal-fraud remand.** A supported, material and preserved reliance or other federal-fraud defect would require its ordinary disposition under Field. The supplied record does not establish such a defect. No Justice chooses a remand merely because more record material could theoretically exist. The affirmance relies on the existing sustained federal fraud predicate at the actual review scope, not a presumption that consumer-fraud trebling necessarily satisfies §523 or a finding that all possible factual objections were waived.

**An expansion to any consequence of any fraud.** The alternative at the other extreme would except all debts associated with the debtor's dishonesty, regardless of an acquisition within (a)(2)(A). The eight reject that expansion. The qualifying acquisition, valid liability, and causal/statutory nexus remain necessary. The financial-condition exclusion and subsection (B)'s distinct requirements are not bypassed; no claim outside the supplied fraud acquisition is decided.

It does not make every obligation of a dishonest debtor nondischargeable. Here the identified overcharged rents, not all lawful rent or every landlord obligation, supply that acquisition. An enforceable enhancement imposed for those fraudulent receipts is a liability for them even though the debtor did not separately obtain the dollars represented by the enhancement. State law determines the valid liability; federal law determines whether it falls within this discharge category. The Court creates neither a new State cause nor a new federal damages remedy.

Field deliberately left debt extent and the obtained-by relationship separate. It therefore supplies the fraud prerequisite, not an already settled result here. Noland, May 13, 1996, requires observance of the Code's actual categories rather than a judicial preference about punitive obligations; it concerns priority and equitable subordination and does not itself settle §523(a)(2)(A). The present result rests on this exception's text and structure. Neither a generalized fresh start nor generalized hostility to fraud can replace that construction.

**C3 — Existing treble liability and conditional punitive characterization.** All eight would affirm nondischargeability of the existing $94,147.50 treble judgment for the $31,382.50 fraudulent acquisition. Even assuming for decision that the enhancement is wholly punitive, that characterization does not remove a valid liability for the qualifying fraud from §523(a)(2)(A). The Court does not find it wholly punitive as a matter of New Jersey law; the lower opinion used that assumption, while Cox v. Sears identified a partly compensatory purpose. No need to settle that State-law characterization arises because the federal result is the same under the stated assumption.

This is not a general nondischargeability rule for penalties. A penalty arising from unrelated conduct, a different statutory category, or an obligation lacking the qualifying fraudulent-acquisition nexus is outside this holding. Section 523(a)(4) and (6) cover distinct wrongs and need not use the same acquisition language; their broader formulations do not compel a cap on liability in (a)(2)(A) once its acquisition requirement is satisfied. The Court neither collapses those categories nor decides their independent elements.

**C4 — Existing attorney's fees and costs.** All eight would hold the existing valid State-law fee and cost obligations nondischargeable insofar as they are imposed because of, and form part of the liability for, this qualifying fraud. No separate fee or cost amount is supplied, and none is calculated or added. The Court does not award Supreme Court fees, new appellate fees, future litigation costs, interest, or an enlarged enhancement. Fees unrelated to the qualifying fraudulent acquisition are not covered merely because the same debtor owes them. A valid underlying obligation and its fraud connection remain necessary; a federal discharge ruling does not create State entitlement to fees.

**C5 — Judgment and scope.** Affirm the Third Circuit's judgment insofar as it sustains the existing full fraud liability's nondischargeability, including the adjudged treble damages and existing fee/cost award at the scope of C1–C4. No new debt is assessed, the amount already obtained is not confused with the treble total, and no recalculation or additional hearing is ordered. The disposition leaves ordinary enforcement of the existing nondischargeable judgment to the proper proceedings; it does not set collection terms, distribution priority, a payment schedule or a new liability against another person.

### Competing constructions and supported alternatives

**Acquired-value ceiling.** The strongest rival concedes that the modifier concerning what was obtained attaches to the acquired money/property rather than directly to the word debt, yet argues that the resulting exception still reaches only an obligation representing what the debtor actually obtained. An enhancement or litigation expense was not received by the debtor; the acquisition words must impose a limit different from the broader debt-for-wrong language in §523(a)(4) and (6). Fresh-start policy favors confining an exception where the text reasonably permits it. This is the Greenberg construction preserved in the neutral packet, not a caricature based solely on a modifier's nearest noun. It would affirm nondischargeability of the qualifying acquired value and reverse the larger extent determination, with any necessary confined allocation of the existing award; it would not itself erase the underlying State liability.

The eight reject that construction because it turns the fraudulent-acquisition limitation into a second definition of debt as restitution, although debt remains the liability legally arising from the claim. The difference from (a)(4) and (6) is that (a)(2)(A) requires this kind of acquisition. A liability can be for that acquisition without each consequence being a second acquisition. The phrase concerning extent still prevents treatment of lawful rent or unrelated liabilities as fraud debt. The 1984 placement of the phrase supplies no clear direction to discharge the nonrestitution consequences of the very fraudulent acquisition Congress excepts; the conclusion does not depend on a policy-based expansion of every fraud-related claim. Legislative history may confirm that conclusion but is unnecessary to the common sufficient textual ground.

**Automatic punitive-debt exclusion.** Noland's insistence on enacted categories and the precise discharge text defeat a general judicial choice to discharge an enhancement simply because it is noncompensatory. That does not turn Noland into a holding on fraud discharge or decide the enhancement's State-law character. The assumed wholly punitive enhancement still belongs to the existing debt for the qualifying acquisition.

**A federal-fraud remand.** A supported, material and preserved reliance or other federal-fraud defect would require its ordinary disposition under Field. The supplied record does not establish such a defect. No Justice chooses a remand merely because more record material could theoretically exist. The affirmance relies on the existing sustained federal fraud predicate at the actual review scope, not a presumption that consumer-fraud trebling necessarily satisfies §523 or a finding that all possible factual objections were waived.

**An expansion to any consequence of any fraud.** The alternative at the other extreme would except all debts associated with the debtor's dishonesty, regardless of an acquisition within (a)(2)(A). The eight reject that expansion. The qualifying acquisition, valid liability, and causal/statutory nexus remain necessary. The financial-condition exclusion and subsection (B)'s distinct requirements are not bypassed; no claim outside the supplied fraud acquisition is decided.

## Public Projection

## Event

In re Cohen. Argued and decided June 30, 1997. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to review the Third Circuit's February 6, 1997 judgment, 106 F.3d 52, lower appellate No. 96-5155. The questions are whether §523(a)(2)(A) limits nondischargeability to the money acquired by fraud or includes the full legally established liability, and whether the existing treble damages, fees and costs fall within that liability.

Cohen co-owned a rent-controlled Hoboken apartment building. The administrator ordered $31,382.50 in refunds; the owners neither paid nor perfected an administrative appeal and sought Chapter 7 relief. The tenants' adversary proceeding produced federal fraud findings and a $94,147.50 treble judgment, with attorney's fees and costs already awarded. The courts below sustained the fraud determination and discharge treatment. This review concerns that existing liability, not a new damages assessment.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate. No Justice is excluded.

The Court unanimously affirms the Third Circuit's judgment sustaining nondischargeability of the existing qualifying fraud liability, including the treble judgment and attributable fees and costs.

## Holdings

### I. Federal fraud remains a necessary predicate

**Holding and operative rule:** On the actual-fraud theory presented under 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A), a qualifying acquisition requires actual and justifiable reliance proved by a preponderance of the evidence. The sustained federal fraud findings permit decision of this debt's extent; a State consumer-fraud label or treble award alone would not establish that predicate.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion of the Court, Parts I–IV, is joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common nine-Justice coalition controls all four holdings and the affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 523(a)(2) addresses debts for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by its specified conduct. Subparagraph (A) covers false pretenses, false representation or actual fraud, but excludes statements respecting the debtor's or an insider's financial condition. Subparagraph (B) separately requires a materially false writing respecting that financial condition, reasonable reliance, and the debtor's causing its making or publication with intent to deceive. This is an actual-fraud acquisition of rents, not a financial-statement case or a decision of every representation theory.

Field v. Mans supplies actual and individualized justifiable reliance; Grogan supplies the preponderance burden. The recipient's knowledge, capacities and circumstances matter. There is no universal duty to investigate the ordinance. A recipient cannot rely on falsity personally known or obvious to that recipient; an actually encountered warning may require inquiry under that recipient-specific standard. Objective imprudence may bear on whether reliance actually occurred, without becoming a prudent-person test. Limited tenant knowledge of the ordinance therefore does not itself defeat reliance. The bankruptcy findings, sustained below, include Cohen's selective knowledge of favorable provisions and deliberate avoidance of rent limits. The Court makes no new findings and identifies no supported material federal-fraud error requiring remand. A material preserved defect would receive its ordinary disposition under Field; the possibility of additional facts supplies no basis for an exploratory proceeding.

### II. The exception covers valid liability for the fraudulent acquisition

**Holding and operative rule:** Once money or property is obtained through the fraud covered by §523(a)(2)(A), the nondischargeable debt includes the legally enforceable liability arising from that acquisition, not merely the value the debtor received. A qualifying acquisition and a legal connection between it and the liability remain necessary; lawful receipts and unrelated debts are outside the rule.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part II in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The Code defines debt as liability on a claim. The acquisition language identifies which receipts give rise to the excepted liability; it does not redefine that liability as restitution alone. The overcharged rents supply the qualifying acquisition here, while lawfully received rents do not. State law establishes enforceable liability, and federal law independently determines its discharge treatment.

Cohen's strongest reading accepts that the modifier attaches to the acquired money or property but argues that unreceived enhancements cannot represent what was obtained. That reading gives the acquisition words a role but too little work to debt. Liability can arise from the qualifying acquisition without every remedial consequence being another acquisition. Sections 523(a)(4) and (6) address distinct wrongs; their different language does not impose a restitution cap on (a)(2)(A) after its acquisition condition is met. Their independent elements are not decided. Neither the placement of the acquisition phrase nor a general fresh-start policy replaces the Code's liability definition. Text and structure suffice; legislative history is not essential to the result.

Field expressly left debt extent and the obtained-by relationship open. Noland's adherence to statutory categories supports disciplined construction, but its priority and equitable-subordination holding does not resolve this discharge question. The Court creates no State cause of action or federal damages remedy and does not except every obligation of a dishonest debtor.

### III. The existing treble judgment is nondischargeable even assuming a wholly punitive enhancement

**Holding and operative rule:** The existing $94,147.50 treble judgment for $31,382.50 in fraudulently acquired rent is nondischargeable in full. Even if the enhancement is assumed wholly punitive, that character does not remove valid liability for this qualifying acquisition from §523(a)(2)(A).

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part III in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The enhancement is an enforceable consequence of the same fraudulent receipts. A court cannot add a general equitable exception for punitive consequences to the category Congress enacted. Noland reinforces respect for statutory categories, without making every penalty nondischargeable. An unrelated penalty, or an obligation outside this qualifying acquisition, remains outside this holding. The Court does not decide that New Jersey's enhancement is wholly punitive: the lower court assumed that characterization, while Cox v. Sears recognized a partly compensatory purpose. The federal result is the same under the assumption, so settling that State-law question is unnecessary. The $94,147.50 figure is the existing total, not a new addition to the $31,382.50 acquisition.

### IV. Existing attributable fees and costs share the debt's treatment; no new award is made

**Holding and operative rule:** Valid State-law attorney's fees and costs imposed because of, and forming part of liability for, this qualifying fraudulent acquisition are also nondischargeable. The Court affirms the existing connected fee-and-cost award with the treble judgment, without adding an amount, awarding new relief or directing further factfinding.

**Authority:** The common nine-Justice coalition joins Part IV and the judgment in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The same liability construction governs the existing fees and costs attributable to establishing or recovering this debt. A valid underlying entitlement and the fraud connection are necessary; federal discharge law supplies neither a new fee entitlement nor coverage for unrelated claims. No separate fee-and-cost amount is specified here, and none is calculated. The decision awards no Supreme Court fees, new appellate fees, future costs, interest or enlarged enhancement. Ordinary enforcement remains in the proper proceedings. The Court sets no distribution priority, collection terms or payment schedule, assesses no liability against another person and orders no recalculation or additional hearing.

## Precedent Treatment

- Field v. Mans preserves actual and justifiable reliance for the actual-fraud theory and its individualized limits. Its express reservation of debt extent is resolved here for this qualifying acquisition; its distinct financial-condition rules remain intact.
- Grogan v. Garner supplies the preponderance burden for the federal discharge exception.
- United States v. Noland reinforces adherence to the Code's enacted categories. Its priority and equitable-subordination rule is not extended into a direct holding on fraud discharge or every punitive debt.
- Cox v. Sears identifies a partly compensatory purpose in New Jersey's treble remedy; the Court leaves the exact State-law characterization undecided because even the wholly punitive assumption does not change the result. No earlier decision is overruled.

## Law After Decision

The actual-fraud acquisition and federal reliance requirements remain necessary. Once satisfied, §523(a)(2)(A) reaches valid liability for that acquisition, including its enforceable enhancement and attributable fees and costs. It is not an exception for every debt associated with dishonesty or every penalty. State law supplies the existing liability; the Code determines its discharge status.
