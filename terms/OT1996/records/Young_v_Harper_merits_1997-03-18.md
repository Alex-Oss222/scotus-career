## Shared operative commitments

All eight Associates support the following propositions at the same level of generality and support affirming the judgment of the Tenth Circuit on that basis. These are provisional direct opinion joins, not merely identical judgment votes. No separate writing is needed to express a supplied material disagreement.

### 1. Community conditional liberty

When the State actually releases a prisoner to live at his own residence, work in the community, and conduct ordinary community life subject to parole-like supervision and conditions, termination of that conditional liberty requires the protections of Morrissey v. Brewer, 408 U.S. 471, 480–490 (1972). The State cannot avoid that protection merely by calling the release preparole, retaining legal custody, identifying overcrowding as a program purpose, or reserving a separate decision about formal parole.

Morrissey protects the existing conditional freedom to live outside prison. It already accounts for the original conviction, continuing sentence, restrictions, supervision, and the State's need to return a person to custody on legally sufficient grounds. Harper's residence, work, and reporting arrangement present that liberty, rather than a routine transfer between prison settings. Five months of release reinforce the concrete nature of the deprivation but are not a new minimum duration for constitutional protection.

Greenholtz v. Inmates, 442 U.S. 1, 7–12 (1979), distinguishes a request for release not yet obtained from the loss of conditional freedom already possessed. The Governor's refusal of formal parole is not automatically a constitutionally adequate revocation proceeding for existing preparole liberty. This conclusion does not hold that Harper was entitled to receive formal parole or that every form of temporary absence from prison has the same constitutional incidents.

Sandin's controlling sufficient, nonexclusive rule requires genuine substantive constraints and a meaningful deprivation for the state-created prison-entitlement route; it expressly preserves directly protected liberty. The Court applies that preservation and Morrissey's more specific rule. It adopts neither the historical atypical-and-significant-hardship formulation nor an inference that mandatory words alone create protected liberty. No new finding that the entire Oklahoma program independently satisfies Sandin's state-entitlement route is necessary.

### 2. Original conditions and the later procedure

Harper's October 1990 orientation and executed acknowledgment tied community status to compliance with release conditions. The Board simultaneously recommended parole and released him. The supported account describes five **apparently** uneventful months, followed by the Governor's denial and the March 14, 1991 telephone direction to return. Those facts support conditional community liberty; they do not conclusively establish that Harper violated no condition, that release was unconditional, or that he personally was then ineligible for parole.

The procedure on which the State relies was promulgated in August 1991, after the return to prison. The earlier procedure in effect at release was silent about the effect of a later parole denial. The August procedure therefore does not establish a parole-denial return condition communicated to Harper in October 1990. Nor can silence about that single issue establish the complete content of the governing scheme, forbid all other lawful return grounds, or make community release irrevocable. The Court rejects reliance on the later procedure to defeat this process claim; it does not invalidate the August procedure prospectively or resolve every possible substantive basis for revocation.

### 3. Process and its operative qualifications

Morrissey's ordinary protections govern termination of this liberty. They protect reliable determination of the legally asserted ground and the decision whether revocation is warranted, while preserving the State's authority to use informal administrative proceedings. The case does not add a criminal-trial model, a new evidentiary burden, a fixed numerical deadline, or an absolute requirement that every final revocation hearing precede initial arrest or detention.

**Preliminary stage.** When the participant is arrested and detained pending revocation, a reasonably prompt inquiry at or reasonably near the place of the alleged violation or arrest determines whether probable cause or reasonable ground supports detention for a parole-condition violation. A person not directly involved in the case makes that determination; the officer need not be a judge and may be a different parole officer. The participant receives notice of the inquiry, its purpose, and the alleged violations; may appear, speak, and provide relevant documents or witnesses; and, on request, may question an adverse informant in his presence. If the hearing officer determines that disclosure of the informant's identity would expose the informant to a risk of harm, confrontation and cross-examination of that informant may be withheld. The officer summarizes the proceedings and gives the reasons and evidence supporting the probable-cause determination. Formal judicial findings are not required. A supported probable-cause determination permits continued detention and return to the institution pending the final decision; no such determination is manufactured here.

**Final stage.** The State must afford an opportunity for a final revocation hearing, if desired, within a reasonable time after custody and before the effective final revocation decision. The inquiry resolves contested relevant facts and whether the established circumstances warrant revocation; the participant may deny a violation or present mitigation explaining why a violation should not result in revocation. Its minimum protections are:

1. Written notice of the claimed violations or asserted revocation grounds.
2. Disclosure of the evidence against the participant.
3. An opportunity to be heard in person and to present witnesses and documentary evidence.
4. Confrontation and cross-examination of adverse witnesses, unless the hearing officer specifically finds good cause for withholding confrontation.
5. A neutral and detached decisionmaker, such as a parole board whose members need not be judges or lawyers.
6. A written statement of the evidence relied on and reasons for revocation.

These proceedings may consider letters, affidavits, and other material that a criminal trial would exclude. Morrissey's reference to two months as not apparently unreasonable on the circumstances it discussed is not a universal deadline or safe harbor. A parolee cannot relitigate issues already determined against him in another forum, including a criminal conviction that supplies a revocation ground. No such prior determination, valid waiver, admission disposing of an actual violation, risk-of-harm finding, or good-cause finding is supplied for Harper, and none is presumed. The preliminary informant-risk qualification and final-stage specific-good-cause qualification remain distinct. Morrissey itself reserved the counsel question; this case announces no separate counsel holding or claim that all later date-eligible counsel doctrine is unresolved.

### 4. Judgment, remedy, and limits

Affirm the Tenth Circuit's judgment that this preparole status requires Morrissey process and its reversal of the denial of habeas relief. The case returns through the ordinary mandate for proceedings consistent with that protected-liberty and process determination. Oklahoma remains able to establish and act upon a legally available revocation ground through constitutionally adequate procedures. The Court does not grant formal parole, order unconditional release, decide that Harper's sentence expired, establish a particular release date, declare every possible return ground invalid, or itself prescribe a new deadline for state proceedings.

This is a merits application of procedural due process, not an exhaustion remand based on hypothetical litigation defects. The question whether any particular subsequent revocation or continuing custody is lawful is left to proceedings applying the governing law and record. The original telephone instruction without the required opportunity for process cannot itself supply the constitutionally adequate revocation determination.

## Material alternative paths and source limits

**Review timing.** Dismissal would follow if the only relevant paper were an unauthorized late rehearing submission and the original judgment's review period had elapsed. That is not the supplied sequence: express leave, treatment as timely, and the pending mandate establish an accepted rehearing proceeding. The strongest objection to accepting jurisdiction is that a lower court cannot extend the statutory certiorari limit by relabeling a late paper. Jenkins's actual-treatment inquiry and its rejection of orders solely enlarging review time preserve that limit. No such manipulative or retrospective extension is supported here. All eight support review on the actual facts, not a universal exception.

**Liberty characterization.** Reversal would be supported if the relevant interest were merely an unreceived discretionary grant of parole or an ordinary internal custody adjustment, with no distinct protected interest. Greenholtz and Meachum supply those limiting principles. The strongest argument for that account is continued service of a valid sentence coupled with the Governor's parole decision and the program's administrative purpose. The argument fails at the required characterization: Harper was living at home and working in the community under conditions resembling parole. Morrissey addresses that actual conditional freedom even though custody and sentence persist. The original-rules chronology independently prevents treating the later procedure as dispositive advance notice. All eight join this direct route.

**Independent state-law entitlement.** The record might support further inquiry under Sandin into substantive program constraints; the compliance acknowledgment supplies a relevant indicator. The strongest argument against an immediate state-entitlement finding is the absence of the full original operative scheme and the difference between release conditions and a substantive restriction on all return authority. The commitments make no such finding. They apply the preserved direct constitutional route, so an exhaustive Sandin inquiry is neither a condition to this disposition nor an independently sufficient alternative holding.

**Process and relief.** A broad rule demanding a completed judicial adversarial hearing before any physical return would exceed Morrissey's distinction between arrest, prompt preliminary review, and final revocation. Conversely, an automatic return merely because formal parole was denied would erase the protected-interest inquiry and the need for a constitutionally adequate determination. ## Public Projection

## Event

Young v. Harper, No. 95-1598. Argued December 9, 1996; decided March 18, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, which reversed the denial of federal habeas relief. The question is whether termination of Oklahoma preparole community release requires the procedures governing revocation of parole.

Harper was released in October 1990 to live at home, work and report to a parole officer under parole-like conditions. After five apparently uneventful months, the Governor denied formal parole; on March 14, 1991, an officer telephoned and directed him to return immediately to prison without the required revocation process. An August 1991 procedure later addressing parole denial postdated that return. The original procedure was silent on the effect of parole denial, and Harper’s acknowledgment tied community status to compliance with release conditions.

The appellate rehearing petition was two days late, but the Tenth Circuit granted leave, treated it as timely and withheld the mandate until denial. Certiorari was sought within 90 days of that denial.

## Holdings

Part I is directly joined by all eight Associate Justices. Parts II–IV are directly joined by all nine Justices. These are majority holdings at their stated scopes; no separate-rationale synthesis is involved.

#### Part I — Accepted rehearing makes this petition timely

**Holding and operative rule:** When the appellate court expressly permits a late rehearing filing, treats it as timely and keeps the proceeding open pending its resolution, the accepted rehearing’s denial is the relevant finality point for the ensuing certiorari period. This petition, filed within 90 days of denial on that sequence, is timely.

**Controlling explanation:** The governing Rule 13.3 measures the period from denial of timely rehearing, and FRAP 40 permits enlargement by court order. Bowman distinguishes an unauthorized late submission from one the court permits and entertains. Jenkins similarly respects the lower court’s actual treatment as substantive rehearing without allowing manipulation of this Court’s statutory period. Here leave was granted, the filing was treated as timely and the mandate was withheld. A merely unauthorized late paper, an en banc suggestion not treated as rehearing, a mandate date alone or a later order entered solely to extend this Court’s filing time would not establish the same result. The Court waives no statutory period and announces no rule that every late submission restarts it.

#### Part II — Existing parole-like community liberty requires protection

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State releases a prisoner to live at home, work and conduct ordinary community life under parole-like supervision and conditions, termination of that existing conditional liberty requires Morrissey’s protections. Retained legal custody, an overcrowding objective, the preparole label and a separate decision about formal parole do not eliminate that interest.

**Controlling explanation:** Morrissey protects substantial conditional freedom already possessed while accounting for a continuing sentence and lawful grounds for return. Greenholtz distinguishes a request for initial release, and Meachum’s ordinary internal-transfer setting does not describe this community life. Sandin expressly preserves directly constitutional liberty alongside its sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement inquiry; no exhaustive program-entitlement finding is needed here. Residence, employment and supervision establish the relevant difference, without making five months a minimum constitutional duration.

The Governor’s denial is not automatically an adequate revocation proceeding. The August 1991 procedure cannot establish a return condition communicated at Harper’s October 1990 release or validate his March return by itself. Yet the earlier procedure’s silence on parole denial does not establish the whole program’s terms, prove compliance with every condition or make release irrevocable. The Court does not invalidate the later procedure prospectively or decide every lawful ground for return. Formal parole, short furloughs, work details and ordinary internal transfers remain distinct.

#### Part III — Morrissey’s ordinary stages and qualifications govern

**Holding and operative rule:** Termination of this community liberty requires the ordinary preliminary and final revocation protections described below. Final revocation must rest on a constitutionally adequate determination, but a completed final hearing is not invariably required before initial arrest or detention.

**Required process and operative qualifications:** When the participant is arrested and detained pending revocation, a reasonably prompt inquiry at or reasonably near the place of the alleged violation or arrest determines probable cause or reasonable ground for detention for a release-condition violation. A person not directly involved in the case decides; that person need not be a judge and may be a different parole officer. The participant receives notice of the inquiry, its purpose and the alleged violations, may appear and speak, and may present relevant documents and witnesses. On request he may question an adverse informant in his presence. If the officer determines that identifying the informant would expose that person to harm, confrontation and cross-examination of that informant may be withheld. The officer summarizes the proceedings and states the reasons and evidence supporting the determination; formal judicial findings are unnecessary. Supported probable cause permits continued detention and institutional return pending final decision.

The State must offer a final revocation hearing, if desired, within a reasonable time after custody and before effective final revocation. It must resolve contested relevant facts and whether the circumstances warrant revocation, allowing denial and mitigation. Minimum protections are written notice of the claimed violations or asserted grounds; disclosure of adverse evidence; an opportunity to be heard personally and present witnesses and documents; confrontation and cross-examination unless the officer specifically finds good cause to withhold them; a neutral and detached decisionmaker, whose members need not be judges or lawyers; and a written statement of the evidence relied on and reasons.

Informal evidence, including letters and affidavits, may be considered. Issues already determined adversely in another forum, including a relevant criminal conviction, need not be relitigated. Morrissey’s discussion of two months establishes no universal deadline or safe harbor. Preliminary informant risk and final-stage specific good cause are distinct exceptions, each permitting rather than requiring withholding upon the necessary finding. No such finding, valid waiver, dispositive admission, prior determination or supported probable-cause decision is established here. This case decides no separate counsel question.

**Controlling explanation:** Morrissey’s flexible administrative process protects reliable identification of a lawful ground and the judgment whether revocation is warranted. Calling the program preparole cannot replace those safeguards with a telephone return instruction. Conversely, procedural protection does not convert revocation into a criminal trial or require a judicial decisionmaker. Temporary detention remains distinct from final deprivation and must receive the appropriate prompt preliminary and final protections. The Court adds no evidentiary burden, fixed deadline, automatic-release consequence or new adjudicatory code.

#### Part IV — Affirmance preserves lawful grounds and bounded relief

**Holding and operative rule:** The Tenth Circuit correctly reversed denial of habeas relief because the termination of Harper’s community liberty required Morrissey process. Proceedings must apply that determination, while Oklahoma retains authority to establish a legally available revocation ground through adequate procedures or another independent lawful basis for custody.

**Controlling explanation:** The procedural violation does not establish formal-parole entitlement, expiration of sentence, absence of every possible release-condition violation or a particular release date. The original telephone instruction does not itself supply an adequate revocation determination. The Court neither directs unconditional release nor prescribes a new deadline for state proceedings; any subsequent revocation or continuing custody must be assessed under the governing law and actual record. Lynce supplies no hypothetical exhaustion defect and no implied waiver here.

## Precedent Treatment

- Morrissey v. Brewer: applied to existing parole-like community liberty with both stages, distinct confrontation exceptions, informal evidence and bounded relief intact.
- Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex: its initial-release distinction is preserved; this case concerns freedom already conferred.
- Meachum v. Fano: its ordinary internal-transfer setting is distinguished from actual community release.
- Sandin v. Conner: its sufficient, nonexclusive state-entitlement rule and preservation of direct liberty are applied without an exhaustive independent state-entitlement finding.
- Bowman v. Loperena: applied to rehearing the lower court permits and entertains, rather than an unauthorized late submission.
- Missouri v. Jenkins, 495 U.S. 33 (1990): actual rehearing treatment determines finality within the statutory limit; orders merely extending this Court’s filing time remain insufficient.
- Lynce v. Mathis: genuine habeas threshold predicates remain necessary; no exhaustion defect or waiver is inferred from missing papers.
- United States v. Gonzales: its sentence-concurrency construction is distinguished from conditional-release process and supplies no authority for summary revocation.

## Law After Decision

Parole-like community freedom receives Morrissey protection by its actual character, irrespective of the preparole name. Existing safeguards retain their ordinary flexibility and limitations, and lawful revocation remains possible. Initial parole eligibility and complete state-program entitlement remain separate questions. Accepted rehearing on the stated sequence makes this review timely without a general enlargement of the certiorari period.

Any further custody must rest on a lawful revocation determination, including permissible interim detention under the stated procedures, or an independent lawful basis.
