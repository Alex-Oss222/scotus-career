## Controlling holdings and express reservation

### I. The jury-element violation is reviewed as forfeited error

**Holding and operative rule:** Materiality is an element of the §1623 perjury offense and must be determined by the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; the judge's determination instead was constitutional error. Johnson's failure to object presents forfeiture under Rule 52(b), not an established intentional waiver extinguishing that error.

**Authority:** Part I of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion is joined by all eight Associates; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Gaudin requires the jury to apply the law to an actual materiality element. Griffith makes that controlling constitutional rule applicable while this case remains on direct review. Wells construed a different statute, §1014; it supplies no reason to remove §1623's genuine element. The District Judge followed then-governing circuit law, but that does not make the instruction correct under the law governing this appeal.

Olano distinguishes forfeiture, a failure timely to assert a right, from valid intentional relinquishment of a known right. Johnson did not object and earlier opposed materiality evidence because circuit law assigned that issue to the judge. Those circumstances do not establish an intentional surrender of the jury right later recognized in Gaudin. The claim therefore remains reviewable under Rule 52(b)'s distinct requirements: error, plainness, effect on substantial rights and the independent discretionary condition. A constitutional violation does not itself resolve the remedy after forfeiture, and collateral-review burdens are not imported into this direct criminal appeal.

### II. Plainness is measured at appellate consideration in this intervening-law setting

**Holding and operative rule:** When the law at trial settled an issue against the defendant but controlling law changes before direct review ends, the error is plain if it is clear at appellate consideration. This resolves that intervening-law setting without deciding every case in which the law was unsettled at trial or dispensing with Rule 52(b)'s other requirements.

**Authority:** Part II of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight direct joins. Scalia assumes plainness without joining this holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The court exercises Rule 52(b)'s correction authority on the appeal before it. Gaudin now makes the allocation error unambiguous. A defendant need not have made a futile objection under settled contrary law to satisfy this particular plainness inquiry after that controlling change.

Olano required clear or obvious error under current law but expressly left error first clarified on appeal unresolved. The Court answers that reserved question at the stated scope rather than treating Olano as having already decided it. The objection that trial judges must be judged by the law then available confuses blame for the earlier instruction with the legal authority to correct a conviction on pending review. Recognizing plainness now does not condemn the judge's adherence to circuit precedent, make nonobjection irrelevant or guarantee relief. Substantial rights and remedial discretion remain separate conditions. The Court does not adopt a rule for every initially uncertain question.

### III. Substantial-rights impact is assumed; classification remains open

**Limiting premise:** The Court assumes in Johnson's favor that the error affected substantial rights and decides the independent discretionary condition. It does not adopt the court of appeals' third-step disposition, classify this omission as structural or nonstructural, or hold that every withheld element does or does not require an outcome-prejudice showing.

**Authority:** Part III of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion is joined by the same eight Justices as Part II. Scalia makes the assumption necessary to reach Part IV but does not join this explanatory discussion. This reservation supplies no affirmative holding about the unresolved classification.

**Explanation of scope:** Gaudin left error classification open. Sullivan held that a reasonable-doubt instruction vitiating the standard governing all findings leaves no valid reasonable-doubt verdict for harmlessness review; it did not classify every omitted element. Roy rejects an automatic structural rule for every omission while preserving its actual-verdict inquiry and the State's burden for the preserved personal-intent habeas error it addressed. Neither that burden nor its particular classification determines this forfeited materiality claim. Because the fourth condition independently resolves this record even on the favorable assumption, the Court need not decide ordinary third-step prejudice, a proposed presumption or this defect's classification. The Court creates no universal structural-error exception to Rule 52(b).

### IV. The independent discretionary condition does not warrant correction here

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming an effect on substantial rights, this forfeited materiality error does not warrant correction under Rule 52(b) because, in the established circumstances, it did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of the proceedings. That conclusion rests on the clear materiality of the source-of-funds account in the particular investigation and the intervening change from settled trial law; it is neither a general overwhelming-evidence exception nor a substitute jury verdict.

**Authority:** Part IV of Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion is joined by all eight Associates; nine direct joins support this independently sufficient ground for affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Olano gives the fourth condition work even when the first three are satisfied. The jury's constitutional role matters to that inquiry; the defendant need not identify a second, independent institutional wrong. Nor must she prove innocence. The question is whether this forfeited violation calls for extraordinary appellate correction on this record.

The grand jury was investigating Fields's drug proceeds. Johnson attributed $80,000–$120,000 used for home improvements to a box of cash Talcott gave her mother in 1985 or 1986. An alternative explanation of those funds directly bore on the investigation and could divert it. Talcott's 1982 death concerns falsity; materiality instead follows from the account's capacity to influence the particular inquiry, without requiring actual reliance. The Court assesses that relationship as overwhelming and essentially uncontroverted in the material before it, rather than inventing a party concession or a jury finding. The record supplies no plausible materiality dispute that would make a new trial serve the protected interest in these circumstances.

The strongest objection is that confidence about a missing element can merely rename appellate factfinding. The Court does not deem that element found, declare the violation harmless under a preserved-error standard or infer materiality from falsity. It assumes the injury and applies the separate forfeiture inquiry, considering the actual omission, the direct account/investigation relationship and the trial court's adherence to settled law before Gaudin. On that confined basis, ordering a new trial would not protect the fairness or reputation of adjudication. Cases with disputed materiality, less decisive evidence or an effect on trial strategy remain open. Trial courts remain obliged to submit genuine elements to juries; likely guilt alone is not a universal reason to deny correction.
