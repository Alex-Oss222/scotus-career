## Controlling holdings

### I. A general acquittal does not categorically bar these ordinary sentencing findings

**Holding and operative rule:** A general acquittal does not prevent a sentencing court from considering reliable relevant conduct proved by a preponderance for ordinary Guidelines sentencing within the punishment legally authorized by the conviction. That permission does not authorize a new conviction, a separate disguised punishment for the acquitted offense, punishment beyond conviction-based authority, removal of an offense element from the jury, or disregard of independent due-process, evidentiary, Guidelines or Rule 32 requirements.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins to this bounded rule and its application. No unrestricted statutory-ceiling theory is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** An acquittal establishes failure to prove the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. It does not determine that every underlying fact was false. Dowling supplies that different-burden distinction; the general verdicts here establish no special factual finding of nonoccurrence. Section 3661 permits consideration of sentencing information, while the applicable Guidelines provide the firearm and relevant-quantity inquiries. Receipt of information and its legal use remain subject to distinct constitutional limits.

On double jeopardy, these ordinary findings calibrate lawful punishment for the offenses of conviction without reopening guilt or entering an independent judgment for the acquitted charges. Witte supports that bounded distinction and reserved this acquitted-conduct application, which the Court now resolves. Retained Grady and Dixon do not make this sentencing inquiry a new prosecution; they remain operative for actual successive prosecutions. The Court does not hold that an unchanged statutory ceiling alone answers every punishment objection.

On due process and jury allocation, McMillan permits ordinary judicial sentencing findings under a preponderance burden within lawful authority. The binding Guidelines consequences are real, but compulsion alone does not establish that these considerations are offense elements. Gaudin requires jury determination beyond reasonable doubt of every element; neither it nor Roy classifies the present sentencing facts as elements. No demonstrated element evasion is sanctioned.

Watts's possession finding is not identical to the full firearm-use offense, and Putra's second transaction is not the convicted sale. Their general acquittals nevertheless do not support the categorical prohibitions entered below. An extraordinary sentence-magnification question, a special factual acquittal and broader sentencing arrangements are not decided. Missing numerical sentencing details supply no finding that every possible increase is modest.

**Precedent treatment:** Dowling and McMillan are applied to the ordinary proof and allocation questions; Witte is extended at its reserved acquitted-conduct boundary only as stated. Gaudin, Roy, Grady and Dixon retain their distinct scopes.

Watts was convicted of possessing cocaine base with intent to distribute and acquitted of using a firearm in relation to a drug offense. Cocaine base was found in a kitchen cabinet and loaded guns and ammunition in a bedroom closet. The judge found firearm possession connected to the drug offense by a preponderance and applied the two-level §2D1.1(b)(1) adjustment. Putra was convicted for involvement in a one-ounce cocaine transaction on May 8, 1992, and acquitted of the corresponding charge involving five ounces on May 9. Both were videotaped. The judge found her involvement in the second transaction by a preponderance and aggregated the quantities under §1B1.3. Each court of appeals judgment rested on a categorical prohibition against the challenged acquitted-conduct use. Each preserved sentencing objection and each judgment receives separate treatment.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and decision.

## Public Action

The petition for a writ of certiorari is granted. The judgments in Watts and Putra are each summarily reversed, and each case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. The summary merits vote is 7–2.

## Holdings

### I. A general acquittal does not categorically bar these ordinary sentencing findings

**Holding and operative rule:** A general acquittal does not prevent a sentencing court from considering reliable relevant conduct proved by a preponderance for ordinary Guidelines sentencing within the punishment legally authorized by the conviction. That permission does not authorize a new conviction, a separate disguised punishment for the acquitted offense, punishment beyond conviction-based authority, removal of an offense element from the jury, or disregard of independent due-process, evidentiary, Guidelines or Rule 32 requirements.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins to this bounded rule and its application. No unrestricted statutory-ceiling theory is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** An acquittal establishes failure to prove the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. It does not determine that every underlying fact was false. Dowling supplies that different-burden distinction; the general verdicts here establish no special factual finding of nonoccurrence. Section 3661 permits consideration of sentencing information, while the applicable Guidelines provide the firearm and relevant-quantity inquiries. Receipt of information and its legal use remain subject to distinct constitutional limits.

On double jeopardy, these ordinary findings calibrate lawful punishment for the offenses of conviction without reopening guilt or entering an independent judgment for the acquitted charges. Witte supports that bounded distinction and reserved this acquitted-conduct application, which the Court now resolves. Retained Grady and Dixon do not make this sentencing inquiry a new prosecution; they remain operative for actual successive prosecutions. The Court does not hold that an unchanged statutory ceiling alone answers every punishment objection.

On due process and jury allocation, McMillan permits ordinary judicial sentencing findings under a preponderance burden within lawful authority. The binding Guidelines consequences are real, but compulsion alone does not establish that these considerations are offense elements. Gaudin requires jury determination beyond reasonable doubt of every element; neither it nor Roy classifies the present sentencing facts as elements. No demonstrated element evasion is sanctioned.

Watts's possession finding is not identical to the full firearm-use offense, and Putra's second transaction is not the convicted sale. Their general acquittals nevertheless do not support the categorical prohibitions entered below. An extraordinary sentence-magnification question, a special factual acquittal and broader sentencing arrangements are not decided. Missing numerical sentencing details supply no finding that every possible increase is modest.

**Precedent treatment:** Dowling and McMillan are applied to the ordinary proof and allocation questions; Witte is extended at its reserved acquitted-conduct boundary only as stated. Gaudin, Roy, Grady and Dixon retain their distinct scopes.

### II. Each categorical reversal requires its own lawful sentencing proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** Each Ninth Circuit judgment is reversed insofar as it categorically forbids the stated acquitted-conduct consideration, and each case is remanded separately for proceedings under §3661, the applicable Guidelines, Rule 32 and ordinary evidentiary requirements. Permission to consider conduct does not establish the correct sentence, validate an otherwise unsupported finding or require automatic reinstatement of an original term.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join the per curiam remedial direction.

**Controlling explanation:** The error concerns the legal prohibition, not a calculation this Court can supply from the reported facts. Reliable findings, the governing burden, legal sentencing authority and properly preserved ordinary objections remain necessary. Koon separates correction of a sentencing legal error from an unexamined determination that a particular term should stand. Each acquittal remains effective against conviction on the acquitted charge. Witte's conditional sentence-coordination discussion supplies no automatic aggregate cap or duplicate-punishment credit here. The Court decides no exact range, term, extraordinary-effect claim or unresolved ordinary evidentiary objection.

**Precedent treatment:** Koon's distinction between legal permission and lawful sentencing application is preserved; Witte's conditional coordination rule is not enlarged into an automatic remedy.

## Precedent Treatment

- Dowling v. United States: applies the distinction between reasonable-doubt acquittal and proof under a lower burden for a different lawful purpose; no special factual verdict is inferred.
- McMillan v. Pennsylvania: applies ordinary judicial sentencing factfinding without making every mandatory consequence an offense element; independent constitutional limits survive.
- Witte v. United States: extends its bounded conviction-based sentencing characterization to the acquitted conduct presented here. Its express reservation had left this question open. Neither its full sentencing-jury reservations nor its conditional §5G1.3 coordination requirements are silently displaced.
- Grady v. Corbin and United States v. Dixon: retain the protection applicable to actual successive prosecutions. The present sentencing inquiry does not create a new prosecution.
- United States v. Gaudin: preserves jury determination beyond reasonable doubt of actual offense elements; the decision does not establish a universal sentencing-element classification.
- California v. Roy: preserves its omitted personal-intent-element and qualified harmlessness rules; it supplies no antecedent classification of these sentencing facts.
- Koon v. United States: preserves correction of sentencing legal error and independent review of lawful calculation and application; no original term is reinstated merely because the categorical bar falls.

## Law After Decision

Effective January 6, 1997, general acquittals alone do not bar the ordinary preponderance-based relevant-conduct uses presented in Watts and Putra. The mandatory nature of the Guidelines does not by itself make these considerations offense elements. Independent due-process, jury, evidentiary and statutory limits remain; the Court adopts no rule validating every factfinding arrangement below a statutory maximum. Extraordinary magnification remains unresolved. The Court does not decide whether the Commission could change its present policy; Scalia and Breyer's opposing statutory views are separate writings, not controlling law.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, concurring; no joins.** He joins the bounded Court opinion, which does not say that every punishment below a statutory ceiling is necessarily punishment only for the conviction. His additional disagreement concerns Commission authority. In his view, §994(b)(1)'s requirement of consistency with pertinent provisions of Title 18 and §3661's protection of sentencing-information consideration forbid a Commission or judicial rule excluding otherwise lawful acquitted-conduct consideration. The Commission may recommend congressional change; it may not itself prohibit a lawful enhancement or impose an otherwise unsupported higher burden solely because the conduct was acquitted. That statutory objection is his own; Thomas's agreement with the narrow constitutional opinion supplies no join to it.

**Justice Breyer, concurring; no joins.** He joins the Court's construction of the Guidelines as written and its constitutional holding. He regards the use of acquitted conduct in mandatory enhancement as a legitimate subject of Commission policy change. Different burdens make judicial findings compatible with acquittals, but that is not the only permissible sentencing-policy consideration. The institutional significance of a jury's acquittal may support excluding that conduct from mandatory enhancement even though the present relevant-conduct provisions contain no such exception. He disagrees with Scalia's proposed statutory restriction on the Commission. Neither concurrence changes today's calculation rules.

**Justice Stevens, dissenting; no joins.** He seeks plenary briefing and argument and would, if the merits are decided now, affirm both sentence-vacatur judgments. Section 3661 permits receiving information and using it in lawful sentencing discretion; it does not itself assign mandatory weight or authorize a binding-range increase on a preponderance finding of additional criminal conduct. Section 1B1.4 addresses selection within the range or departure, while §1B1.3 determines the range through relevant conduct. Section 994(l)'s instruction about incremental punishment for multiple convictions reinforces his narrower authorization reading; the Court's contrary view treats that instruction as a minimum rather than an exclusive limit.

The criminal proof tradition and his McMillan dissent make the mandatory punitive consequence central. His Witte dissent likewise rejects the notion that a broad statutory maximum erases the concrete increment. He distinguishes discretionary use of information from facts that enlarge a binding range, and would not sustain these increases on the existing preponderance findings. In Watts, possession and use differ; his objection does not depend on a finding that the jury rejected possession. He announces no categorical ban on considering an acquitted episode, no independent Grady successive-prosecution bar, and no comprehensive new jury-allocation test. Lawful resentencing may use information in selecting an authorized sentence, but may not simply reimpose these mandatory increases on the same insufficient authorization-and-proof premise. He sets no sentence and would disturb no acquittal.

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting; no joins.** He would receive full merits briefing and oral argument before resolving this important recurring question. Dowling and Witte make the Government's objection to a categorical bar persuasive, and circuit conflict supports review. But Witte expressly reserved acquitted conduct; mandatory sentencing gives the distinction practical force, and disagreement among the reversal supporters about the Commission's authority exposes an unsettled statutory issue. Summary correction with reservations does not provide the examination he considers necessary. He neither affirms the defendants' merits position nor adopts a reasonable-doubt rule; his present dissent concerns the procedure of deciding the merits now.

## Procedure After Action

The single petition is granted and both judgments are summarily reversed. Watts returns to the Ninth Circuit for further proceedings concerning his firearm adjustment; Putra returns separately for further proceedings concerning aggregation of the second transaction. Each court must proceed under the governing sentencing law and preserved objections without the categorical acquitted-conduct bar. Neither acquittal, conviction or original term receives a new adjudication here. No future Supreme Court argument, special findings mandate, sentence cap or retained-jurisdiction return is ordered.
