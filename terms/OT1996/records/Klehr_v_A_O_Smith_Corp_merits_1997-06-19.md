## Controlling holdings

### I. A later predicate does not renew a stale injury

**Holding and operative rule:** A predicate act within four years of suit does not restart civil RICO's limitation period for an earlier actionable injury or permit stale damages merely because the act belongs to the same pattern. This rule applies to continuation of the pattern and to acts injuring someone else; the Court does not choose a universal injury, injury-discovery or injury-and-pattern-discovery trigger.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of Breyer's opinion, joined by the six Justices identified in the topology. Their direct majority adopts this limited rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Agency Holding supplies the borrowed four-year period and the close Clayton Act analogy. A pattern can continue indefinitely, with years between predicates. Restarting recovery of already actionable harm at the latest act would destroy repose and permit a knowledgeable claimant to accumulate treble damages. The criminal last-act analogy cannot displace the civil limitation adopted in Agency Holding. Holmes supplies the direct relation between violation and injury, and NOW preserves RICO's injury and causation requirements; neither previously chose a general accrual trigger. Petitioners distinguish their last-act theory from the Third Circuit's last-act-or-injury/discovery formula, but their theory still fails to the extent it revives old loss. Grimmett's unexplained dismissal supplies no accrual holding. The Court decides the revival defect without resolving the broader trigger dispute.

### II. New injury and previously unprovable damages are different from continuing loss

**Holding and operative rule:** A later wrongful act directly causing a genuinely new injury may support timely recovery for that injury without reopening older damages. Separately, damages genuinely too speculative or incapable of proof to recover earlier may accrue when they occur under Zenith; continued accumulation of calculable loss, inadequate investigation and a later calculation of old harm do not satisfy that qualification.

**Authority:** Parts I–II, with the same six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Zenith and Hanover Shoe preserve recovery from a new injurious transaction while leaving old damages barred, and Zenith separately protects losses that could not earlier be proved. Common product, damage type, defendant or pattern does not conclusively establish a single injury. Nor do later advertisements or repairs establish a new one without the required causal relation. The Eighth Circuit recognized independent-injury accrual and treated the asserted late losses as continuing damage from the original failed storage system. The reviewed allegations concern continuing dairy losses; they do not identify additional harm caused by a late act distinct from that ongoing loss. The farm losses are concrete and calculable, unlike Zenith's previously unprovable future damages. Affirmance rests on this limited application, not a categorical same-source bar. The Court finds no damages amount and does not decide that a fall-1990 repair was a fraudulent predicate. It does not determine the contents of lodging cited but not reproduced in the published excerpts, or foreclose every conceivable later injury from the product.

### III. Concealment does not eliminate reasonable diligence

**Holding and operative rule:** A civil-RICO plaintiff invoking fraudulent-concealment tolling must show that concealment prevented timely discovery despite reasonable diligence. Misleading conduct alone does not toll the period when reasonable inquiry would disclose the claim; extraordinary investigation defeating otherwise successful concealment is not required.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Bailey and Holmberg supply the equitable principle that a wrongdoer should not benefit from concealment that frustrates diligent discovery. That protection does not erase the plaintiff's responsibility to investigate facts reasonably signaling injury and its source. The antitrust borrowing context and civil RICO's encouragement of private investigation reinforce the requirement. The Klehrs' legal argument that affirmative concealment eliminates diligence therefore fails. Their separate fact-specific challenge is outside the review undertaken here, and the existing discovery determination is not retried. This decision concerns civil RICO concealment, not every equitable-estoppel doctrine. Brockamp's tax-specific construction supplies no general ban on federal equitable tolling.

- Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff: applied for the four-year period and Clayton Act analogy; no general civil-RICO trigger is attributed to it or selected here.
- Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research: applied to distinct new injury and genuinely later-provable damages; neither doctrine renews previously recoverable loss.
- Hanover Shoe v. United Shoe Machinery: applied to new injury from a continuing violation without recovery of stale damages.
- Holmes v. SIPC: preserved as the direct-injury and causation requirement; not extended into a universal accrual rule.
- NOW v. Scheidler: preserved; rejecting an economic-motive element did not eliminate injury and causation.
- Bailey v. Glover and Holmberg v. Armbrecht: applied to concealment that frustrates reasonable diligence; no diligence-free tolling rule is adopted.
- United States v. Brockamp: distinguished as a tax-specific limitations construction; no universal anti-tolling rule follows.
- Grimmett v. Brown: its unexplained certiorari dismissal supplies no substantive accrual rule.

The last-predicate-act theory cannot revive stale civil-RICO damages. Timely new injury and genuinely later-provable damages remain distinct paths with their own causal and temporal predicates. The general accrual trigger remains open, and fraudulent-concealment tolling requires reasonable diligence. The Court adopts no categorical rule that one product or pattern can cause only one injury.

**Holding and operative rule:** A predicate act within four years of suit does not restart civil RICO's limitation period for an earlier actionable injury or permit stale damages merely because the act belongs to the same pattern. This rule applies to continuation of the pattern and to acts injuring someone else; the Court does not choose a universal injury, injury-discovery or injury-and-pattern-discovery trigger.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of Breyer's opinion, joined by the six Justices identified in the topology. Their direct majority adopts this limited rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Agency Holding supplies the borrowed four-year period and the close Clayton Act analogy. A pattern can continue indefinitely, with years between predicates. Restarting recovery of already actionable harm at the latest act would destroy repose and permit a knowledgeable claimant to accumulate treble damages. The criminal last-act analogy cannot displace the civil limitation adopted in Agency Holding. Holmes supplies the direct relation between violation and injury, and NOW preserves RICO's injury and causation requirements; neither previously chose a general accrual trigger. Petitioners distinguish their last-act theory from the Third Circuit's last-act-or-injury/discovery formula, but their theory still fails to the extent it revives old loss. Grimmett's unexplained dismissal supplies no accrual holding. The Court decides the revival defect without resolving the broader trigger dispute.

### II. New injury and previously unprovable damages are different from continuing loss

**Holding and operative rule:** A later wrongful act directly causing a genuinely new injury may support timely recovery for that injury without reopening older damages. Separately, damages genuinely too speculative or incapable of proof to recover earlier may accrue when they occur under Zenith; continued accumulation of calculable loss, inadequate investigation and a later calculation of old harm do not satisfy that qualification.

**Authority:** Parts I–II, with the same six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Zenith and Hanover Shoe preserve recovery from a new injurious transaction while leaving old damages barred, and Zenith separately protects losses that could not earlier be proved. Common product, damage type, defendant or pattern does not conclusively establish a single injury. Nor do later advertisements or repairs establish a new one without the required causal relation. The Eighth Circuit recognized independent-injury accrual and treated the asserted late losses as continuing damage from the original failed storage system. The reviewed allegations concern continuing dairy losses; they do not identify additional harm caused by a late act distinct from that ongoing loss. The farm losses are concrete and calculable, unlike Zenith's previously unprovable future damages. Affirmance rests on this limited application, not a categorical same-source bar. The Court finds no damages amount and does not decide that a fall-1990 repair was a fraudulent predicate. It does not determine the contents of lodging cited but not reproduced in the published excerpts, or foreclose every conceivable later injury from the product.

### III. Concealment does not eliminate reasonable diligence

**Holding and operative rule:** A civil-RICO plaintiff invoking fraudulent-concealment tolling must show that concealment prevented timely discovery despite reasonable diligence. Misleading conduct alone does not toll the period when reasonable inquiry would disclose the claim; extraordinary investigation defeating otherwise successful concealment is not required.

**Authority:** Part III of Breyer's opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Bailey and Holmberg supply the equitable principle that a wrongdoer should not benefit from concealment that frustrates diligent discovery. That protection does not erase the plaintiff's responsibility to investigate facts reasonably signaling injury and its source. The antitrust borrowing context and civil RICO's encouragement of private investigation reinforce the requirement. The Klehrs' legal argument that affirmative concealment eliminates diligence therefore fails. Their separate fact-specific challenge is outside the review undertaken here, and the existing discovery determination is not retried. This decision concerns civil RICO concealment, not every equitable-estoppel doctrine. Brockamp's tax-specific construction supplies no general ban on federal equitable tolling.

## Precedent Treatment

- Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff: applied for the four-year period and Clayton Act analogy; no general civil-RICO trigger is attributed to it or selected here.
- Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research: applied to distinct new injury and genuinely later-provable damages; neither doctrine renews previously recoverable loss.
- Hanover Shoe v. United Shoe Machinery: applied to new injury from a continuing violation without recovery of stale damages.
- Holmes v. SIPC: preserved as the direct-injury and causation requirement; not extended into a universal accrual rule.
- NOW v. Scheidler: preserved; rejecting an economic-motive element did not eliminate injury and causation.
- Bailey v. Glover and Holmberg v. Armbrecht: applied to concealment that frustrates reasonable diligence; no diligence-free tolling rule is adopted.
- United States v. Brockamp: distinguished as a tax-specific limitations construction; no universal anti-tolling rule follows.
- Grimmett v. Brown: its unexplained certiorari dismissal supplies no substantive accrual rule.

## Law After Decision

The last-predicate-act theory cannot revive stale civil-RICO damages. Timely new injury and genuinely later-provable damages remain distinct paths with their own causal and temporal predicates. The general accrual trigger remains open, and fraudulent-concealment tolling requires reasonable diligence. The Court adopts no categorical rule that one product or pattern can cause only one injury.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** They join the reasonable-diligence ruling but would resolve accrual by borrowing the Clayton Act injury-accrual rule together with its associated limitations doctrines. Agency Holding's borrowing, read with Zenith and the borrowing principles in Johnson v. Railway Express, Hardin and Lampf, does not in their view justify taking the period while withholding its ordinary trigger. Their approach retains Zenith's qualification for genuinely later-provable loss but supplies no timely claim here. They therefore affirm without joining the Court's reservation of a general trigger.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** He agrees that a later predicate cannot revive an older time-barred injury, that genuinely new injury may accrue separately, and that concealment tolling requires reasonable diligence. He would vacate and remand because he regards the record's treatment as insufficiently specific about each alleged injury, its causal act, discoverability and tolling evidence. Lower courts should perform that inquiry injury by injury and select no universal trigger unless necessary. His position preserves timely new injury and damages previously incapable of proof, does not require knowledge of the legal label RICO, and does not demand extraordinary investigation when reasonable inquiry would be defeated by concealment.

## Procedure After Action

The Eighth Circuit judgment stands. No further Supreme Court-directed proceeding is required by this decision; ordinary mandate issuance gives effect to the affirmance at the reviewed scope.
