## Controlling holdings

### I. A shared hiring hall does not itself identify a fleet

**Holding and operative rule:** A worker may aggregate service on an identifiable fleet for Jones Act seaman status only when the vessels share ownership or operational control. Obtaining assignments through one union hiring hall does not itself supply that connection, although a hiring-hall worker may establish the required substantial connection to an actual vessel or commonly owned or controlled fleet.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a common source of labor does not turn independently directed vessels into one fleet. Chandris requires both contribution to the vessel's work and a connection substantial in nature and duration to a vessel or identifiable fleet. Its actual-assignment inquiry protects an employment relationship, rather than every maritime task a worker performs. Wilander rejects a requirement of traditional navigation duties, but does not eliminate the required vessel connection.

The contrary view treats the operators' use of the same crew market as enough to combine otherwise unrelated engagements. That would make the relevant fleet depend on which vessels happened to employ workers from that hall, without a shared operational relationship. Common ownership or control supplies the necessary objective boundary and connects the inquiry to the employment undertaken. This decision clarifies the fleet alternative; it does not attribute that previously disputed answer to Chandris. No wider common operational arrangement is established here. Daily hiring is not itself disqualifying, and neither permanent employment nor a voyage on the injury day is an absolute condition. Rejecting hall-wide aggregation leaves Papai's distinct same-employer theory for its own examination.

### II. The supported employment relationship cannot establish substantial connection in nature

**Holding and operative rule:** Considering the entire accepted engagement and the supported prior Harbor Tug work, the record permits no reasonable finding that Papai had a vessel connection substantial in nature. Summary judgment therefore must be reinstated against his Jones Act and other seaman-dependent claims; neither the deckhand designation nor a staffing declaration supplies sea-service obligations absent from the actual undertaking.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by the same six Justices as Part I. This is the sufficient status ground for their judgment, applying the fleet boundary and independently assessing the same-employer alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** Chandris directs attention to the actual assignment and preserves factfinding when the properly considered evidence permits competing reasonable conclusions. Here Papai accepted one day of dockside chipping and painting, with no planned sailing or commitment to return. His three or four specifically recalled earlier Pt. Barrow jobs and the immediately preceding Sea Fox job were maintenance. The twelve earlier Harbor Tug engagements do not establish a different continuing sea-service obligation; the Court does not find that every unidentified job was identical.

Secchitano's declaration supplies real contrary staffing evidence: Papai replaced a more regularly assigned deckhand, filled the two-deckhand complement and was dispatched into the operational crew rather than a shoreside maintenance gang. Taking those assertions into account does not change the accepted limits of this engagement. Ordinary maintenance can be part of established crew service; that proposition does not make this discrete job such a continuing relationship. The contract describes qualifications and possible duties, not performance of each duty. General testimony that most earlier hiring-hall work was deckhand work on moving boats does not establish the nature of the Harbor Tug relationship.

The Court decides no withdrawal from navigation merely because the tug was docked with idle engines, and calculates no unsupported percentage. Chandris's existing guide for ordinary arrangements is about thirty percent: a rule of thumb, not an inflexible cutoff or jurisdictional condition, permitting justified departures and genuinely changed essential assignments. Meeting it cannot replace nature; falling below it is not invariably fatal. A brief assignment involving actual sea service, a genuine continuing crew obligation or different supported prior work remains outside this holding. Gizoni's protection of genuine disputes remains intact; it does not require trial when the correctly assessed relationship permits only one legal conclusion.

### III. Reversing the status ruling does not decide the separate vessel-negligence appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** Reversal of the Ninth Circuit's reopening of the seaman claims reinstates status summary judgment only. The case returns for ordinary appellate consideration of the separately challenged post-trial vessel-negligence judgment, which the Ninth Circuit did not reach; the Court neither affirms that judgment on its merits nor automatically orders a new negligence trial.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice Souter's opinion is joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela agrees in this component's disposition without an additional ground; the eight direct rationale joins control.

**Controlling explanation:** The January 20, 1993 appeal challenged both status and the judgment after the vessel-negligence trial. The court of appeals reopened status and did not resolve the latter challenge. Its expectation that a Jones Act case would use a more favorable standard did not adjudicate the independent vessel claim. Removing that expectation cannot substitute for appellate consideration of the actual judgment.

Section 905(b) permits its specified vessel-negligence action subject to its own limits. It excludes seaworthiness as a basis of liability, bars employer indemnity to the vessel and makes its remedy exclusive against the vessel except for LHWCA remedies. Its stevedoring restriction applies where the vessel employed the worker for that service and the injury arose from negligence of those providing it. Its separate shipbuilding, repairing or breaking restriction applies when the employer is the vessel owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator or charterer, and bars the specified action against that employer in any capacity or its employees. The Court decides neither whether this painting is repairing service within that provision nor whether such a contention is properly available here. Section 905(c)'s Outer Continental Shelf reciprocal-indemnity qualification has no established predicate.

The trial court's findings about the ladder, Papai's movement and Low's credibility belong to the unresolved negligence appeal; they are not adverse concessions on the earlier status motion. No finding of negligence, causation, consortium entitlement, jury waiver or damages follows. This remand preserves the lawful appellate task without guaranteeing any independent remedy.

**Questions not reached:** The final compensation order's effect under §905(a) and ordinary administrative issue preclusion are separate questions, both unnecessary to the status holding. The Court neither adopts the lower court's nonforeclosure reasoning nor orders a new award-effects inquiry. The award remains operative in its own system. No refund, credit amount, fee adjustment, modification deadline or factual correction of that award is decided.

## Published noncontrolling positions

Stevens's dissent, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, May 12, 1997, publishes the broader shared-hall connection alternative, the narrower triable replacement/recurring-employment position, and the rejection of this final award's foreclosure under two distinct theories. Their substantive grounds and qualifications are reproduced in the Public Projection's Separate Writings block. Each is noncontrolling; no independent benefit, compensation modification or negligence result follows. Ginsburg retains her full Chandris application; Stevens and Breyer retain their actual-crew position. The Court's status rule, not these alternatives, enters current law.

**Justice Stevens, dissenting in part, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer.** They would affirm reopening of status and the rejection of foreclosure by this award, while joining Part III's preservation of the independent vessel appeal. The actual crew relationship, rather than ownership alone, should determine which recurring hiring-hall work is relevant. Operators who obtain their deckhands through that established market know the character of the work; daily dispatch should not systematically exclude people doing seaman work. This does not make every vessel a worker visits one fleet or dispense with a substantial relationship.

Even on the narrower same-employer theory, Secchitano's replacement and crew-complement evidence, repeated Harbor Tug engagements and ordinary deckhand maintenance permit a crew-service inference. The known one-day dockside job and identified prior maintenance support the employer, but do not compel its conclusion. No declaration label determines federal status, no twelve prior voyages are assumed and the general seventy-percent account is not a Harbor Tug percentage. Ginsburg applies the full Chandris framework; Stevens and Breyer retain their actual-crew approach without acquiring a join to its full temporal discussion. All three find the narrower record sufficient to reopen status without deciding that Papai is a seaman.

They also reject the two distinct award-foreclosure theories. The ALJ held a real hearing, expressly decided crew status and entered a final order; the dissent does not equate it with a voluntary check. Rather, Gizoni's accommodation of pursuing compensation while status is disputed, together with the statutory credit arrangement, supports extending nonforeclosure to this pending judicial claim. In their view §905(a) does not independently make this order an immutable classification, and Astoria's contextual inquiry permits displacement of ordinary preclusion here. Section 903(e) expressly credits specified other compensation or Jones Act payments against LHWCA liability; it does not state a universal reciprocal preclusion exemption. The dissent treats that structure as evidence of the accommodation, while acknowledging that preventing duplicate recovery and honoring final adjudication ordinarily can coexist. It finds no fictitious hearing, automatic estoppel or employer manipulation, and decides no general rule for consensual settlements or judicially reviewed status judgments. The award's own operation remains intact.

```text
§905. Exclusiveness of liability






(a) Employer liability; failure of employer to secure payment of compensation


The liability of an employer prescribed in section 904 of this title shall be exclusive and in place of all other liability of such employer to the employee, his legal representative, husband or wife, parents, dependents, next of kin, and anyone otherwise entitled to recover damages from such employer at law or in admiralty on account of such injury or death, except that if an employer fails to secure payment of compensation as required by this chapter, an injured employee, or his legal representative in case death results from the injury, may elect to claim compensation under the chapter, or to maintain an action at law or in admiralty for damages on account of such injury or death. In such action the defendant may not plead as a defense that the injury was caused by the negligence of a fellow servant, or that the employee assumed the risk of his employment, or that the injury was due to the contributory negligence of the employee. For purposes of this subsection, a contractor shall be deemed the employer of a subcontractor's employees only if the subcontractor fails to secure the payment of compensation as required by section 904 of this title.


(b) Negligence of vessel


In the event of injury to a person covered under this chapter caused by the negligence of a vessel, then such person, or anyone otherwise entitled to recover damages by reason thereof, may bring an action against such vessel as a third party in accordance with the provisions of section 933 of this title, and the employer shall not be liable to the vessel for such damages directly or indirectly and any agreements or warranties to the contrary shall be void. If such person was employed by the vessel to provide stevedoring services, no such action shall be permitted if the injury was caused by the negligence of persons engaged in providing stevedoring services to the vessel. If such person was employed to provide shipbuilding, repairing, or breaking services and such person's employer was the owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator, or charterer of the vessel, no such action shall be permitted, in whole or in part or directly or indirectly, against the injured person's employer (in any capacity, including as the vessel's owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator, or charterer) or against the employees of the employer. The liability of the vessel under this subsection shall not be based upon the warranty of seaworthiness or a breach thereof at the time the injury occurred. The remedy provided in this subsection shall be exclusive of all other remedies against the vessel except remedies available under this chapter.


(c) Outer Continental Shelf


In the event that the negligence of a vessel causes injury to a person entitled to receive benefits under this Act by virtue of section 1333 of title 43, then such person, or anyone otherwise entitled to recover damages by reason thereof, may bring an action against such vessel in accordance with the provisions of subsection (b) of this section. Nothing contained in subsection (b) of this section shall preclude the enforcement according to its terms of any reciprocal indemnity provision whereby the employer of a person entitled to receive benefits under this chapter by virtue of section 1333 of title 43 and the vessel agree to defend and indemnify the other for cost of defense and loss or liability for damages arising out of or resulting from death or bodily injury to their employees.








```

```text
§913. Filing of claims






(a) Time to file


Except as otherwise provided in this section, the right to compensation for disability or death under this chapter shall be barred unless a claim therefore is filed within one year after the injury or death. If payment of compensation has 
been made without an award on account of such injury or death, a claim may be filed within one year after the date of the last payment. Such claim shall be filed with the deputy commissioner in the compensation district in which such injury or death occurred. The time for filing a claim shall not begin to run until the employee or beneficiary is aware, or by the exercise of reasonable diligence should have been aware, of the relationship between the injury or death and the employment.


(b) Failure to file


(1) Notwithstanding the provisions of subsection (a) of this section failure to file a claim within the period prescribed in such subsection shall not be a bar to such right unless objection to such failure is made at the first hearing of such claim in which all parties in interest are given reasonable notice and opportunity to be heard.


(2) Notwithstanding the provisions of subsection (a) of this section, a claim for compensation for death or disability due to an occupational disease which does not immediately result in such death or disability shall be timely if filed within two years after the employee or claimant becomes aware, or in the exercise of reasonable diligence or by reason of medical advice should have been aware, of the relationship between the employment, the disease, and the death or disability, or within one year of the date of the last payment of compensation, whichever is later.


(c) Effect on incompetents and minors


If a person who is entitled to compensation under this chapter is mentally incompetent or a minor, the provisions of subsection (a) of this section shall not be applicable so long as such person has no guardian or other authorized representative, but shall be applicable in the case of a person who is mentally incompetent or a minor from the date of appointment of such guardian or other representative, or in the case of a minor, if no guardian is appointed before he becomes of age, from the date he becomes of age.


(d) Tolling provision


Where recovery is denied to any person, in a suit brought at law or in admiralty to recover damages in respect of injury or death, on the ground that such person was an employee and that the defendant was an employer within the meaning of this chapter and that such employer had secured compensation to such employee under this chapter, the limitation of time prescribed in subsection (a) of this section shall begin to run only from the date of termination of such suit.
```

```text
Upon his own initiative, or upon the application of any party in interest (including an employer or carrier which has been granted relief under section 908(f) of this title), on the ground of a change in conditions or because of a mistake in a determination of fact by the deputy commissioner, the deputy commissioner may, at any time prior to one year after the date of the last payment of compensation, whether or not a compensation order has been issued, or at any time prior to one year after the rejection of a claim, review a compensation case (including a case under which payments are made pursuant to section 944(i) of this title) in accordance with the procedure prescribed in respect of claims in section 919 of this title, and in accordance with such section issue a new compensation order which may terminate, continue, reinstate, increase, or decrease such compensation, or award 
compensation. Such new order shall not affect any compensation previously paid, except that an award increasing the compensation rate may be made effective from the date of the injury, and if any part of the compensation due or to become due is unpaid, an award decreasing the compensation rate may be made effective from the date of the injury, and any payment made prior thereto in excess of such decreased rate shall be deducted from any unpaid compensation, in such manner and by such method as may be determined by the deputy commissioner with the approval of the Secretary. This section does not authorize the modification of settlements.
```

## Public Projection

## Event

Harbor Tug & Barge Co. v. Papai, No. 95-1621. Argued January 13, 1997; decided May 12, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, September 25, 1995, 67 F.3d 203. The questions concern Jones Act seaman status and whether a final LHWCA award forecloses the seaman claims. Papai injured his knee during a one-day dockside painting assignment aboard Pt. Barrow; the parties dispute the legal significance of his staffing role and prior work. The appeal below also challenged the later vessel-negligence trial judgment, which the Ninth Circuit did not reach.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses reopening of the seaman-dependent claims and remands for consideration of the separately appealed vessel-negligence judgment. It does not decide either asserted final-award foreclosure theory.

## Holdings

### I. A shared hiring hall does not itself identify a fleet

**Holding and operative rule:** A worker may aggregate service on an identifiable fleet for Jones Act seaman status only when the vessels share ownership or operational control. Obtaining assignments through one union hiring hall does not itself supply that connection, although a hiring-hall worker may establish the required substantial connection to an actual vessel or commonly owned or controlled fleet.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; six direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a common source of labor does not turn independently directed vessels into one fleet. Chandris requires both contribution to the vessel's work and a connection substantial in nature and duration to a vessel or identifiable fleet. Its actual-assignment inquiry protects an employment relationship, rather than every maritime task a worker performs. Wilander rejects a requirement of traditional navigation duties, but does not eliminate the required vessel connection.

The contrary view treats the operators' use of the same crew market as enough to combine otherwise unrelated engagements. That would make the relevant fleet depend on which vessels happened to employ workers from that hall, without a shared operational relationship. Common ownership or control supplies the necessary objective boundary and connects the inquiry to the employment undertaken. This decision clarifies the fleet alternative; it does not attribute that previously disputed answer to Chandris. No wider common operational arrangement is established here. Daily hiring is not itself disqualifying, and neither permanent employment nor a voyage on the injury day is an absolute condition. Rejecting hall-wide aggregation leaves Papai's distinct same-employer theory for its own examination.

### II. The supported employment relationship cannot establish substantial connection in nature

**Holding and operative rule:** Considering the entire accepted engagement and the supported prior Harbor Tug work, the record permits no reasonable finding that Papai had a vessel connection substantial in nature. Summary judgment therefore must be reinstated against his Jones Act and other seaman-dependent claims; neither the deckhand designation nor a staffing declaration supplies sea-service obligations absent from the actual undertaking.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Souter's opinion, joined by the same six Justices as Part I. This is the sufficient status ground for their judgment, applying the fleet boundary and independently assessing the same-employer alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** Chandris directs attention to the actual assignment and preserves factfinding when the properly considered evidence permits competing reasonable conclusions. Here Papai accepted one day of dockside chipping and painting, with no planned sailing or commitment to return. His three or four specifically recalled earlier Pt. Barrow jobs and the immediately preceding Sea Fox job were maintenance. The twelve earlier Harbor Tug engagements do not establish a different continuing sea-service obligation; the Court does not find that every unidentified job was identical.

Secchitano's declaration supplies real contrary staffing evidence: Papai replaced a more regularly assigned deckhand, filled the two-deckhand complement and was dispatched into the operational crew rather than a shoreside maintenance gang. Taking those assertions into account does not change the accepted limits of this engagement. Ordinary maintenance can be part of established crew service; that proposition does not make this discrete job such a continuing relationship. The contract describes qualifications and possible duties, not performance of each duty. General testimony that most earlier hiring-hall work was deckhand work on moving boats does not establish the nature of the Harbor Tug relationship.

The Court decides no withdrawal from navigation merely because the tug was docked with idle engines, and calculates no unsupported percentage. Chandris's existing guide for ordinary arrangements is about thirty percent: a rule of thumb, not an inflexible cutoff or jurisdictional condition, permitting justified departures and genuinely changed essential assignments. Meeting it cannot replace nature; falling below it is not invariably fatal. A brief assignment involving actual sea service, a genuine continuing crew obligation or different supported prior work remains outside this holding. Gizoni's protection of genuine disputes remains intact; it does not require trial when the correctly assessed relationship permits only one legal conclusion.

### III. Reversing the status ruling does not decide the separate vessel-negligence appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** Reversal of the Ninth Circuit's reopening of the seaman claims reinstates status summary judgment only. The case returns for ordinary appellate consideration of the separately challenged post-trial vessel-negligence judgment, which the Ninth Circuit did not reach; the Court neither affirms that judgment on its merits nor automatically orders a new negligence trial.

**Authority:** Part III of Justice Souter's opinion is joined by all eight Associates. Stone-Zsela agrees in this component's disposition without an additional ground; the eight direct rationale joins control.

**Controlling explanation:** The January 20, 1993 appeal challenged both status and the judgment after the vessel-negligence trial. The court of appeals reopened status and did not resolve the latter challenge. Its expectation that a Jones Act case would use a more favorable standard did not adjudicate the independent vessel claim. Removing that expectation cannot substitute for appellate consideration of the actual judgment.

Section 905(b) permits its specified vessel-negligence action subject to its own limits. It excludes seaworthiness as a basis of liability, bars employer indemnity to the vessel and makes its remedy exclusive against the vessel except for LHWCA remedies. Its stevedoring restriction applies where the vessel employed the worker for that service and the injury arose from negligence of those providing it. Its separate shipbuilding, repairing or breaking restriction applies when the employer is the vessel owner, owner pro hac vice, agent, operator or charterer, and bars the specified action against that employer in any capacity or its employees. The Court decides neither whether this painting is repairing service within that provision nor whether such a contention is properly available here. Section 905(c)'s Outer Continental Shelf reciprocal-indemnity qualification has no established predicate.

The trial court's findings about the ladder, Papai's movement and Low's credibility belong to the unresolved negligence appeal; they are not adverse concessions on the earlier status motion. No finding of negligence, causation, consortium entitlement, jury waiver or damages follows. This remand preserves the lawful appellate task without guaranteeing any independent remedy.

**Questions not reached:** The final compensation order's effect under §905(a) and ordinary administrative issue preclusion are separate questions, both unnecessary to the status holding. The Court neither adopts the lower court's nonforeclosure reasoning nor orders a new award-effects inquiry. The award remains operative in its own system. No refund, credit amount, fee adjustment, modification deadline or factual correction of that award is decided.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis:** Applies its substantial nature-and-duration requirement, actual-assignment rule and conclusive-record boundary; clarifies the disputed fleet alternative through common ownership or operational control. Its approximate thirty-percent guide remains qualified and supplies no numerical disposition here.
- **McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander:** Applies contribution without a traditional-navigation requirement while retaining a substantial employment-related vessel connection and conclusive-record summary judgment.
- **Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni:** Preserves occupational neutrality, the voluntary-unadjudicated-benefits rule, statutory credit and genuine status disputes. Its express reservation of final awards remains open; today's majority does not extend its payment holding to this adjudicated award.
- **Astoria Federal Savings & Loan Association v. Solimino:** Its contextual administrative-preclusion framework remains undisturbed; the Court does not apply it to decide this award's collateral effect.
- **Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos:** The vessel/employer framework used below remains for the unresolved vessel-negligence appeal; status reversal decides no Scindia negligence issue.

## Law After Decision

Effective May 12, 1997, the fleet alternative requires common ownership or operational control; a shared labor source alone is insufficient. The independent nature requirement defeats status on this actual limited engagement even after the replacement declaration and supported same-employer history are considered. Brief actual seagoing service, ordinary maintenance within an established crew relationship and genuinely disputed different records remain governed by Chandris and Wilander. The qualified temporal guide survives without a new cutoff. Final-award exclusivity and preclusion remain open; the three-Justice nonforeclosure theory does not establish law.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Stevens, dissenting in part, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer.** They would affirm reopening of status and the rejection of foreclosure by this award, while joining Part III's preservation of the independent vessel appeal. The actual crew relationship, rather than ownership alone, should determine which recurring hiring-hall work is relevant. Operators who obtain their deckhands through that established market know the character of the work; daily dispatch should not systematically exclude people doing seaman work. This does not make every vessel a worker visits one fleet or dispense with a substantial relationship.

Even on the narrower same-employer theory, Secchitano's replacement and crew-complement evidence, repeated Harbor Tug engagements and ordinary deckhand maintenance permit a crew-service inference. The known one-day dockside job and identified prior maintenance support the employer, but do not compel its conclusion. No declaration label determines federal status, no twelve prior voyages are assumed and the general seventy-percent account is not a Harbor Tug percentage. Ginsburg applies the full Chandris framework; Stevens and Breyer retain their actual-crew approach without acquiring a join to its full temporal discussion. All three find the narrower record sufficient to reopen status without deciding that Papai is a seaman.

They also reject the two distinct award-foreclosure theories. The ALJ held a real hearing, expressly decided crew status and entered a final order; the dissent does not equate it with a voluntary check. Rather, Gizoni's accommodation of pursuing compensation while status is disputed, together with the statutory credit arrangement, supports extending nonforeclosure to this pending judicial claim. In their view §905(a) does not independently make this order an immutable classification, and Astoria's contextual inquiry permits displacement of ordinary preclusion here. Section 903(e) expressly credits specified other compensation or Jones Act payments against LHWCA liability; it does not state a universal reciprocal preclusion exemption. The dissent treats that structure as evidence of the accommodation, while acknowledging that preventing duplicate recovery and honoring final adjudication ordinarily can coexist. It finds no fictitious hearing, automatic estoppel or employer manipulation, and decides no general rule for consensual settlements or judicially reviewed status judgments. The award's own operation remains intact.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit. It must implement the status ruling and consider the still-unresolved appeal from the vessel-negligence judgment under the ordinarily available grounds and statutory limits. The Court sets no new filing deadline, awards no damages, decides no negligence finding or jury waiver, and directs no compensation appeal or modification. Independent remedies remain available only on their own lawful predicates.
