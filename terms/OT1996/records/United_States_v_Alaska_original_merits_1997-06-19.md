## Controlling holdings

### I. The normal baseline governs this coast

**Holding and operative rule:** Alaska's statutory three-mile submerged-land grant is measured from the Convention's normal low-water baseline, including applicable river and bay closing rules and separate belts of qualifying islands. The evidence establishes no firm and continuing general federal rule converting all waters behind fringing islands with entrances under ten miles into inland waters; the earlier Mississippi Sound judgment does not preclude that conclusion.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by all nine Justices. This coalition also directly adopts the Dinkum holding.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. California supplies the Convention framework. Article 4 permits straight baselines in an appropriate island-fringe setting, but does not require their selection; the United States made no such choice here. The international twelve-mile sea does not enlarge the statutory three-mile State grant.

Alaska claims a general federal practice, not specific historic title to Stefansson Sound, so its claim must be tested on that basis. The 1930 assimilation proposal used four-mile closures, its high-seas straits proposal used six-mile entrances, and its inland-sea proposal used ten-mile bay principles. The Chapman Line, Webb letter, Gulf submissions, 1949 materials and 1961 letter do not establish the asserted uniform ten-mile rule. Leasing convenience and avoidance of federal enclaves cannot supply the missing practice.

The 1985 Alabama/Mississippi decision established Mississippi Sound's particular historic status through specific assertions and foreign acquiescence, not the universal formulation now asserted. Preclusion therefore fails even assuming it is available; Mendoza's general rule against nonmutual estoppel against the United States requires no expansion or new original-action exception. The later Alabama/Mississippi and Massachusetts decrees retain their geographic force. The Nome permit decision addresses an artificial extension's added entitlement, not these natural features. No general authority to contract existing state territory or foreclose a different proved inland-water claim is decided.

### II. Dinkum Sands does not satisfy the island definition

**Holding and operative rule:** A naturally formed feature must ordinarily remain above the agreed mean-high-water datum to qualify as an island under Convention Article 10(1). Dinkum Sands' frequent seasonal submergence below that datum defeats island status and a separate three-mile grant; isolated emergence does not create alternating island entitlement, and abnormal temporary inundation of an otherwise enduring island remains distinct.

**Authority:** Parts I–II, with all nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Article 10's high-tide requirement describes an ordinary characteristic of an island. Its drafting history distinguishes exceptional inundation from an ordinarily shifting gravel-and-ice formation. Borax and the California decree support the mean-high-water datum, averaged over approximately nineteen years, rather than an absolute permanence condition.

The joint monitoring study and subsequent measurements support the Master's finding that Dinkum Sands frequently falls below that datum. The survey found no reliably above-datum elevation; limited readings within the error band and later observed emergence do not establish an ordinarily above-water feature. The 1971 chart and 1979 map relied on an earlier survey, and visual appearance does not displace calibrated elevations. Alaska's argument that Article 10 contains no permanence adjective therefore does not warrant a legal coastline appearing and disappearing with ordinary seasonal changes in this formation. Natural accretion or erosion of an established island presents a different issue. No percentage-of-days test is adopted. Article 11's low-tide-elevation rule supplies no separate entitlement at the reported distances exceeding three miles from the mainland and nearby islands.

### III. The Petroleum Reserve instruments clearly retain the disputed submerged estate

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal retention requires clear inclusion of the submerged lands and clear intent to defeat future state title for an appropriate public purpose; geography or general purpose alone does not suffice. The 1923 Petroleum Reserve order's boundary and petroleum function, together with Congress's specific Statehood Act treatment of that reserve, satisfy both requirements and retain the disputed submerged estate, under the same demanding inquiry for equal-footing and statutory territorial-sea lands.

**Authority:** Parts III–IV of O'Connor's opinion, directly adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven also directly adopt the Range holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Shively recognizes Congress's territorial power for appropriate public purposes. Utah Division and Montana require separate clear inclusion and intent to defeat later state title. The order's ocean-side barrier boundary, closures across small lagoons and express three-mile qualification identify the submerged estate. Excluding submerged portions of the same petroleum deposits would defeat the reserve's national-defense purpose. Unlike Montana's unneeded riverbed or Utah Division's reservoir-control rationale, ownership of these deposits performs the reservation's central work.

Section 11(b) preserves exclusive federal legislative authority over federally owned military lands at admission and specifically names the naval petroleum reserve. Read with its defined estate and petroleum purpose, Congress confirmed continued ownership and ratified submerged inclusion. Legislative jurisdiction alone would not establish title, and §11(b) is not rewritten as a generic submerged-title clause. The specific recognition and operative estate answer the express-retention objection. Section 1313(a)'s requirement is not diluted by a presumption favoring federal ownership merely because the Act is a federal grant. Congress's confirmation makes it unnecessary to resolve the disputed independent reach of the Pickett Act; no broad Executive power to defeat future state title is announced.

### IV. The prestatehood Range segregation comes within the wildlife exclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** The 1957 application and legally operative temporary-segregation regulation set the specifically described submerged lands apart for the proposed wildlife refuge before statehood. Statehood Act §6(e)'s exclusion of lands withdrawn or otherwise set apart as wildlife refuges therefore retained those lands, although the application was not a completed final reservation; lands outside its boundaries or lacking comparable legal effect are not decided.

**Authority:** Parts III–IV, with the same seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas neither join the timing/legal-effect rule nor assent to the separate geographic determination.

**Controlling explanation:** Utah Division and Montana require clear inclusion separately from title retention. The changed extreme-low-water boundary, coastal submerged features and stated habitat needs establish inclusion. The application sought protection for polar bears, foxes, seals and whales, including the identified aquatic habitat.

The regulation immediately segregated the land from settlement, location, sale, selection, entry, lease and other public-land disposals, including mining and mineral leasing, only to the extent the proposed reservation, if effected, would prevent them. It left administrative jurisdiction unchanged. Those limits do not remove the presently effective, purpose-specific segregation while the Secretary considers the application. Section 6(e)'s broader alternative reaches land legally set apart in that manner; requiring a completed reservation underreads that alternative. The later choice of all or part of the area does not erase the current segregation of the described land.

Congress's wildlife exclusion supplies clear title-defeating intent; neither general federal ownership nor wildlife purpose alone suffices. Section 6(m)'s incorporation of the Submerged Lands Act performs different work. The 1960 order is not given retroactive effect, and the 1980 enlargement does not fix the original statehood boundary. Alaska's defensive inclusion argument overlaps the application's legal effect and is addressed despite its lack of a separate exception to the Master's geographic discussion. The resulting decree must implement these identified classifications, not assumed new boundaries.

The provision is not rewritten as an express generic submerged-title clause, and federal regulatory jurisdiction alone would not establish ownership. The precise named-reserve recognition and petroleum ownership purpose supply the title conclusion. Because Congress could retain that estate at admission and confirmed it, the result does not require deciding the disputed independent reach of the Pickett Act. No broad Executive power to defeat future state title is announced.

**Petroleum objection of Scalia and Thomas.** The Submerged Lands Act is the operative grant for this post-1953 State, and its exception demands an express retention of submerged lands, in terms embracing the land under water. In their view, necessary inclusion inferred from an exterior boundary and resource purpose cannot replace that requirement; congressional ratification cannot cure an instrument lacking it. Section 11(b)'s legislative-jurisdiction and existing-ownership language does not expressly retain the submerged estate. They would sustain Alaska's exception. They do not extend this statutory analysis to States admitted before the Act or announce that Congress lacks constitutional power to retain submerged lands with adequate language.

**Arctic Range — six-Justice rule.** The 1957 application and the operative temporary-segregation regulation set the specifically described lands apart for the proposed wildlife refuge before Alaska entered the Union; §6(e)'s exclusion for lands withdrawn or otherwise set apart as wildlife refuges therefore retained those submerged lands at statehood. The application was not a completed final reservation, but the statute's broader alternative reaches this legally effective, purpose-specific segregation.

The low-water boundary, submerged coastal features and habitat justification establish inclusion separately from retention. Federal protection of the identified aquatic habitat supports that inclusion under Utah Division/Montana's demanding test. The regulation segregates the lands from the enumerated forms of disposal only to the extent the proposed reservation, if effected, would bar them; it also preserves administrative jurisdiction. Those qualifications are retained. They do not remove the presently effective segregation pending the Secretary's decision. Section 6(e), not the application's mere existence or the 1960 order retroactively, supplies congressional retention. Requiring a completed reservation would underread the broader phrase 'otherwise set apart'; the six reject the contention that 'as' makes final establishment indispensable in this setting.

Section 6(m)'s incorporation of the Submerged Lands Act remains distinct from §6(e)'s wildlife exception. The latter supplies clear title-defeating intent for the specific lands already set apart; neither general federal ownership nor wildlife purpose alone would suffice. The 1980 expansion does not determine the original statehood boundary. Alaska's defensive inclusion argument is addressed because it overlaps with the legal effect of the application, notwithstanding its lack of a separate exception to the Master's geographic discussion.

**Range objection of Scalia and Thomas.** An application preserving a prospective decision from inconsistent disposal is not land already set apart as a refuge. The applicant expressly left all or part of the acreage for later determination, administrative jurisdiction remained unchanged, and the decision on actual refuge creation was substantive rather than ministerial. 'Otherwise set apart' can cover effective refuge creation by means other than formal withdrawal without encompassing every pending application. The two would overrule the United States' exception. They do not join the six's review and resolution of the unexcepted geographic Question 10; they express no contrary geographic finding and regard the timing/legal-effect issue as sufficient. Their refusal must not be counted as assent to clear submerged inclusion.

**Disposition and stage:** For the six, overrule Alaska's three exceptions, sustain the United States' Range exception, adopt the Master's recommendations only as consistent with these rulings, direct submission of a proposed implementing decree, retain jurisdiction to effectuate and supplement it, and have each party bear its own costs. The two join that procedural mechanism only with their different reservation-title dispositions. ## Holdings

### I. The normal baseline governs this coast

**Holding and operative rule:** Alaska's statutory three-mile submerged-land grant is measured from the Convention's normal low-water baseline, including applicable river and bay closing rules and separate belts of qualifying islands. The evidence establishes no firm and continuing general federal rule converting all waters behind fringing islands with entrances under ten miles into inland waters; the earlier Mississippi Sound judgment does not preclude that conclusion.

**Authority:** Parts I–II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by all nine Justices. This coalition also directly adopts the Dinkum holding.

**Controlling explanation:** United States v. California supplies the Convention framework. Article 4 permits straight baselines in an appropriate island-fringe setting, but does not require their selection; the United States made no such choice here. The international twelve-mile sea does not enlarge the statutory three-mile State grant.

Alaska claims a general federal practice, not specific historic title to Stefansson Sound, so its claim must be tested on that basis. The 1930 assimilation proposal used four-mile closures, its high-seas straits proposal used six-mile entrances, and its inland-sea proposal used ten-mile bay principles. The Chapman Line, Webb letter, Gulf submissions, 1949 materials and 1961 letter do not establish the asserted uniform ten-mile rule. Leasing convenience and avoidance of federal enclaves cannot supply the missing practice.

The 1985 Alabama/Mississippi decision established Mississippi Sound's particular historic status through specific assertions and foreign acquiescence, not the universal formulation now asserted. Preclusion therefore fails even assuming it is available; Mendoza's general rule against nonmutual estoppel against the United States requires no expansion or new original-action exception. The later Alabama/Mississippi and Massachusetts decrees retain their geographic force. The Nome permit decision addresses an artificial extension's added entitlement, not these natural features. No general authority to contract existing state territory or foreclose a different proved inland-water claim is decided.

### II. Dinkum Sands does not satisfy the island definition

**Holding and operative rule:** A naturally formed feature must ordinarily remain above the agreed mean-high-water datum to qualify as an island under Convention Article 10(1). Dinkum Sands' frequent seasonal submergence below that datum defeats island status and a separate three-mile grant; isolated emergence does not create alternating island entitlement, and abnormal temporary inundation of an otherwise enduring island remains distinct.

**Authority:** Parts I–II, with all nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** Article 10's high-tide requirement describes an ordinary characteristic of an island. Its drafting history distinguishes exceptional inundation from an ordinarily shifting gravel-and-ice formation. Borax and the California decree support the mean-high-water datum, averaged over approximately nineteen years, rather than an absolute permanence condition.

The joint monitoring study and subsequent measurements support the Master's finding that Dinkum Sands frequently falls below that datum. The survey found no reliably above-datum elevation; limited readings within the error band and later observed emergence do not establish an ordinarily above-water feature. The 1971 chart and 1979 map relied on an earlier survey, and visual appearance does not displace calibrated elevations. Alaska's argument that Article 10 contains no permanence adjective therefore does not warrant a legal coastline appearing and disappearing with ordinary seasonal changes in this formation. Natural accretion or erosion of an established island presents a different issue. No percentage-of-days test is adopted. Article 11's low-tide-elevation rule supplies no separate entitlement at the reported distances exceeding three miles from the mainland and nearby islands.

### III. The Petroleum Reserve instruments clearly retain the disputed submerged estate

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal retention requires clear inclusion of the submerged lands and clear intent to defeat future state title for an appropriate public purpose; geography or general purpose alone does not suffice. The 1923 Petroleum Reserve order's boundary and petroleum function, together with Congress's specific Statehood Act treatment of that reserve, satisfy both requirements and retain the disputed submerged estate, under the same demanding inquiry for equal-footing and statutory territorial-sea lands.

**Authority:** Parts III–IV of O'Connor's opinion, directly adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven also directly adopt the Range holding.

**Controlling explanation:** Shively recognizes Congress's territorial power for appropriate public purposes. Utah Division and Montana require separate clear inclusion and intent to defeat later state title. The order's ocean-side barrier boundary, closures across small lagoons and express three-mile qualification identify the submerged estate. Excluding submerged portions of the same petroleum deposits would defeat the reserve's national-defense purpose. Unlike Montana's unneeded riverbed or Utah Division's reservoir-control rationale, ownership of these deposits performs the reservation's central work.

Section 11(b) preserves exclusive federal legislative authority over federally owned military lands at admission and specifically names the naval petroleum reserve. Read with its defined estate and petroleum purpose, Congress confirmed continued ownership and ratified submerged inclusion. Legislative jurisdiction alone would not establish title, and §11(b) is not rewritten as a generic submerged-title clause. The specific recognition and operative estate answer the express-retention objection. Section 1313(a)'s requirement is not diluted by a presumption favoring federal ownership merely because the Act is a federal grant. Congress's confirmation makes it unnecessary to resolve the disputed independent reach of the Pickett Act; no broad Executive power to defeat future state title is announced.

### IV. The prestatehood Range segregation comes within the wildlife exclusion

**Holding and operative rule:** The 1957 application and legally operative temporary-segregation regulation set the specifically described submerged lands apart for the proposed wildlife refuge before statehood. Statehood Act §6(e)'s exclusion of lands withdrawn or otherwise set apart as wildlife refuges therefore retained those lands, although the application was not a completed final reservation; lands outside its boundaries or lacking comparable legal effect are not decided.

**Authority:** Parts III–IV, with the same seven direct joins. Scalia and Thomas neither join the timing/legal-effect rule nor assent to the separate geographic determination.

**Controlling explanation:** Utah Division and Montana require clear inclusion separately from title retention. The changed extreme-low-water boundary, coastal submerged features and stated habitat needs establish inclusion. The application sought protection for polar bears, foxes, seals and whales, including the identified aquatic habitat.

The regulation immediately segregated the land from settlement, location, sale, selection, entry, lease and other public-land disposals, including mining and mineral leasing, only to the extent the proposed reservation, if effected, would prevent them. It left administrative jurisdiction unchanged. Those limits do not remove the presently effective, purpose-specific segregation while the Secretary considers the application. Section 6(e)'s broader alternative reaches land legally set apart in that manner; requiring a completed reservation underreads that alternative. The later choice of all or part of the area does not erase the current segregation of the described land.

Congress's wildlife exclusion supplies clear title-defeating intent; neither general federal ownership nor wildlife purpose alone suffices. Section 6(m)'s incorporation of the Submerged Lands Act performs different work. The 1960 order is not given retroactive effect, and the 1980 enlargement does not fix the original statehood boundary. Alaska's defensive inclusion argument overlaps the application's legal effect and is addressed despite its lack of a separate exception to the Master's geographic discussion. The resulting decree must implement these identified classifications, not assumed new boundaries.
