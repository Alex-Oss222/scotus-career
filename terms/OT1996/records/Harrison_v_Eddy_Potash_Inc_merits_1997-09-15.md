## Controlling holdings

### I. Material aid from actual delegated power does not require alter-ego rank

**Holding and operative rule:** An employer may be responsible under Title VII when actual delegated supervisory power materially aids creation or maintenance of an actionable hostile environment; the plaintiff must prove that connection by a preponderance of the evidence. Hiring or firing authority, alter-ego identity, higher-management notice, an express threat, a completed personnel action and tangible economic loss are not invariably required, while rank, employment or workplace proximity alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, Parts I–V, has all nine direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common coalition controls all five holdings and the mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** Title VII's inclusion of an employer's agents and Meritor require an actual agency inquiry, distinct from ordinary employment scope and negligence. Andrade, Oncale and Faragher establish the plaintiff-proved connection between delegated authority and the unlawful conditions. Personal purpose or violation of a prohibition does not alone negate that connection; permission to harass is not its prerequisite.

Harrison's account describes Brown using work orders to move her away from her station or buddy into isolated areas, where misconduct allegedly followed. Control over where, with whom and on what task she worked could materially aid abuse even without top-management powers. That evidence supports submission of the route; the Court does not find every incident proved. Conversely, impressive powers do not establish that they aided any particular wrong.

The agency definition required managerial control making Brown the employer's alter ego. Its reference to control over employment conditions did not remove that categorical restriction. Meritor rejects the opposite status-alone rule too, so rejection of Harrison's overbroad proposed formulations was proper. She was entitled to an accurate instruction on a supported route, not every requested sentence.

An actionable environment and sex-based causation remain separate. Harris requires conditions sufficiently severe or pervasive, in all the circumstances, to be objectively hostile or abusive and actually perceived as abusive. Frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation and interference with work inform that inquiry; none is indispensable, and psychological injury, treatment and lost productivity are not prerequisites. Oncale preserves proof that the conditions were imposed because of sex. Grave misconduct alone supplies neither causation nor employer attribution.

### II. The charge prejudicially withheld the supported actual-authority route

**Holding and operative rule:** Considered as a whole against this record, the preserved charge was prejudicially incomplete because it made apparent authority and reasonable reliance cumulative requirements, imposed an alter-ego barrier and omitted the supported actual-authority/material-aid route. If a distinct apparent-authority theory is properly submitted, it requires reasonable belief traceable to the employer's words or conduct and reasonable reliance on that appearance, not merely the supervisor's claimed power; that alternative is not a condition of actual-authority attribution.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II. The actual-authority omission independently suffices for affirmance of the instructional reversal; factual sufficiency of apparent authority is not decided.

**Controlling explanation:** The principal charge was given over objection, and the requested instructions presented the aid issue as well as overbroad status-based theories. Independent review of the whole charge, with prejudice required, differs from reviewing the choice of a particular requested instruction for abuse of discretion. Neither inquiry requires verbatim use of an inaccurate proposal.

The charge's reference to authority otherwise than apparently did not explain material aid or remove the mandatory apparent-authority and reliance elements. Brown's daily control and lack of top-management status made those barriers consequential. Work-order evidence concretely supported the omitted route. A possible jury rejection of another Title VII element does not establish that this general employer verdict rested on an independent, correctly decided ground; no special finding supplies that conclusion. Nor does the separate tort verdict against Brown establish the statutory elements against Potash. This is a record-specific prejudice determination, not automatic reversal for any agency error.

Restatement §27 supplies the limited principal-manifestation clarification. Faragher left apparent authority undecided; its separate observations are not a Court holding. An employee's knowledge of a supervisor's assertion is not a substitute for manifestations attributable to the employer. Knowledge that harassment is forbidden does not erase actual command power. An effectively implemented policy may bear on a properly framed apparent-authority inquiry, but the Court decides neither that route's factual sufficiency nor that ignorance of policy alone establishes it. It does not adopt every apparent-authority formulation below or extend Faragher's actual-delegation rule to every claimed apparent power.

### III. Policy and response do not cure the omitted route; a general complete defense remains open

**Holding and operative rule:** A prohibition of harassment and correction after notice do not themselves negate earlier material aid from actual delegated power or cure the prejudicially incomplete charge. The Court preserves Faragher's reservation concerning a general complete defense based on genuinely effective prevention and unreasonable employee nonuse, including its elements, burden, relation to tangible employment action and any distinct limitation on relief.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part III, including its limited reservation of the Court's general complete-defense question. No complete defense is adopted or supplied to Potash.

**Controlling explanation:** Faragher rejected the ineffective-policy contention on its own proved record while adopting neither a general defense nor categorical rejection of every such defense. Harrison requires her own policy chronology. She discovered a posted policy after some incidents, and it was absent from her new-hire materials. Those facts establish neither universal ignorance nor that every reporting route was unavailable, nonuse was unreasonable or every remedial step was inadequate.

After her July report, Potash investigated, promised lost-time compensation and treatment, changed her crew, reprimanded Brown, placed him on permanent probation for recurrence and restricted contact. Those measures retain their potential relevance to employer care, response and relief; they do not retroactively remove earlier actual aid. Failed dissemination alone is not negligence per se. The investigator's acceptance of Harrison's unwilling participation alongside Brown's asserted perception of consent is not a judicial finding that the conduct was welcome; Meritor distinguishes unwelcome conduct from apparently voluntary participation.

The different policy facts permit their proper consideration, but establish no complete defense here. The charge was not an accurate, separately justified instruction on an established defense. Faragher's reservation does not expand the granted or preserved issues, create a reporting duty or supply an appellate finding against Harrison. Appropriate apparent-authority, negligence and remedial inquiries remain distinct. No omnibus policy immunity, new burden-shifting formula or freestanding policy-publication duty is adopted.

### IV. This appellate record establishes no contractual bar to the independent Title VII action

**Holding and operative rule:** A contractual grievance assertion does not remove federal subject-matter jurisdiction over an independent Title VII claim. Potash cannot obtain dismissal for nonexhaustion on this appellate record, which includes neither its CBA nor the district court's exhaustion ruling and identifies no agreement covering arbitration of statutory claims or delegation of the antecedent question.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part IV and affirmance of the rejection of the employer's cross-appeal.

**Controlling explanation:** Gardner-Denver preserves independent Title VII rights despite contractual grievance arbitration. Gilmer enforces an actual agreement to arbitrate the statutory ADEA claim; it does not supply a missing agreement here or overrule the contractual/statutory distinction. First Options requires courts independently to determine agreement and clear, unmistakable delegation under applicable contract law. A bargaining relationship or asserted grievance process proves neither statutory scope nor delegation. Its conditional rule for review after actual delegation is not reached.

The appellate opinion's two footnotes report the CBA and order omissions and that Potash's brief did not identify statutory arbitration. These are actual limits of its appellate presentation, not a finding that no contract ever existed or a fabricated waiver. The strongest enforcement argument recognizes that a proven forum agreement can create duties without extinguishing substantive rights; it still requires the agreement this record does not establish.

Livadas and Hawaiian Airlines require attention to the actual source of a right and whether disputed contract interpretation is necessary. Whether sex-based conditions and delegated power meet Title VII's elements does not require such interpretation here. Their distinct preemption holdings do not establish immunity from every arbitration agreement. The Court declines the lower court's blanket CBA/FAA rationale. Gilmer reserved the general employment-contract exclusion; that question and the validity of every explicit collective statutory-arbitration agreement remain unresolved. No contract-recovery or new exhaustion proceeding is ordered merely to rescue the unsupported cross-appeal.

### A. Actual authority, managerial rank and the instruction

**Adopted path.** Andrade, February 26, 1997, supplies a plaintiff-proved connection between actual delegated power and the actionable environment. **Strongest contrary path: preserve the charge.** Its reference to control over employment conditions could encompass a shift foreman; its reference to authority beyond apparent authority might be read broadly; refusing the employee's proposed automatic-supervisor instructions was proper. ## 3. Shared issue analysis and competing lawful paths

### A. Actual authority, managerial rank and the instruction

**Adopted path.** Andrade, February 26, 1997, supplies a plaintiff-proved connection between actual delegated power and the actionable environment. Oncale, March 19, preserves attribution separately from sex causation and the Harris threshold. Faragher Part I, September 8, applies that rule while preserving its limits. Each Associate joined all three relevant propositions; Faragher's eight-Associate application demonstrates that daily operational power can matter without a completed personnel act. It does not establish what happened in Harrison.

Here Brown's work assignment power is not simply a title-based inference. The lower opinion, Appendix A at 3a–6a and 27a, reports evidence of orders taking Harrison from her station or buddy into isolated areas, successive work-related excursions followed by alleged sexual misconduct, disciplinary participation and a work-through-lunch order following a refusal. This evidence permits a finding of material aid and requires a legally accurate opportunity to decide that route. It remains evidence, not a new Supreme Court finding that every incident occurred or every element was proved.

The principal attribution charge, Appendix A at 24a–27a, demanded all of its listed agency, apparent-authority and reliance elements. The accompanying definition required managerial control amounting to alter-ego identity. The apparent-authority charge, 28a–31a, again conditioned liability on authority to commit the challenged conduct. Together they foreclosed the separate route even if the jury believed that actual assignment power materially aided the abuse. The phrase addressing authority otherwise than apparently did not supply a material-aid instruction or remove the cumulative requirements.

**Strongest contrary path: preserve the charge.** Its reference to control over employment conditions could encompass a shift foreman; its reference to authority beyond apparent authority might be read broadly; refusing the employee's proposed automatic-supervisor instructions was proper. Those are genuine points against treating every disputed word as error. They do not cure the entire charge: managerial control was expressly tied to alter-ego identity, apparent authority and reliance remained mandatory, and the instructions never asked whether actual delegated power materially aided the environment. The record distinguishes refusal of an inaccurate proposed sentence from refusal of any accurate instruction on the supported theory. Requested instruction 7 contained both a potentially valid aid theory and an invalid status-alone theory; instruction 24 raised aid, while instruction 25 proposed status-based responsibility. No commitment requires the court to give any proposal verbatim.

**Other broader path: impose automatic responsibility from supervisory status.** Rejected by Meritor and the three current-term decisions. Removing an alter-ego prerequisite does not turn every supervisor into the employer for every personal act. Nor does Brown's conceded employment establish the material-aid connection. This path cannot support either a rule or a directed employer judgment.

### B. Whole-charge prejudice and apparent authority

**Adopted path.** The error was prejudicial on this record. The principal instruction was given over objection; the employee requested the omitted route; the appellate court expressly considered the charge as a whole and found the route viable on the evidence, Appendix A at 24a–34a. Daily-control evidence coupled with lack of top-management status made the incorrect barrier consequential. The work-order evidence gave the omitted route a concrete basis, independent of belief that harassment was permitted. No supplied special verdict establishes that the jury necessarily rejected an untainted element independently of attribution.

**Strongest contrary path: error but harmlessness.** A jury could have rejected an actionable environment or discriminatory causation, and the separate verdict against Brown does not establish those statutory elements against Potash. Some proposed instructions were overbroad, while the employer had a policy and responded after notice. Those propositions prevent automatic liability but do not show that this jury's employer verdict rested on an independent, correctly decided ground. A possible lawful explanation for a general verdict cannot cure a charge that excluded a supported liability route. Policy knowledge after some incidents and the later response likewise do not establish that actual authority supplied no material aid. The commitment is a record-specific finding of prejudicial instructional error, not a rule that every instruction error requires reversal or that prejudice is presumed whenever a general verdict is returned.

**Narrower lawful option.** Affirm on the independently sufficient actual-authority omission without deciding whether a correctly instructed jury could find apparent authority. Each Associate accepts that limit. Restatement §27, reproduced at 30a, supports the modest clarification that the employee's reasonable belief must arise from the principal's words or conduct. Faragher did not decide apparent authority; its separate observations cannot supply a Court holding. There is no need to adopt the lower opinion's entire apparent-authority framework, its characterization of apparent permission to harass, or its suggestion that unknown policy by itself supplies sufficient apparent authority. Nor is there a need to extend Faragher's actual-delegation holding to every claimed apparent work power.

### C. Prevention, reporting and response

**Adopted path.** Preserve the distinction among actual authority, apparent authority, negligence and remedy. A policy can affect reasonable belief or employer care when the required facts are present. Prompt investigation and correction can bear on response and relief; they do not retroactively remove earlier actual aid. The account reports that Harrison learned of a posted policy after some incidents and that it was absent from her new-hire materials. It does not establish universal ignorance, no available official, unreasonable nonuse, a particular policy promise, or the legal inadequacy of every later step. The employer's belief that Brown perceived consent does not turn its finding of unwilling participation into a judicial finding that conduct was welcome.

**Strongest contrary path: complete defense from policy and response.** Faragher expressly left open a possible defense involving genuinely effective protection and unreasonable employee nonuse. Harrison's eventual knowledge and Potash's remedial measures distinguish its facts from Faragher's undistributed policy. That difference permits attention to the actual evidence in appropriate inquiries; it does not establish a complete defense, its governing elements or its prerequisites here. The charge was not an accurate, separately justified instruction on a recognized defense. The reservation neither expands this grant and preservation nor supplies an appellate finding against Harrison. All eight decline to adopt a new complete defense or reject every possible such defense in deciding this instructional appeal.

### D. Independent statutory rights and the actual contract record

**Adopted path.** Gardner-Denver, 415 U.S. 36 (1974), preserves the distinction between contractual grievance rights and the independent Title VII right. Gilmer, 500 U.S. 20 (1991), enforced an actual agreement to arbitrate the statutory ADEA claim and distinguished the earlier contractual-arbitration cases. First Options, May 22, 1995, requires courts to determine the antecedent agreement and delegation rather than presume them from an assertion of arbitral power. Its scope is not expanded into a new rule about union representation.

The decisive record limitation is a litigation fact reported in Appendix A at 35a n.1 and 40a n.2: Potash supplied neither the CBA nor the district court's order on exhaustion in the appellate record, and its appellate brief did not identify an agreement to arbitrate statutory claims. That prevents the employer from establishing the statutory obligation it needs for reversal. It is not a finding that no contract ever existed, a fabricated waiver, or an inference from the operator's inability to retrieve a document. Membership in a union and the asserted existence of a grievance procedure do not establish statutory scope or delegation. No disputed contractual interpretation is necessary to decide whether sex-based conduct and delegated work power meet Title VII's elements.

**Strongest contrary path: enforce the contractual procedure.** Gilmer rejects a categorical objection to arbitration of statutory employment claims, and a proven agreement can create enforceable duties without extinguishing substantive rights. A clause could, in another case, present the question of an express collective statutory-arbitration obligation. This path fails as a basis for Potash's requested dismissal because no such clause or delegation is established in the actual appellate record. It cannot be supplied by inventing terms or presuming that a generic grievance process governs statutory claims.

**Broader alternative and limits.** A categorical ruling that no CBA may ever commit statutory claims to arbitration would exceed what is necessary here. A categorical FAA exclusion for every employment or collective agreement likewise does not follow from Gilmer, which reserved the general employment-exclusion question. The lower court's broader rationale is not adopted. Livadas, June 13, 1994, and Hawaiian Airlines, June 20, 1994, preserve independent rights where adjudication needs no disputed contract interpretation, but their distinct preemption holdings do not decide every agreement's enforceability. The narrow ground fully rejects this defense; no contract-recovery remand is ordered merely to rescue the unsupported cross-appeal.

## Holdings

### I. Material aid from actual delegated power does not require alter-ego rank

**Holding and operative rule:** An employer may be responsible under Title VII when actual delegated supervisory power materially aids creation or maintenance of an actionable hostile environment; the plaintiff must prove that connection by a preponderance of the evidence. Hiring or firing authority, alter-ego identity, higher-management notice, an express threat, a completed personnel action and tangible economic loss are not invariably required, while rank, employment or workplace proximity alone is insufficient.

**Authority:** Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, Parts I–V, has all nine direct joins: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common coalition controls all five holdings and the mandate.

**Controlling explanation:** Title VII's inclusion of an employer's agents and Meritor require an actual agency inquiry, distinct from ordinary employment scope and negligence. Andrade, Oncale and Faragher establish the plaintiff-proved connection between delegated authority and the unlawful conditions. Personal purpose or violation of a prohibition does not alone negate that connection; permission to harass is not its prerequisite.

Harrison's account describes Brown using work orders to move her away from her station or buddy into isolated areas, where misconduct allegedly followed. Control over where, with whom and on what task she worked could materially aid abuse even without top-management powers. That evidence supports submission of the route; the Court does not find every incident proved. Conversely, impressive powers do not establish that they aided any particular wrong.

The agency definition required managerial control making Brown the employer's alter ego. Its reference to control over employment conditions did not remove that categorical restriction. Meritor rejects the opposite status-alone rule too, so rejection of Harrison's overbroad proposed formulations was proper. She was entitled to an accurate instruction on a supported route, not every requested sentence.

An actionable environment and sex-based causation remain separate. Harris requires conditions sufficiently severe or pervasive, in all the circumstances, to be objectively hostile or abusive and actually perceived as abusive. Frequency, severity, physical threat or humiliation and interference with work inform that inquiry; none is indispensable, and psychological injury, treatment and lost productivity are not prerequisites. Oncale preserves proof that the conditions were imposed because of sex. Grave misconduct alone supplies neither causation nor employer attribution.

### II. The charge prejudicially withheld the supported actual-authority route

**Holding and operative rule:** Considered as a whole against this record, the preserved charge was prejudicially incomplete because it made apparent authority and reasonable reliance cumulative requirements, imposed an alter-ego barrier and omitted the supported actual-authority/material-aid route. If a distinct apparent-authority theory is properly submitted, it requires reasonable belief traceable to the employer's words or conduct and reasonable reliance on that appearance, not merely the supervisor's claimed power; that alternative is not a condition of actual-authority attribution.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part II. The actual-authority omission independently suffices for affirmance of the instructional reversal; factual sufficiency of apparent authority is not decided.

**Controlling explanation:** The principal charge was given over objection, and the requested instructions presented the aid issue as well as overbroad status-based theories. Independent review of the whole charge, with prejudice required, differs from reviewing the choice of a particular requested instruction for abuse of discretion. Neither inquiry requires verbatim use of an inaccurate proposal.

The charge's reference to authority otherwise than apparently did not explain material aid or remove the mandatory apparent-authority and reliance elements. Brown's daily control and lack of top-management status made those barriers consequential. Work-order evidence concretely supported the omitted route. A possible jury rejection of another Title VII element does not establish that this general employer verdict rested on an independent, correctly decided ground; no special finding supplies that conclusion. Nor does the separate tort verdict against Brown establish the statutory elements against Potash. This is a record-specific prejudice determination, not automatic reversal for any agency error.

Restatement §27 supplies the limited principal-manifestation clarification. Faragher left apparent authority undecided; its separate observations are not a Court holding. An employee's knowledge of a supervisor's assertion is not a substitute for manifestations attributable to the employer. Knowledge that harassment is forbidden does not erase actual command power. An effectively implemented policy may bear on a properly framed apparent-authority inquiry, but the Court decides neither that route's factual sufficiency nor that ignorance of policy alone establishes it. It does not adopt every apparent-authority formulation below or extend Faragher's actual-delegation rule to every claimed apparent power.

### III. Policy and response do not cure the omitted route; a general complete defense remains open

**Holding and operative rule:** A prohibition of harassment and correction after notice do not themselves negate earlier material aid from actual delegated power or cure the prejudicially incomplete charge. The Court preserves Faragher's reservation concerning a general complete defense based on genuinely effective prevention and unreasonable employee nonuse, including its elements, burden, relation to tangible employment action and any distinct limitation on relief.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part III, including its limited reservation of the Court's general complete-defense question. No complete defense is adopted or supplied to Potash.

**Controlling explanation:** Faragher rejected the ineffective-policy contention on its own proved record while adopting neither a general defense nor categorical rejection of every such defense. Harrison requires her own policy chronology. She discovered a posted policy after some incidents, and it was absent from her new-hire materials. Those facts establish neither universal ignorance nor that every reporting route was unavailable, nonuse was unreasonable or every remedial step was inadequate.

After her July report, Potash investigated, promised lost-time compensation and treatment, changed her crew, reprimanded Brown, placed him on permanent probation for recurrence and restricted contact. Those measures retain their potential relevance to employer care, response and relief; they do not retroactively remove earlier actual aid. Failed dissemination alone is not negligence per se. The investigator's acceptance of Harrison's unwilling participation alongside Brown's asserted perception of consent is not a judicial finding that the conduct was welcome; Meritor distinguishes unwelcome conduct from apparently voluntary participation.

The different policy facts permit their proper consideration, but establish no complete defense here. The charge was not an accurate, separately justified instruction on an established defense. Faragher's reservation does not expand the granted or preserved issues, create a reporting duty or supply an appellate finding against Harrison. Appropriate apparent-authority, negligence and remedial inquiries remain distinct. No omnibus policy immunity, new burden-shifting formula or freestanding policy-publication duty is adopted.

### IV. This appellate record establishes no contractual bar to the independent Title VII action

**Holding and operative rule:** A contractual grievance assertion does not remove federal subject-matter jurisdiction over an independent Title VII claim. Potash cannot obtain dismissal for nonexhaustion on this appellate record, which includes neither its CBA nor the district court's exhaustion ruling and identifies no agreement covering arbitration of statutory claims or delegation of the antecedent question.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join Part IV and affirmance of the rejection of the employer's cross-appeal.

**Controlling explanation:** Gardner-Denver preserves independent Title VII rights despite contractual grievance arbitration. Gilmer enforces an actual agreement to arbitrate the statutory ADEA claim; it does not supply a missing agreement here or overrule the contractual/statutory distinction. First Options requires courts independently to determine agreement and clear, unmistakable delegation under applicable contract law. A bargaining relationship or asserted grievance process proves neither statutory scope nor delegation. Its conditional rule for review after actual delegation is not reached.

The appellate opinion's two footnotes report the CBA and order omissions and that Potash's brief did not identify statutory arbitration. These are actual limits of its appellate presentation, not a finding that no contract ever existed or a fabricated waiver. The strongest enforcement argument recognizes that a proven forum agreement can create duties without extinguishing substantive rights; it still requires the agreement this record does not establish.

Livadas and Hawaiian Airlines require attention to the actual source of a right and whether disputed contract interpretation is necessary. Whether sex-based conditions and delegated power meet Title VII's elements does not require such interpretation here. Their distinct preemption holdings do not establish immunity from every arbitration agreement. The Court declines the lower court's blanket CBA/FAA rationale.
