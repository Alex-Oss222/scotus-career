## Event, posture and participation

Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511. Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under §561 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The question is whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad and insufficiently tailored to protecting minors.

The Act was enacted February 8, 1996. The district court's preliminary-injunction order was dated June 11 and entered June 12, 1996; its June 28 clarification preserved June 12 as the appeal-clock entry date. The plaintiffs demonstrated present speech restrictions and practical compliance burdens. The record describes diverse lawful Internet expression and age-verification costs and barriers, especially for noncommercial and small speakers.

Section 223(a)(1)(B) reaches knowing creation or solicitation and initiation of transmission by a telecommunications device of an obscene or indecent communication, knowing its recipient is under eighteen, regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The enacted definition of telecommunications device excludes an interactive computer service. Section (d)(1)(A) separately reaches knowing use of an interactive computer service to send to specified persons under eighteen; (d)(1)(B) reaches display available to a person under eighteen. Their content category covers contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive under contemporary community standards. The corresponding facility offenses require knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for the prohibited activity. Violations carry fines, imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Section (e)(1) protects mere access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the provider's control, including incidental functions that do not create content. Section (e)(2) denies that defense to a conspirator with an entity actively creating or knowingly distributing prohibited communications, or one knowingly advertising them; the conspirator need not personally create content. Section (e)(3) separately denies it for access to an offending network the provider owns or controls. Employer liability under (e)(4) requires within-scope employee or agent conduct plus knowing authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5) provides alternative defenses: good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures under the circumstances to restrict minors' access, or restricted access requiring a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number. Under (e)(6), the FCC may describe suitable measures; it may not approve, sanction, compel or enforce their use or endorse particular products. Their use is evidence of good faith. Interactive services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers.

Section (f)(1) protects lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions against suit. Section (f)(2) bars inconsistent state or local liability for the identified commercial, library and higher-education activities, while allowing complementary regulation confined to intrastate services without inconsistent interstate duties and preserving regulation of conduct outside this section. Section (g) preserves other federal laws. Section (h)'s device definition adds no broadcast-licensee or cable-operator obligations under other indecency provisions; its access-software definition covers content-neutral filtering, selection and transmission tools that do not create or provide the communication's content. These distinct savings provisions supply no absent narrow content definition.

Nine participating Justices satisfy the six-Justice quorum; five votes suffice for judgment and a controlling proposition. No recusal or other exclusion is established. ## Controlling holdings

**Authority for both holdings:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. These eight Justices directly adopt the following propositions, explanations, limits and remedy. O'Connor's agreement with part of the judgment does not join this opinion.

### I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** Sable recognizes the compelling interest in protecting children but forbids reducing adults to children's speech when less restrictive means are available. Ginsberg sustained a confined commercial restriction on material obscene for minors, including a value limitation absent here. Pacifica's scarcity, intrusiveness and broadcast setting do not describe this medium; ACT/Pacifica requires independent protection of adult access even there. Turner concerns content-neutral carriage, not this direct selection of prohibited content. Denver Area's compulsory-blocking holding supplies the practical adult-access analogy, without extending its fractured permission judgments into a universal rule.

The criminal categories reach serious discussion of health, sexuality, art, politics and personal experience. Uncertainty about content and community standards chills speech; scienter cannot tell a speaker which protected discussion is criminal. Knowledge of a minor or reference to specified recipients does not prevent the prohibition from reaching conversations also intended for adults. Other communication channels do not cure exclusion from this one.

The defenses do not remove those burdens. Many small and noncommercial speakers lack feasible age verification; costs, loss of anonymity and exclusion of lawful adults persist. Proposed tagging does not establish the effective protection the defense requires on the present record. User-controlled filtering and precise regulation of unprotected material offer materially less restrictive approaches. The Government has not justified this broad criminal suppression in light of those alternatives. The Court does not find every age screen invalid or all future controls ineffective. Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no independent Fifth Amendment holding is needed.

### II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions

**Holding and operative rule:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Controlling explanation:** The word choice permits removal of the indecency branch without disabling the distinct obscenity rule. The proposed further construction would instead change the offenses' substantive coverage. Knowledge and audience limits do not provide the missing narrow content definition, and a serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be supplied by judicial legislation. The statutory defenses preserve their own terms but do not establish workable protection for the record's diverse speakers. The challenge's varied activities and substantial protected reach make a selective judicial reconstruction inappropriate. O'Connor would retain knowing transmissions solely to minors; the Court does not adopt her conclusion that minors' protected-speech overbreadth is insubstantial or her proposed application severance.

The ruling does not hold that Congress can never regulate protected Internet indecency, decide the proposed blanket ban on such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, or resolve the separate question of extraterritorial efficacy and scope. It does not require one constitutional classification for every Internet service. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, true-threat, fraud, unlawful-solicitation and direct-harmful-contact provisions remain governed by their own predicates; they do not save these overbroad restrictions. A future precisely drawn rule using effective, nonexclusionary age assurance remains open.

- Ginsberg's commercial restriction on material obscene for minors is distinguished because the CDA reaches broader protected categories without its value limitation.

- Turner retains the distinction between content-neutral carriage and content discrimination, with independent scrutiny of the evidentiary fit.
- Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding is applied by analogy to practical adult-access burdens; its separate permission judgments create no universal Internet rule.
- Broadrick supplies the substantial-overbreadth framework; the enacted breadth cannot be repaired by an unavailable narrowing construction. No precedent is overruled.

The challenged indecency and patently-offensive restrictions cannot be enforced at the clarified scope. Content-based criminal regulation of this medium must protect lawful adult expression and satisfy narrow tailoring on the actual record. Obscenity and other independently unprotected conduct retain their own legal treatment. Future, precisely drawn regulation and genuinely effective nonexclusionary age controls remain open; no general Internet-service classification or freestanding Fifth Amendment rule is adopted.

### Components, review and complete operative statutory boundaries

All eight Associates accept the supplied direct-appeal route under Act §561 and the live pre-enforcement speech controversy. The appeal concerns the preliminary order as clarified, not a new permanent injunction or damages claim. No disputed filing date, standing defect or expiration is inferred.

The separate judgment components are: (R1) §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency transmission offense and §223(a)(2)'s associated knowing, intentional facility permission; (R2) §223(d)(1)(A)'s sending to a specified minor or minors and §223(d)(2)'s corresponding facility permission; (R3) §223(d)(1)(B)'s display available to minors and the corresponding §223(d)(2) liability; and (R4) severability and the retained scope of relief. R1 requires knowing making, creation or solicitation and initiation by a telecommunications device, with knowledge that the recipient is under eighteen. R2 and R3 involve knowing use of an interactive computer service and contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs patently offensive under contemporary community standards. R1's device definition excludes an interactive computer service; the provisions cannot be collapsed into one generic Internet offense. The text applies regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The facility offenses require both knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for prohibited activity. The challenged crimes permit a fine, imprisonment up to two years, or both.

The defense is not strict liability for mere carriage. Section (e)(1) protects access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the person's control, including incidental transmission, downloading, intermediate storage and access software, without content creation. Sections (e)(2) and (3) withhold that defense respectively from a conspirator actively involved with creation or knowing distribution, a knowing advertiser of the prohibited communications, and an access provider owning or controlling the offending network. Under (e)(4), employer liability requires within-scope conduct plus knowledgeable authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5), applying to (a)(1)(B), (d), and the corresponding (a)(2) activity, separately protects good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate access-restriction measures under the circumstances, including feasible available technology, OR restricted access using a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult PIN. A speaker need not satisfy both alternatives.

Under (e)(6) the FCC may describe suitable measures for (d), but may not enforce their use, approve or sanction them, or endorse specific products; described measures are evidence of good faith, not an exclusive license. Interactive computer services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers. Section (f)(1) protects otherwise lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions; (f)(2) preserves the stated complementary intrastate and uncovered-conduct powers while barring inconsistent liability within its named commercial-entity, library and higher-education scope. Section (g) preserves other federal law. None of those savings terms narrows the challenged content categories to obscenity or cures their impact on speakers creating protected content.

### Issue-specific alternatives

**Path A: affirm the challenged injunction in full.** The criminal scheme selects protected content, suppresses lawful adult communication and demands impracticable age separation from many small or noncommercial speakers. The context-sensitive terms and knowledge requirements do not supply the missing fit. Sable distinguishes a compelling child-protection purpose from adequate means; ACT/Pacifica preserves adult speech and independent necessity review even in broadcasting; Denver's controlling compulsory-scheme holding addresses actual deterrence and access burdens. Internet communication is not justified by broadcast scarcity or unavoidable household intrusion. Turner and the March 1997 application concern content-neutral carriage, not permission to defer away a content-selected criminal burden.

The strongest objection is that targeted sending to known minors is textually distinct from public display and can operate without excluding any adult. The full-injunction Justices conclude that the actual offenses and defenses remain substantially overbroad as enacted: the transmission provisions reach communications with adult as well as minor recipients; the statutory categories do not adopt Ginsberg's variable-obscenity limits, including its serious-value protection; uncertainty backed by criminal penalties deters protected sexual, health, artistic and political discussion. The Court can preserve obscenity by excising the distinct indecency category, but cannot legislate a new minors-only, harmful-to-minors offense or an effective age-verification system. This is a judgment about substantial protected reach, not a claim that every conceivable application is unconstitutional.

**Path B: retain a separable direct-to-minor core.** O'Connor and Thomas distinguish a communication confined to one or more particular recipients known to be minors from mixed-age conversation and unrestricted display. Government may protect minors through the former restriction without reducing any adult's access. Ginsberg supplies support for age-differentiated restrictions; the intact good-faith and verified-access defenses are supplementary safeguards, not a substitute for evaluating the audience. The strongest objection is that the indecency definitions are broader than harmful-to-minors obscenity and that the Court would rewrite the Act. They answer only at the severability/remedial level: leave a valid direct-recipient application enforceable while barring the adult-burdening reach, and do not add new elements or change the statutory definition. They do not hold all protected speech to minors regulable, settle every minor's access claim, or reject a specific vagueness challenge to a later prosecution. Their disagreement is the demonstrated facial remedy's reach, not permission for general Internet censorship.

Neither path treats parental controls as already universal, free, perfectly effective or technologically implemented. The record supports the practical shortcomings of the enacted defenses for many speakers, not the impossibility of every future feasible age screen. No Justice here uses a public-forum designation as the necessary premise.

Seven Associates support full affirmance of R1–R4. All eight support the display and mixed-audience protection and preservation of independently lawful obscenity enforcement. O'Connor alone would retain the specified minor-only transmission applications. These are Associate component counts, not a final whole-Court 7–2 or 8–1 tally.

### Controlling-ground content available for assembly

The CDA selects speech because of its content and reaches substantial protected adult expression. The compelling purpose of protecting minors does not establish the necessity or fit of the means. Sable supplies that distinction and the rule against reducing adults to material suitable for children. Ginsberg addressed a defined commercial sale to minors under variable-obscenity limits, including the work's value to minors; it does not validate these broader definitions. Pacifica's broadcast context, scarcity and intrusive reception do not describe this Internet record. Actual ACT/Pacifica already requires independent review even within broadcasting. Turner concerns content-neutral cable carriage and preserves classification and independent evidentiary review. Actual Denver's seven-Justice §10(b) rule supplies an analogy about compulsory adult-access burdens; its fractured permission judgments establish no universal cable rule and no Internet classification.

The uncertainty of “indecent” and the contextual patently-offensive standard, backed by criminal sanctions, chills protected discussion and enlarges the overbreadth problem. The Court need not decide whether the provisions independently violate Fifth Amendment vagueness requirements. The knowledge and specified-person wording do not prevent liability in mixed adult/minor communication. A serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be inserted into text that lacks it. The defenses do not cure the demonstrated burdens: practicable verification is unavailable or costly for many small and noncommercial speakers, and proposed tagging does not establish the required effective protection under the then-existing record. This is not a finding that every future age screen is unconstitutional or that all control technology is ineffective.

The complete offense and defense terms in the frozen neutral appendix remain operative descriptions and must not be collapsed. R1's telecommunications-device language, including the enacted exclusion of an interactive computer service from that definition, is distinct from R2/R3's interactive-computer-service language. Facility offenses require knowing permission **and** intent that the facility be used for prohibited activity. Section (e)(1) protects mere access/connection without content creation under its control limitation. Section (e)(2) withdraws that defense from a person who is a conspirator **with an entity actively involved in creation or knowing distribution**, or who knowingly advertises the prohibited communications; it does not require the conspirator personally to create the content. Section (e)(3) separately concerns ownership/control of the offending network. Section (e)(4) retains the within-scope conduct and authorization/ratification or reckless-disregard conditions for an employer. Section (e)(5)(A)'s good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures and (B)'s verified-card/account/adult-code/PIN access restriction are **alternative** defenses. The FCC **may** describe measures under (e)(6); it cannot compel, approve, enforce or endorse them, and their use is evidence of good faith rather than a licensing scheme. The distinct savings provisions do not supply a missing narrow content definition.

The majority rejects the requested judicial narrowing because the enacted offenses and defended construction suppress a substantial protected range and no readily available textual construction supplies the proposed new offense. Severance of the discrete words “or indecent” preserves the obscenity portion of (a); a general severability provision does not authorize rewriting the remaining coverage or inventing an effective verification regime. The decision leaves open the broader proposition that protected Internet indecency could never be regulated; it does not decide the proposed blanket prohibition of all such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, and does not decide the separate extraterritorial efficacy/scope question. Those reservations must not be replaced by O'Connor's contrary minors-overbreadth conclusion.

### O'Connor's separate ground and precise disagreement

O'Connor accepts protection of adults as a limit on any Internet adult-zone approach. Butler, Sable and Bolger prohibit suppressing adult access to protect children; Ginsberg permits genuine age-confined restrictions. The present Internet lacks generally available identity gates, and mixed conversations cannot reliably exclude minors. The display offense therefore forces silence or children's-level expression, and the transmission offenses cannot be enforced against conversations involving another adult merely because a known minor participates. She agrees with that relief.

She would construe (d)(1)(A) to require the sender's knowledge of the recipient's minority, consistently with its wording and constitutional avoidance under DeBartolo. Where the initiating adult knows every recipient is a minor, there is no other adult audience whose access is impaired. Brockett's application severability and the Act's severability provision support retaining that confined application. She recognizes that CDA indecency is broader than Ginsberg's obscenity-as-to-minors category, but finds the challengers have not shown that the additional protected minor speech is **substantial** relative to the legitimate sweep under Broadrick. This is a distinct substantive reason for her narrower remedy, not a holding that minors have no protected speech or that the statute can survive any individual vagueness challenge. Her framework evaluates technology as it exists in 1997; future improvement is not a factual premise curing current burdens. No other Associate joins this writing after reconciliation.

### Exact relief

The seven full-affirmance Associates sustain the preliminary injunction, not a newly issued permanent decree. Under the June 11 order entered June 12 and the June 28 clarification, (a)(1)(B) and associated (a)(2) activity are enjoined **only as to indecency**. Section (a)(2) activity under (a)(1)(A), (C), (D) and (E), and obscenity or child pornography under (a)(1)(B), remains outside the injunction. Sections (d)(1) and (d)(2) are enjoined without a separate obscenity carve-out because they contain no separate textual obscenity branch. The Government retains independently lawful obscenity and child-pornography enforcement. The lower order's no-bond provision and direction to advise concerning further proceedings at the later of thirty-one days after the order or ten days after final appellate review remain its own terms. The clarification did not restart the June 12 appeal clock. No damages, compelled technology, new deadline or additional injunction is created.

## Event

Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511. Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under §561 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The question is whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad and insufficiently tailored to protecting minors.

The Act was enacted February 8, 1996. The district court's preliminary-injunction order was dated June 11 and entered June 12, 1996; its June 28 clarification preserved June 12 as the appeal-clock entry date. The plaintiffs demonstrated present speech restrictions and practical compliance burdens. The record describes diverse lawful Internet expression and age-verification costs and barriers, especially for noncommercial and small speakers.

Section 223(a)(1)(B) reaches knowing creation or solicitation and initiation of transmission by a telecommunications device of an obscene or indecent communication, knowing its recipient is under eighteen, regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The enacted definition of telecommunications device excludes an interactive computer service. Section (d)(1)(A) separately reaches knowing use of an interactive computer service to send to specified persons under eighteen; (d)(1)(B) reaches display available to a person under eighteen. Their content category covers contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive under contemporary community standards. The corresponding facility offenses require knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for the prohibited activity. Violations carry fines, imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Section (e)(1) protects mere access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the provider's control, including incidental functions that do not create content. Section (e)(2) denies that defense to a conspirator with an entity actively creating or knowingly distributing prohibited communications, or one knowingly advertising them; the conspirator need not personally create content. Section (e)(3) separately denies it for access to an offending network the provider owns or controls. Employer liability under (e)(4) requires within-scope employee or agent conduct plus knowing authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5) provides alternative defenses: good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures under the circumstances to restrict minors' access, or restricted access requiring a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number. Under (e)(6), the FCC may describe suitable measures; it may not approve, sanction, compel or enforce their use or endorse particular products. Their use is evidence of good faith. Interactive services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers.

Section (f)(1) protects lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions against suit. Section (f)(2) bars inconsistent state or local liability for the identified commercial, library and higher-education activities, while allowing complementary regulation confined to intrastate services without inconsistent interstate duties and preserving regulation of conduct outside this section. Section (g) preserves other federal laws. Section (h)'s device definition adds no broadcast-licensee or cable-operator obligations under other indecency provisions; its access-software definition covers content-neutral filtering, selection and transmission tools that do not create or provide the communication's content. These distinct savings provisions supply no absent narrow content definition.

## Holdings

**Authority for both holdings:** Ginsburg's opinion of the Court is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer. These eight Justices directly adopt the following propositions, explanations, limits and remedy. O'Connor's agreement with part of the judgment does not join this opinion.

### I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** Sable recognizes the compelling interest in protecting children but forbids reducing adults to children's speech when less restrictive means are available. Ginsberg sustained a confined commercial restriction on material obscene for minors, including a value limitation absent here. Pacifica's scarcity, intrusiveness and broadcast setting do not describe this medium; ACT/Pacifica requires independent protection of adult access even there. Turner concerns content-neutral carriage, not this direct selection of prohibited content. Denver Area's compulsory-blocking holding supplies the practical adult-access analogy, without extending its fractured permission judgments into a universal rule.

The criminal categories reach serious discussion of health, sexuality, art, politics and personal experience. Uncertainty about content and community standards chills speech; scienter cannot tell a speaker which protected discussion is criminal. Knowledge of a minor or reference to specified recipients does not prevent the prohibition from reaching conversations also intended for adults. Other communication channels do not cure exclusion from this one.

The defenses do not remove those burdens. Many small and noncommercial speakers lack feasible age verification; costs, loss of anonymity and exclusion of lawful adults persist. Proposed tagging does not establish the effective protection the defense requires on the present record. User-controlled filtering and precise regulation of unprotected material offer materially less restrictive approaches. The Government has not justified this broad criminal suppression in light of those alternatives. The Court does not find every age screen invalid or all future controls ineffective. Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no independent Fifth Amendment holding is needed.

### II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions

**Holding and operative rule:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Controlling explanation:** The word choice permits removal of the indecency branch without disabling the distinct obscenity rule. The proposed further construction would instead change the offenses' substantive coverage. Knowledge and audience limits do not provide the missing narrow content definition, and a serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be supplied by judicial legislation. The statutory defenses preserve their own terms but do not establish workable protection for the record's diverse speakers. The challenge's varied activities and substantial protected reach make a selective judicial reconstruction inappropriate. O'Connor would retain knowing transmissions solely to minors; the Court does not adopt her conclusion that minors' protected-speech overbreadth is insubstantial or her proposed application severance.

The ruling does not hold that Congress can never regulate protected Internet indecency, decide the proposed blanket ban on such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, or resolve the separate question of extraterritorial efficacy and scope. It does not require one constitutional classification for every Internet service. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, true-threat, fraud, unlawful-solicitation and direct-harmful-contact provisions remain governed by their own predicates; they do not save these overbroad restrictions. A future precisely drawn rule using effective, nonexclusionary age assurance remains open.

## Precedent Treatment

- Sable, Butler and Bolger preserve adult access despite the compelling interest in protecting minors; child protection does not alone justify suppressing adult expression.
- Ginsberg's commercial restriction on material obscene for minors is distinguished because the CDA reaches broader protected categories without its value limitation.
- Pacifica retains its contextual broadcast force and supplies no reduced Internet protection.
- ACT/Pacifica's independent review of content-selected restrictions and adult access remains intact; the Internet record is separately examined.
- Turner retains the distinction between content-neutral carriage and content discrimination, with independent scrutiny of the evidentiary fit.
- Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding is applied by analogy to practical adult-access burdens; its separate permission judgments create no universal Internet rule.
- Broadrick supplies the substantial-overbreadth framework; the enacted breadth cannot be repaired by an unavailable narrowing construction. No precedent is overruled.

## Law After Decision

The challenged indecency and patently-offensive restrictions cannot be enforced at the clarified scope. Content-based criminal regulation of this medium must protect lawful adult expression and satisfy narrow tailoring on the actual record. Obscenity and other independently unprotected conduct retain their own legal treatment. Future, precisely drawn regulation and genuinely effective nonexclusionary age controls remain open; no general Internet-service classification or freestanding Fifth Amendment rule is adopted.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part.** Butler, Sable and Bolger protect adult access; Ginsberg permits genuine restrictions confined to minors. The Internet presently lacks generally usable identity gates, so the display offense and mixed adult/minor conversations cannot be saved by treating the network as divided into adult and child zones. She agrees with the injunction to that extent and with preservation of independent obscenity enforcement.

She would retain the transmission provisions only where the initiating adult knows all recipients are minors, with corresponding facility liability confined to those underlying communications. She construes §223(d)(1)(A) to require knowledge of minority, relying on its wording and DeBartolo's constitutional-avoidance principle. Such a communication has no other adult audience whose access is lost. Brockett's application-severability principle and the statutory severability provision support preserving that application. Although the CDA's content categories are broader than Ginsberg's obscenity-for-minors rule, she finds no showing under Broadrick that the additional protected minor speech is substantial relative to the provisions' legitimate sweep. She does not deny minors all First Amendment protection or foreclose individual vagueness challenges. Her view relies on current technology, not predicted improvements. No other Justice joins her separate writing.
