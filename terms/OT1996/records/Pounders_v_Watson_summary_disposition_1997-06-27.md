## Event, posture and participation

Pounders v. Watson, No. 96-1383. Certiorari and summary disposition on June 27, 1997, without oral argument, reviewing the Ninth Circuit's December 13, 1996 judgment reported at 102 F.3d 433. The petition presents the constitutional limits on summary contempt for the specified in-court questioning and the operation of former §2254(d) on the state findings and the constitutional inquiry. There was no prior plenary grant or merits argument.

During a six-defendant murder trial, Watson asked her client whether he had faced the death penalty and, after an immediate admonition, whether he faced life without parole. The judge found deliberate disobedience and serious prejudice to the jury. Watson disputed notice and intent and explained that the testimony concerned her client's state of mind. The judge adjudged contempt in her presence and imposed two days' confinement by written order dated June 22, 1994, with service deferred to protect trial continuity. She later had another opportunity to explain through counsel. The jury received an instruction to disregard the punishment questions; the record does not independently establish its effect.

The state appellate habeas orders supplied no explanatory rationale. The federal District Court denied habeas on September 8, 1994. Watson served the two-day sentence September 16–17; possible State Bar consequences keep the dispute live, without a finding of completed discipline. The Ninth Circuit directed entry of habeas relief because it found insufficient disruption to justify summary procedure, expressly leaving notice and willfulness undecided. Its opinion did not accept those findings merely by reserving them.

## Controlling holdings

**Authority for the following holdings:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg join the entire per curiam opinion. Their six direct votes control the summary reversal and the bounded grounds below. Stone-Zsela dissents on the merits; Stevens and Breyer oppose summary merits disposition and request plenary consideration without deciding the eventual merits.

### I. The appellate court unduly restricted the direct-contempt power

**Holding and operative rule:** The narrow power to adjudicate direct contempt summarily is not confined to noisy or physical obstruction, a pattern of violations pervading the trial, or a separate finding that misconduct would recur without immediate punishment. The Ninth Circuit's excessive reliance on the limited duration of Watson's questioning and absence of demonstrated future repetition cannot sustain its summary-process ruling on this record; the Court does not finally resolve her unaddressed notice and willfulness claims.

**Controlling explanation:** Oliver retains the exceptional power for misconduct openly before the judge, with its essential elements actually observed, disturbing the court's business and requiring vindication of judicial authority. Mere observation, irritation or a short sanction is insufficient. Wilson establishes that intentional conduct may obstruct an ongoing trial without noise or violence. Sacher permits deferral of punishment to protect trial continuity and representation; delay alone does not defeat qualifying summary action. McConnell involved announced questioning that was not carried out after a recess, unlike the renewed punishment question here after an immediate admonition.

The trial judge found deliberate defiance and serious jury prejudice. Those are the trial court's findings, not an independently established account of the jurors' minds. At the scope of the process question decided below, the renewed question and the threat to the jury's lawful consideration support correction of the appellate restriction. Protecting that decision process is part of trial administration. The strongest contrary argument is that instructions and ordinary later proceedings could address completed misconduct. But continuation after the contempt finding does not establish that the action already taken was unnecessary; neither Wilson nor Sacher makes a lengthy pattern or immediate imprisonment essential. The Court need not attribute a formally announced categorical rule to the Ninth Circuit to reject its reasoning here. It does not approve summary punishment for every evidentiary violation, eliminate Oliver's limits, or comprehensively redefine the exception.

### II. Former §2254(d) governs the remaining claims without converting constitutional legality into fact

**Holding and operative rule:** Former 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) governs this noncapital habeas application filed before AEDPA. Its conditional protection of qualifying state historical findings does not conclusively determine the constitutional legality of summary procedure, and the Ninth Circuit's express nondecision of notice and willfulness does not amount to accepting those findings.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh supplies the application-date rule for chapter 153. Sumner preserves the former statute's factual presumption, while Thompson distinguishes historical circumstances from an independently reviewed ultimate constitutional question in its setting. Neither decides every contempt issue by label.

The presumption concerns a competent state court's factual determination after a merits hearing involving the applicant and the State or its officer or agent, evidenced by findings, opinion or other reliable adequate writing. The exceptions concern unresolved factual merits; inadequate factfinding procedure; inadequately developed material facts; lack of jurisdiction over subject matter or applicant; unconstitutional failure to appoint counsel for an indigent applicant; absence of a full, fair and adequate hearing; other denial of due process; or a pertinent state record, considered as a whole, failing fairly to support the finding. At a federal evidentiary hearing, after due proof of the state determination, the applicant must establish error by convincing evidence unless one of the first seven circumstances is established, otherwise appears or is admitted, or the federal court finds the eighth.

The trial judge's written findings therefore retain whatever protection the statute gives them. Watson's disagreement does not automatically establish an exception or require a hearing. Conversely, the appellate court left her knowledge of a sufficiently definite order and deliberate disobedience undecided. Those claims cannot disappear through characterization of its silence as assent. A defense depending on facts beyond the judge's observation requires its applicable due-process assessment if that predicate is established. The Court decides no such predicate and makes no finding of judicial bias.

### III. No additional nonretroactivity ground is introduced

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court declines to introduce a Teague defense that is not established as preserved and fairly included in this review record. That choice is not a finding of categorical waiver, a determination of finality or novelty, or a change to either contemporary Teague exception.

**Controlling explanation:** Schiro treats nonretroactivity as a nonjurisdictional defense and permits discretionary refusal of a late presentation; it does not command refusal in every case. Caspari concerns a consistently preserved subsidiary defense limiting the constitutional rule and writ under review. That premise is not established here. Preservation and fair inclusion have not been established; no ruling treats silence as waiver or determines finality. The Court resolves the presented summary-process ground without creating a new collateral bar or adjudicating an unestablished finality date.

### IV. Reversal returns the claims the court of appeals did not decide

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's summary-process ruling and dependent instruction to grant habeas relief are reversed. The case is remanded for consideration of the notice and willfulness claims expressly left unresolved and lawful further disposition under the governing former habeas statute.

**Controlling explanation:** Removing the appellate court's sufficient ground for the writ does not resolve every other claim. Its own opinion reserved notice and deliberate disobedience, and this Court's confined review supplies no substituted merits decision on them. The mandate therefore neither finally validates all aspects of the contempt judgment nor directs final denial of habeas relief. Watson has served the two-day sentence; the mandate orders no repeated confinement, new contempt prosecution, automatic disciplinary result, new evidentiary hearing or particular custody deadline. The reported possible professional consequences preserve a live controversy without establishing a final disciplinary disposition.

## Precedent and resulting law

- Oliver and Cooke retain the narrow, observed direct-contempt exception and ordinary notice-and-hearing protections outside it; observation or a contempt label alone is insufficient.
- Wilson is applied to interference with ongoing trial administration without a requirement of noise or violence.
- Sacher preserves protective deferral of qualifying punishment; it does not impose a universal repeated-violation requirement.
- McConnell is distinguished because its threatened questioning was not carried out after the recess.
- Harris's demand for ordinary process in its different grand-jury setting is preserved; Rule 42's federal procedure supplies an analogy, not the governing California statute.
- Lindh applies the former chapter 153 framework to this pre-enactment application.
- Sumner preserves qualifying factual deference, and Thompson preserves the distinction between historical facts and ultimate constitutional application at its actual scope.
- Schiro permits declining a late nonjurisdictional defense; Caspari's consistently preserved-defense premise is not established. No precedent is overruled or narrowed by the Court.

The received direct-contempt power cannot be restricted by treating a pervasive pattern, physical stoppage, future recurrence or immediate imprisonment as indispensable. Its existing observation and institutional-need limits remain. This appellate process ruling no longer supports habeas relief, but the unresolved notice and intent claims remain open under the former statute. Stone-Zsela's proposed added necessity boundary and the plenary-review dissent are not controlling law.

## Published noncontrolling continuity

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** Stone would affirm habeas relief on a narrower due-process ground. He accepts the findings that Watson knew the order and deliberately violated it, preserves the underlying trial order, and takes the judge's assessment of serious jury prejudice seriously. Watson explained herself at the bench and again through counsel; his objection is not lack of every warning or opportunity to speak. A single event may warrant contempt, without repeated or pervasive misconduct.

He would require a particularized connection between summary adjudication and protection of an ongoing proceeding or authority that cannot practicably await ordinary process. Observation establishes what the judge saw, but does not alone show why an ordinary hearing cannot reliably adjudicate punishment. Here the questioning ended, curative trial-management measures were available, the trial continued for roughly two weeks without another violation and confinement was deferred. Summary adjudication could not undo the completed prejudice. Taken together, those circumstances fail to establish the need to dispense with ordinary criminal-contempt protections.

Terry and Oliver preserve an exceptional power tied to judicial protection. McConnell requires no more power than the occasion warrants. Wilson concerned refusal of immunized testimony needed for an ongoing trial, which he distinguishes from completed questioning. Sacher permits deferred punishment where summary process is otherwise justified; delay is therefore not independently dispositive. To the extent those authorities permit this summary conviction without the additional necessity showing, Stone would narrow them. His proposed boundary rests on notice and an effective opportunity to defend while preserving immediate action against violence, refusal, ongoing obstruction and comparably urgent threats. A preference for procedural speed supplies no concrete reliance sufficient to defeat that protection, and the proposal does not itself reopen completed contempt judgments.

Stone agrees that the former habeas statute applies. He would not initiate an omitted nonjurisdictional Teague defense under Schiro merely to defeat this claim, and preservation is not established here. If a preserved and fairly included defense were properly available, Caspari would require examining the law at finality: an additional necessity rule not dictated by the earlier cases would be procedural, would create no substantive immunity from contempt and would not qualify as a watershed accuracy rule. If a preserved and fairly included bar applies to his proposed new rule, he would reject relief on that ground, without approving every feature of the summary contempt. No such bar is decided on this record. He would leave California free to pursue ordinary contempt proceedings with adequate notice and opportunity to defend if state law permits, and require another judge only if the governing neutrality or embroilment rule requires one.

**Stevens, dissenting from summary disposition, joined by Breyer.** They would receive full briefing and argument before deciding the merits. Assuming for this procedural objection that Watson defied an order and committed contempt, they question whether summary adjudication is so clearly proper that the habeas judgment should be reversed without plenary consideration. Two questions in a long trial, subsequent continuation and punishment directed at completed conduct raise substantial issues concerning necessity and ordinary criminal safeguards.

McConnell's attention to actual interference and the concerns expressed in Frankfurter's Sacher dissent warrant fuller consideration, including the substantial question whether a disinterested judge should hear a charge in these circumstances. The dissent in Sacher supplies their argument, not a controlling rule displacing its majority. They acknowledge the immediate warning, Wilson's recognition of nonphysical obstruction and Sacher's permission to defer punishment. They impose no minimum number of violations, find no judicial bias and make no final determination that due process was violated. Their requested plenary review is not a vote to affirm habeas relief or a direction to conduct a new contempt prosecution.

Both separate grounds remain noncontrolling. Stone's proposed narrower necessity boundary, possible ordinary-proceedings remedy and contingent Teague analysis are not adopted by the Court. Stevens/Breyer reserve the eventual constitutional merits and advocate plenary consideration only. No actual neutrality violation, new contempt charge, disciplinary outcome or final habeas entitlement is found.

### I. The appellate court unduly restricted the direct-contempt power

**Holding and operative rule:** The narrow power to adjudicate direct contempt summarily is not confined to noisy or physical obstruction, a pattern of violations pervading the trial, or a separate finding that misconduct would recur without immediate punishment. The Ninth Circuit's excessive reliance on the limited duration of Watson's questioning and absence of demonstrated future repetition cannot sustain its summary-process ruling on this record; the Court does not finally resolve her unaddressed notice and willfulness claims.

**Controlling explanation:** Oliver retains the exceptional power for misconduct openly before the judge, with its essential elements actually observed, disturbing the court's business and requiring vindication of judicial authority. Mere observation, irritation or a short sanction is insufficient. Wilson establishes that intentional conduct may obstruct an ongoing trial without noise or violence. Sacher permits deferral of punishment to protect trial continuity and representation; delay alone does not defeat qualifying summary action. McConnell involved announced questioning that was not carried out after a recess, unlike the renewed punishment question here after an immediate admonition.

The trial judge found deliberate defiance and serious jury prejudice. Those are the trial court's findings, not an independently established account of the jurors' minds. At the scope of the process question decided below, the renewed question and the threat to the jury's lawful consideration support correction of the appellate restriction. Protecting that decision process is part of trial administration. The strongest contrary argument is that instructions and ordinary later proceedings could address completed misconduct. But continuation after the contempt finding does not establish that the action already taken was unnecessary; neither Wilson nor Sacher makes a lengthy pattern or immediate imprisonment essential. The Court need not attribute a formally announced categorical rule to the Ninth Circuit to reject its reasoning here. It does not approve summary punishment for every evidentiary violation, eliminate Oliver's limits, or comprehensively redefine the exception.

### II. Former §2254(d) governs the remaining claims without converting constitutional legality into fact

**Holding and operative rule:** Former 28 U.S.C. §2254(d) governs this noncapital habeas application filed before AEDPA. Its conditional protection of qualifying state historical findings does not conclusively determine the constitutional legality of summary procedure, and the Ninth Circuit's express nondecision of notice and willfulness does not amount to accepting those findings.

**Controlling explanation:** Lindh supplies the application-date rule for chapter 153. Sumner preserves the former statute's factual presumption, while Thompson distinguishes historical circumstances from an independently reviewed ultimate constitutional question in its setting. Neither decides every contempt issue by label.

The presumption concerns a competent state court's factual determination after a merits hearing involving the applicant and the State or its officer or agent, evidenced by findings, opinion or other reliable adequate writing. The exceptions concern unresolved factual merits; inadequate factfinding procedure; inadequately developed material facts; lack of jurisdiction over subject matter or applicant; unconstitutional failure to appoint counsel for an indigent applicant; absence of a full, fair and adequate hearing; other denial of due process; or a pertinent state record, considered as a whole, failing fairly to support the finding. At a federal evidentiary hearing, after due proof of the state determination, the applicant must establish error by convincing evidence unless one of the first seven circumstances is established, otherwise appears or is admitted, or the federal court finds the eighth.

The trial judge's written findings therefore retain whatever protection the statute gives them. Watson's disagreement does not automatically establish an exception or require a hearing. Conversely, the appellate court left her knowledge of a sufficiently definite order and deliberate disobedience undecided. Those claims cannot disappear through characterization of its silence as assent. A defense depending on facts beyond the judge's observation requires its applicable due-process assessment if that predicate is established. The Court decides no such predicate and makes no finding of judicial bias.

### III. No additional nonretroactivity ground is introduced

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court declines to introduce a Teague defense that is not established as preserved and fairly included in this review record. That choice is not a finding of categorical waiver, a determination of finality or novelty, or a change to either contemporary Teague exception.

**Controlling explanation:** Schiro treats nonretroactivity as a nonjurisdictional defense and permits discretionary refusal of a late presentation; it does not command refusal in every case. Caspari concerns a consistently preserved subsidiary defense limiting the constitutional rule and writ under review. That premise is not established here. Preservation and fair inclusion have not been established; no ruling treats silence as waiver or determines finality. The Court resolves the presented summary-process ground without creating a new collateral bar or adjudicating an unestablished finality date.

The case is remanded for consideration of the notice and willfulness claims expressly left unresolved and lawful further disposition under the governing former habeas statute.

**Controlling explanation:** Removing the appellate court's sufficient ground for the writ does not resolve every other claim. ## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** Stone would affirm habeas relief on a narrower due-process ground. He accepts the findings that Watson knew the order and deliberately violated it, preserves the underlying trial order, and takes the judge's assessment of serious jury prejudice seriously. Watson explained herself at the bench and again through counsel; his objection is not lack of every warning or opportunity to speak. A single event may warrant contempt, without repeated or pervasive misconduct.

He would require a particularized connection between summary adjudication and protection of an ongoing proceeding or authority that cannot practicably await ordinary process. Observation establishes what the judge saw, but does not alone show why an ordinary hearing cannot reliably adjudicate punishment. Here the questioning ended, curative trial-management measures were available, the trial continued for roughly two weeks without another violation and confinement was deferred. Summary adjudication could not undo the completed prejudice. Taken together, those circumstances fail to establish the need to dispense with ordinary criminal-contempt protections.

Terry and Oliver preserve an exceptional power tied to judicial protection. McConnell requires no more power than the occasion warrants. Wilson concerned refusal of immunized testimony needed for an ongoing trial, which he distinguishes from completed questioning. Sacher permits deferred punishment where summary process is otherwise justified; delay is therefore not independently dispositive. To the extent those authorities permit this summary conviction without the additional necessity showing, Stone would narrow them. His proposed boundary rests on notice and an effective opportunity to defend while preserving immediate action against violence, refusal, ongoing obstruction and comparably urgent threats. A preference for procedural speed supplies no concrete reliance sufficient to defeat that protection, and the proposal does not itself reopen completed contempt judgments.

Stone agrees that the former habeas statute applies. He would not initiate an omitted nonjurisdictional Teague defense under Schiro merely to defeat this claim, and preservation is not established here. If a preserved and fairly included defense were properly available, Caspari would require examining the law at finality: an additional necessity rule not dictated by the earlier cases would be procedural, would create no substantive immunity from contempt and would not qualify as a watershed accuracy rule. If a preserved and fairly included bar applies to his proposed new rule, he would reject relief on that ground, without approving every feature of the summary contempt. No such bar is decided on this record. He would leave California free to pursue ordinary contempt proceedings with adequate notice and opportunity to defend if state law permits, and require another judge only if the governing neutrality or embroilment rule requires one.

**Stevens, dissenting from summary disposition, joined by Breyer.** They would receive full briefing and argument before deciding the merits. Assuming for this procedural objection that Watson defied an order and committed contempt, they question whether summary adjudication is so clearly proper that the habeas judgment should be reversed without plenary consideration. Two questions in a long trial, subsequent continuation and punishment directed at completed conduct raise substantial issues concerning necessity and ordinary criminal safeguards.

McConnell's attention to actual interference and the concerns expressed in Frankfurter's Sacher dissent warrant fuller consideration, including the substantial question whether a disinterested judge should hear a charge in these circumstances. The dissent in Sacher supplies their argument, not a controlling rule displacing its majority. They acknowledge the immediate warning, Wilson's recognition of nonphysical obstruction and Sacher's permission to defer punishment. They impose no minimum number of violations, find no judicial bias and make no final determination that due process was violated. Their requested plenary review is not a vote to affirm habeas relief or a direction to conduct a new contempt prosecution.
