## Controlling holdings

##### New York may distinguish treatment refusal from intentional suicide assistance

**Holding and operative rule:** A State does not deny equal protection by permitting a competent person to refuse unwanted medical treatment while prohibiting intentional assistance in suicide when the distinction reasonably rests on bodily consent, legal causation, intent and professional responsibility and serves legitimate protective interests. New York's distinction satisfies ordinary rational-basis review on the physicians' claim for competent, terminally ill patients; neither the shared eventual consequence of death nor the different practical options of patients dependent on life support requires the State to permit lethal assistance.

**Authority:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. Those five directly support the rule, explanation and limits; the unanimous judgment does not make the four judgment concurrences full rationale joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The law draws no suspect classification, and the presented claim identifies no established fundamental entitlement to the intentional assistance sought. Cruzan grounds treatment refusal in bodily integrity and informed consent; its assumed competent-refusal premise does not establish a right to another person's lethal assistance. Casey preserves a defined reproductive choice and its complete regulation standard, not an unrestricted right to every medical choice.

Equal wording alone does not answer the physicians' objection: patients who depend on life support have a practical option others lack. Heller requires a relationship to that actual disadvantage, and Romer rejects an unexplained disability without demanding a separate hostility finding. Here the patient's refusal ends an unwanted intervention and ordinarily lets the underlying condition take its course. Providing lethal means intentionally to cause death involves a different purpose, causal role and professional responsibility. Good-faith palliation can foresee a shorter life without adopting death as its purpose. The State may preserve bodily consent while protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable patients against error, pressure and abuse, and maintaining medicine's responsibility to care.

Beach permits reasonable generalizations without formal findings or empirical certainty. The distinction need not describe every patient's or physician's intention in every instance: a patient's wish to die, or the label attached to an intervention, does not alone settle purpose or causation. Those possible overlaps do not make the general protective line irrational. The availability of narrower regulation does not require its adoption under ordinary review. Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid bare harm and irrational prejudice; the supported protective connection here supplies a legitimate explanation for the actual difference in treatment.

**Operative limits:** The Court does not authorize forced treatment, displace competent refusal or valid advance directives, permit discriminatory denial of lawful pain care, or punish good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen. A materially different individual claim, including discriminatory administration, remains open on its own facts and legal grounds. No individual entitlement to a lethal prescription or new hearing is awarded. Cruzan's assumption concerning artificial nutrition and hydration is not enlarged into a comprehensive holding on every refusal decision.

## Published noncontrolling continuity

The following positions are published in Vacco v. Quill, June 26, 1997. Their exact scope and adoption relationships are noncontrolling except for each Justice's separately specified joins to the Court's opinion.

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion and also explains why the narrower interest of a competent person suffering greatly near imminent death need not be decided. New York permits qualified physicians to relieve pain, even with medication producing unconsciousness and a foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, protection against mistaken judgments of competence, terminal condition and voluntariness sufficiently supports the prohibition even if that narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards. She does not find that pain care is universally delivered or invariably successful, require legislative exhaustion, or adopt a freestanding constitutional pain-treatment standard. Ginsburg substantially adopts these separate reasons. Breyer joins them except insofar as the concurrence joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** He accepts a sufficient basis for the general distinction but does not join the Court's ordinary-review and causation/intent account in full. A patient withdrawing treatment and one requesting lethal medication may share an intention to hasten impending death; physicians may seek relief of suffering in both settings. Terminal sedation further complicates a universal distinction. His Cruzan dissent treats dignity and the manner of unavoidable death as serious individual interests; Casey's protected reproductive choice supplies a bounded analogy requiring attention to real burdens and clinical circumstances. Neither creates an unrestricted autonomy rule or an assisted-death right. Protective concerns sustain a general prohibition while potentially carrying different weight in an exceptional competent patient's well-supported case. He finds no such entitlement established here and orders no hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** The important patient and physician interests require justification commensurate with their weight, rather than a merely formal same-treatment syllogism. Harlan's Poe analysis, bodily-integrity principles and the defined reproductive and medical-privacy interests support that substantive inquiry. Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and euthanasia supplies the sufficient ground: reliably identifying responsible voluntary choice and containing a legalized lethal practice present serious unresolved problems. Palliation and treatment refusal differ in purpose, bodily intrusion and the likelihood of implementing a responsible patient's choice, though both can shorten life. Those differences and protective concerns justify this line. He reaches that conclusion through the stated substantive inquiry and does not join the Court's ordinary rational-basis method.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons concerning the reserved imminent-death and suffering interest and the laws' permission for palliation. She does not join the principal opinion, a declaration validating every future application, or every word of O'Connor's principal-opinion joins.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** He agrees that the identified protective interests justify the distinction, while describing the potential liberty as the combination of control over the manner of death, willing professional assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. He does not decide whether that interest is fundamental. The law permits the pain treatment central to it despite foreseeable death; failures in delivery and the possibility of sedation do not establish a legal prohibition. He does not assert that every person's suffering can be relieved. A law forbidding necessary palliation would raise a different question. These reasons accompany his qualified O'Connor join and do not adopt the principal opinion or deny every end-of-life liberty.

### Principal ground and limits

No suspect classification or established fundamental entitlement to the requested intentional assistance triggers heightened equality review of this claim. That conclusion is reached independently from the existing law and claim; it cannot be borrowed from the same-day Glucksberg decision. Cruzan's protection against unwanted bodily intervention does not itself establish the requested assistance entitlement, and actual Casey has its own reproductive scope.

The State allows competent patients to refuse treatment and prohibits intentional assistance in suicide. The practical difference for terminally ill patients who do or do not depend on life-sustaining treatment must still be assessed; identical wording is not the end of equal protection. Bodily consent, legal causation and purpose explain a reasonable general line. Withdrawal respects a refusal of intervention and ordinarily leaves the underlying disease to cause death; intentional lethal assistance supplies an additional means intended to cause death. Good-faith pain treatment may foresee a life-shortening effect without having that as its prohibited purpose. The State may reasonably protect life, guard against mistake, pressure and abuse, and preserve professional responsibility while respecting refusal. It need not eliminate the refusal protection to equalize all consequences, nor extend it to every different act that may hasten death.

The line need not describe every patient's or physician's actual intent in every case. Foreseeable death is not automatically purpose to kill, and neither a patient's wish nor the label “withdrawal” establishes all relevant intent. Legal uncertainty at a boundary does not itself defeat a reasonable general distinction. Actual Beach permits relevant generalization; actual Heller and Romer still require connection to the real disadvantage and preserve Moreno/Cleburne's limit. No finding of hostility is an indispensable prerequisite to review. The Court does not apply the historical Heller result, presume every assisted-death request involuntary, treat a less restrictive alternative as automatically required by ordinary review, or foreclose a materially different individual challenge.

### Substantive separate positions

**Stevens.** The State has a sufficient basis for the general distinction, but not an invariably true physiological or psychological taxonomy. A patient withdrawing support and a patient requesting medication can share a wish to hasten impending death; a physician's purpose can likewise concern suffering in both settings. Terminal sedation further complicates a categorical intent/causation account. His Cruzan dissent grounds the importance of individual dignity and the manner of impending death. Actual Casey supplies a supported analogy concerning defined personal choice, real burdens and clinical circumstances, not the historical opinion's unbounded autonomy language and not a newly controlling assisted-death right. Protective interests justify a general rule without necessarily resolving the exceptional competent patient's case. His writing reserves such a question; it does not find a particular application unconstitutional or require a hearing.

**O'Connor, with Ginsburg's substantial adoption and Breyer's qualified join.** There is no general entitlement to suicide, but the narrower interest of a competent person enduring great suffering near imminent death need not be decided here. New York legally permits qualified physicians to alleviate that suffering, including medication causing unconsciousness and potentially hastening death. Even assuming a cognizable narrower interest, safeguards against mistaken judgments of competence, terminal status and voluntariness justify the prohibition presented. Her Cruzan concurrence permits States to develop safeguards in the first instance. This is neither a finding that pain relief is universally delivered or successful, nor a rule making legislative consideration an exhaustion requirement, nor a new pain-treatment test. Ginsburg adopts substantially these reasons and nothing broader by inference. Breyer's qualified join excludes the principal-opinion joins.

**Souter.** The physicians' and patients' interest is important enough to demand commensurate justification even without recognizing a fundamental assistance right. Harlan's Poe approach, bodily integrity and the actual reproductive and privacy precedents require more than an unexplained label or abstract disapproval. Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and euthanasia supplies the decisive explanation: whether safeguards can reliably preserve responsible voluntary choice and contain the practice presents a serious unresolved empirical problem. Palliation and refusal differ in purpose, bodily intrusion and the likelihood of implementing a responsible choice, even though each may hasten death. Those reasons support the permitted/prohibited line. His reasoning is independently adopted for this case; a cross-reference to a contemporaneous separate writing may avoid repetition but cannot make that writing intervening precedent. He publishes his separate sufficient ground instead of silently joining a lower level of review.

**Breyer.** He agrees that the identified interests justify the distinction, while describing the potential liberty more carefully: personal control over the manner of death, professional medical assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering together. He does not decide whether that combined interest is fundamental. Its central suffering concern is not directly prohibited by the state law, which permits sufficient pain medication despite foreseeable death. Failures in delivery or the possibility of sedation do not establish a legal ban, and he does not assert that every person's suffering can be relieved. A law actually forbidding necessary palliation would present a different question. His qualified O'Connor join and his own explanation do not join a categorical denial of all end-of-life liberty.

### Reconciliation audit

The provisional model's narrow common proposition is retained as a limited area of substantive agreement. Its proposed full principal joins for Souter and Ginsburg, and its suggestion that the four historical judgment concurrences could become a uniform ordinary-rationality rationale merely by removing categorical rhetoric, are not retained. Exact source review matters: Souter's `95-1858.ZC2` expressly requires commensurate justification, not merely rational classification; Ginsburg's `ZC3` adopts O'Connor's reserved-interest reasons; Breyer's `ZC4` expressly withholds the principal joins; Stevens's `ZC1` affirms the general line while retaining independent liberty and particular-application reasoning. The historical principal opinion itself already qualified its intent generalization in footnote 12 and preserved particularized claims in footnote 13. Merely stating those qualifications anew is therefore not a sufficient changed premise for full new joins.

Actual Heller's proof-burden holding and Romer strengthen the requirement to relate a generalization to the actual disadvantage, and actual Casey replaces the historical autonomy passages. Those changes support the bounded grounds supplied here; they do not erase Souter's different level of inquiry or the others' express reservations. The no-same-day-precedent rule requires an independent statement of the grounds, but does not by itself change anyone's constitutional method. No material judgment departure remains. A subsequent proposal to turn these partial agreements into full principal joins must state the exact revised propositions and answer the recorded objections; it cannot count eight judgment supporters as eight full rationale supporters.

### Assembly audit conclusion

Final compatibility preserves the reconciled substantive positions without a new departure. Actual Casey, Heller and Romer supply their recorded law, not the historical formulations displaced in this timeline. The four historical judgment-concurrence grounds remain distinct because neither removal of an imagined universal history-only premise nor unanimity on outcome answers their recorded objections. No historical-departure label is manufactured where no material Associate judgment departure is retained. Authorship changes follow the current assignment rule and supply no substantive vote premise.

## Public Projection

## Event

Vacco v. Quill, No. 95-1858. Argued January 8, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. Its April 2, 1996 judgment, 80 F.3d 716, held New York's distinction unconstitutional as applied to competent terminally ill persons in the circumstances presented. The question is whether allowing refusal of unwanted treatment while prohibiting intentional assistance in suicide denies equal protection. New York seeks reversal; the physician respondents seek affirmance. The three initially named patients died before the District Court's decision; the operative appellants below were three physicians. The related due-process case, Washington v. Glucksberg, is separately decided today.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The Court reverses the judgment below and remands.

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

The Second Circuit's equal-protection invalidation is reversed. On remand the courts must reject the presented challenge to New York's treatment-refusal/intentional-assistance distinction. Independent treatment-refusal, lawful pain-care and good-faith palliation protections are undisturbed.

## Holdings

##### New York may distinguish treatment refusal from intentional suicide assistance

**Holding and operative rule:** A State does not deny equal protection by permitting a competent person to refuse unwanted medical treatment while prohibiting intentional assistance in suicide when the distinction reasonably rests on bodily consent, legal causation, intent and professional responsibility and serves legitimate protective interests. New York's distinction satisfies ordinary rational-basis review on the physicians' claim for competent, terminally ill patients; neither the shared eventual consequence of death nor the different practical options of patients dependent on life support requires the State to permit lethal assistance.

**Authority:** Thomas's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy. Those five directly support the rule, explanation and limits; the unanimous judgment does not make the four judgment concurrences full rationale joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The law draws no suspect classification, and the presented claim identifies no established fundamental entitlement to the intentional assistance sought. Cruzan grounds treatment refusal in bodily integrity and informed consent; its assumed competent-refusal premise does not establish a right to another person's lethal assistance. Casey preserves a defined reproductive choice and its complete regulation standard, not an unrestricted right to every medical choice.

Equal wording alone does not answer the physicians' objection: patients who depend on life support have a practical option others lack. Heller requires a relationship to that actual disadvantage, and Romer rejects an unexplained disability without demanding a separate hostility finding. Here the patient's refusal ends an unwanted intervention and ordinarily lets the underlying condition take its course. Providing lethal means intentionally to cause death involves a different purpose, causal role and professional responsibility. Good-faith palliation can foresee a shorter life without adopting death as its purpose. The State may preserve bodily consent while protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable patients against error, pressure and abuse, and maintaining medicine's responsibility to care.

Beach permits reasonable generalizations without formal findings or empirical certainty. The distinction need not describe every patient's or physician's intention in every instance: a patient's wish to die, or the label attached to an intervention, does not alone settle purpose or causation. Those possible overlaps do not make the general protective line irrational. The availability of narrower regulation does not require its adoption under ordinary review. Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid bare harm and irrational prejudice; the supported protective connection here supplies a legitimate explanation for the actual difference in treatment.

**Operative limits:** The Court does not authorize forced treatment, displace competent refusal or valid advance directives, permit discriminatory denial of lawful pain care, or punish good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen. A materially different individual claim, including discriminatory administration, remains open on its own facts and legal grounds. No individual entitlement to a lethal prescription or new hearing is awarded. Cruzan's assumption concerning artificial nutrition and hydration is not enlarged into a comprehensive holding on every refusal decision.

## Precedent Treatment

- Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health: applies its bodily-integrity and informed-consent foundation to explain the refusal/assistance distinction. Its assumption of a competent person's constitutional refusal right and its approval of Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes retain their actual scope.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey: preserves protected reproductive choice, individualized viability, timely necessary life-and-health care and the complete six-step standard. That established liberty supplies no general right to intentional death assistance; no component is displaced.
- FCC v. Beach Communications: applies its permission for relevant generalizations and conceivable legitimate grounds without formal findings or certainty, while retaining its limits against irrational or impermissibly motivated classifications. Its economic setting does not itself decide this medical distinction.
- Heller v. Doe: applies the proof-burden holding's requirement of a relationship to the actual disadvantage and the separate family-participation holding's acceptance of relevant generalization without a least-restrictive-means requirement. Neither component is changed.
- Romer v. Evans: applies ordinary rationality to the law's actual operation without a separate hostility-proof prerequisite; distinguishes its unexplained blanket withdrawal of ordinary protection from the justified conduct distinction here.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno: preserves the prohibition on a bare desire to harm a disfavored group as a justification.
- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center: preserves the requirement of a rational connection and the limit against irrational prejudice.

## Law After Decision

New York may enforce its intentional-assistance prohibition against the equality claim presented while continuing to respect lawful treatment refusal and palliation. The controlling addition is the rationality of this particular distinction, effective June 26, 1997. Ordinary rational-basis doctrine is applied without a new scrutiny tier, proof burden or general medical-regulation exemption. The judgment neither recognizes an assisted-death entitlement nor decides every individual application.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion and also explains why the narrower interest of a competent person suffering greatly near imminent death need not be decided. New York permits qualified physicians to relieve pain, even with medication producing unconsciousness and a foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, protection against mistaken judgments of competence, terminal condition and voluntariness sufficiently supports the prohibition even if that narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards. She does not find that pain care is universally delivered or invariably successful, require legislative exhaustion, or adopt a freestanding constitutional pain-treatment standard. Ginsburg substantially adopts these separate reasons. Breyer joins them except insofar as the concurrence joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** He accepts a sufficient basis for the general distinction but does not join the Court's ordinary-review and causation/intent account in full. A patient withdrawing treatment and one requesting lethal medication may share an intention to hasten impending death; physicians may seek relief of suffering in both settings. Terminal sedation further complicates a universal distinction. His Cruzan dissent treats dignity and the manner of unavoidable death as serious individual interests; Casey's protected reproductive choice supplies a bounded analogy requiring attention to real burdens and clinical circumstances. Neither creates an unrestricted autonomy rule or an assisted-death right. Protective concerns sustain a general prohibition while potentially carrying different weight in an exceptional competent patient's well-supported case. He finds no such entitlement established here and orders no hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** The important patient and physician interests require justification commensurate with their weight, rather than a merely formal same-treatment syllogism. Harlan's Poe analysis, bodily-integrity principles and the defined reproductive and medical-privacy interests support that substantive inquiry. Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and euthanasia supplies the sufficient ground: reliably identifying responsible voluntary choice and containing a legalized lethal practice present serious unresolved problems. Palliation and treatment refusal differ in purpose, bodily intrusion and the likelihood of implementing a responsible patient's choice, though both can shorten life. Those differences and protective concerns justify this line. He reaches that conclusion through the stated substantive inquiry and does not join the Court's ordinary rational-basis method.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons concerning the reserved imminent-death and suffering interest and the laws' permission for palliation. She does not join the principal opinion, a declaration validating every future application, or every word of O'Connor's principal-opinion joins.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** He agrees that the identified protective interests justify the distinction, while describing the potential liberty as the combination of control over the manner of death, willing professional assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. He does not decide whether that interest is fundamental. The law permits the pain treatment central to it despite foreseeable death; failures in delivery and the possibility of sedation do not establish a legal prohibition. He does not assert that every person's suffering can be relieved. A law forbidding necessary palliation would raise a different question. These reasons accompany his qualified O'Connor join and do not adopt the principal opinion or deny every end-of-life liberty.
