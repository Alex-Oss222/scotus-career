## Controlling holdings

##### I. The asserted physician-assistance right is not fundamental

**Holding and operative rule:** The Due Process Clause does not establish a fundamental right for a competent, terminally ill adult to obtain a willing physician's intentional assistance in suicide through a lethal prescription. The asserted class-wide entitlement therefore receives no heightened protection; a materially different, more particularized claim is not foreclosed merely by this disposition.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas. The same five Justices directly support both holdings and their limits. The other four Justices concur in the judgment on the separate grounds identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses the claim actually made: a competent person approaching death seeks willing medical assistance to cause death, without demanding compulsory service or public financing. Its terminal-illness and competence limits matter. Neither an unrestricted wish to die nor a generic appeal to autonomy adequately describes it.

The text and structure of the Due Process Clause, the relevant precedents and the enduring legal treatment of assistance in suicide do not establish this entitlement as a fundamental liberty. The tradition of prohibiting such assistance bears on this claimed right; the Court does not adopt an exclusive rule requiring the most specifically imaginable historical analogue for every constitutional liberty. Cruzan explains refusal of unwanted treatment through bodily integrity, battery and informed consent. It assumed a competent person's right to refuse lifesaving nutrition and hydration and upheld Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. Ending an unwanted intervention does not itself establish permission for another person intentionally to supply lethal means.

Casey retains the protected reproductive decision, individualized viability, timely necessary life-and-health care and its complete six-step regulation standard. Mazurek requires application of that entire standard, including its purpose and residual-interference protections. Those established holdings are not reduced to a generic substantial-obstacle rule or displaced by history alone. They concern a defined reproductive liberty, however, and do not recognize every important medical decision as fundamental. The involvement of a willing professional is not itself dispositive: protected reproductive care also involves assistance. What remains unestablished here is constitutional protection for this distinct intentional lethal act. The refusal and reproductive precedents therefore do not require the asserted extension.

**Limits:** The Court does not decide forced treatment, interference with competent refusal or valid directives, denial of lawful pain care, punishment of good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen, or discriminatory administration. The reserved claim must be materially different from the class entitlement rejected here; the reservation neither recognizes that entitlement nor creates a new pending action.

##### II. Washington's prohibition rationally serves its protective interests

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the asserted assistance entitlement is not fundamental, Washington's prohibition is valid against this substantive-due-process challenge if it is rationally related to legitimate state interests. It meets that standard by protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable people against error and pressure, maintaining medical responsibility and containing the risks of a legalized lethal practice; the Ninth Circuit's class-wide invalidation cannot stand.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies direct majority support. This rationality determination completes the rejection of the claim; the absence of a fundamental right alone is not treated as sufficient to dispose of every constitutional objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The State may protect human life without grading the value of particular lives. Terminal illness does not eliminate the risks of mistaken diagnosis, depression, coercion, family pressure, unequal care or indifference toward vulnerable persons. A physician's involvement does not guarantee a reliable determination that a request is informed, responsible and voluntary. Washington may also preserve the professional duty to care and guard against expansion from the proposed class into voluntary or involuntary euthanasia.

The physicians answer that competent adults near death can choose deliberately and that safeguards could confine the practice. The Court does not find that any named person lacked capacity or that any physician acted improperly. The issue is whether the State can reasonably address risks attending authorization of the practice as a class. Difficulty assessing voluntariness, predicting terminal decline and enforcing the proposed boundary supplies the required connection. Rational review does not require certainty that every risk will occur, a finding that every request is involuntary, or proof that prohibition is the best available policy. Nor must the State adopt a less restrictive scheme solely because one can be proposed.

The legal availability of treatment refusal and good-faith pain relief preserves their distinct functions; the challenged prohibition does not require unwanted intervention or forbid palliation because a shorter life is foreseeable. That permission is not a guarantee that every patient's suffering is relieved. States remain free to authorize and regulate assisted death within applicable constitutional limits. This judgment sustains Washington's chosen protective response to the claim before the Court without imposing a national legislative policy.

**Remedy and scope:** The Ninth Circuit must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted substantive-due-process right to physician-assisted suicide. The remand does not authorize lethal prescriptions, command a new patient-specific hearing or adjudicate a materially different claim merely because it could arise.

## Published noncontrolling continuity

The following positions are published in Washington v. Glucksberg, June 26, 1997. Their exact scope and adoption relationships are noncontrolling except for each Justice's separately specified joins to the Court's opinion.

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion but does not decide whether a competent person facing imminent death and great suffering has a cognizable interest in controlling those circumstances. Washington permits qualified physicians to alleviate suffering with medication even to unconsciousness and despite foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, the risks of erroneous judgments about terminal condition, competence and voluntariness sufficiently support the prohibition even if the narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards and democratic consideration. This neither guarantees effective delivery of pain care, eliminates suffering associated with sedation, requires legislative exhaustion nor creates a freestanding constitutional pain-care standard. Ginsburg adopts substantially these separate reasons. Breyer joins the separate analysis except insofar as it joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** No person has complete autonomy to end life regardless of others' interests, and the State may maintain a general prohibition. That conclusion does not decide a competent individual's well-supported claim concerning the manner of an unavoidable imminent death. His Cruzan dissent and Casey's defined protected choice support attention to dignity, intolerable suffering and genuine justification. Preservation of life does not automatically outweigh every such interest. Mistake, coercion and professional-integrity concerns justify the general rule but may carry different weight in an exceptional case. Palliation does not logically eliminate every form of suffering or the distinction between conscious relief and terminal sedation. He also questions whether intent and causation invariably distinguish treatment withdrawal, sedation and lethal prescriptions; those overlaps do not establish this class-wide entitlement. He finds no individual entitlement proved here and orders no additional hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** Harlan's Poe analysis asks whether the restraint is arbitrary or purposeless in light of the relative importance and fit of the individual and state interests. Traditions retained and abandoned inform that inquiry; neither longstanding prohibition alone nor an abstract autonomy formula resolves it. Responsible terminally ill patients seeking medical counsel and assistance present an important interest connected to bodily integrity, treatment refusal and defined reproductive choice. Casey's complete framework is not a universal medical test. He accepts the competent and voluntary premises and provisionally accepts the professional premises for this claim without inventing incapacity or misconduct.

Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and expansion into euthanasia supplies the sufficient justification. Family pressure alone would not settle the matter, because proposed physician screening is intended to answer it. The harder problem is reliably assessing a knowing, responsible decision and ensuring that physicians preserve the asserted boundaries. The disputed evidence concerning safeguards and the Dutch experience establishes a serious empirical uncertainty, not an incontrovertible foreign-law conclusion. Courts cannot presently displace the State's protective judgment with adequate assurance; legislatures can investigate and develop experience.

His conclusion rejects this entitlement now without permanently foreclosing recognition, demanding legislative experiments, imposing a judicial moratorium or assuming inevitable logical expansion of every protected right. Legislative foot-dragging and a materially different individual record remain for another case. Incidental difficulty does not automatically require compelling justification, and not every liberty claim demands elaborate balancing: the importance of the interest and the burden determine the inquiry. His view does not become the Court's general standard.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons about the unresolved imminent-death and suffering interest and the legal availability of palliation. She does not join the principal opinion or a declaration sustaining every future application. Her agreement creates no additional independent theory.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** The potential liberty combines personal control over the manner of death, willing medical assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. Describing it merely as any right to suicide misses the strongest claim without establishing that claim as fundamental. Poe, Cruzan and the defined personal-liberty authorities support serious consideration. Because the law does not forbid the pain treatment central to that interest, the present case need not decide its status; the stated protective interests suffice here. Delivery failures are not the same as a legal prohibition. He recognizes sedation and does not find every person's suffering treatable. A prohibition of necessary palliation would present a materially different case. These reasons supplement his qualified O'Connor join; they do not adopt the principal opinion or one universal liberty method for all judgment supporters.

### Principal ground and actual Casey

The asserted entitlement to willing physician assistance in intentionally bringing about death is not established as a fundamental right by the Due Process Clause. The inquiry carefully describes that claim, considers the enduring legal treatment of suicide assistance and its place in ordered liberty, and distinguishes the accepted refusal and reproductive precedents. It must not evade the presented terminal-illness and competence qualifications by describing only an unlimited desire to die. Cruzan's battery, informed-consent and bodily-integrity setting supports refusal of unwanted intervention; its disposition assumed the relevant competent refusal right and upheld Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. It does not itself establish assistance by another to cause death.

Actual Casey preserves the patient's previability choice, individualized viability and timely necessary life-and-health care. Its complete six-step regulation standard was directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter. Kennedy joined the retained protected-choice foundation, not all six steps. The standard identifies duty and protected baseline; actual purpose/function; concrete caused interference and affected population; independent prohibitions followed by the distinct severe and meaningful-interference inquiries; clinical judgment and truthful communication; and violation-specific relief. Neither historical Casey language nor a generic “undue burden” replacement may displace that actual rule. Its existence means history alone cannot silently annul an established protected choice; its reproductive scope does not establish every important medical choice as a new fundamental right. O'Connor and Souter's complete-framework support must be preserved when evaluating their present qualifications. Ginsburg and Breyer's later adherence is not a retroactive 1992 join.

At the claim's presented scope, Washington's law rationally serves preservation of life, prevention of suicide, protection of medical responsibilities, protection of vulnerable persons against mistake, coercion, prejudice and indifference, and avoidance of expansion into euthanasia. The physician-patient setting and terminal illness do not eliminate diagnostic error, difficulty assessing a truly voluntary decision or practical limits on policing a legalized lethal practice. These are rational prevention grounds, not findings that any named patient lacked capacity or that any doctor acted improperly. The account need not rank the interests or find the ban the best policy. It leaves more particularized claims open without suggesting that the rejected general assistance interest already receives heightened protection. No forced treatment, legal denial of palliation or every medical regulation is approved by this decision.

The historical principal opinion is not accurately described as relying **only** on history or as declaring every future application constitutional: it also supplies rational protective grounds and expressly preserves a genuinely different particularized claim. Assembly must not manufacture a new coalition by contrasting a narrow draft with those nonexistent categorical historical propositions. Nor may it declare a universal exclusive most-specific-history test that Kennedy's recorded reservation rejects.

**O'Connor, with Ginsburg's substantial adoption and Breyer's qualified join.** She agrees that no generalized suicide entitlement controls. **Stevens.** No person has complete autonomy to end life regardless of the interests of others, and the State can maintain a general prohibition. ## Public Projection

## Event

Washington v. Glucksberg, No. 96-110. Argued January 8, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. Its March 6, 1996 en banc judgment, 79 F.3d 790, invalidated Washington's assisted-suicide prohibition as applied to competent terminally ill adults seeking physician assistance. ## Holdings

##### I. The asserted physician-assistance right is not fundamental

**Holding and operative rule:** The Due Process Clause does not establish a fundamental right for a competent, terminally ill adult to obtain a willing physician's intentional assistance in suicide through a lethal prescription. The asserted class-wide entitlement therefore receives no heightened protection; a materially different, more particularized claim is not foreclosed merely by this disposition.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas. The same five Justices directly support both holdings and their limits. The other four Justices concur in the judgment on the separate grounds identified in Opinion Topology.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court addresses the claim actually made: a competent person approaching death seeks willing medical assistance to cause death, without demanding compulsory service or public financing. Its terminal-illness and competence limits matter. Neither an unrestricted wish to die nor a generic appeal to autonomy adequately describes it.

The text and structure of the Due Process Clause, the relevant precedents and the enduring legal treatment of assistance in suicide do not establish this entitlement as a fundamental liberty. The tradition of prohibiting such assistance bears on this claimed right; the Court does not adopt an exclusive rule requiring the most specifically imaginable historical analogue for every constitutional liberty. Cruzan explains refusal of unwanted treatment through bodily integrity, battery and informed consent. It assumed a competent person's right to refuse lifesaving nutrition and hydration and upheld Missouri's evidentiary safeguard for an incompetent patient's wishes. Ending an unwanted intervention does not itself establish permission for another person intentionally to supply lethal means.

Casey retains the protected reproductive decision, individualized viability, timely necessary life-and-health care and its complete six-step regulation standard. Mazurek requires application of that entire standard, including its purpose and residual-interference protections. Those established holdings are not reduced to a generic substantial-obstacle rule or displaced by history alone. They concern a defined reproductive liberty, however, and do not recognize every important medical decision as fundamental. The involvement of a willing professional is not itself dispositive: protected reproductive care also involves assistance. What remains unestablished here is constitutional protection for this distinct intentional lethal act. The refusal and reproductive precedents therefore do not require the asserted extension.

**Limits:** The Court does not decide forced treatment, interference with competent refusal or valid directives, denial of lawful pain care, punishment of good-faith palliation merely because death is foreseen, or discriminatory administration. The reserved claim must be materially different from the class entitlement rejected here; the reservation neither recognizes that entitlement nor creates a new pending action.

##### II. Washington's prohibition rationally serves its protective interests

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the asserted assistance entitlement is not fundamental, Washington's prohibition is valid against this substantive-due-process challenge if it is rationally related to legitimate state interests. It meets that standard by protecting life, preventing suicide, guarding vulnerable people against error and pressure, maintaining medical responsibility and containing the risks of a legalized lethal practice; the Ninth Circuit's class-wide invalidation cannot stand.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies direct majority support. This rationality determination completes the rejection of the claim; the absence of a fundamental right alone is not treated as sufficient to dispose of every constitutional objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The State may protect human life without grading the value of particular lives. Terminal illness does not eliminate the risks of mistaken diagnosis, depression, coercion, family pressure, unequal care or indifference toward vulnerable persons. A physician's involvement does not guarantee a reliable determination that a request is informed, responsible and voluntary. Washington may also preserve the professional duty to care and guard against expansion from the proposed class into voluntary or involuntary euthanasia.

The physicians answer that competent adults near death can choose deliberately and that safeguards could confine the practice. The Court does not find that any named person lacked capacity or that any physician acted improperly. The issue is whether the State can reasonably address risks attending authorization of the practice as a class. Difficulty assessing voluntariness, predicting terminal decline and enforcing the proposed boundary supplies the required connection. Rational review does not require certainty that every risk will occur, a finding that every request is involuntary, or proof that prohibition is the best available policy. Nor must the State adopt a less restrictive scheme solely because one can be proposed.

The legal availability of treatment refusal and good-faith pain relief preserves their distinct functions; the challenged prohibition does not require unwanted intervention or forbid palliation because a shorter life is foreseeable. That permission is not a guarantee that every patient's suffering is relieved. States remain free to authorize and regulate assisted death within applicable constitutional limits. This judgment sustains Washington's chosen protective response to the claim before the Court without imposing a national legislative policy.

**Remedy and scope:** The Ninth Circuit must direct entry of judgment for Washington on the asserted substantive-due-process right to physician-assisted suicide. The remand does not authorize lethal prescriptions, command a new patient-specific hearing or adjudicate a materially different claim merely because it could arise.
