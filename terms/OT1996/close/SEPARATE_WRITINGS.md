## 1996-11-04 — California v. Roy, No. 95-2025; October Term 1996, chunk 1.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** He joins Part II and supports vacatur and remand because the Ninth Circuit's necessity formulation does not replace the whole-record Chapman inquiry required for the preserved personal-intent claim. Gaudin protects jury determination of elements without making every omission structural; Sullivan's destruction of the reasonable-doubt verdict differs from this assessable omission. O'Neal puts on the State the burden to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the violation did not contribute to the verdict. He would neither supply intent himself, shift the burden to Roy nor demand innocence. If a properly available violation fails the State's showing, relief for the affected convictions must permit a reasonable retrial opportunity. Preservation, exhaustion, independent habeas requirements and statutory transition remain separate. He makes no final harmlessness finding and sets no retrial deadline. He does not join Parts I or III's additional reasoning.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part; no joins.** He joins Parts I and II but would affirm the habeas-relief judgment on this record. His Rose concurrence and his agreement with Yates require rigorous inquiry into the error's contribution to the actual verdict. The distinct omitted purpose, the supported inference of companion assistance without a robbery purpose, the disputed advance-plan account and the absence of an established independent purpose finding prevent the State from proving harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. This does not establish that every defense assertion is true or that the jury rejected all of Hudspeth's testimony. Stevens accepts that the Ninth Circuit's general necessary-findings formula is too broad; he does not adopt it as the exclusive rule for every omission. A proper actual-contribution inquiry nevertheless supports the judgment already reached. No identified unfinished material task justifies another remand merely to repeat an adverse Chapman determination. He would leave conditional relief for the two Mannix convictions in place, with a reasonable retrial opportunity and no immediate unconditional release.

**Justice Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Ginsburg.** They join Parts I and II but would affirm. For this omitted-purpose error, harmlessness requires that the jury's other determinations effectively embrace Roy's required purpose, or that the evidence and findings establish that the jury could not have reached what it did find without also finding that purpose. The State must establish that connection beyond a reasonable doubt. Carella's separate opinion and Scalia's Yates opinion explain why independent appellate certainty that a properly instructed jury would convict cannot replace the jury's actual findings; Sullivan and Gaudin preserve the jury's constitutional role. Whole-record review identifies what the actual verdict embraces; it does not authorize an appellate substitute verdict.

Knowledge and assistance do not necessarily establish purpose. Neither the Clark findings nor general capacity answers the missing Mannix purpose. The conditional nonkiller clause, the distinction between intentional assistance to first-degree murder and purpose to facilitate robbery, the robbery-related purpose of the killing, and the cross-reference to deficient aiding instructions prevent treating the special circumstances as an established equivalent finding. The asserted preservation objection does not itself supply forfeiture, and the record does not identify an unresolved genuine finding question requiring another remand. A valid equivalent finding could make an omission harmless, so their rule is not automatic structural reversal. They would affirm conditional relief only for the two Mannix convictions, allowing a reasonable retrial period. They adopt no innocence prerequisite or discretionary-habeas rule, and Ginsburg joins no such proposition.

## 1996-11-18 — Ohio v. Robinette; No. 95-891

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He would vacate the Ohio judgment and remand. He joins the no-script rule, the objective continued-seizure inquiry, and the Court's requirements to examine voluntariness and any necessary causal relationship to unlawful detention. A State cannot convert uninterrupted official restraint into consent merely by describing it as a request. Immediate questioning, documents, tone, positioning and the absence of an objective release signal bear on whether the motorist remained seized and whether the choice was real. A clear warning is strong evidence and may be required by state law or policy. He reaches no final admissibility result and preserves suppression where the applicable inquiries fail. He agrees to federal review and to setting aside the motive-based exit-order ground without adding a reason on those components; his chosen formal disposition remains vacatur, not reversal.

**Justice Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She accepts federal review under governing Long despite her earlier preference in Evans for a different jurisdictional presumption. The Ohio opinion's interwoven analysis supplies no clearly independent state basis. She agrees that federal law requires no universal release script and that actual consent must be assessed under the total circumstances. Her agreement does not decide that Robinette's search was valid or that no detention continued. She emphasizes that Ohio may use its own Constitution to require a clear warning, provided it rests the rule on an independent state ground. Federal correction of this judgment does not displace that authority. She does not join Stevens's conclusion that this record already requires suppression.

**Justice Stevens, dissenting.** He accepts review and rejects an indispensable federal warning formula, but would affirm suppression on the actual encounter. His Bostick dissent's attention to the practical force of police authority, together with Royer and Brown's separation of consent from unlawful detention, leads him to treat the continued official direction after completion of traffic business as restraint requiring an independent justification. The deputy's question before departure continued that restraint; no separate suspicion or intervening circumstance supplies a lawful extension or breaks the connection to consent. Returning the license and Robinette's initial sense of freedom do not decide the effect of the subsequent direction. The deputy's courtesy, lack of a drawn weapon and Robinette's education bear on voluntariness, but furnish no intervening break from the detention. Stevens does not invalidate the initial stop or a lawful exit order because of hidden motive, and he proposes no automatic suppression rule after every illegality. His disagreement is with the Court's refusal to affirm suppression on this record, not with Ohio's freedom to require warnings under independent state law.

## 1996-11-25 — Hopwood v. Texas; Fifth Circuit No. 94-50664, 78 F.3d 932; no invented Supreme Court docket; OT1996, chunk 1, matter 6.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** Kennedy joins Parts I, III and IV–V but does not join Part II. He would assume a potentially compelling educational interest and invalidate the actual process for inadequate tailoring. Croson supports rigorous application without an unnecessary categorical prohibition; his Metro reasoning rejects assigning a contribution to an applicant merely from racial identity. The combination of different thresholds and separate decision rules remains unjustified despite individual exceptions within each track. He agrees that the appellate diversity prohibition must be removed from this remand but reserves the affirmative compelling-interest question. He neither requires identical numerical evaluation in every future program nor approves the later design.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part; Thomas joins Section A.** Section A rejects nonremedial racial preferences as a justification for burdening an individual applicant. The equal-protection right belongs to the person, and educational aims do not turn racial ancestry into an entitlement or establish that group membership predicts educational contribution. The actual five-Justice Bakke Part V-C proposition is acknowledged; these Justices would expressly disapprove it to the extent it authorizes nonremedial preferences. They therefore dissent from vacating the diversity prohibition and from Part II, while joining the narrow invalidity holding and the individual causation/remedy provisions. They accept the same-decision allocation and preponderance requirement, but their substantive view excludes nonremedial preferences from the lawful counterfactual. Individual experiences may still be considered under lawful nonracial criteria.

In Section B, which Thomas does not join, Scalia declines the broader institutional rule and further remedial inquiry in Part III. Even assuming a broader responsible unit, the record does not establish that these preferences were necessary to remove a traceable barrier. He recognizes Fordice as existing authority and does not seek to overrule it, but would resolve this offered defense without the additional remand inquiry. Thomas instead joins Part III’s bounded recognition of the State’s duty; that join does not equate enrollment targets with removal of inherited barriers. Neither Justice supports a new injunction or punitive award, treats benevolent purpose as eliminating statutory intent, or deems every constitutionally valid remedial act forbidden merely because it considers race.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** The Chief Justice joins Parts I–III, IV-D and V. He agrees with the individual-relief remand and punitive denial, without joining Parts IV-A–C. He would assess claimed individualization by actual operation: the order of decisions, thresholds, separate review, numerical aims, discretion, beneficiary categories, whether assumptions can genuinely be rebutted, and workable alternatives. A small formal weight for race is no answer when it determines outcomes near the admission threshold. Conversely, a program lacking quotas, separate tracks and numerical aims is not invalid merely because race is one consideration among several; no particular such program is approved. The combined 1992 design made race decisive across broad categories without the supported necessity and individualized fit he requires.

He understands a permissible educational objective to require concrete benefits from meaningful institutional inclusion, an explanation of the mechanism connecting the classification to those benefits, and attention to actual burdens. Racial arithmetic, generalized societal discrimination, reputation, hostility outside the institution, and assumptions that identity predicts viewpoint cannot substitute for that showing. For corrective measures he additionally requires objective evidence of the persisting effect, scope fitted to it, meaningful continuing opportunity for others, respect for legally protected existing interests, and termination when the basis ends. The State bears its justification burden. These additional formulations are his separate grounds, not additions to the Court’s controlling rule.

His causation position distinguishes the established unequal process from a claimed lost seat or monetary loss. A defendant may defeat admission-related compensation by proving that a specified lawful process would have produced the same denial, without erasing the violation. He would preserve nominal and declaratory recognition and fee-free lawful consideration if practically available, leaving the proper cause, defendant, immunity and precise relief for determination below. He agrees with the disposition of the separate statutory-intent component without adopting the Court’s statutory rationale.

## 1996-12-02 — Tyler Murray v. Montrose County School District RE-1J; Tenth Circuit Nos. 93-1466 and 93-1474; no Supreme Court docket assigned.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents. He would affirm the Tenth Circuit and reach the preserved geographic legal question while treating the individualized former-placement and later-progress disputes as moot. In his view, IDEA's preference for education with nondisabled children is distinct from geographic proximity: the regulations make proximity and the neighborhood school mandatory considerations, but permit another arrangement when the individualized IEP requires it. He would reject an independent burden-shifting neighborhood-school presumption without approving convenience-based transfers, diminishing the duty to use supplementary aids, or declaring distance irrelevant. A district's assertion that its specialized program is better would not establish compliance.

He would recognize section 1415(e)(2)'s additional-evidence channel in a live dispute, including relevant subsequent progress with its temporal relevance explained, while giving due weight to the administrative proceedings without surrendering independent legal judgment. He would order no new evidence here: the aids proffer does not establish the geographic-presumption theory, the independent unexhausted aids issue is not reached, and the later IEP removes the placement/progress remedy. He declines the wider evidentiary disagreement and any ruling on Northside's factual adequacy. His proposed legal answer and affirmance do not command a majority; the Court holds the controversy moot.

## 1996-12-09 — In re Askin; Fourth Circuit No. 94-5428, 47 F.3d 100; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied; October Term 1996.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** He agrees that the former wire/electronic exclusions cover the intercepted radio segment carrying both voices and that the 1994 deletion cannot retroactively make completed surveillance unlawful. His statutory position treats the place of acquisition as controlling under the former cordless exclusion. He would affirm the lower court's complete former-Title-III noncoverage ruling for that acquired segment and does not join the Court's additional oral-coverage route. Statutory noncoverage, on his view, neither authorizes interception at another point nor determines constitutional privacy.

He joins rejection of the categorical Fourth Amendment ruling and would state a further conditional rule: absent participant consent, a willing informant or knowing exposure, a landline or cellular caller does not surrender privacy merely because the other participant uses a vulnerable cordless handset without the caller's knowledge. *Katz* protects the speaker's own private words. *White* assigns the risk that an interlocutor will reveal or transmit them; it does not eliminate privacy merely because unknown agents can independently intercept a retransmission the caller did not select or know about. This rule leaves knowing exposure, participant consent and the cordless user's own expectation distinct. The record does not establish Askin's knowledge, so he would not finally apply the rule.

He accepts *Gelbard*'s statutory just-cause principle, but proposes a separate constitutional ground for this criminal-trial setting. When compelled questions are direct fruits of unlawful acquisition of the witness's own protected calls, and no recognized exception provides a lawful basis, refusal should constitute just cause under §1826(a). Coercing this speaker's testimony through his own unlawfully intercepted words gives the exclusionary rule a direct deterrent function. Question-specific derivation, personal rights and ordinary remedial exceptions keep the proposal bounded. *Pinson* and *Wellons* do not themselves establish this extension; *Calandra*'s grand-jury rule is left intact.

He would require the lower court to determine knowledge or knowing exposure, direct derivation of each question, and any preserved independent source, attenuation, objectively reasonable good-faith reliance or other lawful basis. If the governing premises establish lack of knowledge and direct derivation from unlawful acquisition without an applicable exception, the contempt adjudication must be set aside. He grants no automatic immunity from questions and no final discharge on this record. The Court's eight-Justice opinion leaves that independent constitutional just-cause question unresolved.

## 1996-12-10 — O'Gilvie v. United States; Nos. 95-966 and 95-977

## 1996-12-16 — M.L.B. v. S.L.J.; No. 95-853

Kennedy concurs in the judgment on due process alone. Boddie, Lassiter and Santosky identify the established parental interest and the need for fair procedures without creating a civil appeal in every case. Under Mathews, the permanent loss, risk of error, practical value of a usable record and governmental burdens must be assessed in the appellate structure Mississippi actually provided. Here the unaffordable record barrier defeats that review. He supports sufficient record access and reversal, but does not join the Court's extension of Griffin's equality rationale or treat wealth as a suspect classification. This is judgment-only agreement, not an additional opinion join.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, dissents on two grounds. Due process protects the established parental interest through an adequate hearing, but Boddie's indispensable initial access does not itself compel financing an additional civil appeal. Separately, Griffin and Mayer's criminal-access exception should not be extended beyond Kras and Ortwein's ordinary civil cost-allocation rule. Permanent termination is exceptionally grave, yet seriousness and the convergence of fairness and equality do not identify an additional appellate subsidy. The dissent questions the limiting principle for other civil claims while accepting the protected parental relationship. It neither finds the termination correct nor equates poverty with unfitness, and it leaves voluntary state support available. Griffin, Mayer and Bounds are not proposed for overruling; the dissent confines its claim to nonextension here.

## 1997-01-06 — DeAngelis v. El Paso Municipal Police Officers' Association; Fifth Circuit No. 93-8700, 51 F.3d 591; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment; no joins.** He joins the retaliation holding but rejects statutory insufficiency on hostile environment. The repeated sex-derogatory message, distribution to more than seven hundred colleagues, connections to DeAngelis, recurring comments and workplace incidents, humiliation, claimed loss of authority, promotion deterrence and refusal to identify the columnist permit a rational jury's severe-or-pervasive finding under Harris. Physical threats, supervisory power, reduced performance and the written medium are not statutory barriers. He therefore reaches the preserved expressive defense.

In his view, this award punishes protected editorial opinion or satire solely for its sex-derogatory viewpoint. Such civil liability is content based and is not narrowly tailored here to the compelling interest in eradicating sex discrimination. The publication itself is the independently asserted wrong; the record supplies no separate discriminatory employment act, true threat, authority-backed directive or denial of employment or associational rights to which the expression is integral or evidentiary. Hurley preserves the expressive inquiry, and Mitchell distinguishes punishment of protected belief from use of expression to establish an independently unlawful act. He would prohibit Title VII damages resting solely on such protected opinion or satire, while preserving liability for discriminatory decisions, quid pro quo demands, true threats, authority-backed directives and expression integral to or used as evidence of independently unlawful employment conduct. His rule is confined to this expressive application and does not declare that speech cannot itself be workplace harassment. That constitutional ground alone supports his hostile-environment affirmance. On retaliation, he finds no material employment or membership disadvantage caused by the complaint and leaves stronger threats, harassment and public-shaming cases open.

**Justice Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justice Ginsburg.** They join Part II but would reverse the hostile-environment portion and remand. Harris protects disadvantageous conditions without proof of lost output. The lower opinion's reliance on destroyed opportunity, palpable performance deterioration, missing advances and absence of a superior harasser materially narrowed its review of the jury verdict. Its correct abstract recitation of Harris and the substantial contrary contextual evidence do not cure that error. Repeated sex-based attacks, broad circulation, mocking, humiliation and alleged impairment of command warrant review without those added thresholds. Ginsburg's Harris explanation makes professional disadvantage relevant without measurable productivity loss. They find a review error, not liability as a matter of law. Mitchell and Hurley keep the preserved expressive defense distinct; it and any remaining properly preserved grounds must be resolved before liability could stand. They would restore neither damages nor a particular allocation and reject both automatic newsletter immunity and automatic loss of protection for an offensive editorial.

## 1997-01-06 — United States v. Watts; United States v. Putra; No. 95-1906, one Rule 12.4 petition concerning two Ninth Circuit judgments

**Justice Scalia, concurring; no joins.** He joins the bounded Court opinion, which does not say that every punishment below a statutory ceiling is necessarily punishment only for the conviction. His additional disagreement concerns Commission authority. In his view, §994(b)(1)'s requirement of consistency with pertinent provisions of Title 18 and §3661's protection of sentencing-information consideration forbid a Commission or judicial rule excluding otherwise lawful acquitted-conduct consideration. The Commission may recommend congressional change; it may not itself prohibit a lawful enhancement or impose an otherwise unsupported higher burden solely because the conduct was acquitted. That statutory objection is his own; Thomas's agreement with the narrow constitutional opinion supplies no join to it.

**Justice Breyer, concurring; no joins.** He joins the Court's construction of the Guidelines as written and its constitutional holding. He regards the use of acquitted conduct in mandatory enhancement as a legitimate subject of Commission policy change. Different burdens make judicial findings compatible with acquittals, but that is not the only permissible sentencing-policy consideration. The institutional significance of a jury's acquittal may support excluding that conduct from mandatory enhancement even though the present relevant-conduct provisions contain no such exception. He disagrees with Scalia's proposed statutory restriction on the Commission. Neither concurrence changes today's calculation rules.

**Justice Stevens, dissenting; no joins.** He seeks plenary briefing and argument and would, if the merits are decided now, affirm both sentence-vacatur judgments. Section 3661 permits receiving information and using it in lawful sentencing discretion; it does not itself assign mandatory weight or authorize a binding-range increase on a preponderance finding of additional criminal conduct. Section 1B1.4 addresses selection within the range or departure, while §1B1.3 determines the range through relevant conduct. Section 994(l)'s instruction about incremental punishment for multiple convictions reinforces his narrower authorization reading; the Court's contrary view treats that instruction as a minimum rather than an exclusive limit.

The criminal proof tradition and his McMillan dissent make the mandatory punitive consequence central. His Witte dissent likewise rejects the notion that a broad statutory maximum erases the concrete increment. He distinguishes discretionary use of information from facts that enlarge a binding range, and would not sustain these increases on the existing preponderance findings. In Watts, possession and use differ; his objection does not depend on a finding that the jury rejected possession. He announces no categorical ban on considering an acquitted episode, no independent Grady successive-prosecution bar, and no comprehensive new jury-allocation test. Lawful resentencing may use information in selecting an authorized sentence, but may not simply reimpose these mandatory increases on the same insufficient authorization-and-proof premise. He sets no sentence and would disturb no acquittal.

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting; no joins.** He would receive full merits briefing and oral argument before resolving this important recurring question. Dowling and Witte make the Government's objection to a categorical bar persuasive, and circuit conflict supports review. But Witte expressly reserved acquitted conduct; mandatory sentencing gives the distinction practical force, and disagreement among the reversal supporters about the Commission's authority exposes an unsettled statutory issue. Summary correction with reservations does not provide the examination he considers necessary. He neither affirms the defendants' merits position nor adopts a reasonable-doubt rule; his present dissent concerns the procedure of deciding the merits now.

## 1997-01-07 — Old Chief v. United States; No. 95-6556

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part; no joins.** He joins the Rule 403 holding and reversal because the comparison is between two means of proving the same complete legal status, not between proof and no proof. Ordinary prosecutorial narrative control has limited force for status detached from the charged episode. An unconditional, legally complete admission supplies that element, while identifying the similar assault adds no narrative link to present possession and creates acute propensity danger. He preserves the Government's ability to prove a distinct nonpropensity purpose under Rule 403 and rejects any general right to stipulate away event narrative or require acceptance of partial or qualified admissions.

He differs on remedy. In his view, the close resemblance to violent conduct and the unnecessary character information prevent the Government from showing harmlessness on this record. He would vacate the conviction and remand for a new trial rather than leave harmlessness open. At retrial the Government could use the adequate admission or another sanitized means for status; prior-offense details could be introduced only for a distinct lawful purpose satisfying Rule 403. His prejudice conclusion and mandatory new-trial remedy command no additional join and do not change the Court's five-Justice remand.

**Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.** They would affirm because the authentic judgment directly proves a statutory element. A defendant's offer neither removes that element nor makes ordinary specific proof irrelevant; the Government retains its burden and its legitimate interest in persuasive evidence. Huddleston's safeguards and the Rule's substantial-outweighing requirement preserve meaningful trial discretion. They recognize the similar-assault propensity danger but would not find the name and basic nature of this qualifying conviction inadmissible as a matter of law merely because an admission proves the same legal status. They question the compulsory-substitution consequence and the sharp distinction between status proof and narrative proof, without treating relevance as conclusive admissibility or prosecutorial choice as immune from Rule 403.

They do not rely on the curable instruction wording as a new ground, and they do not treat the credibility instruction as waiver of the separate objection. Once the Court's error rule governs, they accept the ordinary Williamson/Kotteakos harmlessness process and oppose automatic retrial; that conditional remedy position is not a vote to find error or reverse. No omitted-element constitutional error or Roy substitute standard is claimed.

## 1997-01-13 — Walmer v. Department of Defense; Tenth Circuit No. 93-3377, 52 F.3d 851; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied

**Justice Scalia, concurring, joined by Justice Thomas.** They join both Court parts and regard the specific military relationship as sufficient. They also draw on Bowers's recognition of authority to regulate the specified conduct: a reasonable conduct-based personnel policy may rest on a permissible moral judgment as well as military discipline. Their Romer dissent distinguished such judgment from a bare wish to harm persons. They do not claim that Bowers already decided equal protection, that every private aversion is legitimate or that the Court has overruled Romer. Beach's tolerance for reasonable predictions and imperfect fit supports this narrower service policy. No individual-performance finding or least restrictive alternative is required. This additional conduct-morality ground is theirs alone and is not attributed to the other three equality affirmers.

**Justice Souter, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Justices Stevens and Ginsburg.** They join the attainder holding but would vacate the equality-based denial and remand. Heller and Romer require examination of the connection between this actual disadvantage and a legitimate objective. Rich concerned fraudulent denial on entry under another regulation, so treating it as conclusive leaves this admitted-acts classification insufficiently examined. Stevens's Beach position likewise requires a relationship to the actual distinction, and Ginsburg's Virginia position supports applying the proper inquiry without importing that case's sex-classification tier.

They accept substantial deference to reasonable military predictions, the availability of conceivable legitimate reasons and Walmer's burden to establish entitlement to preliminary relief. They find legal inadequacy in foreclosure by the older case, not proof that the Army lacks a rational basis. They demand neither actual-purpose proof from the Government, heightened scrutiny, an individualized impairment finding nor a new evidentiary hearing. On remand the courts should reassess the merits question under ordinary rationality and address irreparable injury, comparative harm and public interest if the renewed merits assessment makes them material. Those factors were never found satisfied. They would neither enjoin separation automatically nor finally invalidate the policy.

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part; no joins.** He joins the legislative-predicate attainder holding and would vacate the equality-based denial under a different inquiry. His Part A treats mandatory separation for homosexual acts as an intentional sexual-orientation classification, whether described as conduct or a deliberate status proxy. Bowers does not exempt it from equality review. Romer supplies an ordinary-review floor and a warning against unexplained class disadvantage, but does not itself allocate a justification burden to the Government. He would make that doctrinal change here: the Government must identify an actual military objective and establish a real relationship between it and the intentional classification, without relying on moral disapproval, private bias or stereotype.

His reason is that merely conceivable explanations would permit this intentional disadvantage without establishing its actual connection to the military function invoked. Professional judgments and reasonable contemporaneous predictions about discipline, cohesion, readiness and command receive substantial but nonconclusive weight; an assertion of deference does not carry the Government's burden. Experience and institutional knowledge may suffice without scientific certainty or a universal formal-findings requirement. Part A independently supports his remand because the lower courts treated the claim as foreclosed, placed the practical burden on Walmer to disprove the rule, and did not resolve the military justification his proposed allocation requires.

His separable Part B additionally adopts intermediate scrutiny: the Government must identify an actual important military objective and establish a substantial relationship to it, with substantial weight to supported professional judgment. Neither Romer nor the public-university sex cases already supplies that tier. Separating Part B leaves Part A's independent Government burden intact.

He retains Bowers's specified-conduct holding, ordinary regulation of materially relevant conduct and the absence of a constitutional entitlement to service. He requires neither least restrictive means nor an individual exception in every case, and he does not infer that military concerns lack support from the absence of an individual performance finding. The district court should assess the military showing on a developed record and apply all preliminary-relief requirements. He would order no injunction, immediate status change or final invalidation. Any concrete personnel commitments or settled arrangements must be shown in the actual record and given their proper remedial weight; abstract command responsibility, convenience, institutional preference or expected continued exclusion does not establish protected reliance. He makes no finding here that such commitments exist and adds no automatic transition requirement. His Part A burden and Part B tier each remain noncontrolling.

## 1997-01-14 — Abebe-Jira v. Negewo, No. 95-8873; Eleventh Circuit No. 93-9133.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in Parts I and V and dissents from the damages judgment. They accept the statutory forum and reject a categorical political-question bar, but find no sufficient congressional authority for the asserted domestic damages action. The prohibition of torture does not itself authorize a federal cause of action. A forum for independently actionable law-of-nations torts does not necessarily authorize judicial recognition of every later norm; the common-law setting and lower-court practice are the strongest affirmative arguments, but do not establish the needed source for these Justices.

Suter requires an actual source of private liability; O'Melveny rejects the manufacture of a federal rule from general federal interests; Meyer keeps jurisdiction and substantive action distinct. Franklin presupposed a cognizable action, and their concurrence there relied on a narrower enacted-remedies ground. Central Bank's binding received-securities holding remains confined to its established setting, and Musick, Peeler concerns incidents of already recognized liability. Neither supplies the missing ATS premise. Sanchez-Espinoza's domestic-immunity result is not a categorical bar to foreign-official suits. ## 1997-05-27 — Clinton v. Jones; No. 95-1853; 520 U.S. 681 (1997).

### Stone-Zsela, concurring; no joins

Stone-Zsela joins the Court and its entire judgment. His concurrence proposes a more explicit objective method for distinguishing presidential functions from private conduct. A court should first identify the conduct specifically and the presidential authority claimed; then determine whether objectively established circumstances connect the conduct to performing, directing or supervising a responsibility entrusted by the Constitution or federal law, including Fitzgerald's outer perimeter; then check whether a reasonably informed officeholder would understand it as presidential responsibility rather than a private act. Legal classification remains the judge's task. An ordinary observer's assessment can expose a self-serving label but cannot create or eliminate constitutional power, and subjective good faith is not dispositive. Forrester supports the functional starting point, not this particular proposed check.

Title, location, staff, security, public resources or an official label alone does not establish presidential function. Nor does alleged illegality, retaliation or personal motivation automatically remove an otherwise official act from Fitzgerald's protection. Directing military operations and supervising a department ordinarily perform presidential functions even when their legality is disputed. A private encounter does not become presidential government merely because an official event or staff is involved. The present pre-presidential allegations require no decision of a contested presidential-act classification; the proposed method is guidance in his separate opinion.

He agrees that the actual categorical stay fails and that Landis preserves tailored relief on demonstrated need. He separates official-action review from personal damages immunity: courts may assess executive legality even when Fitzgerald forecloses a particular damages remedy. He would be prepared in a properly presented case to reconsider or limit Fitzgerald concerning proved treason or grave, deliberate constitutional wrongdoing. He adopts no such exception here, and an allegation of unlawfulness alone supplies none.

His broader view is that purely private conduct does not become a presidential function because it occurs during the term, including conduct that may violate criminal law. Criminal liability nonetheless requires an applicable offense, jurisdiction, proof and resolution of any immunity or supremacy issue. He reserves prosecution timing, federal or state criminal jurisdiction, criminally charged official acts, comparable state-court civil proceedings, the post-inauguration defamation defense and all merits questions. His concurrence changes no controlling law on those subjects.

### Breyer, concurring in the judgment; no joins

Breyer agrees that no automatic immunity protects the presented private conduct and that the existing stay is premature because the President has not supplied a specific reasoned explanation of need. He agrees with preservation of Fitzgerald's official-function immunity and the undeveloped press-secretary defense. His disagreement concerns the constitutional protection of presidential time, not the judgment or a finding that this trial already interferes with official duties.

Article II places the entire executive responsibility in one nationally elected individual. The President can delegate tasks but cannot delegate ultimate responsibility; Congress and courts can act through other members or substitutes. Significant interference with presidential performance therefore affects the Executive as an institution. Fitzgerald protects against diversion of time and energy as well as distortion of official decisions by feared liability. Harlow, Imbler, Tenney and Mitchell likewise recognize litigation burdens as relevant to effective public service. These authorities do not establish present immunity for private acts, but they support constitutional protection when a particular judicial demand threatens official performance.

The historical writings of Story, Adams, Ellsworth and Jefferson support functional protection of ongoing duties without establishing their broadest immunity assertions. Voluntary presidential testimony proves no unlimited power to compel civil-litigation demands. United States v. Nixon involved criminal evidence and individual liberty and expressly left civil-evidence balancing open. Youngstown's review helps determine executive authority itself; its burdens do not automatically settle the distraction caused by a private damages action. Breyer will not rest constitutional protection on predictions that such suits will be few, sanctions will eliminate every burden or ordinary discretion necessarily will suffice.

Once the President gives a reasoned explanation identifying a conflict between a particular judicial demand and public duties, Article II constrains scheduling. In the ordinary civil case where postponement would cause no overwhelming or enormous harm to the plaintiff, the court must avoid significant interference with those duties. Judicial power to second-guess a reasonable presidential explanation of scheduling needs is very limited. This is a constitutional requirement, not merely optional courtesy. Bare reliance on office remains insufficient, and the President retains the initial obligation to explain the need. Breyer does not decide the result if delay would cause the extraordinary plaintiff harm he identifies, or prescribe a fixed hearing, new proof standard, automatic stay or new appeal. The precise contours, possible future presumptions and consequences of an actual irreconcilable conflict remain open. His further formulation is noncontrolling.

The additional condition gives independent work to the securities nexus and avoids a general federal law of confidential information. He reserves family, friendship, government-service, ordinary-employment and commercial relationships in which the source is not participating in the transaction. Full disclosure to the source removes the theory's deception; innocent receipt and equal access do not establish it. He does not attribute his limit to Central Bank or narrow that decision's separate private-assistance rule.

Scalia concurs in the Rule 14e-3(a) holding and the mail-fraud result but dissents on §10(b). Criminal lenity prevents punishment under an insufficiently clear extension of source deception beyond deception of a party to the securities transaction. That objection does not defeat §14(e)'s express prevention authority, which supports the bounded tender-offer rule. Nor does it establish immunity from mail fraud. He joins the mail-fraud reversal and remand without adopting Thomas's entire independent analysis or the Court's securities-dependent explanation. He does not adopt every part of Thomas's agency-theory objection.

Thomas concurs in the mail-fraud result and dissents from both securities rulings. For §10(b), he regards the source fraud as complete independently of any eventual trade; the government's proposed distinctions do not supply a coherent statutory securities connection, and a court cannot replace the agency's actual theory with an improved one. For §14(e), he accepts power to adopt some preventive rules but rejects this justification and application even if misappropriation could otherwise violate §10(b). Establishing that information came from the bidder does not demonstrate why proving the relevant breach is so difficult that the rule should omit it; knowledge of source is not knowledge of breach. He does not invalidate every preventive rule, classic target-insider application or differently justified protection. On mail fraud, Carpenter independently permits prosecution of the fraudulent taking and use of confidential information as property, regardless of whether the same conduct satisfies the securities statutes. He therefore supports reversal and remand for preserved alternate objections without automatic reinstatement or a new factual finding.

## 1997-06-26 — Raines v. Byrd; No. 96-1671.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment, joined by Ginsburg.** Official injury is not categorically excluded by Article III: Coleman and authorized institutional representation prevent that shortcut. Whether this injury is personal rather than official, or concrete rather than abstract, is fairly debatable. Souter instead gives decisive weight to Allen's separation-of-powers foundation and Valley Forge's restraint against unnecessary constitutional adjudication. The Members seek judicial intervention in an unauthorized internal and interbranch contest, while a beneficiary deprived of an actual spending or tax benefit may present a conventional controversy satisfying injury, causation and redressability. These conditions make the present claim constitutionally insufficient. This is neither prudential abstention, an absolute political-question bar, nor a universal requirement that another plaintiff exist.

**Stevens, dissenting.** A Member's opportunity to vote on the law's ultimate text is an individual constitutional function. The Act's present alteration of that function is concrete without waiting for a particular cancellation; Coleman supports protection of official voting power, and Bender's prohibition on unauthorized representation does not defeat a Member's own injury. Stevens finds the claim ripe and would affirm the declaration on Article I grounds. The Act permits the President, after a bill becomes law, to cancel in whole a defined discretionary dollar amount, new-direct-spending item or limited tax benefit under its conditions. Chadha requires bicameralism and presentment for repeal. Giving cancellation a different name or intelligible standards does not turn removal of enacted legal force into execution; Field's contingent execution and ordinary impoundment do not answer the distinct spending-obligation and tax effects. His ground is allocation of lawmaking authority, not a finding that the delegation's standards are insufficiently intelligible. He adds no private presidential-reservation theory and decides no later cancellation remedy.

The cancellation permission requires determinations that the action reduces the deficit, does not impair essential Government functions and does not harm the national interest; the special message must be sent within five calendar days excluding Sundays. Cancellation takes effect upon both Houses' receipt; enacted disapproval nullifies it and restores the provision from its original effective date. A disapproval bill cannot itself be canceled. Disapproval requires legislation, and the special procedures do not abolish ordinary later legislation. The Act has no force or effect on or after January 1, 2005. None of these limitations, in Stevens's view, changes the Article I character of a repeal after enactment.

**Breyer, dissenting.** The systematic impairment of the Members' existing constitutional work is sufficiently concrete. Coleman does not limit official injury to one decisive voting event, and express congressional authorization removes a prudential barrier while reducing separation-of-powers concerns; it does not enlarge Article III. The prospect of a later private suit cannot erase present injury if that injury exists. Breyer finds standing and ripeness and would proceed to the merits. He expressly reserves the Act's constitutionality and does not join Stevens's merits conclusion or vote to sustain the Act.

## 1997-06-26 — Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union; No. 96-511.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part.** Butler, Sable and Bolger protect adult access; Ginsberg permits genuine restrictions confined to minors. The Internet presently lacks generally usable identity gates, so the display offense and mixed adult/minor conversations cannot be saved by treating the network as divided into adult and child zones. She agrees with the injunction to that extent and with preservation of independent obscenity enforcement.

She would retain the transmission provisions only where the initiating adult knows all recipients are minors, with corresponding facility liability confined to those underlying communications. ## 1997-06-26 — Vacco v. Quill; No. 95-1858; October Term 1996, chunk 13.

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion and also explains why the narrower interest of a competent person suffering greatly near imminent death need not be decided. New York permits qualified physicians to relieve pain, even with medication producing unconsciousness and a foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, protection against mistaken judgments of competence, terminal condition and voluntariness sufficiently supports the prohibition even if that narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards. She does not find that pain care is universally delivered or invariably successful, require legislative exhaustion, or adopt a freestanding constitutional pain-treatment standard. Ginsburg substantially adopts these separate reasons. Breyer joins them except insofar as the concurrence joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** He accepts a sufficient basis for the general distinction but does not join the Court's ordinary-review and causation/intent account in full. A patient withdrawing treatment and one requesting lethal medication may share an intention to hasten impending death; physicians may seek relief of suffering in both settings. Terminal sedation further complicates a universal distinction. His Cruzan dissent treats dignity and the manner of unavoidable death as serious individual interests; Casey's protected reproductive choice supplies a bounded analogy requiring attention to real burdens and clinical circumstances. Neither creates an unrestricted autonomy rule or an assisted-death right. Protective concerns sustain a general prohibition while potentially carrying different weight in an exceptional competent patient's well-supported case. He finds no such entitlement established here and orders no hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** The important patient and physician interests require justification commensurate with their weight, rather than a merely formal same-treatment syllogism. Harlan's Poe analysis, bodily-integrity principles and the defined reproductive and medical-privacy interests support that substantive inquiry. Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and euthanasia supplies the sufficient ground: reliably identifying responsible voluntary choice and containing a legalized lethal practice present serious unresolved problems. Palliation and treatment refusal differ in purpose, bodily intrusion and the likelihood of implementing a responsible patient's choice, though both can shorten life. Those differences and protective concerns justify this line. He reaches that conclusion through the stated substantive inquiry and does not join the Court's ordinary rational-basis method.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons concerning the reserved imminent-death and suffering interest and the laws' permission for palliation. She does not join the principal opinion, a declaration validating every future application, or every word of O'Connor's principal-opinion joins.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** He agrees that the identified protective interests justify the distinction, while describing the potential liberty as the combination of control over the manner of death, willing professional assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. He does not decide whether that interest is fundamental. The law permits the pain treatment central to it despite foreseeable death; failures in delivery and the possibility of sedation do not establish a legal prohibition. He does not assert that every person's suffering can be relieved. A law forbidding necessary palliation would raise a different question. These reasons accompany his qualified O'Connor join and do not adopt the principal opinion or deny every end-of-life liberty.

## 1997-06-26 — Washington v. Glucksberg; No. 96-110; October Term 1996, chunk 13.

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion but does not decide whether a competent person facing imminent death and great suffering has a cognizable interest in controlling those circumstances. Washington permits qualified physicians to alleviate suffering with medication even to unconsciousness and despite foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, the risks of erroneous judgments about terminal condition, competence and voluntariness sufficiently support the prohibition even if the narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards and democratic consideration. This neither guarantees effective delivery of pain care, eliminates suffering associated with sedation, requires legislative exhaustion nor creates a freestanding constitutional pain-care standard. Ginsburg adopts substantially these separate reasons. Breyer joins the separate analysis except insofar as it joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** No person has complete autonomy to end life regardless of others' interests, and the State may maintain a general prohibition. That conclusion does not decide a competent individual's well-supported claim concerning the manner of an unavoidable imminent death. His Cruzan dissent and Casey's defined protected choice support attention to dignity, intolerable suffering and genuine justification. Preservation of life does not automatically outweigh every such interest. Mistake, coercion and professional-integrity concerns justify the general rule but may carry different weight in an exceptional case. Palliation does not logically eliminate every form of suffering or the distinction between conscious relief and terminal sedation. He also questions whether intent and causation invariably distinguish treatment withdrawal, sedation and lethal prescriptions; those overlaps do not establish this class-wide entitlement. He finds no individual entitlement proved here and orders no additional hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** Harlan's Poe analysis asks whether the restraint is arbitrary or purposeless in light of the relative importance and fit of the individual and state interests. Traditions retained and abandoned inform that inquiry; neither longstanding prohibition alone nor an abstract autonomy formula resolves it. Responsible terminally ill patients seeking medical counsel and assistance present an important interest connected to bodily integrity, treatment refusal and defined reproductive choice. Casey's complete framework is not a universal medical test. He accepts the competent and voluntary premises and provisionally accepts the professional premises for this claim without inventing incapacity or misconduct.

Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and expansion into euthanasia supplies the sufficient justification. Family pressure alone would not settle the matter, because proposed physician screening is intended to answer it. The harder problem is reliably assessing a knowing, responsible decision and ensuring that physicians preserve the asserted boundaries. The disputed evidence concerning safeguards and the Dutch experience establishes a serious empirical uncertainty, not an incontrovertible foreign-law conclusion. Courts cannot presently displace the State's protective judgment with adequate assurance; legislatures can investigate and develop experience.

His conclusion rejects this entitlement now without permanently foreclosing recognition, demanding legislative experiments, imposing a judicial moratorium or assuming inevitable logical expansion of every protected right. Legislative foot-dragging and a materially different individual record remain for another case. Incidental difficulty does not automatically require compelling justification, and not every liberty claim demands elaborate balancing: the importance of the interest and the burden determine the inquiry. His view does not become the Court's general standard.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons about the unresolved imminent-death and suffering interest and the legal availability of palliation. She does not join the principal opinion or a declaration sustaining every future application. Her agreement creates no additional independent theory.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** The potential liberty combines personal control over the manner of death, willing medical assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. Describing it merely as any right to suicide misses the strongest claim without establishing that claim as fundamental. Poe, Cruzan and the defined personal-liberty authorities support serious consideration. Because the law does not forbid the pain treatment central to that interest, the present case need not decide its status; the stated protective interests suffice here. Delivery failures are not the same as a legal prohibition. He recognizes sedation and does not find every person's suffering treatable. A prohibition of necessary palliation would present a materially different case. These reasons supplement his qualified O'Connor join; they do not adopt the principal opinion or one universal liberty method for all judgment supporters.

## 1997-06-27 — Pounders v. Watson; No. 96-1383.

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** Stone would affirm habeas relief on a narrower due-process ground. He accepts the findings that Watson knew the order and deliberately violated it, preserves the underlying trial order, and takes the judge's assessment of serious jury prejudice seriously. Watson explained herself at the bench and again through counsel; his objection is not lack of every warning or opportunity to speak. A single event may warrant contempt, without repeated or pervasive misconduct.

He would require a particularized connection between summary adjudication and protection of an ongoing proceeding or authority that cannot practicably await ordinary process. Observation establishes what the judge saw, but does not alone show why an ordinary hearing cannot reliably adjudicate punishment. Here the questioning ended, curative trial-management measures were available, the trial continued for roughly two weeks without another violation and confinement was deferred. Summary adjudication could not undo the completed prejudice. Taken together, those circumstances fail to establish the need to dispense with ordinary criminal-contempt protections.

Terry and Oliver preserve an exceptional power tied to judicial protection. McConnell requires no more power than the occasion warrants. Wilson concerned refusal of immunized testimony needed for an ongoing trial, which he distinguishes from completed questioning. Sacher permits deferred punishment where summary process is otherwise justified; delay is therefore not independently dispositive. To the extent those authorities permit this summary conviction without the additional necessity showing, Stone would narrow them. His proposed boundary rests on notice and an effective opportunity to defend while preserving immediate action against violence, refusal, ongoing obstruction and comparably urgent threats. A preference for procedural speed supplies no concrete reliance sufficient to defeat that protection, and the proposal does not itself reopen completed contempt judgments.

Stone agrees that the former habeas statute applies. He would not initiate an omitted nonjurisdictional Teague defense under Schiro merely to defeat this claim, and preservation is not established here. If a preserved and fairly included defense were properly available, Caspari would require examining the law at finality: an additional necessity rule not dictated by the earlier cases would be procedural, would create no substantive immunity from contempt and would not qualify as a watershed accuracy rule. If a preserved and fairly included bar applies to his proposed new rule, he would reject relief on that ground, without approving every feature of the summary contempt. No such bar is decided on this record. He would leave California free to pursue ordinary contempt proceedings with adequate notice and opportunity to defend if state law permits, and require another judge only if the governing neutrality or embroilment rule requires one.

**Stevens, dissenting from summary disposition, joined by Breyer.** They would receive full briefing and argument before deciding the merits. Assuming for this procedural objection that Watson defied an order and committed contempt, they question whether summary adjudication is so clearly proper that the habeas judgment should be reversed without plenary consideration. Two questions in a long trial, subsequent continuation and punishment directed at completed conduct raise substantial issues concerning necessity and ordinary criminal safeguards.

McConnell's attention to actual interference and the concerns expressed in Frankfurter's Sacher dissent warrant fuller consideration, including the substantial question whether a disinterested judge should hear a charge in these circumstances. The dissent in Sacher supplies their argument, not a controlling rule displacing its majority. They acknowledge the immediate warning, Wilson's recognition of nonphysical obstruction and Sacher's permission to defer punishment. They impose no minimum number of violations, find no judicial bias and make no final determination that due process was violated. Their requested plenary review is not a vote to affirm habeas relief or a direction to conduct a new contempt prosecution.

## 1997-06-27 — Printz v. United States; Mack v. United States; Nos. 95-1478 and 95-1503; OT1996, chunk 13.

**Souter, Part II of the opinion announcing the judgment.** His reason for remand is that appropriate executive assistance cannot, in his view, mean compulsory administrative support without an obligation to pay fair value. Federalist Nos. 27 and 44 describe an auxiliary executive role and the force of the constitutional oath; Nos. 36 and 45 reinforce the execution examples. They support properly directed assistance under an enacted command within the officer's branch function, not an automatic duty created by an oath alone. New York continues to protect legislative choice. Early administrative examples alone would not establish his conclusion.

The officers' assertions that their budgets make no provision for this work and that they face unauthorized-expenditure liability therefore require development and consideration. He regards that issue as a condition on sustaining the existing compulsory arrangement, not as a reason to invent a damages action. He finds no compensation amount, taking, liability, pleaded money cause or payment defendant established and orders no appropriation. The Court leaves the fiscal question open, permitting his stronger view to be considered without adopting it. No other Justice joins that affirmative fair-value rationale.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment and dissenting from Part I; Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join the structural and conditional-judgment grounds.** New York's accountability principle cannot be avoided by addressing state executive officers instead of legislatures. The Brady inquiry and its implicit required acceptance of forms allocate federal implementation to unwilling state offices. Reasonable effort concerns the amount of compelled work, not the State's choice whether to undertake it. A valid commercial object and an assumed promise of reimbursement do not cure that structural defect. Garcia and Baker regulate state activity; Testa and FERC retain distinct adjudicative or cooperative settings; Voting Rights Coalition rests on constitutional election sources absent here.

They would reverse as to mandatory checking and implicit acceptance. They would not declare the destruction and requested-reasons clauses unconstitutional, adjudicate genuinely voluntary or separately triggered applications, or give officers a general license to retain or misuse information. General nonparticipation is not proof that a particular form or request never existed. They would reserve absent dealers' transmission and waiting questions, rather than declare the entire remainder severed and valid.

Part I nevertheless supplies the law governing these cases. Under that rule the actual fiscal objections remain unresolved, and leaving unconditional appellate approval intact would foreclose a real part of the controversy. They therefore accept this confined vacatur and remand while maintaining their preferred structural reversal. They join no compensation entitlement, fiscal cure, source-and-function rationale, state-law veto or search for hypothetical new claims. Their agreement with the mandate does not erase their substantive disagreement.

O'Connor adds, without the others' joins to this additional qualification, that voluntary participation and federally funded contractual cooperation remain available, and that purely ministerial reporting requirements are not resolved by the compelled investigation at issue. That reservation creates no exemption for every task described as ministerial.

**Stevens, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the judgment; Ginsburg and Breyer join.** They would affirm the confined interim duties and their conditional safeguards. Congress directly governs commercial handgun transfers, retains the substantive policy and coercive consequences, and requires temporary, resource-sensitive information assistance. The scheme requires no state legislation, assumption of private property and liability, independent enforcement regime, arrest or prosecution. New York's contrary setting remains binding; supremacy and oath obligations, Garcia, Baker and the cooperation recognized in FERC and Testa support appropriate assistance within an established federal power. Election duties retain their own sources.

The budget and state-law assertions deserve an answer, but do not establish a constitutional payment condition or justify returning this constitutional judgment. The duty's actual functions and the responsibilities left to the State supply their answer; they do not presume free implementation or nonexistent exposure. Reasonable effort can account for resources without becoming optional whenever a budget lacks a line item. Their affirmance decides no state-law liability, taking or every possible fiscal claim. It requires no severance and provides no general permission for permanent state conscription.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the judgment.** He would affirm both judgments and deny the requested injunction against the confined duties. Congress may require temporary, narrowly bounded assistance using lawfully available government records as an incident of otherwise valid private-transaction regulation, with federal substantive rules and federal responsibility for coercive enforcement. The mandatory eligibility assessment is real; calling supporting tasks ministerial does not eliminate it or its cost.

He reads reasonable effort in the whole interim scheme, its available-record tasks and the absence of a command to stop transfers or arrest purchasers. Including does not itself mean only. Ordinary checking and interpretation remain required; the construction adds no commerce element or administrative power and does not excuse all inquiry. He would authorize no compulsory independent field investigation, permanent state enforcement organization or transfer of general federal regulatory responsibility. The conditional explanations, destruction and information protections retain their separate statutory predicates; adverse dealer notification remains discretionary.

His position narrows his earlier categorical objection to compelled executive administration. The reason for that refinement is the difference between incidental records work and independent regulatory or coercive responsibility, not convenience or a claim that state work is costless. New York's compulsory-legislation prohibition, Lopez's source and no-rewriting limits, and the distinct election, state-court, preemption and voluntary-cooperation rules remain. His position creates no permanent duty, retroactive officer liability or reopening of a completed judgment. The asserted expenditures do not change his conclusion on these limited duties, and no severance or further fiscal proceeding is required. He leaves materially different commands and unpresented applications for another case.

**Breyer, additional opinion concurring in Part I and dissenting from the judgment.** Dividing execution between federal and state officials can preserve state autonomy and avoid expansion of a centralized federal bureaucracy. Federalism alone does not establish that separate federal implementation always protects liberty better. Comparative federal arrangements can illustrate that possibility, but are neither American constitutional authority nor proof that a foreign system can be transplanted here. His sufficient domestic grounds remain valid commerce power, necessary implementation, reasonable effort and the State's retained responsibilities. This supplements his full join in Stevens's opinion; it creates no general cost-benefit override, cheap-command exception or compensation cure.

## 1997-09-08 — Faragher v. City of Boca Raton; Eleventh Circuit No. 94-4878; no separate Supreme Court docket supplied; OT1996, chunk 13.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part; no other Justice joins.** He joins Part I but would vacate for a limited agency determination.
