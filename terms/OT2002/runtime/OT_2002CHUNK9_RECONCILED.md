## 104. Massaro v. United States — April 23, 2003

### Components and common operative conclusions

**M1 — first collateral presentation.** Failure to raise ineffective assistance of trial counsel on direct appeal does not bar a first presentation of that claim under section 2255, whether or not the defendant had new appellate counsel and whether or not the trial record appeared sufficient to present the claim. This permission does not require every such claim to await collateral proceedings. A genuinely suitable claim may be adjudicated on direct appeal.

**M2 — adjudication and hearing.** Reverse the procedural-default judgment and remand for ordinary section 2255 adjudication. The district court must assess deficient performance and a reasonable probability of a different lawful outcome under Strickland. The statutory hearing rule remains: a hearing is required unless the motion and the files and records of the case conclusively show that the prisoner is entitled to no relief. A hearing is not automatic merely because an assistance claim is alleged; the district court applies that rule to the actual allegations and available record. No finding of ineffectiveness, bullet inadmissibility, new-trial entitlement or actual prejudice is made here.

**M3 — boundaries.** This ruling removes the asserted direct-appeal prerequisite for the assistance claim. It does not excuse a late or unauthorized successive motion, reopen a claim actually adjudicated on direct appeal, or decide that adjudication's preclusive consequences. The underlying evidentiary appeal and the counsel claim remain distinct. Section 2254's state-exhaustion problem is not imported into this federal motion.

The sufficient basis is the fit between the claim and its forum. A trial transcript records counsel's acts but commonly fails to disclose reasons, investigation and alternatives; apparent record sufficiency does not establish that further facts are immaterial. A new-counsel/record-only exception makes collateral access depend on an avoidable preliminary dispute over what a different record might have shown. Permitting initial factual development in the sentencing court protects accurate resolution without reducing Strickland's substantive burden. Frady retains ordinary collateral-default rules for trial claims; Edwards retains a separately established adequate state default on an assistance claim offered as cause; Bousley retains its plea-intelligence default. None resolves this distinct category of first federal trial-counsel review.

## 105. City of New York v. Patrolmen's Benevolent Association — April 23, 2003

### Components and common operative conclusions

**P1 — governing constitutional standard.** These admitted racial employment assignments are governed by the common substantial-justification standard, not categorical strict scrutiny. Government must establish an important lawful operational objective, a substantial evidence-supported relationship between the actual assignments and that objective, and justification for coverage and actual officer burdens, including a response to concrete practicable alternatives with comparable operational value and materially less racial disadvantage. Supported preservation of effective policing and public safety may qualify; racial identity is not itself proof of language ability, professional skill or community trust. Completed violence, a commissioned study, a prior liability judgment, strict necessity and universal least-restrictive means are not prerequisites.

**P2 — preserved judgment-as-a-matter-of-law claim.** Affirm denial of judgment for the City. Viewed with the support owed to the officers' evidence, the record permits rejection of the City's asserted substantial connection or burden justification even under the correct standard. Evidence of permanent compulsory assignments, ordinary implementation over weeks, workplace mistrust, reactions adverse to the newly assigned officers, and concrete alternatives concerning volunteers, actual language skills and training defeats an entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. ## 111. Illinois ex rel. Madigan v. Telemarketing Associates, Inc.

**Event:** May 5, 2003; argued March 3, 2003; review of the Illinois Supreme Court's constitutional dismissal of the fraud litigation.

**Associate disposition:** All eight Associates reverse and remand the fraud dismissal. The First Amendment permits adjudication of the pleaded deliberate affirmative deception. It does not permit a fraud judgment resting on the professional fee percentage or failure to volunteer that percentage alone. The stipulated disposition of other claims remains effective.

### Common sufficient propositions and application

**M1 — Deliberate factual deception.** A professional fundraiser may be held to the otherwise applicable antifraud law for knowingly making a materially false factual representation about how donated funds will be used in order to mislead the donor. The State must establish the representation's actual meaning in context, material falsity, the required intentional deception and the other elements and remedial predicates of the particular cause it invokes. A solicitation's connection to protected charitable advocacy does not immunize that deliberate deception.

**M2 — The percentage/disclosure boundary.** A high fundraising fee does not itself establish fraud, and protected solicitation does not carry a general obligation to announce that fee. The fee and disposition of receipts may be relevant evidence of the falsity of an independently established representation, but may not substitute for the representation, deception or culpability. A general statement that donations help a charity does not become materially false solely because only fifteen percent reaches the charity.

**M3 — The pleading and remedy application.** Allegations that donors were told at least ninety percent would go to veterans or that contributions would pay no labor expenses because all workers were volunteers identify factual representations independently testable against the alleged arrangements. Those allegations support allowing the fraud litigation to proceed under M1–M2. Illinois must establish the actual claims under the applicable state law; a private common-law reliance element must not be transplanted indiscriminately into a distinct public enforcement cause. Ordinary proceedings determine the meaning, truth, state of mind, causal predicates and available relief.

**Proof precision shared by all eight.** For common-law fraud, Illinois requires clear and convincing proof: Hofmann v. Hofmann, 94 Ill. 2d 205, 222–225 (1983). In re Witt, 145 Ill. 2d 380, 390–391, 397 (1991), identifies material factual falsity, knowledge and purpose to induce reliance, justifiable reliance and resulting damage, and distinguishes actual intent to deceive from misconduct that violates a different duty. Those state-law requirements support the deliberate-fraud route restored here. Witt's separate disciplinary burden under Rule 753(c)(6) does not establish the burden for every statutory consumer-protection action. The Court's disposition does not equate the elements of distinct public statutory causes with the common-law elements or announce clear-and-convincing evidence as a universal new First Amendment requirement. Any surviving statutory theory must independently comply with M1–M2 and its actual state-law requirements; the lower court applies those requirements on remand. This qualification is an express join condition because the complaint invokes more than one cause.

Schaumburg, Munson and Riley protect charitable solicitation from percentage-based exclusions and compulsory fee-disclosure devices while leaving genuine antifraud enforcement available. Edenfield preserves the distinction between truthful solicitation and deception; it does not reclassify charitable advocacy as ordinary commercial advertising. Black's requirement that liability rest on the actual unprotected feature supports the distinction by analogy; its criminal threat elements and burden are not civil-fraud requirements. Scheidler's acquisition requirement governs extortion and supplies no charitable-fraud immunity.

## 112. Kaupp v. Texas

**Event:** May 5, 2003; submitted on petition papers without oral argument; direct review of the Texas conviction judgment.

**Associate disposition:** All eight Associates vacate and remand. The removal was an arrest, concededly unsupported by probable cause. The resulting statements are inadmissible on the supplied existing record because Texas has not carried its attenuation burden. The state courts must apply that exclusion ruling and ordinary preserved harmless-error and further-proceedings law to the conviction.

### Common sufficient propositions and application

**K1 — Arrest and consent.** Officers arrested Kaupp when the coordinated nighttime encounter became physical custody: after awakening the seventeen-year-old at about three in the morning, they handcuffed and transported him from his bedroom in underwear and without shoes for station-house questioning. In the whole communicated encounter, his response of agreement to the officers' announcement and his cooperation were submission to authority, not voluntary consent to that custodial removal. Texas disclaims probable cause, so the arrest was unlawful. The father's permission to enter is not the son's consent to arrest.

**K2 — Attenuation.** Miranda warnings and Fifth Amendment voluntariness do not by themselves break the causal connection between an unlawful arrest and a statement. Brown and Dunaway require consideration of temporal proximity, genuine intervening circumstances, the purpose and flagrancy of the unlawful conduct, and warnings, with the State bearing the attenuation burden. Here continuous custody, the brief crime-scene stop and prompt interrogation implement the purpose of the unsupported seizure; removing handcuffs and giving warnings supply no meaningful intervening event. Texas's asserted consent and customary use of handcuffs do not carry that burden. The statements are fruits of the arrest on this record.

**K3 — Disposition.** Vacatur removes the judgment sustained by treating the prestatement custody as consensual. Further proceedings address the conviction under the exclusion ruling, including any properly preserved harmless-error question. The mandate does not authorize a new evidentiary opportunity to invent an intervening event absent from the supplied record.

Mendenhall and Bostick supply the objective whole-encounter inquiry. Dunaway and Hayes distinguish supported limited investigation from involuntary station-house detention. Brown and Robinette distinguish voluntary conduct from causal independence. Ornelas and the common Wheat rule preserve supported historical facts while requiring an independent constitutional assessment; police custom cannot turn compulsory custody into consent. Dickerson's warning safeguard does not replace the separate Fourth Amendment inquiry.

## 113. Rene v. MGM Grand Hotel, Inc.

**Event:** May 5, 2003; additional merits review on the preserved sex-harassment claim from the Ninth Circuit's en banc reversal of employer summary judgment.

**Common legal commitments of all eight Associates:** Oncale supplies same-sex coverage and an independent because-of-sex requirement. Sexual contact or severe abuse does not conclusively establish that requirement. A victim's description of the attackers' motive as hostility toward homosexuality does not itself abandon a pleaded sex-harassment claim or establish a controlling legal classification. An actual female coworker, sexual desire and any particular orientation are not required proof channels. The dispute is the inference supported by this record under Rule 56.

## 115. Harry v. Marchant

**Event:** May 19, 2003; additional merits review of the hospital stabilization dismissal.

**Associate disposition:** All eight Associates reverse the stabilization dismissal and remand. In re Baby K already forecloses a categorical rule that section 1395dd(b) imposes no duty unless the hospital actually transfers or discharges the patient. Normil's alleged emergency-department delay must be evaluated under the enacted duty and ordinary claim requirements.

### Common sufficient propositions and application

**H1 — The statutory trigger.** Once a participating hospital determines that an arriving person has an emergency medical condition, section 1395dd(b)(1) requires available examination and treatment necessary for stabilization or a transfer satisfying subsection (c). The transfer-related definition of stabilization measures the required protection against likely material deterioration within reasonable medical probability; it does not add actual movement outside the hospital as an additional trigger. Baby K's rejection of a no-intended-transfer exemption governs this question.

**H2 — Application and the inpatient distinction.** The pleaded delay before ICU admission presents the duty while Normil remained in emergency treatment. Subsequent admission cannot retroactively erase a violation completed during that earlier phase. What the hospital determined, when it did so, which stabilizing measures were available, whether any delay breached that duty and what harm the violation caused are matters for ordinary adjudication under H1. Moving the patient to an ICU within the same hospital is not a statutory transfer. Neither the diagnostic notation nor death is treated as proof of every element.

**H3 — Remedy and retained conditions.** Remand the stabilization claim against the participating hospital for application of H1–H2, with Roberts's rejection of an additional improper-motive requirement and its direct-harm, causation, State personal-injury-damages, equitable-relief and two-year requirements. The separate screening disposition and section 1981 remand retain their existing status. State medical law remains effective except insofar as it directly conflicts with an applicable EMTALA duty.

The hospital's request for an actual-transfer prerequisite is rejected in full. A general endpoint for genuinely admitted inpatients depends on a different phase and facts not adjudicated in this pleading dismissal; the preadmission allegations independently require reversal under the settled rule. On remand, the court must separate any alleged preadmission violation from a claim resting solely on later inpatient care. Baby K supplies no indefinite post-stabilization duty. No later regulatory inpatient rule enters this decision.

The existing statutory alternatives retain their separate terms. Treatment refusal requires an offer of the necessary care, an explanation of risks and benefits, refusal and all reasonable efforts to obtain written informed refusal. Transfer refusal separately requires an offered subsection-(c)-compliant transfer, risk-and-benefit information, refusal and the same reasonable written-refusal efforts. An unstabilized transfer requires the informed written patient or representative request, or the qualified signed risk-benefit medical certification, together with every appropriate-transfer requirement: available risk-minimizing treatment; receiving space, qualified staff, acceptance and care; the available emergency records and required request/certification and on-call information; qualified transport and necessary life support; and applicable additional regulatory conditions. When no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a qualified medical person's certification requires consultation, the physician's required determination and subsequent countersignature; every certification summarizes risks and benefits. Transfer is a permitted alternative, not a requirement to move all patients. No alleged refusal or transfer is treated as satisfying these conditions.

## 116. Roe II v. Aware Woman Center for Choice, Inc.

**Event:** June 2, 2003; additional merits review of the Eleventh Circuit judgment, 253 F.3d 678, on the clinic defendants' retained petition. The assigned questions concern FACE's application to clinic personnel and public pseudonymity. No cross-petition seeking better pleading relief for Roe is supplied.

**Associate disposition:** All eight Associates affirm the reviewed judgment: the FACE complaint receives the directed dismissal with a reasonable opportunity to amend, and Roe may proceed publicly by pseudonym with disclosure of her identity as necessary to the court and defense. The statutory ruling rejects a clinic-personnel exemption but preserves FACE's actual service-related purpose and intentional-conduct predicates. The anonymity ruling is a confined procedural determination on the intimate medical subject and the offered defense disclosure.

### Component P1 — Clinic personnel and the statute's actual coverage

**Common rule.** FACE can reach clinic personnel who use force, threats of force or physical obstruction intentionally to injure, intimidate or interfere with a person, or attempt such conduct, because she is or has been obtaining or providing reproductive health services, or with the statutory purpose of intimidating her, another person or a class from obtaining or providing those services. The actor's occupation and presence inside the treating clinic create no categorical exemption. Reproductive health services include medical, surgical, counseling and referral care concerning reproduction, pregnancy, termination and carrying pregnancy to term; related hospital treatment is not excluded merely because it would occur away from the defendant clinic.

The statutory act, intent and service-related causal or intimidating purpose remain distinct. An allegation of unsuccessful treatment, a medical error, an unwanted touch or restraint is not by itself an allegation or proof of every FACE element. Conversely, calling a restraint medical care does not establish that it lacked the enacted purpose. The actual reason for the alleged restraint is adjudicated under those elements. American Life League supplies these boundaries directly; NOW v. Scheidler's refusal to insert an absent economic motive does not permit deletion of FACE's express service nexus.

Roe alleges forcible restraint after she demanded that the abortion stop and an ambulance take her elsewhere. Seeking hospital treatment for the alleged reproductive-treatment complication can satisfy the services category, and physically preventing that access can satisfy interference if the remaining intent and statutory purpose are present. On the supplied pleading judgment, amendment must give fair notice of the connection between defendants' intentional acts and her obtaining or seeking the alternative reproductive care. The existing allegations and serious claimed injuries are not liability findings. Swierkiewicz and Leatherman prohibit an invented heightened evidentiary pleading requirement while preserving the claim's substantive elements. The absence of a cross-petition bars converting defendants' petition into an award of better pleading relief to Roe.

**Protective-motive qualification.** If an actor's sole actual purpose is protective medical action and the required service-access purpose or causation is absent, FACE liability fails. The reported concession supplies no factual finding that this was the defendants' actual motive. A benevolent label is not an independent immunity: if the defendant actually acted with the statutory intent and reproductive-service nexus, a claim does not fail solely because the defendant also asserts a protective objective. The packet does not require a comprehensive mixed-motive burden-shifting framework, and none is added.

**Private action and attribution.** Roe must satisfy the private-action condition of obtaining or seeking services in a reproductive-health facility and establish each defendant's legally attributable violation. American Life League's protected-expression boundaries and the exclusive-own-minor parent/guardian exception remain governing: the latter excludes every FACE penalty and civil remedy for described activity directed exclusively at the actor's own minor. No fact here invokes that exception. The statutory election of $5,000 per attributable violation remains permissive before final judgment in place of actual compensatory damages, subject to the actual private-action and liability conditions; no monetary entitlement follows from the pleading ruling.

### Component P2 — Public pseudonymity and defense fairness

**Common procedural rule and application.** The ordinary rule requires parties to be named publicly. A court may allow a pseudonym when a substantial privacy interest in the intimate subject of the litigation outweighs the need for public identification in the particular case, provided fitted disclosure preserves fair adjudication and the defense's ability to investigate and answer. Rules 10(a) and 26(c), applied with the court's authority to protect sensitive litigation information, permit that exceptional administration; embarrassment alone is not enough.

This suit necessarily reveals an abortion and detailed allegations about injury to reproductive organs and internal treatment. Roe offers to disclose her actual identity to the defendants, separating public identification from the information needed to defend. The record therefore supplies both an unusually intimate privacy interest and an available protection for defense fairness. Public pseudonymity is appropriate; the court may require necessary confidential identification, controlled discovery and other case-specific disclosure as defense needs actually require. The proceedings and legal grounds remain public subject to properly fitted protection of the identifying information. This is neither blanket closure nor concealment of identity from the opposing parties.
