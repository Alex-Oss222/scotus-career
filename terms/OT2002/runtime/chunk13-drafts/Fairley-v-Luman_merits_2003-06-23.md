## Holdings

#### Part I. Prolonged detention requires a meaningful response to a serious identity error

**Holding and operative rule:** Due process requires reasonable, timely identity verification during prolonged detention when repeated specific protests, readily accessible reliable checks and modest checking burdens demonstrate a serious avoidable risk that the wrong person remains confined. The twelve-day detention on Fairley's twin's warrants, despite the supported identity dispute and accessible conclusive checks, permits the jury's finding that the available procedure was inadequate.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews weighs the actual liberty deprivation, the risk of error and useful safeguards, and the government's administrative demands. Gilbert distinguishes lawful initial action from the justification for its continuation. Fairley's repeated protests concerned identity, and the evidence included different license numbers, a substantial weight difference and readily comparable fingerprints of both brothers. The City conceded the ease of that comparison. Using those available checks could have resolved a grave, ongoing loss of liberty at modest cost. The jury could find a violation on that evidence without the Court making new facts.

Baker rejects an automatic constitutional claim from every mistaken-warrant arrest or brief detention. It does not authorize twelve days of inaction despite this concrete, readily verifiable problem. The controlling rule turns on the duration, reliable notice of error, practical checking opportunity and actual burden together. It requires a meaningful response to the supported identity question, not unquestioning acceptance of every protest.

#### Part II. A distinct policy-caused injury can support municipal liability

**Holding and operative rule:** A municipality is liable for a proved constitutional deprivation caused by its own deliberately indifferent policy or effective custom, including an identified omission concerning a known or sufficiently obvious recurring constitutional need. Employee verdicts rejecting different constitutional injuries do not defeat that claim; the plaintiff must separately establish the municipal deficiency, required notice and fault, and actual cause.

**Controlling explanation:** Monell makes the City's own policy the source of liability. Collins requires proof of the underlying federal injury before attribution, and Carter requires an identified culpable omission, notice and causation. Gibson preserves those requirements while rejecting an automatic inference from employees' lack of personal liability. The evidence here met each distinct inquiry: ordinary booking practices were followed, the internal investigation confirmed that compliance, and the chief acknowledged that mistaken-warrant arrests recur and twins pose a particular identification problem. Together with the unused reliable checks and continued confinement, this evidence supports deliberate municipal omission and actual cause. It is not an aggregation of unrelated employee negligence.

Heller forecloses municipal responsibility for an injury the factfinder determined did not occur. The individual verdicts concerned arrest and excessive force. They did not decide whether the City's practices caused the separately proved continued-detention wrong. Preserving those verdicts therefore remains consistent with sustaining the City's liability for this different injury. A municipality has no qualified-immunity defense to its own established violation.

#### Part III. The record supports the causal period and existing awards

**Holding and operative rule:** A transfer to another custodian does not automatically interrupt the causal effects of an uncorrected municipal identification policy; liability extends to actual compensable loss proved to result from that policy. The supported finding that this defect continued through the four-day County interval sustains the $11,250 verdict, and the preserved challenges establish no ground to disturb the $92,211.83 attorney-fee award.

**Controlling explanation:** Reeves requires review of the whole record while preserving reasonable jury inferences and avoiding appellate credibility choices. The record permits the jury to find that the unresolved identity mistake foreseeably continued after transfer and caused the entire compensable confinement. That conclusion rests on the continuing defect, not on a rule assigning all subsequent custodial conduct to the City. Carey requires actual injury rather than an assigned price for an abstract constitutional right; the proved loss of liberty supplies the compensable harm here. The evidence defeats the request for judgment as a matter of law, and the separate abuse-of-discretion standard supplies no reason to disturb denial of a new trial. The sustained municipal judgment supports the dependent fee award under §1988. Discretionary fee determinations receive abuse-of-discretion review, while governing legal rules are reviewed independently.

## Precedent Treatment

Mathews is applied to the risk, cost and value of identity verification during continuing confinement. Gilbert supplies the distinction between initial justification and continuing deprivation. Baker is distinguished by the materially prolonged and readily correctable identity error. Monell, Collins and Carter retain their separate constitutional-injury, municipal-policy, fault and causation requirements. Gibson is applied to a distinct policy injury despite personal exoneration. Heller remains controlling when the relevant constitutional injury did not occur; it does not convert verdicts on different claims into that finding. Reeves governs verdict review, and Carey preserves compensation for actual injury rather than the abstract value of a right.

## Law After Decision

Courts must assess continued wrong-person confinement under the actual verification opportunity, risk, duration and burden, separately from the initial arrest. Municipal liability remains tied to a proved deliberate policy defect causing that injury, even when employees prevail on different claims. A later transfer requires an actual causation inquiry. These established doctrines sustain the present monetary judgment and fee award; they do not depend on the identity of the later custodian alone.
