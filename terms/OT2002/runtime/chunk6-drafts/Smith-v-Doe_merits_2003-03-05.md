## Controlling holdings

### The governing inquiry considers both intended character and actual combined operation

**Holding and operative rule:** A court first determines whether the legislature intended a civil or criminal measure. An established civil design may be overcome only by the clearest proof that the challenged measure's purpose or effects are punitive, assessed cumulatively through its supported design and actual operation; no civil label or single punitive indicator is conclusive.

**Authority:** Part I of __LEAD_AUTHOR__'s opinion, joined by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common method controls; Part II applies it for the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Hudson's sequential intent-and-effects inquiry and Seling's requirement to examine the operation actually challenged. Mendoza-Martinez supplies relevant considerations within that inquiry: restraint or disability, historical punishment analogues, the role of scienter, retribution and deterrence, connection to conduct already criminal, an alternative protective purpose and excess in relation to that purpose. They are considerations to assess together, not separate burdens or individually decisive conditions.

Hendricks shows that a civil designation can be overcome by cumulative punitive operation; its custody facts are not facts about Alaska's registry. W.P. sustained notification on the supported combination of risk grading, individual exceptions and likely-encounter recipients. That decision neither supplies missing Alaska safeguards nor disposes of a materially different scheme by analogy alone. Alaska's continuing duties, duration, publication and protective design must be assessed together with contrary evidence, including the absence of direct residence, employment or movement prohibitions. Reporting is not quarterly physical appearance, and public Internet search access is not compelled viewing.

The parties' competing analogies therefore require an examination of the State's own measure, rather than a conclusion from its title, the availability of old conviction records or an assumed private reaction. Paul P. governs a distinct limited residential-information privacy interest. A possible defect under that doctrine does not itself establish punishment under the Ex Post Facto Clause.

### Alaska's retrospective scheme is punitive as applied to pre-enactment offenders

**Holding and operative rule:** A legislature's designation of a conviction-based regulatory measure as civil is relevant but not conclusive for Ex Post Facto purposes. Courts must assess the enacted design and the measure's cumulative operation. Where a measure imposes continuing affirmative obligations and public-notification consequences exclusively by reason of an earlier criminal conviction, its duration, restrictive operation, historical punishment analogues and relationship to a genuinely prospective regulatory function must be considered together. A legitimate public-safety purpose does not by itself resolve punitive classification, because punishment may also serve deterrence and protection. Alaska's enacted design does not establish a civil choice that triggers Part I's heightened override, so its purposes and effects are examined directly, with the burden on respondents. Alaska's challenged retrospective system combines compulsory long-term or lifelong reporting with broad public identification, imposed by reason of prior convictions and without an ordinary mechanism for ending those obligations upon demonstrated rehabilitation or materially changed circumstances. Considered together, these features amount to additional punitive consequences when imposed upon persons whose covered offenses preceded enactment. The Ex Post Facto Clause prohibits that retrospective application. The State remains free to establish lawful prospective registration requirements, publish conviction information through independently lawful means, and impose justified punishment as part of the law governing future offenses.

**Authority:** Part II of __LEAD_AUTHOR__'s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Breyer: five Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Alaska placed the registration duties in its criminal-procedure title and wrote them into plea and conviction-judgment administration; it placed the public registry in its health-and-safety title and pursued a genuine protective objective. That mixed design makes the civil label relevant but not conclusive. Under Ursery and Hudson, the heightened override protects an established civil choice, not every asserted beneficial aim, so the Court examines purposes and effects directly. Respondents still must show punishment; no presumption of punishment arises.

The Mendoza-Martinez considerations are weighed together. The obligations attach only because of a prior conviction, last fifteen years or for life, require quarterly verification of respondents and are enforced by criminal prosecution. The State assembles current identifying information about each registrant and makes it available to an unrestricted audience. No ordinary mechanism ends the obligations upon demonstrated rehabilitation or materially changed circumstances; Doe I's custody-court rehabilitation finding makes that absence concrete without establishing anything about every registrant. Protective purposes are legitimate, but punishment also deters and protects, and the scheme's duration and reach exceed what that protective relationship explains.

The dissent's strongest ground is that the design is established as civil and that a searchable registry of accurate information, with no direct residence, employment or movement restraint, cannot supply the punitive showing. Those features are weighed: reporting is not quarterly physical appearance, and search access is not compelled viewing. They do not offset compulsory lifetime reporting and official identification that no showing can end. W.P. sustained notification because risk grading, individual exceptions and likely-encounter recipients connected the burden to protection; Alaska supplies none of those connections. Hendricks and Seling look to cumulative actual operation and do not make custody indispensable.

The holding classifies this retrospective combination; it does not make every registration duty, public conviction record or conviction-based regulation punishment.

The ordinary presumption of constitutionality resolves a genuine residual doubt but does not override a balance that tips. He does not join Part III or decide whether the combination would overcome a clearly established civil design, and he adopts no presumption of punishment or registry-hearing requirement.

**O'Connor, dissenting, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The four join Part I and dissent from Parts II and III and the judgment; they would reverse. They find civil intent established. Registration's placement in the criminal-procedure title does not alone classify it as punishment, especially when that title includes nonpunitive subjects; the registry's health-and-safety placement supports the protective character. Plea notice and the conviction judgment's statement of registration requirements inform a person of an independently imposed legal consequence rather than establish that the sentencing judge selected the duty.

They then examine the entire operation under the clearest-proof standard. Lifetime duties and unrestricted current-information access are serious burdens, and the absence of a rehabilitation exit matters. But the scheme confines no one, directly prohibits no residence, work or movement, and requires no in-person quarterly appearance. A viewer must navigate to and search the registry, which provides no comment forum beneath a person's entry. Those features weaken the proposed equivalence to compelled public humiliation or criminal supervision. Knowing failure to comply creates a separate offense; conviction's role in a protective classification is not alone punishment. The lack of W.P.'s tailoring warrants careful review but does not itself supply the demanded punitive proof. The Court's cumulative account remains insufficient, for these four, to overcome the established civil design. They reject the Court's treatment of ordinary notice and mixed codification as enough to withhold that design's demanding rebuttal protection.
