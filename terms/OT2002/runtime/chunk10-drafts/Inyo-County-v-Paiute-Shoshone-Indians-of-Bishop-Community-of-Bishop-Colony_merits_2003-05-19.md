## Controlling holdings

### Section 1983 does not authorize this sovereign-prerogative claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A tribe and its sovereign enterprise asserting the particular sovereign right to withhold evidence from otherwise ordinary county criminal process are outside §1983's protected claimant capacity for that claim. The Tribe and its wholly owned casino corporation therefore cannot use §1983 to enforce the sovereign exemption asserted here; their capacity to assert an independently presented ordinary private right is a distinct question outside this adjudicated claim.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above. Stevens supports rejection of the §1983 claim through the character of the asserted right rather than this claimant-capacity rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1983 provides a remedy for covered federal civil rights, rather than a general means of enforcing every sovereign prerogative. Will v. Michigan Department of State Police requires attention to the statute's sovereign setting. Vermont Agency of Natural Resources v. United States ex rel. Stevens likewise reads statutory personhood in context; its sovereign-defendant rule under a different enactment cannot simply be transferred to every tribal claimant. The capacity inquiry here follows the sovereign right actually asserted.

The Tribe and corporation claim that their sovereign status prevents the County from obtaining tribally controlled records despite probable cause to investigate the identified offenses. Attaching a Fourth Amendment label does not convert that claimed sovereign privilege into the private civil right §1983 protects. The objection that a tribe is a juridical body capable of owning searched property is substantial, but it does not establish statutory coverage for the particular sovereign capacity exercised here. The holding accordingly concerns that capacity and claim, not every tribal entity's ability to possess constitutional rights. Monell v. Department of Social Services concerns municipal attribution of a violation; defendant responsibility cannot create the missing statutory claim.

The complaint-review record includes deadbolt cutters and seizure of documents containing information about 78 additional employees without an opportunity for tribal redaction. Those allegations are preserved. The judgment under review rests on the asserted sovereign exemption, and an independently preserved ordinary execution defect has not been established as a claim presented for this review. The Court therefore resolves the statutory sovereign-prerogative theory without deciding the legality of those execution details. The requested qualified-immunity ruling was conditional on a viable §1983 claim; that predicate fails here.

### The independently pleaded federal theory requires its own adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** Failure of the asserted §1983 cause does not dispose of the separately pleaded federal sovereignty or preemption claim. That claim must receive a source-specific examination of jurisdiction, authorized cause, claimant and defendant requirements, substantive federal Indian law, and available remedy before the action is dismissed in full or prospective protection is granted.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above; Stevens separately agrees that the independent federal theory returns for adjudication.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 1331 and 1362 identify federal jurisdiction; they do not themselves supply a damages cause. A substantial federal claim can support jurisdiction even when one statutory theory fails. The Tribe pleaded an independent federal basis that the appellate §1983 ruling did not adequately address. The source and scope of that claim are necessary antecedents to a decree on sovereign protection from process, and the record and adjudication below do not supply the completed inquiry required for that decree.

Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc. preserves immunity from direct contract suit absent clear waiver or unequivocal congressional authorization, while distinguishing immunity from underlying duties and other enforcement. It therefore does not equate contract-suit immunity with immunity from a criminal warrant. Williams v. Lee supplies a self-government principle whose application must be tested against the actual process and governing federal source. Conversely, the County's conceded authority over the investigated crimes does not answer every question about process directed at tribal records. The offered employee-consent arrangement describes a possible practical accommodation, not an established federal right or completed consent. Properly sourced prospective protection must stand on its own merits and equitable predicates. The residual theory's ultimate process-immunity merits remain open because that necessary source-and-record inquiry was not completed below.

## Holdings

### Section 1983 does not authorize this sovereign-prerogative claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A tribe and its sovereign enterprise asserting the particular sovereign right to withhold evidence from otherwise ordinary county criminal process are outside §1983's protected claimant capacity for that claim. The Tribe and its wholly owned casino corporation therefore cannot use §1983 to enforce the sovereign exemption asserted here; their capacity to assert an independently presented ordinary private right is a distinct question outside this adjudicated claim.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above. Stevens supports rejection of the §1983 claim through the character of the asserted right rather than this claimant-capacity rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1983 provides a remedy for covered federal civil rights, rather than a general means of enforcing every sovereign prerogative. Will v. Michigan Department of State Police requires attention to the statute's sovereign setting. Vermont Agency of Natural Resources v. United States ex rel. Stevens likewise reads statutory personhood in context; its sovereign-defendant rule under a different enactment cannot simply be transferred to every tribal claimant. The capacity inquiry here follows the sovereign right actually asserted.

The Tribe and corporation claim that their sovereign status prevents the County from obtaining tribally controlled records despite probable cause to investigate the identified offenses. Attaching a Fourth Amendment label does not convert that claimed sovereign privilege into the private civil right §1983 protects. The objection that a tribe is a juridical body capable of owning searched property is substantial, but it does not establish statutory coverage for the particular sovereign capacity exercised here. The holding accordingly concerns that capacity and claim, not every tribal entity's ability to possess constitutional rights. Monell v. Department of Social Services concerns municipal attribution of a violation; defendant responsibility cannot create the missing statutory claim.

The complaint-review record includes deadbolt cutters and seizure of documents containing information about 78 additional employees without an opportunity for tribal redaction. Those allegations are preserved. The judgment under review rests on the asserted sovereign exemption, and an independently preserved ordinary execution defect has not been established as a claim presented for this review. The Court therefore resolves the statutory sovereign-prerogative theory without deciding the legality of those execution details. The requested qualified-immunity ruling was conditional on a viable §1983 claim; that predicate fails here.

### The independently pleaded federal theory requires its own adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** Failure of the asserted §1983 cause does not dispose of the separately pleaded federal sovereignty or preemption claim. That claim must receive a source-specific examination of jurisdiction, authorized cause, claimant and defendant requirements, substantive federal Indian law, and available remedy before the action is dismissed in full or prospective protection is granted.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above; Stevens separately agrees that the independent federal theory returns for adjudication.

**Controlling explanation:** Sections 1331 and 1362 identify federal jurisdiction; they do not themselves supply a damages cause. A substantial federal claim can support jurisdiction even when one statutory theory fails. The Tribe pleaded an independent federal basis that the appellate §1983 ruling did not adequately address. The source and scope of that claim are necessary antecedents to a decree on sovereign protection from process, and the record and adjudication below do not supply the completed inquiry required for that decree.

Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc. preserves immunity from direct contract suit absent clear waiver or unequivocal congressional authorization, while distinguishing immunity from underlying duties and other enforcement. It therefore does not equate contract-suit immunity with immunity from a criminal warrant. Williams v. Lee supplies a self-government principle whose application must be tested against the actual process and governing federal source. Conversely, the County's conceded authority over the investigated crimes does not answer every question about process directed at tribal records. The offered employee-consent arrangement describes a possible practical accommodation, not an established federal right or completed consent. Properly sourced prospective protection must stand on its own merits and equitable predicates. The residual theory's ultimate process-immunity merits remain open because that necessary source-and-record inquiry was not completed below.
