## 96. Clackamas Gastroenterology Associates, P.C. v. Wells

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name:** Clackamas Gastroenterology Associates, P.C. v. Wells

**Citation or Docket:** 538 U.S. 440

**October Term:** 2002

**Matter Type:** MERITS

**Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS. Review of the identified lower-court judgment on the listed question.

**Simulated Event Date:** 2003-04-22 (historical).

**Question Presented:** Whether physician shareholders and directors of a professional corporation count as employees for the Americans with Disabilities Act’s fifteen-employee coverage threshold, and what standard governs that determination.

**Question Granted:** The historical review issue is stated above in substance; threshold matters and any separable question not necessary to judgment are identified below.

**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit reversal of summary judgment for the medical practice. The district court excluded the four physician shareholders as analogous to partners; the court of appeals treated the corporate form as supporting their classification as employees.

**Relief Requested:** The practice seeks restoration of judgment that it lacks the requisite number of employees. Wells seeks treatment of the physician directors as employees and continuation of her discrimination action.

**Material Record:** Deborah Wells worked as a bookkeeper for a medical practice organized as a professional corporation and alleged disability discrimination after her termination. The practice’s four physician shareholders also served as directors and performed medical work. Their inclusion or exclusion determines whether the practice meets the statutory numerical threshold on the disputed record. Evidence concerning their authority to manage the enterprise, control their own work, participate in profits and losses, and shape corporate decisions bears on that classification. Corporate organization and employment-related documents exist, but neither supplies a complete answer about actual control. No merits finding on the alleged discrimination follows merely from deciding statutory coverage.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The coverage question precedes adjudication of discrimination but must not be resolved through an invented finding that every owner controls the business or that every corporate worker is a subordinate employee. The record requires classification of each relevant individual and counting under the statutory period. Formal titles, tax treatment, and contract labels are evidence rather than conclusive rules. The statutory employee threshold and the substantive merits remain separate.

**Relevant Dates:** Historical event date: April 22, 2003. Darden supplies the relevant pre-cutoff common-law framework. The statutory count concerns the required number of working days and calendar weeks in the applicable current or preceding year, not a single-day snapshot selected for litigation.

**Participation Matter:** No case-specific recusal or nonparticipation is identified.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

**Current Through:** Governing law through June 28, 2002.

##### Issue 1: Employee status of owner-directors

**Controlling Law:** Darden interprets an inadequately elaborated statutory employee definition by reference to the common law of agency. It requires assessment of the incidents of the relationship, including control, rather than a single label or a purpose-driven substitute. Christensen treats supported nonbinding guidance according to its persuasive force rather than automatically giving it controlling deference. Walters v. Metropolitan Educational Enterprises counts genuine employment relationships on each working day for Title VII’s materially matching numerical language, whether or not the employee worked or was paid that day; payroll is evidence, not conclusive status. Applying that relationship-counting principle here remains separate from deciding whether each physician is an employee.

**Current Reusable Doctrine:** Examine the right to control and the actual relationship in context. No single incident, including ownership, tax treatment, title, or benefit participation, is necessarily dispositive. A different statutory definition can require a different rule; it cannot be assumed from a broad remedial purpose alone.

**Other Governing Law:** The ADA’s employee definition and its requirement of fifteen or more employees during the prescribed twenty-week period supply the statutory question. Ordinary corporate law identifies powers and responsibilities but does not itself determine the federal employee classification.

**Material Other Authority:** The EEOC’s guidance identifies considerations particularly relevant to proprietors: authority to hire or fire and set rules; supervision; reporting obligations; influence over the organization; the parties’ expressed intent; and sharing profits, losses, and liabilities. Its value depends on consistency with the statute and agency principles.

**Existing Rule’s Reach:** Darden rejects exclusive reliance on organizational labels and supplies the general method. Applying that method to shareholder-directors requires facts about whether they operate the business as proprietors or are subject to its control as workers.

**Open Point:** How the common-law control inquiry applies to these four physicians and whether the existing record permits classification without further factual assessment.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The district court analogized the physician shareholders to partners and excluded them. The Ninth Circuit emphasized the practice’s decision to use a corporation, reasoning that it should not obtain corporate advantages while treating its directors as partners for the federal employee count. Wells argues that the corporation is legally distinct, that it employs persons who perform services, and that contractual and benefit arrangements can support employee status. Her strongest rule prevents a business from evading coverage merely by giving subordinate workers small ownership stakes. The practice argues that the physicians control the enterprise and therefore resemble proprietors rather than persons subject to an employer’s direction. Its strongest rule focuses on actual authority and acknowledges that ownership alone is insufficient. The United States and EEOC urged a control-based assessment. The competing lower-court categorical approaches each risk deciding the federal question from only one feature of the relationship.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court could reverse or vacate the categorical inclusion rule and remand for application of common-law control factors to each physician. It could affirm inclusion if the properly established facts show that the corporation controls the physicians as employees, or exclude them if the record establishes genuine proprietor authority. A formal corporate rule would include owner-directors without examining meaningful control; a categorical owner rule would risk excluding workers who hold nominal shares but lack power. A remand on classification preserves the discrimination action only if the statutory coverage requirements are ultimately satisfied and does not establish discriminatory treatment.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A common-law holding can govern proprietor-directors under this statutory definition while preserving statutes with materially different definitions. It can make control central and the EEOC considerations useful without converting six considerations into a mandatory score or placing all shareholders outside protection. The twenty-week and employee-number requirements remain independently applicable.

**Uncertainty:** The extent of each physician’s management power and susceptibility to supervision must be assessed on the actual evidentiary record. Formal agreements may diverge from practical authority. Remand does not establish a particular employee count before those relationships are examined.

#### E. SOURCES

Clackamas Gastroenterology Associates, P.C. v. Wells, 538 U.S. 440 (record and procedural history).

## 101. Women's Medical Center of Northwest Houston v. Bell

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Women's Medical Center of Northwest Houston v. Bell.
* **Citation or Docket:** 248 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2001); No. 00-20037.
* **October Term:** 2002.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS; additional review of an interlocutory preliminary-injunction judgment.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2003-04-22, an assigned merits-review date rather than a historical Supreme Court decision date.
* **Question Presented:** Whether the preliminary injunction against Texas's annual abortion-volume licensing threshold rested on an erroneous equal-protection standard, and whether three regulations measuring care by patients' dignity, self-esteem, and subjective expectations were sufficiently definite to permit enforcement.
* **Question Granted:** Additional merits review of the licensing-classification and vagueness issues resolved by the Fifth Circuit. No historical Supreme Court grant is asserted.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Fifth Circuit dissolved the injunction against the 1999 licensing amendments but preserved the injunction against three regulations and remanded for further proceedings.
* **Relief Requested:** Providers seek continued protection against the expanded licensing obligation and challenged standards. Texas officials seek enforcement pending adjudication of the merits.

The 1999 amendments replaced a requirement covering offices used primarily for abortions, previously measured by a majority of patients, with a threshold of more than 300 abortions in a twelve-month period. Offices performing 300 or fewer remained exempt. Twelve previously unlicensed physicians were brought within the scheme; four were plaintiffs. The requirements included licensing fees, staffing qualifications, inspections, infection-control policies, and a quality-assurance committee. Providers estimated additional procedure costs and raised confidentiality and administrative concerns.

Dr. Hansen, an Austin physician performing approximately 950 to 1,050 abortions annually, testified that he would stop rather than obtain a license. Many of his patients sought medically indicated abortions following fetal diagnoses after fifteen weeks; he described himself as Austin's only provider of such care in a private office. That testimony identifies a potentially important access issue, but does not itself establish the availability, capacity, delay, expense, or clinical suitability of other care.

The district court declined preliminary relief on the undue-burden theory, but enjoined the volume classification as irrational and three regulations as vague. The providers did not appeal denial of preliminary relief on the undue-burden ground. This appeal therefore does not reopen that ruling; any access claim still available in further district-court proceedings retains its own posture and proof requirements. Sections 139.51(1) and (2) required enhancement of patients' dignity and self-esteem; § 139.2(43) measured care's quality against each patient's expectations. A drafting witness acknowledged the absence of objective criteria for assessing enhancement. Violating these provisions did not itself constitute a crime, but civil fines and license revocation were available. Operating without a required license was separately a misdemeanor.

**Threshold and vehicle matters:** This is review of preliminary relief, not a final liability judgment. Legal conclusions receive independent review, factual findings clear-error review, and the ultimate injunction decision abuse-of-discretion review. The appeal concerns the grounds for the injunction actually entered. It does not automatically reopen every provision of the preexisting 1985 licensing system or convert the separate access claim into a finally adjudicated issue. The amendments took effect September 1, 1999; newly covered offices had to obtain licenses by January 1, 2000.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

**Current Through:** Governing law effective before April 22, 2003.

**Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** Planned Parenthood of the Rocky Mountains Services Corp. v. Owens (February 24, 2003); A Woman’s Choice–East Side Women’s Clinic v. Newman (March 24, 2003).

Ordinary economic classifications survive when reasonably conceivable legitimate grounds rationally support the distinction. FCC v. Beach Communications does not require a legislature to document a scientific discontinuity at every numerical boundary or choose the best line. An asserted rational basis must nevertheless be a legitimate reason related to the classification; irrational hostility does not become legitimate by recitation.

Casey's complete six-step framework remains independently applicable to a properly presented access claim: identify the law's operation, purpose, actual added burden, corresponding justification, effects on truthful communication and clinical judgment, and fitted relief. A baseline violation, obstructive purpose, or substantial obstacle is invalid; other severe interference requires compelling justification and narrow tailoring; meaningful lesser interference requires substantiated reasons, fit, and an answer to concrete, practicable, substantially less burdensome alternatives. Ordinary rational-basis analysis of a provider classification does not answer that distinct claim. A health label and absence of demonstrated animus do not conclusively decide practical burdens. The unappealed preliminary ruling does not warrant substituting an incomplete standard for any properly pending district-court merits inquiry. Owens requires protection for timely clinically necessary treatment of established serious health threats, not merely imminent death; a bypass or defense helps only within its actual legal conditions. Newman requires a concrete justification and consideration of practicable alternatives when a compulsory delivery rule adds an established physical encounter. Its ordinary prospective proof rule requires neither a universal causal percentage nor waiting for completed denial of care. These holdings inform an access claim actually before the court; they neither convert all licensing into a compulsory-trip rule nor reopen the unappealed preliminary ruling.

Due process requires fair notice and sufficient limits on enforcement discretion. Professional regulation may use contextual standards, but serious sanctions cannot depend entirely on unknowable personal expectations. The severity of sanctions and interference with protected activity matter to the required clarity. Preliminary relief also requires irreparable injury, an appropriate balance of harms, and consistency with the public interest; likelihood of success alone is insufficient.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The physicians argue that one abortion per working day does not establish a high-volume practice, that the additional offices have no demonstrated history of unsafe care, and that 300 emerged from political compromise rather than medical evidence. They also contend that compliance costs and threatened provider withdrawal undermine access. On vagueness, a physician cannot assure that each patient feels enhanced self-worth or that care exceeds her undisclosed expectations, especially when medical care necessarily involves painful information and unwanted outcomes.

The officials invoke patient safety, infection control, regular inspection, and an administrable exemption for smaller practices. A rational line need not identify the exact point at which risk suddenly increases. They defend dignity and self-esteem as familiar professional values and argue that administrative clarification can resolve uncertainty. They also note the absence of prior sanctions under the challenged provisions.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may preserve, dissolve, or narrow each component of the preliminary injunction separately. It may sustain the classification against the particular rationality challenge while leaving a supported access claim available for adjudication. The record does not permit a final judgment that every licensing requirement is harmless in every practice. Conversely, anticipated expenses and one physician's stated decision do not conclusively prove an unconstitutional access barrier. The subjective regulations can be addressed without rewriting objective infection-control, staffing, or inspection requirements.

Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307 (1993); Grayned v. City of Rockford, 408 U.S. 104 (1972); Village of Hoffman Estates v. Flipside, Hoffman Estates, Inc., 455 U.S. 489 (1982).

## 102. Franchise Tax Board of California v. Hyatt

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Franchise Tax Board of California v. Hyatt
* **Citation or Docket:** 538 U.S.488; No. 02-42
* **October Term:** 2002
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS; review of the identified judgment on the presented questions.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2003-04-23 (historical calendar placement).
* **Question Presented:** Whether the Full Faith and Credit Clause requires Nevada to apply California statutes immunizing its tax agency from a Nevada action alleging intentional torts committed during a tax audit.
* **Question Granted:** The historical review issue is stated above in substance; threshold matters and any separable question not necessary to judgment are identified below.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Nevada Supreme Court allowed the intentional-tort claims to proceed, while directing dismissal of negligence claims as a matter of comity.
* **Relief Requested:** The California Franchise Tax Board seeks dismissal based on California immunity; Hyatt seeks continuation of the intentional-tort litigation.
* **Material Record:** Hyatt claimed to have moved from California to Nevada before receiving substantial licensing income. The Board disputed the date of his move and imposed assessments and civil fraud penalties. Hyatt alleged that audit activity directed into Nevada included negligent and intentional tortious conduct. Nevada used its own immunity principles, protecting discretionary conduct but not the intentional misconduct alleged here. The truth of the tort allegations and validity of the tax assessments are not adjudicated in this review.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Review concerns the refusal to extend statutory immunity in the identified tort suit. The merits of tax liability, choice of law for every claim, personal damages, and any later enforcement judgment are distinct. The governing question does not automatically reopen the general sister-state-suit rule.
* **Relevant Dates:** The residency dispute concerns1991 and1992; the event date is April 23, 2003.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** No separate companion matter is added to this closed list.
* **Participation Matter:** No case-specific recusal or nonparticipation is identified. Historical participation is reported only in Section III.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Governing law through June 28, 2002.
* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** ArticleIV, §1;28 U.S.C. §1738 at its proper scope; §1257 review; distinguish full faith and credit to judgments from conflicts between legislation.

##### Issue 1: Sister-state legislation and local adjudication

* **Controlling Law:** Baker v. General Motors Corp. requires recognition of a judgment’s lawful adjudicative effect and rejects a general public-policy escape for judgments. That rule does not make another State’s statute a judgment or extend its regulatory force automatically. No controlling holding overrules Nevada v. Hall in this sister-state setting.
* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** Give judgments their rendering-state effect subject to jurisdiction and due process; analyze conflicting statutes under the distinct choice-of-law rule. Sovereign immunity, statutory choice, comity, and substantive tort liability are separate.
* **Other Governing Law:** Full Faith and Credit Clause; California’s immunity statute; Nevada’s immunity and intentional-tort rules.
* **Material Other Authority:** Nevada v. Hall and Sun Oil Co. v. Wortman permit application of a competent forum’s law despite a conflicting sister-state statute. Carroll v. Lanza preserves the limit against hostility to another State’s public acts.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** A valid final judgment commands stronger recognition than a sister State’s legislative immunity policy. The forum must have a legitimate connection and cannot discriminate merely from hostility.
* **Open Point:** Whether California’s audit function requires a special full-faith-and-credit rule overriding Nevada’s otherwise legitimate intentional-tort policy.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below:** Comity warrants negligence immunity, but Nevada’s own treatment of intentional torts and local injury justifies allowing those claims. **Board:** Tax administration is a core sovereign responsibility; exposure in another State undermines its ability to perform that function, so California immunity should travel with the agency. **Hyatt:** Nevada can protect persons injured within its territory under the same intentional-tort principle it applies to its own government. A categorical policy of hostility is not established merely by refusing another State’s broader immunity. Neither side can turn this interlocutory issue into a finding of misconduct or tax invalidity.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirmance allows the intentional-tort claims to proceed under ordinary law; it awards nothing and leaves the negligence disposition intact. A rule compelling California immunity would require dismissal of those claims. A narrower remand could address unequal treatment if the actual forum rule materially discriminated against the sister State, but such a finding cannot be invented.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** The court may hold that full faith and credit does not force substitution of a sister State’s immunity statute for a competent forum’s evenhanded tort law. A special core-sovereign-function exception would reach audits and comparable functions and requires a constitutional basis beyond policy preference.

#### E. SOURCES

Franchise Tax Board of California v. Hyatt, 538 U.S.488; No. 02-42 (record and procedural history).

## 103. Gorski v. New Hampshire Department of Corrections

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Gorski v. New Hampshire Department of Corrections.
* **Citation or Docket:** 290 F.3d 466 (1st Cir. 2002); No. 01-1995 below.
* **October Term:** 2002.
* **Entry Stage and Event:** Counterfactual merits review on a timely petition taken for OT2002 consideration; no historical grant or exact petition wording is asserted.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2003-05-27 (assigned review date).
* **Question Presented:** Whether the pleaded pregnancy-based harassment states a hostile-environment claim and whether the separate summary-judgment record supports a discriminatory refusal of transfer.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The First Circuit reinstated the hostile-environment claim but sustained judgment against the distinct transfer theory.
* **Material Record:** Gorski alleged that prison supervisors repeatedly disparaged her pregnancy, discouraged transfer and pressured her during medically directed leave. The district court rejected the harassment theory at pleading, then allowed discovery on a transfer theory. That record established that she had not requested a transfer and the identified supervisors lacked transfer authority. The harassment allegations were not converted into an evidentiary record merely by discovery on the other theory.
* **Relief Requested:** Preserve or overturn reinstatement of the harassment claim; any request to enlarge Gorski's transfer relief requires a proper cross-petition.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Keep Rule 12(b)(6) and Rule 56 distinct. The timeliness defense rejected below remains separate from the issues framed here. Constructive discharge, employer attribution and remedies are not established by a sufficient complaint.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** June 28, 2002, subject to any effective intervening decision.
* **Controlling Law:** Harris requires objectively and subjectively abusive conditions assessed in all circumstances, sufficiently severe or pervasive, without a psychological-injury prerequisite. Oncale permits sex causation without desire or a mandatory opposite-sex comparator. Swierkiewicz requires fair-notice pleading, not allegations proving an evidentiary prima facie case. Faragher and Ellerth require material aid from actual delegated authority for the supervisory-attribution route; rank alone does not establish it, and negligence remains separate. They do not make awareness of a policy or nonreporting an automatic complete defense.
* **Other Governing Law:** The PDA, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e(k), includes pregnancy within Title VII's sex-based prohibition. Rules 8, 12 and 56 govern the different procedural stages. Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer preserves Congress's Title VII remedy against covered state employers; sovereign status does not supply a categorical exemption.
* **Open Point:** Application of notice pleading to this hostile-environment account. A claim that resignation was compelled requires its own proof; it is not synonymous with harassment.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Gorski treats the comments, work-related pressure and discouragement as a combined discriminatory environment. The Department disputes legal severity and the transfer claim's factual basis. An account of representative incidents is not necessarily an exhaustive list of trial evidence. Conversely, a promise that discovery might reveal discrimination does not itself cure a genuinely deficient claim.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may sustain reinstatement under Rule 8 without deciding liability. A contrary pleading judgment would require explaining why the account fails despite the identified protected basis and employment setting. The transfer evidence does not support an adjudicated denial by an authorized decisionmaker, but that does not make discouraging remarks irrelevant to harassment. No immediate damages or constructive-discharge award is available on this posture.

#### E. SOURCES

Gorski v. New Hampshire Department of Corrections, 290 F.3d 466; First Circuit opinion, No. 01-1995, May 24, 2002. Title VII §§ 701(k), 703(a); Rules 8, 12 and 56. Harris, Oncale, Swierkiewicz, Faragher, Ellerth and Fitzpatrick govern their respective questions.
