## Event, chronology and factual posture

## Controlling holdings

### I. Deliberate factual deception may be proved without regulating fundraising percentages

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Amendment permits an action against a charitable fundraiser for material factual representations knowingly used to deceive donors about the use of their contributions. High fundraising costs or failure to volunteer the fee percentage alone cannot establish fraud; the State must prove the independently identified representation, its materially false contextual meaning, intentional deception and the remaining requirements of its actual cause and remedy.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Schaumburg, Munson and Riley protect charitable solicitation from percentage restrictions and compelled fee disclosures while preserving genuine antifraud enforcement. A charity may spend money communicating its cause, and a statement that a contribution helps the charity does not become false merely because expenses consume most receipts. Information and advocacy undertaken during solicitation may themselves perform charitable work. The Court therefore rejects both a percentage presumption and a general duty to announce unfavorable financial information.

The alleged promises that at least ninety percent would go to veterans and that contributions would pay no labor expenses because all workers were volunteers present a different question. Their meaning and truth can be tested independently of whether an eighty-five-percent fee was reasonable. The contractual allocation may be evidence contradicting a specific promise; it cannot supply a promise that was never made. Requiring the State to establish actual, deliberate factual deception answers the claim that fraud litigation necessarily imposes the same restraint as a forbidden fee cap. Edenfield recognizes the distinct treatment of deception, but does not reduce charitable advocacy to ordinary commercial advertising. These particular allegations permit the fraud litigation to proceed.

**Precedent treatment:** Schaumburg, Munson and Riley are applied to preserve protected solicitation and the fee/disclosure boundary while permitting genuine fraud enforcement. Edenfield supplies the deception distinction at its own scope.

### II. Each restored cause retains its own proof and remedial requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** For the intentional common-law fraud restored here, Illinois must establish by clear and convincing evidence a materially false factual representation, knowledge of falsity, intent to mislead, successful deception through justifiable reliance, and resulting damage. A distinct public statutory theory must satisfy its own elements and evidentiary burden and the First Amendment boundary in Part I; it cannot evade that boundary by relabeling a fee cap.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join.

**Controlling explanation:** Hofmann supplies Illinois's rule that fraud is not presumed and requires clear and convincing proof. Witt identifies the factual falsity, knowledge, intended inducement, justifiable reliance and damage required for common-law fraud, and distinguishes actual deceptive intent from conduct violating a different duty. Those demanding requirements keep the burden on Illinois and protect truthful fundraising while allowing proof of the deliberate deception pleaded here.

The complaint invokes common-law and statutory causes. Neither Witt's disciplinary burden nor the elements of a private common-law action establish every requirement of a public consumer-protection cause. The lower court must identify the invoked theory and apply its actual law, including its culpability, causation, injury and remedy requirements. The clear-and-convincing rule stated here governs the intentional fraud at issue; it is not announced as a universal constitutional burden for all statutory enforcement. Reversal removes the constitutional dismissal of sufficiently pleaded affirmative deception. The State must then prove its claims under these rules, and any injunction must address established deception rather than lawful advocacy or the fee level itself.

**Precedent treatment:** Hofmann v. Hofmann, 94 Ill. 2d 205, 222–225 (1983), supplies the common-law proof burden. In re Witt, 145 Ill. 2d 380, 390–391, 397 (1991), supplies the fraud elements and deliberate-intent distinction without supplying a universal statutory burden.
