## Event, record and chronology

Roell v. Withrow, No. 02-69. Plenary merits decision, April 29, 2003. Argued February 26, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 288 F.3d 199. The question is whether informed participation before judgment can establish consent to final civil adjudication by a magistrate judge under 28 U.S.C. §636(c)(1), despite failure to follow the prescribed written-consent procedure, or whether later confirmation alone can create consent.

Withrow consented orally and in writing; one defendant also gave written consent. Roell and Garibay participated throughout after notice of the right to refuse. The magistrate judge mistakenly believed that the consent forms already existed. After the jury returned a defense verdict, appellate inquiry produced later consent letters. The Fifth Circuit treated the absence of express consent before judgment as a nonwaivable defect and nullified the judgment. The defendants seek recognition of their earlier choice and consideration of the remaining appellate issues. Withrow maintains that the advance safeguards are indispensable.

## Controlling holdings

### I. Knowing voluntary participation can establish contemporaneous consent

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the first sentence of §636(c)(1), a specially designated full-time magistrate judge may exercise final civil authority when every party actually consents; informed, voluntary participation before judgment after notice of the need for consent and the right to refuse can establish that consent despite deficient written documentation. The party invoking that authority must establish the contemporaneous knowing choice; silence without notice and later willingness alone cannot supply it.

**Authority:** The complete five-Justice Court-opinion coalition identified in Opinion Topology directly adopts this statutory rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress conditioned this authority on consent but did not require a specific written request in the first sentence of §636(c)(1), as it expressly did for the different category of other part-time magistrate judges. The distinction between an actual choice and its documentation gives effect to both the substantive condition and the mandatory procedural safeguards. Peretz recognizes the importance of consent in the additional-duties setting, and Schor distinguishes personal Article III choice from structural limits; the present statutory inquiry requires the actual civil consent established by this record.

Roell and Garibay knew that they could insist on an Article III judge and voluntarily continued before the magistrate judge until judgment. That informed course supplies consent. The judge's mistaken belief about existing forms does not establish express consent, and the later letters do not create a new prejudgment choice. Avoiding strategic objections after an adverse result reinforces the enacted distinction but cannot replace actual consent. The dissent's express-election reading gives the recording procedure the same substantive effect as Congress's different specific-written-request provision; the first sentence instead turns on the actual informed choice. Because that condition was met, §636(c)(3) supplies ordinary direct appellate review.

**Precedent treatment:** Peretz is distinguished as an additional-duties decision and supplies no automatic civil consent. Schor informs the personal-choice and structural distinction without displacing §636's conditions.

### II. The prescribed consent safeguards remain mandatory

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 636(c)(2) and Rule 73 require written clerk notice when the action is filed, communication of the parties' decisions to the clerk, and execution and filing of joint or separate consent forms within the period specified by local rule. Renewed advice must preserve freedom to refuse without adverse substantive consequences; judges may not learn an individual response unless all parties consent, and the referral procedures must protect voluntariness.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Court-opinion coalition directly adopts the complete rule and the distinction between its breach and genuinely absent consent. The dissent also treats these procedural commands as mandatory but gives their breach a different effect on final adjudicative and appellate authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The notice, recording and nondisclosure commands protect a free choice and must be followed by courts and parties. Recognizing actual informed consent does not make those commands optional. Their violation does not, however, negate the choice already made on this record. The statutory special-designation requirement remains an independent condition. Section 636(c)(4) separately permits the court to vacate a reference for good cause on its own initiative or for extraordinary circumstances shown by a party; those distinct conditions are not substitutes for the consent question decided here.

**Precedent treatment:** Peretz and Schor leave the enacted protections for this civil referral in force; neither authorizes disregarding the mandatory consent procedure.

#### A. DECISIONAL POSTURE

I would distinguish contemporaneous consent from its recording and from a new choice after judgment. Convenience cannot supply absent authority, but a missing form need not erase a choice actually made.

#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Reverse the Fifth Circuit’s jurisdictional nullification and remand for remaining appellate issues.

**Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Recognize the demonstrated informed consent during these proceedings without summarily affirming the medical-care verdict. Preserve the obligation to follow advance notice and recording procedures.

**Remedial Reach:** This judgment validates the consent basis in these proceedings, not uninformed or coerced referrals or all judgments lacking forms.

**Practical Relief:** The appellate court must review the remaining issues on their merits rather than vacate solely for absent advance writing. District courts must continue recording consent as directed; postjudgment letters may confirm the actual earlier choice but cannot repair its absence. If a different record genuinely disputes knowledge or voluntariness, findings precede validation.

#### C. ISSUE POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: Actual informed consent and mandatory recording

**Threshold and Merits Reach:** Adjudicative authority must exist when exercised; the record concerns participation after notice, not letters alone.

**Question Stone Resolves:** Can informed conduct establish §636(c)(1) consent despite defective recording?

**Stone’s Legal Position:** Resolve the open statutory-form question without weakening actual consent.

**Reach Calibration:** Intermediate. The safe harbor governs informed voluntary participation before judgment. A writing-only rule collapses distinct text. Mere silence or hindsight ratification would exceed consent.

**Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Under §636(c)(1), actual voluntary consent may be established by informed participation after notice of the right to an Article III judge, despite failure to obtain the prescribed writing. The party invoking that authority must establish the contemporaneous knowing choice; later willingness alone is insufficient. Roell and Garibay’s informed participation supplies consent here, while the recording violation remains a failure to follow mandatory procedure.

**Reach Within the Question:** Full-time magistrate civil adjudication under the stated provision, excluding objecting, coerced and uninformed parties and preserving different express-writing provisions.

**Next-Case Guidance:** Examine notice and actual participation before judgment. Until a materially disputed record is presented, apply the substantive consent requirement and prescribed recording process, not a presumption from silence.

**Transition:** Harper applies the construction to still-open direct-review cases within its scope; Reynoldsville allows no reliance-only selective application. Final judgments remain subject to actual finality and collateral rules, and the construction cannot retroactively manufacture a choice that never existed.

**Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Section 636(c)(1) conditions authority on consent without the express-writing qualification Congress used elsewhere; §636(c)(2) and Rule 73 impose its protective recording procedure. Peretz and Schor recognize the legal importance of consent in distinct settings but cannot supply it by analogy. The statutory distinction carries the result; avoidance of strategic objections corroborates rather than creates authority.

**Precedent Treatment:** Resolve the form question through §636; distinguish Peretz’s additional duties and Schor’s Article III waiver setting rather than treat either as blanket consent.
