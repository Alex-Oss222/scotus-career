## Event, posture, and record

Argued April 28, 2003; decision June 2, 2003, on writ of certiorari to the Louisiana Supreme Court, 815 So. 2d 27. The question is whether retained discretion over reserve-unit classification permits the State to disallow costs produced by the FERC system agreement as imprudent. Entergy seeks removal of the disallowance; the commission defends prudence review of the classification.

MSS-1 allocates generating-capacity costs among affiliated operating companies. The August 5, 1997 amendment permits extended-reserve units to count as available under criteria applied by the operating committee. It requires demonstrable capability and intent to return an ERS unit to meet future system requirements; the committee's decision must be evidenced in its minutes and consider current and future needs, projected ERS duration, maintenance cost, and return-to-service cost. Those conditions describe the federal method, not a finding that every classification complied. The state order found imprudence, not a violation of that agreement. The actual disallowed payment figures were made in 1996 and used in calculating 1997–1998 retail rates, although the state prudence rationale addressed treatment after August 5, 1997. The complete report's footnote 4 controls that chronology qualification.

## Holdings

### Binding allocation and retained utility discretion

**Holding and operative rule:** A state retail-rate commission must give binding effect to costs assigned by an operative FERC tariff and may not disallow them on a prudence theory that substitutes a different interstate allocation, including when the tariff entrusts an operational input to utility discretion. The party claiming preemption must identify the applicable federal obligation and the actual contradiction; state regulation remains available for genuinely independent retail matters that leave that obligation effective.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court; all nine Justices identified in Opinion Topology join this holding and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Nantahala Power & Light Co. v. Thornburg bars a State from treating a utility as if it received a different federally allocated share of power. Mississippi Power & Light Co. v. Mississippi ex rel. Moore applies that rule to a prudence objection even when FERC has not separately decided the precise managerial question. Those decisions address the practical effect of a state order: costs that a federal allocation assigns cannot be made unrecoverable by substituting the State's preferred allocation.

Here MSS-1 allocates shared generating-capacity costs under the system agreement. Its automatic adjustment mechanism, authorized by 16 U.S.C. §824d(f), assigns the classification decision and its governing criteria to the operating committee. The commission rejected the payment consequences of keeping extended-reserve units in that calculation. Calling the objection review of an antecedent discretionary choice does not separate the resulting disallowance from the allocation it defeats. Federal approval of the method need not include a separate finding that this particular choice was prudent.

AT&T v. Central Office Telephone supplies the analogous rule that relief cannot alter binding tariff obligations while genuinely independent duties remain distinct. Sprietsma v. Mercury Marine requires the actual federal conflict found here: agency silence about a business choice would not itself establish preemption. The identified allocation, rather than an abstract preference for uniformity, controls.

### The actual order and the asserted compliance alternative

**Holding and operative rule:** The challenged order cannot be sustained by a newly asserted tariff-violation theory when it rests on conflicting prudence review and contains no finding of noncompliance. The commission must remove that disallowance and give effect to the federal allocation in further rate proceedings.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The commission expressly declined to decide tariff compliance and upheld its disallowance on prudence grounds. An appellate assertion of violation supplies no missing agency finding. The earlier FERC finding concerned the agreement before its August 5, 1997 amendment; it does not establish a violation of the amended agreement. Review therefore addresses the actual prudence order. The merits and proper initial forum of a distinct tariff-compliance controversy lie outside that order and lack an adjudicated factual basis here; the Court leaves those questions open on those grounds.

## Precedent Treatment

- Nantahala Power & Light Co. v. Thornburg, 476 U.S. 953 (1986): applies the prohibition on trapping federally allocated costs through inconsistent retail treatment. Its assumed alternative-source-purchase question remains distinct from this allocation dispute.
- Mississippi Power & Light Co. v. Mississippi ex rel. Moore, 487 U.S. 354 (1988): applies the bar against prudence review that defeats the allocation without requiring FERC to have separately decided the precise prudence objection.
- AT&T v. Central Office Telephone, Inc., June 15, 1998: applies the binding-tariff distinction by analogy, preserving genuinely independent duties and its communications-law scope.
- Freightliner Corp. v. Myrick, April 18, 1995: preserves the requirement of an operative federal predicate rather than displacement inferred from regulatory absence.
- Geier v. American Honda Motor Co., May 22, 2000: preserves actual conflict with an operative federal method, rather than regulatory overlap or compliance alone.
- Sprietsma v. Mercury Marine, December 3, 2002: applies its actual-conflict requirement and distinguishes regulatory inaction from the binding allocation here.

## Law After Decision

A tariff allocation retains its binding effect when the federal arrangement specifies how and by whom a discretionary input is selected. State prudence review cannot make the assigned costs unrecoverable by attacking that input. Independent retail regulation continues within the actual-conflict boundary; a distinct compliance controversy receives its own governing law and proper forum.
