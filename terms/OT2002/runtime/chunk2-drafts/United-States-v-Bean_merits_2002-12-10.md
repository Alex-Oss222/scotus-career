## Event, record and lawful reach

United States v. Bean, No. 01-704. Merits decision, December 10, 2002. Argued October 16, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit. Bean sought relief from a federal firearms disability following his conviction in Mexico after ammunition was found in his vehicle. ATF returned his application without investigating or deciding its merits because an appropriations restriction prohibited expenditure on individual relief applications. A district court made its own fitness assessment and restored his firearms privileges; the Fifth Circuit affirmed. The question is whether 18 U.S.C. §925(c) authorizes that initial judicial determination without an agency denial.

Plenary argued merits decision. Certiorari granted January 22, 2002, on the statutory review question; argument occurred October 16 and decision is December 10. The grant assumes the operative disability. Ventura on November 4 and Syngenta on November 5 are earlier current-term authority; same-date parallel decisions supply none. No companion is involved.

## Controlling holdings

### Judicial review requires an actual administrative denial

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 925(c) requires an actual administrative denial before a district court may review an individual's restoration application. Return without merits consideration because Congress prohibits the agency from spending funds on the application is not that denial; the district court therefore lacks authority under this provision to make the initial restoration determination, and the restoration order must be vacated and the petition dismissed.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court; all nine Justices in the complete-opinion coalition adopt the statutory trigger, decisionmaker allocation, evidentiary qualification and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 925(c) entrusts the initial judgment to the Secretary: the circumstances concerning the disability and the applicant's record and reputation must establish that the applicant is unlikely to act dangerously to public safety and that relief would not be contrary to the public interest. The person whose application is denied may seek review in the district of residence. That sequence makes an agency determination the subject of review, not an optional preliminary step.

The provision permits additional evidence in the court's discretion when failing to admit it would result in a miscarriage of justice. That qualified permission operates within an authorized review proceeding. It neither requires new evidence in every review nor transfers the first public-safety judgment to the court. Bean's favorable judicial evidence cannot supply the absent denial.

Bean's constructive-denial argument would keep relief practically accessible, but it replaces review of agency action with initial licensing. This is not an exhaustion requirement excused because resort would be futile; the invoked statute supplies no independent initial judicial power. Kokkonen and Goldsmith distinguish useful relief from granted authority. Syngenta confirms that auxiliary power cannot create a missing statutory predicate. Ventura preserves the agency's assigned first judgment; its exception when no lawful agency work remains does not authorize substitution of a judicial fitness assessment or expenditure that Congress has forbidden.

The funding restriction has made this statutory avenue unavailable on the present facts. That consequence cannot change the enacted trigger. The distinct provision for continued licensed operations during a pending application and the requirement to publish a grant of administrative relief supply neither a denial nor original judicial restoration authority.

**Scope:** This ruling governs the §925(c) review mechanism and the disability assumed by the grant. Whether a foreign conviction qualifies under §922(g)(1) and independent constitutional challenges are outside the question granted and remain undecided. Other independently authorized claims require their own legal predicates.
