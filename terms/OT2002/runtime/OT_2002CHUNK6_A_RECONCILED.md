## United States v. Bach — March 5, 2003

### Common proposed disposition and operative grounds

All eight Associates would affirm the Eighth Circuit's reversal of the provider-material suppression order and return the prosecution for ordinary further proceedings. The judgment under Supreme Court review is the circuit judgment; the Supreme Court disposition is therefore affirmance, not reversal of that judgment. The later home-search ruling is not the suppressed-material ruling under review.

The Fourth Amendment does not require an officer to be physically beside the provider's personnel when those personnel retrieve the identified account material at their own facility under the described judicial warrant. The constitutionally relevant safeguards are the antecedent judicial authorization, its specified account and evidentiary scope, and a retrieval serving that authorized search. On the supported facts the technicians carried out retrieval rather than deciding probable cause or selecting an independent investigative scope. Physical attendance would not add a necessary constitutional protection against the particular intrusion shown.

The Court can assume Bach's personal protected interest in the communications. Guest forbids automatic loss of a protected confidential account interest merely from limited entrustment, but its actual holding about credentials and subscriber records need not be expanded to dispose of this execution question. Ortiz/Hurtado establishes that information retrieval is a search distinct from taking equipment; its personal-arrest exception does not authorize this remote retrieval. Here the warrant supplies antecedent search authority. Ayeni forbids a purposeless additional private audience in the home and preserves genuine assistance; the provider's technical execution at its own facility does not create that distinct intrusion.

The federal execution statute's express physical-presence condition is not a constitutional text and does not govern the state-issued warrant merely because its fruits are used federally. A state execution restriction likewise does not, without an applicable federal exclusion ground, establish a Fourth Amendment violation. The proposed disposition rejects the physical-presence ground actually presented. It neither depends on a general loss-of-privacy theory nor approves private enlargement of the warrant. A separate statutory exclusion ground cannot be awarded without its own applicable source and remedy; none is established in the approved question and record.

### Alternative paths and strongest objection

**Affirmance path:** Judicial authorization and bounded technical assistance make this execution reasonable without physical officer attendance. The strongest objection is that an absent officer cannot contemporaneously prevent private oversearch and remains responsible for execution. That concern requires scrutiny of actual authority and scope. It does not establish that standing next to the technician is indispensable where the supported retrieval presents no delegated probable-cause choice or independent investigative scope. Responsibility is not transferred simply because the custodian performs the technically necessary acts.

**Suppression path:** A constitutionally essential official-presence requirement could protect against unsupervised private discretion. Its strongest supported foundation is the execution tradition reflected in statutory presence conditions and Ayeni's limits on outsiders. It fails on this packet because the statutory condition and the unrelated home audience do not establish the proposed constitutional requirement, and the necessary discretionary oversearch premise is absent. It would require a different supported execution fact, not inference from physical absence alone.

## Additional facts and their precise significance

The registration provisions appear in the criminal-procedure title; the public registry provisions appear in the Health, Safety, and Housing title. The former title also contains nonpunitive subjects, including recovered property, confidentiality, criminal-justice information and civil postconviction proceedings. After enactment, the plea rule requires written notice of registration requirements and the ascertainable period before acceptance of a plea. The conviction judgment must state those requirements and the ascertainable lifetime or shorter period. These are notice and judgment-content commands. They do not themselves make the registration duty a term selected by the sentencing judge, and they do not establish that these respondents' original pre-Act sentences contained registration obligations. At the same time, the Legislature's deliberate integration of the duty with conviction and plea administration is relevant evidence rather than an irrelevant typographical fact.

The public must navigate to the registry and search for information. The State makes the material available to an unrestricted audience; it does not compel every Internet user to receive or view it. ## Supported legal paths

### Established civil intent followed by the clearest-proof inquiry

Ursery, Hendricks, Hudson and Seling first identify the intended character and then require the clearest proof to overcome an established civil design. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas find that design established here. Mixed codification does not defeat it: an administrative registry belongs naturally with health and safety, and criminal-procedure placement does not invariably signify punishment. Giving a person accurate notice before a plea and in the judgment is compatible with informing him of a legal consequence independently imposed for public protection.

For those four, the strongest contrary evidence is the offense-only lifetime classification, recurring duties enforced by criminal sanctions, unrestricted availability of current information and absence of an exit based on rehabilitation. They assess those features cumulatively, as Seling requires. The search-service implementation, no on-record commentary feature, no compulsory in-person updates, and absence of direct restrictions materially distinguish both historical public humiliation and continuing criminal supervision. The punitive showing is insufficient under the governing demanding standard. The State's choice may be broad without thereby being proved criminal punishment. That conclusion is confined to the ex post facto question.

Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer likewise do not rest their punitive conclusion on codification alone. ### Ambiguous intent and direct examination of the combined operation

Souter treats the new intent evidence differently. The placement of registration in criminal procedure and its express inclusion in plea and conviction-judgment administration are meaningful evidence of a penal connection. Placement of the public registry in a health-and-safety title and the genuine protective objective point the other way. The mixed subjects in the criminal-procedure title weaken a categorical inference but do not eliminate the combination. He does not find the Legislature's choice sufficiently clear to give an asserted civil designation the full insulating force of the clearest-proof requirement in this application.

This is a Justice-specific application of the sequential inquiry, not a claim that an earlier majority already adopted a comprehensive burden rule for every ambiguous enactment. Souter's complete Ursery and Hudson joins make the established civil choice the premise for demanding contrary proof; his Kurth Ranch join and Hendricks and Seling positions require examination of actual operation when legal form and practical burden diverge. Those authorities support distinguishing a demonstrated civil choice from one whose character remains materially ambiguous. The finding of ambiguity is an inference from this neutral supplement and those prior positions, not a recovered statement in this case.

Souter would therefore examine the actual purposes and effects without demanding that respondents first overcome a clearly selected civil character that he has not found. He does not presume punishment, shift to the State a new requirement to prove innocence of punitive purpose, or specify a new numerical or preponderance burden. Respondents still must establish that the challenged retrospective measure imposes punishment. This remains a close balance of substantial punitive and civil indications for Souter. The ordinary presumption of constitutionality resolves the remaining doubt in the State's favor; he does not demand the heightened clearest-proof override or affirmatively find that the evidence unambiguously validates the scheme. The content and implementation support that bounded conclusion that respondents have not established punishment on the less deferential route.

The civil indications that prevent a punitive conclusion in this close balance are the record's noncustodial reporting arrangement, absence of compulsory in-person supervision or direct conduct prohibitions, the continuing informational protective function, and the accessible search service rather than forced public confrontation. The lack of a comment feature further weakens the analogy to a state-arranged forum for humiliation. These are cumulative considerations, not categorical exemptions. Lifetime duration, the absence of individualized tailoring and Doe I's evidence make this a serious case; they do not, in his assessment, turn this supported operation into punishment. A separate privacy violation remains a different question.

The strongest objection to Souter's route is that the protective design sufficiently establishes civil intent despite ordinary provisions ensuring notice, so that withholding clearest-proof deference gives procedural integration too much significance. He responds that the first step cannot be skipped by treating a beneficial aim as an unequivocal classification. The strongest objection to his result is that permanent publicly accessible current data remain a substantial state-created burden even when no viewer is compelled. He accepts the burden but distinguishes its protective informational operation from criminal punishment on the record actually supplied.

### Punitive combined lifetime operation despite the search-service limits

Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer retain the punitive path. The State continuously maintains an unrestricted public current-information dossier through lifetime duties triggered by completed offenses, without tailoring audience or duration to present risk and without a route responsive to supported rehabilitation. W.P.'s risk grading, individual exceptions and likely-encounter recipients supplied concrete civil fit that is absent here. Its outcome therefore cannot substitute for examining Alaska's operation.

They accept that this website neither forces viewers to confront a registrant nor supplies an on-record comment forum. That substantially qualifies the historical-shaming analogy: the case does not involve stocks, a compelled public display before a gathered audience, or a demonstrated public discussion of either respondent. Their ground instead concerns enduring compulsory official availability and replenishment of current life information. Publication remains an affirmative state act even when a member of the public must search to retrieve it. No finding of greater actual exposure, harassment, business loss or relocation is needed or invented.

The strongest opposing ground is that accurate information, limited reporting duties and no direct restraint describe a civil regulatory measure whose excessive breadth can be addressed through other constitutional doctrines. The three respond that actual operational punishment need not take a custodial form. The combined offense trigger, indefinite compulsory updates, unrestricted audience and absence of any response to changed conditions supply the demanded punitive proof. Doe I's supported finding makes that mismatch concrete without becoming a universal zero-risk finding. The link to criminal plea and judgment administration supports their cumulative assessment but does not independently decide it.

Their legal ground remains the combined lifetime operation imposed on these respondents. It does not make every registry, every public conviction, or every quarterly reporting duty punishment. Confidential registration alone is not independently invalidated. A universal risk-hearing process is not selected as the remedy; any preservation of confidential registration depends on the governing law's severability and lawful administration.

### Operative issue and competing paths

**Sustain the stop:** The immediately reported sequence of wrong-side passing and cutting off other vehicles, qualified model identification, color, partial plate, route, direction and prompt interception of the moving vehicle together support a reasonable inference of contemporaneous observation of dangerous driving. The strongest objection is that anyone who sees a car can falsely describe both its identifiers and its conduct; the officer independently verified no misconduct or caller honesty. No predictive corroboration is categorically demanded, but some supported assurance concerning the allegation remains necessary. Here the police have no accountable informant, independent conduct corroboration or other sufficient assurance on that point.

The strongest objection to rejection is that it discounts the specificity and immediacy of a report concerning public, moving conduct and may require waiting for another injury-producing maneuver. The rejecting path accepts the details' evidentiary weight but finds their added reliability inadequate. It prescribes neither a universal waiting duty nor a mandatory checklist; a better-supported anonymous report can justify action. Mere urgency cannot decide whether this accusation is reliable.
