## 135. Lerman v. Board of Elections in the City of New York

### Proposed judgment and common ground

All eight Associates would affirm the Second Circuit's invalidation of the electoral-district residence condition for otherwise qualified petition witnesses and its remand for conforming judgment. The supported reasonable expectation of repeat exposure and the short election cycle preserve the controversy; the completed 1999 primary is not rerun. No new 2003 petition drive is assumed.

**Common operative proposition:** New York may not exclude otherwise qualified in-state petition circulators from witnessing candidate petitions solely because they reside outside the electoral district. Petition circulation is protected interactive political expression; the residence condition directly excludes willing speakers and associational assistance. Local support is shown by the signers' district qualification. Statewide subpoenas, supplied addresses and ordinary verification furnish accountability without excluding the speaker by district, and the unrestricted notary/commissioner alternative contradicts the asserted indispensability of local residence.

Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer support the Anderson–Burdick analysis: this direct exclusion imposes a severe speech and associational burden and fails narrow tailoring to the asserted fraud, accountability, local-support and administrative interests. Thomas supports the invalidity, application and same facial remedy under traditional strict scrutiny for this direct restriction of political speech; he does not join a general method that makes the applicable scrutiny depend on balancing the restriction against administrative interests.

Facial relief concerns the separable district-residence condition operating against otherwise qualified in-state witnesses. Its accountability and local-support rationales do not supply a lawful sweep sufficient to preserve that condition. Other qualifications retain their own terms. The independent constitutional ground resolves the presented relief without deciding the separate Molinari-stipulation theory.

### Proposed operative propositions

**Hostility, six Associates:** A reasonable jury could find the combined proposition, unnecessary touching, sexualized assignments and materials, public sex-directed humiliation and final sexist typing demand sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter Duncan's working conditions. Harris requires the entire course to be considered in its employment context; Reeves protects a reasonable cumulative inference without insulating every verdict from Rule 50. Some incidents' distance in time, cessation of the original proposition and criticism directed at men remain relevant, but they do not compel the inference that the sex-directed course was merely offensive. DeAngelis involved the materially different newsletter record; it establishes no minimum catalogue of acts or immunity for sexualized work assignments.

**Constructive discharge, six Associates supporting affirmance:** A hostile environment does not itself establish a compelled resignation. Constructive discharge requires conditions objectively so intolerable that a reasonable employee would feel compelled to resign; realistically available corrective alternatives bear on whether resignation was compelled. On this record, responsible GM management had removed the planter and begun an EEO process after the April complaint, Duncan had agreed but had not supplied her account, and the College offered another location. The final demand and earlier complaints matter, but the supported response and alternative preclude a reasonable finding that resignation was then compelled. The rule does not require remaining in danger, enduring futile procedures or waiting indefinitely, and the mere existence of an investigation is not a safe harbor. Earlier hostile-environment injury retains its distinct analysis.

The two constructive-discharge dissenters accept the objective-compulsion inquiry and the relevance of realistic corrective alternatives, but reject its conclusive application against the verdict. They do not derive constructive discharge automatically from harassment.
