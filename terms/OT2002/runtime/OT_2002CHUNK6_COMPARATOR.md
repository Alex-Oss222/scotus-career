## 65. United States v. Bach

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Eighth Circuit reversed the district court’s suppression ruling.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Judge Beam wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Murphy and Melloy. This lower-court decision is the comparator for the added merits review.

**Historical Holding:** The court held that the Fourth Amendment did not require an officer’s physical presence during Yahoo’s execution of the warrant. It treated the federal execution statute as inapplicable to a state warrant and declined to make a possible state-law defect dispositive of federal constitutional reasonableness.

**Historical Authority:** The Fourth Amendment and the warrant-execution statutes furnished the historical analysis. The opinion assumed the relevant privacy interest rather than deciding that all provider-held communications lack protection.

**Historical Sources:** United States v. Bach, 310 F.3d 1063.

## 66. Smith v. Doe

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit, 6–3, and held the scheme nonpunitive for ex post facto purposes.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Kennedy wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O’Connor, Scalia, and Thomas. Justice Souter concurred in the judgment. Justice Stevens dissented; Justice Ginsburg dissented with Justice Breyer.

**Historical Holding:** The historical majority treated the Act as a civil regulatory measure whose effects did not negate legislative intent. It distinguished registration and publication from confinement and physical restraint. The dissent emphasized the scheme’s severity, permanence, and lack of an individualized risk determination.

**Historical Authority:** The historical reasoning drew on historical civil-sanction and commitment cases. Its account of Kansas v. Hendricks is not the governing holding and cannot replace it.

**Historical Sources:** Smith v. Doe, 538 U.S. 84.

## 67. United States v. Wheat

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the denial of suppression.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Judge Goldberg wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Loken and Bogue. The lower-court judgment is the historical comparator for this added merits review.

**Historical Holding:** The circuit held that the anonymous, contemporaneous report of erratic driving, corroborated as to the vehicle and location, justified the stop without independent observation of illegal driving. It distinguished an observable traffic hazard from the concealed-gun allegation in Florida v. J. L.

**Historical Authority:** The historical opinion applied Terry v. Ohio and anonymous-tip cases. Its treatment of other historical precedents does not supersede the controlling holdings of Florida v. J. L. or Illinois v. Wardlow.

**Historical Sources:** United States v. Wheat, 278 F.3d 722.

## 68. Johnson & Johnston Associates Inc. v. R.E. Service Co., Inc.

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

* **Historical Outcome and Vote:** No historical Supreme Court merits disposition is identified. The historical anchor is the Federal Circuit's March 28, 2002 en banc reversal, 12–1.

* **Historical Opinion Structure:** A twelve-judge per curiam opinion; separate concurrences by Clevenger, Rader, Dyk and Lourie; Newman dissented. Rader's broader foreseeability proposal was not the court's express-disclosure holding.

* **Historical Holding:** The court treated disclosed but unclaimed steel substrates as dedicated to the public and unavailable through equivalents, overruling the inconsistent portion of YBM Magnex, Inc. v. International Trade Commission. It reversed infringement and dependent monetary relief.

* **Historical Authority:** The Federal Circuit opinion is the judgment under review. Newman's objection and the separate concurrences do not become unanimous propositions.

* **Historical Sources:** Full reported opinion and separate writings.

## 69. Cook County v. United States ex rel. Chandler

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Supreme Court affirmed unanimously.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Souter wrote for all nine Justices.

**Historical Holding:** The Court held that municipal corporations, including counties, are persons under the False Claims Act. The later treble-damages amendment did not repeal municipal liability, and the remedy’s compensatory as well as punitive features distinguished the County’s analogy to general municipal punitive-damages immunity.

**Historical Authority:** The historical analysis concerned statutory corporate meaning, the False Claims Act’s development, and the historical Vermont Agency decision. Vermont Agency’s governing State-coverage and standing rules are stated in Section I; municipal coverage remains distinct.

**Historical Sources:** Cook County v. United States ex rel. Chandler, 538 U.S. 119.

## 70. Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Ayers

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Supreme Court affirmed. The Court was unanimous on the indivisible-injury apportionment issue and divided 5–4 on cancer-fear damages.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Ginsburg wrote the Court’s opinion. Justices Stevens, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas joined the cancer-fear holding. Justice Kennedy dissented from that part with Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O’Connor and Breyer; Justice Breyer also wrote separately.

**Historical Holding:** The Court permitted damages for genuine and serious fear of cancer accompanying asbestosis and held that the railroad could be liable for the entire indivisible injury when its negligence contributed, without apportionment to nonrailroad exposures.

**Historical Authority:** The historical Court distinguished Gottshall and Buckley and applied the Federal Employers’ Liability Act against the background treatment of indivisible injuries. Gottshall’s emotional-distress limits and Buckley’s treatment of exposure without physical injury retain their distinct scope under Section I.

**Historical Sources:** Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Ayers, 538 U.S. 135.

## 71. Odom v. Antrican

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Fourth Circuit affirmed the denial of Eleventh Amendment immunity and declined pendent review of the other dismissal issues.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Judge Niemeyer wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Hamilton and Jackson. The circuit judgment is the comparator for this sim-granted matter.

**Historical Holding:** The court held that Ex parte Young was available for prospective compliance with federal Medicaid requirements despite the Spending Clause basis of the program and the likely cost to the State. It did not adjudicate every asserted right or the ultimate adequacy of North Carolina’s program.

**Historical Authority:** The historical opinion applied federal official-capacity relief doctrine and distinguished retrospective treasury relief. The governing Coeur d’Alene holding, rather than its historical counterpart, supplies the governing prior rule here.

**Historical Sources:** Odom v. Antrican, 290 F.3d 178.

## 72. United States v. Buckland

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

* **Historical Outcome and Vote:** The en banc Ninth Circuit sustained the sentence, eight to three in the judgment, in its January 18, 2002 opinion as amended May 7, 2002.

* **Historical Opinion Structure:** Trott’s opinion had six full adherents; T. G. Nelson joined except the stacking portion. Hug concurred in the judgment through a separate opinion; Reinhardt and T. G. Nelson joined its Part V criticism of stacking. Tashima dissented, joined by Reinhardt and Paez. The stacking rationale therefore had six votes, not every vote for affirmance.

* **Historical Holding:** Section 841 survived the facial attack. The majority found no prejudice based on quantity evidence and, alternatively, consecutive-sentence requirements, and separately declined discretionary correction. Its quantity discussion used the later 500-gram mixture threshold; that historical reasoning is distinguished from the offense-date thresholds in Sections I and II. The May amendment corrected a Guidelines range, not the imposed 324-month term.

* **Historical Authority:** A lower-court comparator only. Its quotation of the historical Apprendi prior-conviction exception is not the governing rule, and historical later Supreme Court sentencing cases are not imported as intervening controlling holdings.

* **Historical Sources:** Amended en banc opinion and separate writings.

## 73. Cuyahoga Falls v. Buckeye Community Hope Foundation

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Supreme Court unanimously reversed the constitutional rulings and directed the appropriate disposition of the abandoned Fair Housing Act theory.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice O’Connor wrote for all nine Justices. Justice Scalia concurred, joined by Justice Thomas.

**Historical Holding:** The Court found no sufficient evidence that city officials acted with discriminatory intent and rejected the substantive due-process theory based on administering the referendum procedure. It did not decide the abandoned Fair Housing Act claim on its merits.

**Historical Authority:** The historical decision applied equal-protection intent and substantive due-process principles to the record and treated private petitioning separately from discriminatory governmental action.

**Historical Sources:** Cuyahoga Falls v. Buckeye Community Hope Foundation, 538 U.S. 188.

## 74. Woodford v. Garceau

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Supreme Court reversed, 6–3, holding AEDPA applicable to the post-enactment habeas application.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Justice Thomas wrote for Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, Scalia, and Kennedy. Justice O’Connor concurred in the judgment. Justice Souter dissented, joined by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Historical Holding:** The Court held that a case becomes pending for the relevant AEDPA purpose when the prisoner files an application for habeas relief, rather than when he seeks counsel or a stay. The dissent considered the earlier proceeding sufficient under McFarland.

**Historical Authority:** The historical opinion construed Lindh v. Murphy and McFarland v. Scott in their distinct settings. The entering entries, including McFarland’s separate majorities and limited stay purpose, remain the operative prior law.

**Historical Sources:** Woodford v. Garceau, 538 U.S. 202.

## 75. American Amusement Machine Association v. Kendrick

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

**Historical Outcome and Vote:** Historically, the Seventh Circuit reversed denial of preliminary relief and remanded for an injunction.

**Historical Opinion Structure:** Judge Posner wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Diane Wood and Williams.

**Historical Holding:** The circuit held that the challenged violent games were protected expression and that the City’s evidence and analogy to obscenity did not justify its restrictions on minors. It distinguished sexual obscenity and emphasized the expressive tradition of violent narratives.

**Historical Authority:** The historical decision used First Amendment principles, the games and studies in the record, and comparisons with other expressive media. The circuit judgment is the decision under review; no Supreme Court merits disposition is identified.

**Historical Sources:** American Amusement Machine Association v. Kendrick, 244 F.3d 572.

## 76. Okpalobi v. Foster

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

On March 12, 2001, the Fifth Circuit sitting en banc reversed and vacated the injunction and remanded for dismissal; the opinion was revised March 16. Judge Jolly's standing analysis received ten votes. Only seven of the fourteen participating judges joined his Eleventh Amendment analysis, so that discussion was not an en banc majority holding. Judges Higginbotham and King favored resolution on standing without the broader immunity analysis; Judge Benavides also joined the standing disposition in part. Judges Parker, Wiener, Stewart, and Dennis dissented from the disposition. The decision did not adjudicate Act 825's abortion merits. The assigned March 31, 2003 review date denotes additional merits review, not a historical Supreme Court merits decision.

## 77. Center for Reproductive Law and Policy v. Bush

### SECTION III: HISTORICAL COMPARATOR SUPPLEMENT

#### A. MERITS COMPARATOR

The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal on September 13, 2002. Judge Sonia Sotomayor wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges McLaughlin and Leval. The court bypassed First Amendment standing under its interpretation of the already-controlled-merits exception, treated Planned Parenthood Federation as disposing of that claim, dismissed due process on prudential grounds, and recognized competitive-advocate standing while rejecting equal protection under rational-basis review. It relied on the government's authority to favor a position through funding. The historical judgment did not grant relief to the advocates, and no Supreme Court merits result is assigned to this counterfactual review. Historical authority: Center for Reproductive Law and Policy v. Bush, 304 F.3d 183.
