## Event, scope and chronology

Dastar Corp. v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp., No. 02-428. Merits decision, June 2, 2003. Argued April 2, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The questions concern liability under Lanham Act §43(a) for uncredited copying of public-domain expression without the requisite source confusion, and whether §1117(a) permits doubling a profits award for deterrence if federal liability exists. The underlying book-copyright claim is outside the grant.

Fox arranged production of the television series Crusade in Europe based on Eisenhower's book. The series' copyright expired in 1977. After respondents began distributing restored videos, Dastar acquired copies of the original series, edited and shortened them, added material and packaging, and manufactured its own World War II Campaigns in Europe sets in 1995. Its credits identified its own production operation and omitted the earlier series. Dastar did not relabel respondents' finished tapes.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed §43(a)(1)(A) liability and twice Dastar's profits on the theory that substantial bodily appropriation without attribution established reverse passing off. It separately reversed summary judgment on the book-copyright claim and remanded it. The district court's state unfair-competition ruling depended on the federal ruling; the appellate court did not separately decide that claim. Dastar seeks reversal of the federal liability and award, while respondents seek to retain both.

## Controlling Holdings

### I. Origin of goods identifies the tangible producer

**Holding and operative rule:** For an origin-of-goods claim under §43(a)(1)(A), including a claim concerning communicative goods, origin means the producer of the tangible goods offered for sale, not the creators of expression incorporated in them. A claimant must establish false designation and the statute's required likelihood of confusion about a legally protected source, affiliation, connection, association, sponsorship or approval; uncredited copying alone does not supply those elements.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the participating Court. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg directly join both holdings, their applications and the remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Dastar's manufacture of its own sets identifies it as their producer. Origin does not become a different statutory concept because a purchaser values a product for the expression it contains. Wal-Mart, Two Pesos and Qualitex protect qualifying source-identifying interests under their own distinct requirements. They do not establish ownership of every feature or expressive contribution. Sears, Compco and Bonito Boats prevent use of adjacent law to recreate exclusivity that the intellectual-property scheme withholds, while preserving legitimate protection against source deception.

Respondents argue that attribution would permit copying while preventing a copier from claiming the creative source as its own. But that reading turns the origin provision into an authorship-credit obligation with no comparable term and no clear stopping point among the authors and source materials contributing to a work. The Act's source function cannot carry that obligation. Dastar's identification of itself as producer was not false as to the tangible products it manufactured. Its copying of the public-domain series therefore does not establish the adjudicated origin claim.

Actual relabeling of another producer's finished goods and likely confusion concerning sponsorship or approval remain governed by their statutory elements. Section 43(a)(1)(B) separately addresses misrepresentations in commercial advertising or promotion about the nature, characteristics, qualities or geographic origin of goods, services or commercial activities. Such a claim requires its own pleading, proof and procedural basis; it is not a substitute adjudicated ground for this award. The book-copyright claim, including its work-for-hire and renewal issues, is outside the grant. The series' public-domain status does not resolve it.

**Precedent treatment:** Wal-Mart, Two Pesos and Qualitex supply the source-protection distinction. Sears, Compco and Bonito Boats' anti-recreation principle informs the rejection of a perpetual authorship duty without displacing genuine source protection or independent copyright rights.

### II. The profits award falls with its sole federal basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Both base profits and any enhancement resting solely on the rejected origin theory must be removed. Recovery on another surviving claim requires adjudication of that claim's own elements and remedial authority.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion of the eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Wal-Mart requires relief dependent on a rejected Lanham Act theory to be separated from independently sustainable claims. The federal profits award here rests on the failed attribution ground. Disapproval of Dastar's copying and a desire to deter repetition cannot provide the missing cause of action. The question whether deterrence permits doubling an otherwise valid §1117(a) award lacks its necessary liability predicate and is left undecided for that reason. Section 1117(a)'s adjustment and compensation-versus-penalty requirements remain applicable when a valid claim supplies the predicate. The underlying book dispute and the dependent state-law treatment retain their distinct procedural positions.

**Precedent treatment:** Wal-Mart's separation of dependent relief and independent causes is applied to the full profits award, rather than merely its doubling increment.

**Complete statutory reach.** The origin-of-goods construction covers communicative goods as well as other tangible goods and rejects the asserted creative-author attribution theory, including Dastar's description of itself as producer. The rule is not merely an absence-of-survey or absence-of-confusion-evidence disposition that permits the identical authorship theory to return with more evidence. Actual passing off of one's goods as another's and reverse passing off of another producer's physical goods remain within §43(a)(1)(A) when its elements are met. The statute also separately addresses likely confusion about affiliation, connection, association, sponsorship and approval; identifying the tangible producer does not erase those provisions.

Section 43(a)(1)(B) separately reaches misrepresentations in commercial advertising or promotion about the nature, characteristics, qualities or geographic origin of goods, services or commercial activities. The historical opinion's example of misleading purchasers about how different copied content is describes a potential claim requiring those predicates. It is not a finding, preserved claim, or substitute ground sustaining this award. ## Public Projection

## Event

Dastar Corp. v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp., No. 02-428. Merits decision, June 2, 2003. Argued April 2, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The questions concern liability under Lanham Act §43(a) for uncredited copying of public-domain expression without the requisite source confusion, and whether §1117(a) permits doubling a profits award for deterrence if federal liability exists. The underlying book-copyright claim is outside the grant.

Fox arranged production of the television series Crusade in Europe based on Eisenhower's book. The series' copyright expired in 1977. After respondents began distributing restored videos, Dastar acquired copies of the original series, edited and shortened them, added material and packaging, and manufactured its own World War II Campaigns in Europe sets in 1995. Its credits identified its own production operation and omitted the earlier series. Dastar did not relabel respondents' finished tapes.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed §43(a)(1)(A) liability and twice Dastar's profits on the theory that substantial bodily appropriation without attribution established reverse passing off. It separately reversed summary judgment on the book-copyright claim and remanded it. The district court's state unfair-competition ruling depended on the federal ruling; the appellate court did not separately decide that claim. Dastar seeks reversal of the federal liability and award, while respondents seek to retain both.

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg participated. Breyer took no part in the consideration or decision; no reason is publicly stated.

## Holdings

### I. Origin of goods identifies the tangible producer

**Holding and operative rule:** For an origin-of-goods claim under §43(a)(1)(A), including a claim concerning communicative goods, origin means the producer of the tangible goods offered for sale, not the creators of expression incorporated in them. A claimant must establish false designation and the statute's required likelihood of confusion about a legally protected source, affiliation, connection, association, sponsorship or approval; uncredited copying alone does not supply those elements.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the participating Court. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg directly join both holdings, their applications and the remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Dastar's manufacture of its own sets identifies it as their producer. Origin does not become a different statutory concept because a purchaser values a product for the expression it contains. Wal-Mart, Two Pesos and Qualitex protect qualifying source-identifying interests under their own distinct requirements. They do not establish ownership of every feature or expressive contribution. Sears, Compco and Bonito Boats prevent use of adjacent law to recreate exclusivity that the intellectual-property scheme withholds, while preserving legitimate protection against source deception.

Respondents argue that attribution would permit copying while preventing a copier from claiming the creative source as its own. But that reading turns the origin provision into an authorship-credit obligation with no comparable term and no clear stopping point among the authors and source materials contributing to a work. The Act's source function cannot carry that obligation. Dastar's identification of itself as producer was not false as to the tangible products it manufactured. Its copying of the public-domain series therefore does not establish the adjudicated origin claim.

Actual relabeling of another producer's finished goods and likely confusion concerning sponsorship or approval remain governed by their statutory elements. Section 43(a)(1)(B) separately addresses misrepresentations in commercial advertising or promotion about the nature, characteristics, qualities or geographic origin of goods, services or commercial activities. Such a claim requires its own pleading, proof and procedural basis; it is not a substitute adjudicated ground for this award. The book-copyright claim, including its work-for-hire and renewal issues, is outside the grant. The series' public-domain status does not resolve it.

**Precedent treatment:** Wal-Mart, Two Pesos and Qualitex supply the source-protection distinction. Sears, Compco and Bonito Boats' anti-recreation principle informs the rejection of a perpetual authorship duty without displacing genuine source protection or independent copyright rights.

### II. The profits award falls with its sole federal basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Both base profits and any enhancement resting solely on the rejected origin theory must be removed. Recovery on another surviving claim requires adjudication of that claim's own elements and remedial authority.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion of the eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Wal-Mart requires relief dependent on a rejected Lanham Act theory to be separated from independently sustainable claims. The federal profits award here rests on the failed attribution ground. Disapproval of Dastar's copying and a desire to deter repetition cannot provide the missing cause of action. The question whether deterrence permits doubling an otherwise valid §1117(a) award lacks its necessary liability predicate and is left undecided for that reason. Section 1117(a)'s adjustment and compensation-versus-penalty requirements remain applicable when a valid claim supplies the predicate. The underlying book dispute and the dependent state-law treatment retain their distinct procedural positions.

**Precedent treatment:** Wal-Mart's separation of dependent relief and independent causes is applied to the full profits award, rather than merely its doubling increment.

## Precedent Treatment

- Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc., March 22, 2000: applies the distinction between copying and the required statutory source interest, and its separation of dependent Lanham Act relief from independent copyright and state-law grounds. Its product-design distinctiveness rule remains at that scope.
- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: preserves qualifying nonfunctional restaurant trade dress under the separate distinctiveness and confusion requirements; it supplies no ownership of incorporated expression.
- Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co., March 28, 1995: applies its source-identification function and separate eligibility limits without extending protection to authorship as such.
- Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co., 376 U.S. 225: applies its distinction between protecting against source deception and recreating exclusive control over available subject matter.
- Compco Corp. v. Day-Brite Lighting, Inc., 376 U.S. 234: applies the same distinction to the proposed attribution obligation.
- Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc., 489 U.S. 141: extends its anti-recreation reasoning to the proposed perpetual authorship duty under the origin provision, preserving independent source and copyright claims.

## Law After Decision

The goods-origin rule applies to communicative products as well as other tangible goods: a manufacturer's failure to credit creators of copied expression does not itself misidentify the producer of its goods. Genuine product-source and sponsorship deception remain subject to §43(a)(1)(A)'s elements; §43(a)(1)(B) retains its separate advertising predicates. Liability and recovery must rest on the actual statutory cause. The distinct valid-book-copyright inquiry remains governed by copyright law.
