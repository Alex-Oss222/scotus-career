## Internal Holdings

### I. The candidates' injuries and continuing class claim permit decision

**Holding and operative rule:** A candidate's loss of willing petition advocates and diversion of campaign resources are concrete injuries even when enough signatures are ultimately collected. Here the continuing certified class of candidates exposed to the challenged provision supplies the live prospective controversy after the named candidates' election ended.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls the threshold determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that eventual ballot qualification does not restore the political association and resources lost through the challenged exclusions. Buckley recognizes the protected conversation conducted by petition advocates; Lerman applies that protection to candidate petitions. Those injuries belong to the candidates themselves because the qualifications restrict their chosen means of communicating and obtaining support. Prospective relief requires a continuing controversy as well as past injury. The certified class covers candidates whose nominations have been or will be challenged under section 7-10 and remains exposed to the common conditions. That class controversy supplies the present basis for decision. Lerman's supported individual repeat exposure and this case's class exposure are distinct grounds for reaching a continuing election restriction.

### II. The State and district boundaries cannot exclude these advocates

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments by excluding otherwise qualified, accountable candidate-petition advocates because they reside outside Illinois for a Senate nomination or outside the seventh congressional district for the House nomination, including residents of other States. Under Anderson–Burdick, courts weigh the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise asserted interests and the extent to which those interests require the restriction: severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests, while reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests; these direct geographic exclusions impose severe burdens that the asserted grounds fail to justify.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight direct joins control both applications and the graduated method. Thomas supports both judgments under traditional strict scrutiny.

**Controlling explanation:** Meyer protects the political conversation involved in collecting signatures. Buckley requires examination of the speakers excluded and the actual verification need. Anderson and Burdick preserve reasonable, nondiscriminatory administration while requiring correspondingly stronger justification for severe burdens. Lerman establishes that candidate witnessing retains this expressive character and invalidates an unsupported in-state district screen. Requiring an advocate to relocate imposes the same severe exclusion here.

Qualified signers demonstrate local electoral support, and qualified voters select nominees. Circulators ask for that support and communicate the candidates' views. State or district residence does not establish boundary knowledge or the ability to obtain valid signatures. Circulator certification and the signature-challenge process address those tasks directly. The candidate's eventual success and access to other speakers do not restore the excluded association. Cartwright's valid support requirement measures the electorate's support, a different function.

The interstate application also requires its own justification. Lerman's statewide process does not establish Illinois authority over every nonresident. But the Board's actual local-support, valid-signature, integrity and outside-influence grounds supply no necessary connection between residence and this advocacy. The Board did not advance the process-consent or demonstrated nonresident-fraud grounds identified by the court below. Enforcement difficulty cannot be presumed from a boundary alone. Reserving voting to Illinois electors does not justify restricting candidates' association with outside advocates; a desire to exclude their political influence targets the protected association itself. These reasons defeat both geographic applications.

### III. Otherwise eligible residents may circulate without voter enrollment

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois may not exclude otherwise qualified, accountable candidate-petition advocates merely because they are unregistered voters, including persons already resident in and eligible to register within the represented electorate. Under the graduated standard stated in Part II, the substantial restriction on the candidates' chosen advocates is not justified by the asserted nomination, local-support and signature-verification interests; the injunction therefore protects eligible unregistered Illinois residents in the Senate application and eligible unregistered seventh-district residents in the House application.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six direct joins control this rule and its application. Thomas supplies a seventh judgment vote under traditional strict scrutiny. O'Connor and Breyer dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ability to register does not answer the loss of an otherwise qualified political advocate. Buckley rejects voter status as the necessary qualification for the petition conversation where retained mechanisms serve the actual verification task more directly. Lerman establishes that the candidate setting preserves that protected function. The candidates here lost willing advocates and incurred additional expense; the burden is a restriction on their chosen speakers, not simply a step in counting electoral support.

Accessible enrollment does distinguish a resident from a person who would have to move. It does not establish why the resident's voter status is needed to solicit or attest qualified signatures. Qualified signers show support; certification, challenges and fraud enforcement address reliability. The formal nomination setting supplies no sufficient functional distinction from Buckley's protected-speaker reasoning. This is a substantial restriction on circulation that those asserted interests do not justify.

The partial dissent would preserve enrollment only for eligible residents while leaving outsiders free of it, limiting relief to the applications it considers unconstitutional. The Court rejects the remaining resident restriction on its own merits: the same protected task and verification means leave that exclusion unjustified. ### IV. The existing injunction protects the class against both common barriers

**Holding and operative rule:** The injunction against both challenged circulator qualifications remains operative for the certified candidate class and the responsible officials within the court's jurisdiction. Its enforcement must give effect to the invalidation of nonregistration and residence outside the represented electorate as grounds for rejecting otherwise valid circulation, while preserving independent lawful signature requirements.

**Authority:** Part IV is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven direct joins control full affirmance and implementation. O'Connor and Breyer join its geographic application only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the existing class injunction matches the common conditions adjudicated here. A candidate-specific exemption would leave those same conditions operating against the exposed class. Buckley and Lerman distinguish invalid speaker exclusions from the retained means of verifying qualified electoral support. That distinction gives the affirmed order its operative scope: officials must implement the invalidations through the procedures specified in the judgment, and lawful independent defects remain adjudicable. The existing injunction supplies the vehicle for that enforcement.

## Holdings

### I. The candidates' injuries and continuing class claim permit decision

**Holding and operative rule:** A candidate's loss of willing petition advocates and diversion of campaign resources are concrete injuries even when enough signatures are ultimately collected. Here the continuing certified class of candidates exposed to the challenged provision supplies the live prospective controversy after the named candidates' election ended.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls the threshold determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that eventual ballot qualification does not restore the political association and resources lost through the challenged exclusions. Buckley recognizes the protected conversation conducted by petition advocates; Lerman applies that protection to candidate petitions. Those injuries belong to the candidates themselves because the qualifications restrict their chosen means of communicating and obtaining support. Prospective relief requires a continuing controversy as well as past injury. The certified class covers candidates whose nominations have been or will be challenged under section 7-10 and remains exposed to the common conditions. That class controversy supplies the present basis for decision. Lerman's supported individual repeat exposure and this case's class exposure are distinct grounds for reaching a continuing election restriction.

### II. The State and district boundaries cannot exclude these advocates

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments by excluding otherwise qualified, accountable candidate-petition advocates because they reside outside Illinois for a Senate nomination or outside the seventh congressional district for the House nomination, including residents of other States. Under Anderson–Burdick, courts weigh the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise asserted interests and the extent to which those interests require the restriction: severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests, while reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests; these direct geographic exclusions impose severe burdens that the asserted grounds fail to justify.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight direct joins control both applications and the graduated method. Thomas supports both judgments under traditional strict scrutiny.

**Controlling explanation:** Meyer protects the political conversation involved in collecting signatures. Buckley requires examination of the speakers excluded and the actual verification need. Anderson and Burdick preserve reasonable, nondiscriminatory administration while requiring correspondingly stronger justification for severe burdens. Lerman establishes that candidate witnessing retains this expressive character and invalidates an unsupported in-state district screen. Requiring an advocate to relocate imposes the same severe exclusion here.

Qualified signers demonstrate local electoral support, and qualified voters select nominees. Circulators ask for that support and communicate the candidates' views. State or district residence does not establish boundary knowledge or the ability to obtain valid signatures. Circulator certification and the signature-challenge process address those tasks directly. The candidate's eventual success and access to other speakers do not restore the excluded association. Cartwright's valid support requirement measures the electorate's support, a different function.

The interstate application also requires its own justification. Lerman's statewide process does not establish Illinois authority over every nonresident. But the Board's actual local-support, valid-signature, integrity and outside-influence grounds supply no necessary connection between residence and this advocacy. The Board did not advance the process-consent or demonstrated nonresident-fraud grounds identified by the court below. Enforcement difficulty cannot be presumed from a boundary alone. Reserving voting to Illinois electors does not justify restricting candidates' association with outside advocates; a desire to exclude their political influence targets the protected association itself. These reasons defeat both geographic applications.

### III. Otherwise eligible residents may circulate without voter enrollment

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois may not exclude otherwise qualified, accountable candidate-petition advocates merely because they are unregistered voters, including persons already resident in and eligible to register within the represented electorate. Under the graduated standard stated in Part II, the substantial restriction on the candidates' chosen advocates is not justified by the asserted nomination, local-support and signature-verification interests; the injunction therefore protects eligible unregistered Illinois residents in the Senate application and eligible unregistered seventh-district residents in the House application.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six direct joins control this rule and its application. Thomas supplies a seventh judgment vote under traditional strict scrutiny. O'Connor and Breyer dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ability to register does not answer the loss of an otherwise qualified political advocate. Buckley rejects voter status as the necessary qualification for the petition conversation where retained mechanisms serve the actual verification task more directly. Lerman establishes that the candidate setting preserves that protected function. The candidates here lost willing advocates and incurred additional expense; the burden is a restriction on their chosen speakers, not simply a step in counting electoral support.

Accessible enrollment does distinguish a resident from a person who would have to move. It does not establish why the resident's voter status is needed to solicit or attest qualified signatures. Qualified signers show support; certification, challenges and fraud enforcement address reliability. The formal nomination setting supplies no sufficient functional distinction from Buckley's protected-speaker reasoning. This is a substantial restriction on circulation that those asserted interests do not justify.

The partial dissent would preserve enrollment only for eligible residents while leaving outsiders free of it, limiting relief to the applications it considers unconstitutional. The Court rejects the remaining resident restriction on its own merits: the same protected task and verification means leave that exclusion unjustified. Relief against the whole challenged condition follows because its resident application also violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments, rather than from a need to make every remedial line uniform.
