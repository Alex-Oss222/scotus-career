#### A. LEGAL POSTURE

#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

* **Stone’s Controlled Judgment or Disposition:** Vacate the interlocutory judgment to the extent necessary to apply the holdings below and remand both constitutional theories. Reject criminal nonuse as a categorical answer to the Fifth Amendment claim and reject a chase-specific intent requirement for the independent hospital-interrogation claim.
* **Stone’s Remedy and Remand Position:** Martinez may pursue damages for a constitutional injury he proves Chavez personally caused. The court must determine each surviving theory and the applicable personal defense before entering a liability judgment; damages require their own causal proof. The remand attributes neither the shooting nor an interruption of care to Chavez.
* **Remedial Reach:** This is an individual damages action with unresolved facts. Relief concerns Martinez’s proved injury and Chavez’s conduct, not a class or an unproved interrogation policy.
* **Practical Relief:** On return of the mandate, the lower court must identify the genuinely disputed material facts, permit their resolution through ordinary procedure, and apply the announced rules to the recorded questioning, medical interruptions, and available deliberation time. Chavez must have an opportunity to establish the proposed reliance defense. A compensatory judgment must identify the proved interrogation loss rather than borrow the shooting loss. #### C. ISSUE POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: The privilege protects against completed testimonial compulsion

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** Criminal nonuse is undisputed, so it presents a legal question within interlocutory review. Historical disputes about the encounter remain for the lower court on the properly supported plaintiff-favorable account.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Does deliberate coercive extraction of self-incriminating testimony violate the privilege without later criminal use, and does Verdugo-Urquidez foreclose that answer?
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Resolve the open no-use issue in favor of a completed constitutional violation; treat Verdugo-Urquidez’s trial-right discussion as dicta.
* **Reach Calibration:** Broad. Constitutional text, the privilege’s protective function, and the presented no-use question justify a rule governing deliberate testimonial coercion. A sweeping Miranda damages rule would erase the distinction between compulsion and warnings. An intermediate use-dependent rule would leave the asserted compelled act unprotected whenever prosecution is abandoned.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** An officer violates the Self-Incrimination Clause by deliberately coercing self-incriminating testimonial answers in a criminal investigation when the person’s will is overborne, whether or not the answers are later used. Martinez must prove by a preponderance of the evidence the officer’s coercive act and the testimonial and incriminating character of the answers. Compensatory damages require separate proof of causal loss; failure to establish such loss does not defeat the completed constitutional violation or otherwise available nominal relief. Warnings alone, unwanted questioning alone, and vulnerability not exploited by the officer do not satisfy that rule. Severe pain and repeated pleas support the inquiry here but do not establish its contested predicates by appellate finding.
* **Reach Within the Question:** The holding addresses actual governmental testimonial compulsion in a criminal investigation. It does not convert every evidentiary exclusion or Miranda omission into §1983 damages.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Dickerson makes Miranda’s case-in-chief safeguards constitutional but does not decide this civil claim. McKune protects against substantial automatic penalties for refusing incriminating disclosures before a prosecution; its reasoning confirms that protection need not await use, although its remedy was not damages. Verdugo-Urquidez decided the Fourth Amendment’s territorial reach, so its unnecessary description of the Fifth Amendment cannot settle this different injury. The privilege’s text and function carry decisive weight: an officer’s completed compulsion cannot become permissible because another official declines prosecution. Mincey’s involuntary hospital statements illustrate why a treatment setting demands attention to actual pressure, without making an exclusion ruling sufficient proof of civil liability.
* **Precedent Treatment:** Reaffirm Dickerson’s constitutional safeguards without extending warning liability; extend McKune’s protection-before-use reasoning to the completed coercive act; decline Verdugo-Urquidez’s dictum as controlling. Distinguish Mincey’s exclusion remedy from the separate damages elements. Apply Carey’s distinction between a proved constitutional deprivation and actual compensatory loss, preserving otherwise available nominal relief without dispensing with proof of the violation or a valid personal defense.
* **Next-Case Guidance:** Lower courts must identify actual officer-caused compulsion rather than infer it from hospitalization or lack of warnings. They must decide the disputed interrogation facts here under that rule.
* **Transition:** The new no-use holding governs this case and civil cases still open on direct review, including claims concerning earlier conduct subject to the separately adjudicated personal defense. It does not reopen final judgments or supply a collateral-review vehicle, because a merits rule does not eliminate finality and remedial prerequisites.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** The record lacks findings establishing the contested coercion and injury; the lower court must make them. Other officers’ shooting liability is outside this grant.

##### Issue 2: Independent due-process abuse and a properly bounded personal defense

* **Threshold and Merits Reach:** The preserved due-process theory survives independently of Issue 1. The Court may decide its legal standard and immunity rule, but may not resolve genuine historical disputes through the interlocutory appeal.
* **Question Stone Resolves:** Whether the alleged hospital interrogation can constitute conscience-shocking executive abuse, and what personal defense applies to an established violation.
* **Stone’s Legal Position:** Apply Lewis’s existing contextual rule; propose replacing Harlow and Anderson’s freestanding clearly-established-law defense with actual reasonable reliance on authorizing law, expressly overruling Denius’s contrary immunity rule.
* **Reach Calibration:** Broad. A complete merits inquiry followed by a defined reliance defense protects bodily liberty while preserving genuine legal authorization. A sweeping elimination of every defense would disregard reasonable reliance and substantive factual-mistake allowances. An intermediate immunity rule requiring a materially identical earlier opinion would undermine protection against the alleged deliberate abuse.
* **Stone’s Proposed Holding:** Deliberate exploitation of extreme medical distress can violate substantive due process when, in its actual deliberative context, it constitutes conscience-shocking abuse rather than a justified effort to obtain information. Martinez must prove Chavez’s own culpable conduct and causation of the constitutional deprivation by a preponderance of the evidence; compensable loss is a separate remedial showing, and evidentiary involuntariness is relevant but not conclusive. After an established violation, Chavez may avoid individual damages only by proving identified authoritative law directly authorizing the conduct, genuine reliance, and an objectively reasonable basis for treating that authority as applicable and valid. The official bears the preponderance burden on disputed reliance facts; the court independently determines the asserted authority’s legal content. Legal silence, general investigative power, and another official’s assurance do not suffice.
* **Reach Within the Question:** Medical pauses, continuing treatment, urgency, repeated distress, and actual opportunities to stop all matter. The substantive standard retains reasonable factual judgments; the proposed defense addresses legal authorization and individual damages rather than creating a cause of action or municipal liability.
* **Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Lewis’s purpose-to-harm requirement responded to a rapidly evolving chase with no practical deliberation. It is not a universal threshold for an officer who can pause and reassess hospital questioning. The roughly ten minutes of questioning over forty-five minutes requires actual evaluation of those opportunities, not an inference that either an emergency or deliberate abuse necessarily existed throughout. Harlow and Anderson presently protect objective legal uncertainty. Denius expressly rejected actual-authoritative-reliance as a prerequisite to that defense; Saucier’s binding remand retained the existing objective inquiry, and Hope confirmed that an official need not have consulted the law. Stone would overrule Denius’s contrary immunity rule and displace those decisions’ retention of objective immunity to this stated extent, preserving their distinct merits and procedural holdings. The substantive contextual rule already accounts for urgency and reasonable factual mistakes, so he would require actual reasonable reliance rather than permit legal uncertainty alone to defeat an otherwise established violation. Section 1983’s accountability function supplies the decisive reason for requiring genuine authorizing law rather than a prior factual twin.
* **Precedent Treatment:** Reaffirm Lewis’s contextual executive-abuse standard and distinguish its chase-specific culpability rule. Replace Harlow and Anderson’s objective-uncertainty defense at the stated scope and expressly overrule Denius’s rejection of an actual-authoritative-reliance requirement, while preserving its intimate-information and legal-consultation holdings. Displace Saucier, Elder and Hope only insofar as they retain the replaced immunity rule; preserve their independent legal-review, interlocutory and no-factual-twin principles. Under the fallback, all relevant November 1997 authority must be considered, and later decisions furnish no postdated notice.
* **Next-Case Guidance:** On remand, separate the supported facts, constitutional culpability, causal injury, and any actual legal reliance. A legitimate request to preserve a witness’s account is not automatically abusive, but investigative importance does not authorize deliberate exploitation of distress.
* **Transition:** The proposed defense governs this and other nonfinal civil cases, with an opportunity to prove actual reliance even for earlier conduct. Final judgments remain final absent an independently lawful reopening ground; collateral proceedings retain their own limits. The substantive Lewis inquiry is an application of existing law, not a new universal culpability rule.
* **Boundary or Reserved Question:** Specific reliance, disputed interrogation conduct, and injury need findings absent from the interlocutory record. The conduct of the shooting officers lies outside the question.
* **Conditional Position or Fallback:** If criminal use remains necessary, dismiss only that Fifth Amendment theory and preserve the independent due-process inquiry. If objective immunity remains, apply November 1997 law, including Mincey and relevant circuit authority at their actual specificity; Lewis, Dickerson, and Hope cannot themselves furnish earlier notice. Neither fallback treats the interrogation as lawful by default.

## Controlling holdings: canonical text

### I. The precedent question

**Holding and operative rule:** United States v. Verdugo-Urquidez's description of the Self-Incrimination Clause as a trial right did not decide whether domestic coercive interrogation can support the civil claim presented here. That comparison is not a binding holding on this question; its persuasive force must be assessed with the authorities that actually address the privilege.

**Authority:** Part I of the lead opinion is joined directly by all nine Justices and is the opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a decision's explanation must be read against the question it resolved. Verdugo-Urquidez concerned the Fourth Amendment's application to a foreign search of a nonresident alien's property. Its comparison with the Fifth Amendment did not adjudicate this domestic interrogation claim, its cause of action or its remedy. Treating that comparison as a holding would substitute an answer to a different case for examination of the criminal-case language, prospective privilege and actual-compulsion authorities. Its status does not decide the present claim in Martinez's favor: the Justices disagree about that claim on the separate grounds identified below.

**Precedent treatment:** Verdugo-Urquidez retains its foreign-search holding; its Fifth Amendment comparison has persuasive rather than controlling force on this damages question.

### II. The Self-Incrimination Clause claim

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The judgment sustaining this Self-Incrimination Clause damages theory is reversed. O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer support that disposition; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg oppose it. No common rationale commands five votes.

**Authority and explanation:** Part II of the lead opinion, adopted by Souter and Breyer, concludes that Martinez has not shown a need to add this freestanding damages protection to the existing privilege, immunity, penalty and exclusion protections. Thomas's separate Part A, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, instead concludes that the absence of a criminal case or use defeats the asserted completed violation under the Clause's text. The first rationale turns on the justification for additional protection and could recognize such protection on a different showing; the second turns on an absent constitutional event. Neither is a logical subset establishing the same outcomes whenever it applies as the other. Marks therefore supplies no unified controlling rationale. The reversal binds this claim.

**Precedent treatment:** Dickerson's constitutional case-in-chief safeguards and McKune's protection against substantial automatic penalties for refusing incriminating disclosures remain in force. **Precedent question.** Verdugo-Urquidez decided the Fourth Amendment's application to the foreign search of a nonresident alien's property. **Fifth Amendment path A — reject this no-use damages theory.** The text protects against compelled witness status in a criminal case. The prospective ability to assert the privilege, receive sufficient use protection, or resist a substantial penalty does not necessarily turn every improperly extracted statement into a completed damages claim. Dickerson establishes Miranda's constitutional case-in-chief protection but expressly supplies no automatic civil remedy. McKune preserves prospective protection against substantial automatic penalties without deciding this damages question. The strongest contrary argument is that a right to refuse an incriminating answer cannot be meaningful if deliberate compulsion is constitutionally harmless until use. The response is the continuing prospective privilege, exclusion, and independent constitutional prohibition of abusive methods; the response must not reduce every right outside trial to a nonconstitutional aspiration.

**Fifth Amendment path B — permit the actual-compulsion claim.** Actual official compulsion of incriminating testimony invades the privilege when it defeats protected silence; subsequent nonuse does not undo that invasion. The penalty decisions, Miranda's protection of choice during questioning, and the distinction between actual coercion and a merely omitted warning support that route. Cooper supplies particularly relevant preconduct circuit authority. The strongest objection is that the Court's exclusion and immunity cases protect against criminal use rather than recognize every form of extraction as an independently completed damages wrong. The path therefore requires actual incriminating compulsion, not a Miranda omission alone, and must separately justify conduct-date notice and personal responsibility.

**Due-process path A — preserve the claim for its independent analysis.** The conscience-shocking-abuse inquiry concerns Chavez's own methods and culpability in the actual hospital setting. Deliberate exploitation of acute pain and fear to overcome a person's will can be executive abuse without a later prosecution, without the interrogator firing the shots, and without a universal physical-injury prerequisite. Lewis requires attention to real opportunity for deliberation and competing obligations; its high-speed-pursuit purpose-to-harm rule is not a universal hospital-interrogation test. Rochin and Mincey are material to the character of coercive methods; Cooper confirms the preconduct circuit distinction between actual abusive compulsion and a warning defect. The strongest defense is the legitimate need to preserve a potentially dying witness's account, short aggregate questioning and continued treatment. Those facts matter, but do not conclusively establish the asserted legitimate purpose or negate deliberate abuse under plaintiff-favorable assumptions.

**Due-process path B — direct a defense judgment.** Even unwanted, exclusion-producing questioning is not necessarily egregious executive abuse. The supplied individual conduct consists of questions during continuing care, with pauses for treatment, not Chavez's infliction of the wounds or withholding of necessary care. Alternatively, pre-1997 exclusion decisions and Cooper's calculated, prolonged interrogation may not have made personal damages liability apparent in this hospital setting. The strongest objection is that the official may deliberately exploit an existing wound as effectively as a wound he inflicted, and Cooper rejects both a physical-brutality prerequisite and a factual-twin immunity demand. A defense vote must therefore identify the deficient constitutional or notice premise, not invent a general permission to coerce an uncharged patient.

## Holdings

### I. The precedent question

**Holding and operative rule:** United States v. Verdugo-Urquidez's description of the Self-Incrimination Clause as a trial right did not decide whether domestic coercive interrogation can support the civil claim presented here. That comparison is not a binding holding on this question; its persuasive force must be assessed with the authorities that actually address the privilege.

**Authority:** Part I of the lead opinion is joined directly by all nine Justices and is the opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a decision's explanation must be read against the question it resolved. Verdugo-Urquidez concerned the Fourth Amendment's application to a foreign search of a nonresident alien's property. Its comparison with the Fifth Amendment did not adjudicate this domestic interrogation claim, its cause of action or its remedy. Treating that comparison as a holding would substitute an answer to a different case for examination of the criminal-case language, prospective privilege and actual-compulsion authorities. Its status does not decide the present claim in Martinez's favor: the Justices disagree about that claim on the separate grounds identified below.

**Precedent treatment:** Verdugo-Urquidez retains its foreign-search holding; its Fifth Amendment comparison has persuasive rather than controlling force on this damages question.

### II. The Self-Incrimination Clause claim

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The judgment sustaining this Self-Incrimination Clause damages theory is reversed. O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer support that disposition; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg oppose it. No common rationale commands five votes.

**Authority and explanation:** Part II of the lead opinion, adopted by Souter and Breyer, concludes that Martinez has not shown a need to add this freestanding damages protection to the existing privilege, immunity, penalty and exclusion protections. Thomas's separate Part A, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, instead concludes that the absence of a criminal case or use defeats the asserted completed violation under the Clause's text. The first rationale turns on the justification for additional protection and could recognize such protection on a different showing; the second turns on an absent constitutional event. Neither is a logical subset establishing the same outcomes whenever it applies as the other. Marks therefore supplies no unified controlling rationale. The reversal binds this claim.

**Precedent treatment:** Dickerson's constitutional case-in-chief safeguards and McKune's protection against substantial automatic penalties for refusing incriminating disclosures remain in force. Neither earlier holding decided this civil-damages claim. Marks preserves the distinction between a majority judgment and a common governing rationale.

### III. The independent due-process claim and objective immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Criminal nonuse does not dispose of a separately supported claim that an officer's own interrogation methods constituted conscience-shocking executive abuse. The availability, scope and merits of that claim require a context-sensitive inquiry into the officer's conduct, knowledge, opportunity to deliberate, legitimate investigative needs and causal responsibility, together with separate consideration of the asserted objective defense under law existing when the officer acted.

**Authority:** Part III is the opinion of the Court, joined directly by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These six adopt the independent inquiry and exact remand instruction. Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg additionally state stronger application conclusions in their separate writings.

**Controlling explanation:** Lewis makes the required culpability responsive to the executive setting. Its purpose-to-harm rule for a rapidly evolving pursuit does not govern every hospital interrogation. Reasonable urgent questioning and voluntary disclosure differ from deliberate exploitation of extreme suffering to overcome resistance. Conversely, Mincey's exclusion of an involuntary hospital statement does not by itself establish this civil constitutional wrong. The lower court must analyze the independent claim rather than equate coercion for exclusion with damages liability.

Harlow and Anderson preserve particularized objective immunity. Elder requires all relevant conduct-date authority, while Hope rejects an indispensable factual twin. The November 28, 1997 inquiry must examine Rochin's brutal-investigation boundary, Mincey's actual hospital-coercion rule, Cooper v. Dupnik's actual circuit recognition of coercion-based civil claims, and other relevant then-existing authority. Cooper's planned and prolonged interrogation and its distinction between coercion and warnings must be considered at their actual scope. Lewis, Dickerson and Hope organize current analysis but cannot supply later notice. The legitimate purpose of obtaining an injured witness's account does not alone answer whether the methods employed were constitutionally abusive. Continuing treatment likewise does not alone resolve that question. Johnson and Behrens preserve the boundary between legal immunity review on supported assumptions and resolution of disputed historical facts.

**Precedent treatment:** Lewis is applied in its contextual rather than universal-intent scope. Rochin and Mincey retain their investigative-abuse and exclusion functions. Harlow, Anderson, Denius, Elder, Saucier and Hope retain objective immunity and their distinct procedural limits; the remand applies that existing defense. Johnson and Behrens preserve the interlocutory fact boundary.

## Precedent Treatment

- Verdugo-Urquidez retains its Fourth Amendment foreign-search holding; its Fifth Amendment comparison does not settle this civil claim.
- Dickerson retains Miranda's constitutional case-in-chief protection, and McKune retains its prospective protection against substantial automatic penalties. The Fifth Amendment judgment supplies no common new general rationale replacing those holdings.
- Lewis supplies the setting-sensitive executive-abuse inquiry; its rapid-pursuit purpose-to-harm rule remains tied to that setting.
- Rochin and Mincey supply the relevant investigative-abuse and hospital-coercion authorities, with their actual remedies respected. Cooper v. Dupnik is relevant preconduct circuit authority at its actual scope rather than binding Supreme Court merits law.
- Harlow and Anderson retain particularized objective immunity. Denius retains its express rejection of an actual-reliance prerequisite. Elder requires all relevant preconduct law; Saucier retains the separate defense and interlocutory limits; Hope rejects an indispensable factual twin. These current principles do not backdate notice.
- Johnson and Behrens retain the distinction between assumed-fact legal review and factual-sufficiency disputes.
- Marks supplies no unified Fifth Amendment rationale for the two different judgment-supporting grounds.

## Law After Decision

The Fifth Amendment damages theory in this case fails under a binding majority judgment without a common rationale. Verdugo-Urquidez supplies no binding answer to the distinct interrogation question. Independently supported executive-abuse claims require their own contextual merits and objective conduct-date immunity analysis; absence of prosecution does not dispose of them. The Court's due-process instruction preserves both lawful immunity and continuation of a supported claim, depending on that analysis.

## Separate Writings

**Souter and Breyer, lead Part II.** The existing system protects the privilege through prospective assertion, adequate use and derivative-use immunity, protection against penalties for refusing unprotected disclosures, and exclusion. An additional extraction-based damages protection requires a demonstrated need and a limiting principle sufficient to justify its costs. Martinez has not established that need here. They reject a categorical claim that the privilege operates only at trial. They expressly leave open whether absence of Miranda warnings can support a section 1983 action in any circumstances; this actual-compulsion case does not present that warning-only question.

**Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** O'Connor and Scalia join Part A: the criminal-case condition has not occurred, so the asserted completed Self-Incrimination Clause violation fails. The privilege may still be asserted prospectively, compelled testimony requires sufficient use protection, and government may not demand surrender of immunity as a condition of public employment or contracting. McKune's automatic-penalty holding remains applicable within its scope. The precise commencement of a criminal case need not be fixed where none occurred. In Part B1, joined by Scalia, Thomas would reject the substantive-due-process claim on the attributed interrogation conduct: personal culpability and a justified constitutional ground are required, and evidentiary coercion alone cannot establish the extension sought. In Part B2, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, he independently would grant conduct-date immunity. Rochin and Mincey establish serious investigative limits, but their exclusion settings and Cooper's materially different planned interrogation did not sufficiently settle this hospital-questioning damages application in November 1997. O'Connor's agreement rests only on that personal defense and does not declare the conduct constitutional. Neither due-process ground demands additional physical injury in every case.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stevens joins all three parts; Ginsburg joins Parts II and III. Part I distinguishes mere failure to give warnings, for which Kennedy and Stevens accept ordinary exclusion as the protection, from actual coercion. Part II treats severe official compulsion of incriminating testimony as an immediate invasion of the privilege; Kastigar and the penalty cases protect the choice before prosecution, and Verdugo-Urquidez decided a different issue. Part III permits legitimate questioning of a person in pain, including urgent efforts and voluntary accounts, but finds intentional exploitation of suffering to extract testimony constitutionally different. On the supported plaintiff-favorable exploitation account, the preconduct investigative and coercion authorities defeat the asserted legal defense. He joins the Court's remand while retaining that stronger application. He makes no new finding that Chavez threatened to delay treatment.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The supported brutal interrogation immediately invaded protected liberty. Miranda's constitutional status, the immunity and penalty cases, and Mincey's treatment of abusive hospital questioning support a distinction between actual compulsion and a warning omission. Rochin, Mincey and Cooper supply the pertinent preconduct warning; his agreement with the remand preserves his conclusion that the asserted legal defense should fail on that account.

**Ginsburg, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Helplessness, extreme distress, repeated pleas and persistent questioning together support actual unconstitutional compulsion and due-process abuse. Her conclusion does not depend on a finding that Martinez believed treatment would be withheld or that Chavez intended that impression. She joins Kennedy's actual-compulsion and due-process parts, but not his warning-only part; she adopts no categorical warning-remedy bar. She would reject the asserted legal defense on that totality while joining the independent remand.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He would recognize a completed Self-Incrimination Clause violation when an officer deliberately overbears a person's will to obtain self-incriminating testimonial answers in a criminal investigation, whether or not prosecution follows. Martinez would prove the coercive act and testimonial and incriminating character by a preponderance. Unwanted questioning, warning omission, or vulnerability unexploited by the officer would not suffice. Dickerson's constitutional safeguards and McKune's before-use protection support that rule; Mincey's exclusion remedy remains distinct. Compensatory loss requires separate causal proof; otherwise available nominal relief remains possible after a proved violation and subject to the personal defense and preserved claim.

He also would replace Harlow and Anderson's objective-uncertainty defense with protection for proved genuine and objectively reasonable reliance on identified authoritative law directly authorizing the conduct. The officer would bear the preponderance burden on disputed reliance facts, while the court determines the authority's legal content; silence, general investigative power and another official's assurance would not suffice. That proposal would overrule Denius's contrary immunity rule and displace Saucier, Elder and Hope only to the extent they retain that defense, preserving their distinct substantive and procedural rules. His proposed remand would afford Chavez an opportunity to prove actual reliance for the earlier conduct. Under the existing objective defense, he joins the Court's due-process remand and its November 1997 notice inquiry. He would also remand the Fifth Amendment theory for the actual coercion, personal causation, defense and injury determinations.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit. The lower court must give effect to reversal of the Self-Incrimination Clause damages theory and determine the independent due-process claim's availability, scope and merits. It must identify the supported historical assumptions, assess Chavez's own methods, knowledge, deliberative opportunity, legitimate needs and causal responsibility, and separately determine objective immunity from all relevant law existing by November 28, 1997. Material historical disputes must be resolved in the proper forum under ordinary procedure. A surviving claim proceeds to the personal-responsibility, causation, injury and lawful remedial determinations; a sufficient legal defense warrants judgment on that claim.
