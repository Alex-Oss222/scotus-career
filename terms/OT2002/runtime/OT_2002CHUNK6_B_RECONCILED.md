## Johnson & Johnston Associates Inc. v. R.E. Service Co., Inc.

### Reconciled judgment and common propositions

All eight Associates affirm the Federal Circuit's judgment: no infringement by equivalents under the asserted patent, with the dependent willfulness determination and damages, fees and expenses removed. They join the same sufficient legal ground. When a patent specification expressly identifies a particular substitute for a claimed component but the issued claims select the other component and omit that identified substitute, the patentee may not use equivalents to recapture the substitute under that patent. The rule is objective; neither subjective intent to surrender nor an individual competitor's reliance is required.

Here the claims specify aluminum, the specification expressly identifies stainless steel as an alternative, and the accused products use steel. Literal noninfringement and factual equivalence remain accepted. The latter does not answer the legal availability of equivalents. The retained doctrine continues to protect qualifying equivalents outside this precise express-disclosure boundary. No general foreseeability bar, mandatory separate-patentability inquiry, inferred prosecution amendment or finding of deceptive drafting is adopted. The existence of continuation patents does not change the boundary of this patent or decide rights under another patent.

### Comparator verification and adversarial comparison

There is no historical Supreme Court merits alignment to replicate or depart from. The en banc judgment below was twelve to one, with the separate writings identified above. It is the judgment under review and persuasive primary analysis, not a prediction source giving any Associate a vote. Rader's general foreseeability proposal was not the en banc court's sufficient rule; Lourie's objections identify its new factual and technical burdens. Clevenger's concern is coherence of lower-court precedent. Dyk supplies factual distinctions concerning Graver Tank and a conjecture about preservation; that conjecture is not an established Supreme Court ground. Newman gives the strongest opposing account: informative disclosure should not itself destroy equivalents, the surviving Graver Tank claims omitted manganese, and separate claims in a continuation negate subjective abandonment. Each argument must be answered at its actual scope.

The eight retain the specificity route because the issued instrument expressly presents steel as the unclaimed alternative to aluminum. Section 112 requires disclosure and definition of claimed subject matter; those distinct functions do not make every informative disclosure an enforceable claim. Markman and Vitronics place the public instrument's legal meaning with the court without eliminating technical evidence or external infringement factfinding. Warner-Jenkinson preserves element-specific equivalents and requires legal boundaries to precede residual infringement facts. Festo governs actual patentability-related narrowing and its rebuttable surrender consequences; no amendment is established here, and neither its trigger nor its purpose and scope burdens may be imported as a fictitious reason to decide this case.

Graver Tank remains in force. Its surviving narrower claims supported equivalents, but its original broader issued claims encompassed the accused subject matter before being invalidated. That history distinguishes the entirely unclaimed expressly identified substitute here; it does not permit describing the broader claims as surviving or the equivalent as within the surviving literal claims. Miller v. Bridgeport Brass and Mahn v. Harwood support the claim-and-disclosure notice principle in their reissue settings, rather than deciding this equivalents question themselves. Newman is correct that claim primacy alone cannot abolish equivalents; the additional express-alternative trigger supplies the bounded legal distinction. Neither Newman's general innovation argument nor Rader's general foreseeability proposal provides a reason to change an individual commitment on this record.

## Cook County v. United States ex rel. Chandler

### Reconciled judgment and common propositions

All eight Associates affirm the Seventh Circuit's rejection of categorical county exclusion. A county acting as a municipal corporation is a person subject to the FCA's civil false-claims provisions in a private-relator action. The 1986 increase from double to treble damages did not implicitly remove municipal corporations from that covered class. The action may proceed to adjudication of the actual statutory elements and defenses; the ruling awards no damages and finds no fraud.

The original civil person category included municipal corporations in its legal setting. The complete 1863 enactment and Cowles's official report confirm the relevant text and corporate setting; Cowles decided federal suability on county obligations, not FCA liability. The County's distinction between voluntarily incorporated cities and state-created counties does not remove counties' legal corporate personality. The earlier criminal provisions' reference to natural persons and imprisonment does not make every civil defendant a natural person. Separate statutory remedies apply according to their terms and the entity to which they sensibly apply.

A fixed statutory damages multiplier can serve punitive and compensatory purposes. The multiplier may compensate for recovery delay and ancillary loss and accommodate a relator's share, while exceeding full compensation in particular cases. The county-coverage conclusion requires neither the fiction that all treble damages are compensation nor an award calculation in this suit. City of Newport's municipal punitive-damages presumption in the §1983 setting does not erase the FCA's existing defendant category when Congress strengthens its remedy. The presumption against implied repeal and unchanged class language answer the County's proposed removal from all FCA liability.

## Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Ayers

### Reconciled judgments and proposition coalitions

Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg affirm the judgments on both reviewed grounds. O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer affirm rejection of nonrailroad-exposure apportionment but would set aside the damages judgments on the cancer-fear component and remand for damages proceedings under their narrower injury rules. The latter three do not seek a defense judgment on the underlying asbestosis claims. All eight join the same indivisible-injury rule. The five affirmers directly join the same bounded fear-eligibility rule and its application to the preserved categorical objection. The three opponents' narrower rules must not be counted as joins in that rule merely because all require genuine injury.

The five-Associate fear rule is: An employee suffering from actionable asbestosis caused in whole or in part by the railroad's negligence may recover for genuine and serious fear of cancer as a present element of the pain and suffering associated with that physical disease. Neither probable future cancer nor a separate physical manifestation of the emotional injury is indispensable; the employee must prove the fear, its genuineness and seriousness, and its relationship to the physical injury or the negligent conduct causing it. Asbestosis alone does not establish the emotional injury, and neither future cancer nor increased disease risk is itself awarded here.

The eight-Associate allocation rule is: Once railroad negligence contributes to an indivisible injury covered by §51, the railroad is answerable to the employee for that injury's damages without a reduction merely reflecting other employers' shares of asbestos exposure. Negligence, causal contribution and actual indivisibility remain necessary; proof of genuinely divisible harm presents a different attribution question. Section 53's employee-comparative-negligence adjustment, the actual settlement credits and any contribution or indemnity remedy under its own applicable law remain distinct. Section 53 also forbids an employee-negligence diminution where a contributing railroad violation of an employee-safety statute is established; none is established here. No general federal contribution action is created.

### Actual questions, instruction and preserved remedy

The supplementary neutral facts are used as affirmative published recitations. Norfolk sought the conjunctive probability-of-cancer and physical-manifestation prerequisites. It did not request the separate genuine-and-serious charge, a particularized fear-damages verdict or plaintiff-specific sufficiency review of that character. The significantly-impaired-life-and-work formulation was presented neither at trial nor in this Court. The actual charge linked reasonable cancer fear to proven physical injury, and the surrounding directions prohibited damages for cancer or an increased risk of cancer. The general verdicts disclose no fear-specific amount.

Those facts sustain the five affirmers' distinction between categorical eligibility and an unpresented individualized proof or alternative-instruction challenge. The five do not hold every conceivable reasonable-fear charge adequate, find every worker's distress serious, or infer a particular fear award. A defendant may use ordinary proof review and request proper injury instructions; the absence of an itemized award does not itself require retrial. Their affirmance answers the actual categorical demands and leaves the existing judgments intact on the questions reviewed.

The independent supplement withdrew O'Connor's, Kennedy's and Thomas's initial seriousness-only instructional remand and proposed eight-Associate affirmance. Reconciliation accepts that withdrawal of the unsupported instruction-only route. It does not treat the initial category agreement as immune from comparison with the actual historical writings. The preserved question still permits a Justice to reject the broader injury category itself and seek a narrower legally compensable category. Such a dissent is different from ordering a new trial merely to add unrequested adjectives to an otherwise accepted rule.

### Competing legal grounds and available accommodations

**Bodily-injury and present-fear ground — Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.** Urie treats manifested occupational disease as statutory injury. Gottshall and Buckley sharply restrict stand-alone emotional claims and symptomless exposure, while their actual holdings do not bar emotional consequences of existing disease. Traditional bodily-injury damages, illustrated by the common-law rule reflected in Restatement §456, encompass emotional disturbance resulting from the bodily harm or the conduct causing it. The supported asbestos exposure, manifested asbestosis and testimony linking that exposure and disease to heightened cancer risks supply the relevant connection. A claimant need not show that asbestosis will itself transform into cancer. The claim seeks compensation for present fear, not the value of the unaccrued disease. Permitting a later action for distinct cancer does not compensate the present fear of a worker who never develops it, and it does not permit duplicate payment for the same injury.

The strongest opposing argument is that diagnosed disease provides an arbitrary vehicle for fear about another uncertain illness, threatens speculative awards and depletes compensation for future severely injured people. The five answer with the actual-disease category, genuine-and-serious proof, ordinary evidence review and the limited questions presented. They do not rely on an invented severity finding, reduce the adopted requirement to reasonable apprehension alone, or treat a resource prediction as an established fact about Norfolk's solvency. No new damages trial follows from a different unrequested charge.

**Direct-relationship category objection — O'Connor and Kennedy; Breyer joins subject to his further qualification below.** These Justices read the traditional bodily-injury exception to require a direct relation between the physical injury and the emotional harm. Correlation between the same exposure, asbestosis and possible later cancer does not by itself establish that relation for contemplative future-disease fear. They distinguish immediate fright and emotional consequences of the physical condition itself from learning of an uncertain associated disease risk. They would treat the latter as requiring the independent limits of Gottshall and Buckley rather than automatically carrying it into the ordinary bodily-injury damages category. Seriousness is necessary but does not alone solve that category objection. Their legal position does not assert a new medical finding that was absent from the neutral record; it evaluates the supported relationship and theory of recovery. The opposing majority ground answers that the negligent conduct producing the bodily harm can also produce the related fear, even without one disease biologically causing the other. O'Connor and Kennedy do not accept that as a sufficient reason to expand this category.

These Justices reject the current damages judgments because the charge authorized the disputed category, not merely because Norfolk failed to request their preferred words. Their objection would require damages proceedings excluding cancer-fear recovery on the present broad asbestosis-associated theory while preserving recovery for the proved physical disease and its otherwise compensable consequences. The general verdict prevents a court from simply subtracting a known fear amount, but the category error is the legal reason for their requested damages remand. They join no exposure-share deduction and seek no reopening of the underlying railroad-liability finding.

**Breyer's further limitation and exception.** Breyer agrees with the direct-relationship and speculative-valuation objections, but rejects an account making all cancer fear categorically unrecoverable. He would add to the genuine-and-serious requirement a rule generally denying recovery where all three conditions hold: actual development of the feared disease can neither be expected nor ruled out for many years; fear of that disease remains separately compensable if the disease occurs; and the fear concerns risks not significantly different in kind from the background risks all individuals face. If a condition does not hold, that general exclusion does not apply. Even if all three hold, unusually severe fear remains compensable when it significantly and detrimentally affects the claimant's ability to carry on everyday life and work. Physical manifestations, psychiatric treatment and professional testimony are not universal requirements of this alternative.

The strongest answer is that present asbestosis and the heightened mesothelioma testimony distinguish the risk from ordinary background risk, and that his additional impairment rule was unpresented. Breyer considers the category question sufficiently before the Court to adopt a stricter common-law boundary despite the absence of a request for this exact verbal formulation. He regards difficulty valuing incremental future-risk fear and preserving compensation for later disease as reasons to constrain the category; the five affirmers regard the existing bodily-injury boundary and ordinary trial controls as sufficient. Thus he opposes affirmance and seeks damages remand under his complete narrower rule. This handoff makes no claimant-specific sufficiency finding, assigns no amount to fear and invents no proof about everyday impairment. On remand his rule would govern the recoverable category and any properly available application proceedings; it would not guarantee a renewed award or require reopening evidence outside ordinary procedure. His qualified position cannot be flattened into either a universal fear bar or a mere requirement to add serious to the existing charge.

## Woodford v. Garceau

### Reconciled judgments and exact common rule

Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would vacate the Ninth Circuit's judgment granting habeas relief and remand for review under the applicable AEDPA merits standards. Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would reject the State's temporal challenge and affirm at the granted scope, preserving the existing appellate relief judgment. No Associate decides the underlying constitutional merits or the State's additional merits request on this transition record.

The five directly support this bounded common rule: For the ordinary chapter 153 amendments to §2254 governing adjudication of the habeas application, including the merits standard and replacement factual presumption at issue, pre-enactment counsel, stay and other preparatory proceedings do not preserve former law when no application requesting adjudication of the writ was filed before April 24, 1996. A filing that actually submits a habeas claim for merits adjudication must be assessed by its content, not its caption; a prospective issue specification supporting preparation and interim protection is different. The rule is specific to the statutes governing the merits application. It does not say that a habeas proceeding cannot exist before that application, invalidate McFarland's actual preparatory jurisdiction, or supply a universal commencement rule for every judicial or AEDPA purpose.

Garceau had counsel, a stay and leave-to-file proceedings before enactment. The District Court actually evaluated identified nonfrivolous issues in maintaining the stay. His specification identified issues that might be raised in a later petition; it was not a present request to adjudicate the writ. The actual merits application arrived July 2, 1996. The distinction is between the judicial functions and requested relief, not between an active court and an administrative docket or between identified and unidentified constitutional issues.

The three opposing Associates adopt the narrower first-preliminary-assessment route: Where the court issuing a §2251 stay has already assessed the prospects for habeas success under the then-governing §2254 standards before enactment, that pending habeas undertaking should retain those merits standards through the later application. The authenticated pre-enactment nonfrivolous-issues determination supplies that premise here. They need not decide whether an entirely unexamined appointment request alone would suffice, and do not call the specification a final merits application. Their rule is statutory and provision-specific; it is not a constitutional entitlement to every filing-date doctrine.

### Verified comparator and premise assessment

The historical Court reversed six to three. Among the Associates, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas joined the Court's opinion; O'Connor concurred in the judgment on a §2254-specific textual ground; Souter's dissent was joined by Ginsburg and Breyer. All actual writings and both majority footnotes were read at Cornell and cross-checked in the official report, 538 U.S. 202. The source specifies the preliminary issues determination and a prospective specification, and distinguishes the granted temporal question from the petitioner's additional request for a merits ruling.

The comparator's shorthand understated the dissent: its sufficient ground is the district court's first preliminary assessment of habeas success while protecting preparation, not the bare fact that counsel or stay papers had been filed. It also understated O'Connor's disagreement. She accepts §2254's application-date result but objects to using the general civil-action commencement analogy to question McFarland and to suggesting that the merits had never been before the court in any sense. Her own ground is that §2254, both before and after AEDPA, regulates an application for the writ. Those distinctions are retained rather than inherited historical opinion labels.

The simulated McFarland, Lindh and Carriger holdings were examined individually. McFarland recognizes qualifying preapplication counsel and limited §2251 pendency, not an all-purpose temporal rule. Lindh protects its already-filed noncapital application through the chapter 153/chapter 154 contrast. Carriger extends the relevant treatment to its already-filed capital application and identifies the particular merits and factual-presumption provisions. None makes a preliminary stay assessment a submitted application, and none expressly excludes the dissent's structural extension. Their greater precision and different authors do not constitute a material changed premise compelling Stevens to dissent or Breyer to join the application-date rule.

Abdur'Rahman's current-term distinction between procedural correction and renewed merits litigation was read in its full public context. It concerns the actual object and lawful channel of a Rule 60 request after an existing merits judgment; it does not decide when a first habeas undertaking begins for the transition provision. Its treatment of pre-AEDPA merits and later authorization proceedings is provision-specific. It provides an analogy to both sides but does not displace Breyer's first-look ground or Stevens's ability to distinguish a §2254 application from protective preparation. Clay likewise interprets the particular finality trigger rather than prescribing a universal beginning for habeas cases. ### Alternative paths and opinion-ready legal explanations

**Section 2254 application ground — Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The statute's pre- and post-AEDPA versions speak in terms of applications for a writ; the amended merits standard and factual presumption regulate adjudication of that application. This gives separate effect to McFarland's counsel and §2251 protection before the application and to Lindh/Carriger's protection once the specified application was pending at enactment. The court's genuine preliminary consideration of constitutional issues is consistent with protective authority without having received the merits request. The specification's prospective language and later July 2 filing permit that distinction on this record.

The strongest contrary argument is that §2251, the counsel statute and §2254 form a connected process, and the court had already used the former standards to decide whether preparation deserved protection. The five answer that those earlier standards governed the earlier protective decision, whereas §2254's application-triggered adjudicatory work began later. Statutory assistance and a court-set preparation deadline do not create a statutory promise that the later application will receive every prior review rule. This answer does not deny a real pending case or overrule the statutory appointment and stay decision. It is sufficient without the broad proposition that no habeas suit or case can begin until an application is filed, without a general civil-procedure rule overriding McFarland, and without deciding every amendment's temporal reach.

**First-preliminary-assessment ground — Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** Related statutory provisions should be read together. Counsel appointed to prepare a §2254 application sought §2251 protection, and the court assessed the potential claims' legal prospects before enactment. The transition rule should attach when the court first used the relevant habeas-relief standards in that continuing undertaking. Otherwise preparation protected as potentially meritorious under one standard could be displaced before the authorized work was complete, or counsel would have an incentive to rush an incomplete application. Lindh's structural inference and the concrete judicial assessment support retaining former law without treating every preparatory contact as sufficient.

The strongest contrary argument is that §2254's words attach to an application and that the specification expressly anticipated a later pleading. The three recognize that separate applications and interim proceedings perform different work. They nevertheless read the statutes together for this transition because the merits standard had already entered the protective judicial determination. Their rationale preserves the existing appellate writ against the State's temporal objection; it supplies no fresh constitutional merits adjudication and no universal right to permanent old law. The additional merits request is not a substitute for the specific ground and merits record the granted issue lacks.
