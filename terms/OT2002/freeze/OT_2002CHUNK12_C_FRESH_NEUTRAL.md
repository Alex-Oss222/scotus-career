## Oakley: the legally operative condition

The written sentence forbids additional children during the five-year probation term unless the court is shown that Oakley meets his existing children's needs and can meet the additional child's needs. That original wording has a financial-capacity feature. The five years follow incarceration; the sentence imposed three years on one count and imposed and stayed eight years on the others.

The Wisconsin Supreme Court majority authoritatively construed the condition more narrowly. Paragraph 20 states that efforts to support children as required by law, without intentional refusal, satisfy it. Footnote 21 treats support according to earning and payment ability as sufficient; footnotes 28–30 expressly distinguish willful refusal from income level and full financial ability. The federal review must assess that controlling state construction. It may not treat the condition as an absolute prohibition continuing against a parent making every legally required good-faith effort solely because he cannot fully support all children.

The written order's judicial-showing requirement remains part of the record; the majority did not replace its wording with a reproduced amended order. The construction specifies what suffices for compliance and restricts the permissible substantive showing. Bablitch's footnote 33 predicts amendment upon a good-faith showing; that prediction is not an entered amendment and is unnecessary to give the majority's construction its legal force. Bradley and the reconsideration concurrence dispute the consistency between the original language and the majority's account. Their objection identifies the textual tension, but does not authorize the reviewing Court to substitute the dissent's broader reading for the state court's controlling construction.

The live constitutional issue therefore concerns a direct, temporary reproductive restriction linked to intentional nonsupport and a judicial demonstration of lawful support effort. It is not a general wealth qualification imposed on every citizen, a permanent destruction of reproductive capacity, or an order prescribing contraception, sterilization, or abortion. Any constitutional objection must identify why the restriction remains impermissible after the narrowing construction is given full effect. Any sustainment must be confined to that construction, without approving revocation for inability despite genuine lawful efforts.

## Oakley: competing lawful paths and entering law

The strongest sustainment path treats the condition as individualized, time-limited supervision of a convicted intentional nonsupport offender. Compliance depends on lawful effort, so the condition does not punish inability; the State seeks rehabilitation and prevention of additional unsupported obligations. Griffin's probation principle and Turner permit genuine restrictions associated with lawful supervision. The objection to this path is that the State directly restricts conception outside confinement, rather than merely requiring support, and must justify that distinct interference even when another lawful sanction is more severe.

The strongest invalidation path accepts the state construction and argues that a direct restriction on having another child, contingent on judicial clearance of support behavior, goes beyond enforcing the financial obligation itself. Skinner protects procreation; Turner rejects automatic extinction of family liberty following conviction; Zablocki distinguishes direct interference with family choice from direct support enforcement. The objection is that Zablocki concerned the ordinary population and a broader condition, whereas this offender's deliberate nonsupport and finite supervision support more latitude. The Court must answer that distinction and the actual offense-related prevention rationale. Bearden removes inability as a permissible basis for coercive enforcement but does not alone invalidate this narrowed condition. Gerber gives Turner review to the specified prison proposal, not strict scrutiny to every probation condition. Casey, Owens, and Newman provide no universal probation test.

## Star: actual unequal obligations

The original participating manufacturers accepted conduct restrictions and permanent nonrefundable monetary obligations. Qualifying subsequent participants accepted the conduct restrictions but owe no settlement payments while their market shares remain at or below the greater of their 1998 shares or 125 percent of their 1997 shares. The opinion describes the preferential eligibility as signing within 90 days; above the ceiling, the monetary obligation tracks market share. Thus participation does not universally entail payments equivalent to the nonparticipant's escrow.

The conduct commitments include limits on youth-directed and cartoon advertising, brand sponsorships, specified lobbying, industry organizations, suppression of research, and misrepresentation. They are obligations even for a grandfathered participant owing no money. Nonparticipants do not undertake those settlement restrictions. Their escrow is measured by cigarettes sold to Virginia consumers directly or through intermediaries. They retain interest; principal secures the described judgment or settlement, qualifying annual excess is releasable, and otherwise unused principal returns after 25 years. This creates security, not an adjudication of the manufacturer's liability.

Star's precise comparison includes both the grandfathered zero-payment participant and its claim that all escrow secures future harms while only 5 percent of participating manufacturers' payments addresses future harm. The opinion reports the consent-decree allocation on which Star relies; the claimed fiscal comparison is not a constitutional demand for equal monetary burdens. The strongest answer concerns the actual package of conduct restrictions, nonrefundable payments where applicable, voluntary settlement of claimed liability, retained interest, release conditions, and prospective security. No reasoning may rely on equivalent cash payments by all participants or on a presumption that Star committed the settling firms' alleged misconduct. ## Star: actual interstate tracing burden

The presented T3 burden is not limited to loss of capital. Star argues that sales through independent out-of-state distributors require it to trace eventual Virginia sales or alter contractual distribution arrangements at greater interstate cost. Virginia responds that distributors already record and report cigarettes receiving Virginia excise stamps under the excise-tax provisions. The official opinion supplies this account of the existing reporting mechanism and its assessment of incremental burden. It does not prove every manufacturer's particular cost or establish a concession that tracing is costless.

The legal alternatives are independent of T2. Under ordinary Commerce analysis the Court examines the whole operation for geographic discrimination or outside-price control; absent those, Pike asks whether the presented incidental burden is clearly excessive relative to the local benefits, taking the nature of the interest and less burdensome means into account. An ultimate Virginia consumer-sales measure does not itself command an outside price. Existing stamp records bear specifically on tracing costs; access still entails a burden. Neither settlement participation labels nor economic rationality alone defeats the Commerce claim.

West Lynn Creamery requires examination of linked competitive effects, not a presumed local-producer subsidy. Walsh preserves the distinction between regulation of the relevant local transaction and an outside-price command. Its public Scalia and Thomas writings preserve distinct grounds rather than participation in ordinary Pike balancing. A rejection of the pleaded tracing theory can be limited to the described mechanism, without a universal finding that all distribution arrangements or future applications have negligible costs. No new numerical threshold or factual compliance-cost finding is necessary.
