### The seven-Associate common ground

Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the complete certificate rule and application. A certificate requires a substantial constitutional showing, exceeding mere nonfrivolousness or good faith. The court assesses whether reasonable jurists could debate the district court's actual application of the governing habeas restrictions. It does not first decide the appeal's full merits, then use that final decision to deny permission to appeal. A claim indisputably barred by a governing habeas provision does not become appealable merely because the abstract constitutional question is debatable.

Sections 2254(d)(2) and (e)(1) remain distinct: unreasonableness of the state decision and the presumption of correctness of particular factual determinations are not merged into a requirement to prove objective unreasonableness by clear and convincing evidence. At certification the question is whether the district court's treatment under the applicable provisions is debatable. The Court does not remove those provisions from the later appeal or hold that ultimate relief follows from a certificate.

The cumulative evidence supports that debate even with the State's individualized explanations and credibility findings considered. Numbers are relevant without establishing a quota or conclusive presumption; office history is relevant without proving these prosecutors' present intent; retrospective findings receive their statutory treatment without becoming immune to examination of non-demeanor evidence. The strongest opposing evidence remains the substantive differences among jurors, the combined reasons for particular strikes, and the credibility finding. The seven conclude that those arguments belong in the authorized merits appeal rather than establishing that no reasonable debate is possible.
