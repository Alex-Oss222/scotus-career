## D1 — jurisdiction and remedy

All seven apply Syngenta’s holding that the All Writs Act and ancillary settlement-enforcement authority do not independently permit removal of a new state action. A potentially conclusive settlement defense does not supply original jurisdiction. Kokkonen’s distinction between lawful decree enforcement and a separate controversy, and Peacock’s refusal to turn enforcement power into jurisdiction over a new obligation, reinforce the point.

Petitioners asserted federal-officer removal under §1442 at argument. That assertion defeats an inference that every independent theory has been abandoned; it does not establish preservation or the statute’s predicates. The appellate judgment concerning Isaacson must therefore be vacated and remanded for consideration of any properly preserved independent ground. If none supplies jurisdiction, the federal court must remand Isaacson to state court. Federal preclusion adjudication in that action must await the jurisdictional determination. This is a legal consequence of the rejected basis used below, not a production order to cure an operator’s missing research.

The competing course of affirming federal authority because the settlement court is best placed to interpret its judgment fails under Syngenta. The competing course of ordering unconditional immediate state-court remand does not account for the asserted independent ground. None of the Associates commits to the merits of §1442 or treats respondents’ agreement that Syngenta requires remand as a concession resolving every alternative ground.

**Justice-specific support:** Scalia authored Kokkonen; O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg joined its full jurisdictional analysis. Thomas authored Peacock, whose independent-jurisdiction requirement all seven present Associates joined. Breyer’s Peacock join supplies his own direct participation in that boundary. Syngenta is controlling earlier current-term law for each Justice.

## D2 — the common legal boundary and the genuine disagreement

### Bounded constitutional inquiry

Each Associate supports the following narrow proposition: an absent claimant who identifies a material conflict in the original representation and an asserted absence of protection for that interest is not foreclosed merely by the original judgment’s general statement of adequacy, the existence of notice, or the opportunity to object or opt out. The court must determine whether the asserted binding effect rests on constitutionally adequate representation. A claim that a settlement was poor, a certification ruling erroneous, or a payment disappointing does not establish that defect.

This conclusion applies Matsushita Part IV-A’s independent constitutional prerequisite and resolves only the concrete form of collateral objection presented here. It does not turn the reservation in Part IV-B into a general right to retry certification from the beginning. Ordinary finality continues to govern a person’s own adjudication and a genuinely binding representative adjudication. The earlier Agent Orange judgments are neither nonexistent nor irrelevant: their actual findings, grounds, scope, and precedential force must be addressed. The fact that an objector raised an argument does not itself make that objector the authorized representative of everyone later advancing it. Conversely, the use of a different claimant’s name or a newly described category does not itself defeat an otherwise binding adjudication.

The seven reject petitioners’ broadest procedure-only proposition: good-faith use of Rule 23, coupled with no demonstrated fraud or collusion, does not invariably end the constitutional inquiry. That would permit an unprotected material conflict to be cured by the very certification finding being challenged, contrary to Matsushita’s separation of actual representation from procedural opportunities. They also reject respondents’ broadest proposition if understood to make every adequacy ruling perpetually provisional and open to wholesale de novo retrial. Their disagreement is whether the supported original structure already resolves this particular objection.

### Correct-standard remand: O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Breyer

These four would vacate the appellate determination that the settlement cannot bind Stephenson and remand for application of the constitutional standard to the original proceedings. They would not reinstate Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal by treating the earlier adequacy findings as conclusive merely because they exist.

The remand rests on an identifiable defect in the appellate analysis. The Second Circuit treated the absence of post-1994 direct payments, together with Amchem and Ortiz, as establishing inadequate representation and stated that the ultimate merits had no bearing on adequacy. The merits of Stephenson’s tort claim are not to be decided as a substitute for representation. But common defenses, the contemporaneously understood risks, and the actual distribution arrangement can bear on whether the represented interests were materially opposed and whether that opposition was protected. They cannot be categorically excluded from that inquiry. Nor can an individual’s eventual placement on the losing side of an uncertain compensation arrangement alone prove that the arrangement lacked representative authority when adopted.

The lower courts must examine the original constitutional objection with the earlier decisions’ actual holdings and grounds in view: the common-defense basis of the 1987 ruling, the compensation-eligibility basis of Ivy/Hartman, and the asserted conflict in allocating a finite fund while releasing later claims. Any determination of a binding prior adjudication must address the relationship through which it purported to bind Stephenson; any determination of a constitutional defect must identify the unprotected material interest, not simply the date of diagnosis. Actual representation during allocation matters even though the manufacturers negotiated the lump sum before the court adopted the allocation.

The original refusal to appoint future-claimant counsel is established in Joint Appendix 129–130; the remand does not ask whether that refusal occurred or presume that some separate lawyer might have existed. These four distinguish the absence of that particular safeguard from the ultimate constitutional conclusion. They require an assessment whether the original differences were materially adverse in this arrangement and whether the protection actually supplied was constitutionally sufficient, considering the serious common risks, direct-payment program, assistance program, and adjudicated grounds together. Cost alone cannot excuse a material unprotected conflict. Conversely, the absence of a separately appointed attorney does not establish a universal constitutional subclass requirement. This is the precise application on which they disagree with the affirming three.

This does not prescribe new discovery, an evidentiary hearing, a numerical deference measure, or a new burden of proof. It requires reconsideration under the correct legal distinction through the ordinary procedures appropriate to the Rule 12(b)(6) posture and the properly considered settlement record. Whether the existing record permits disposition without further fact development remains a judicial application question. The original settlement is not vacated wholesale. Dismissal of Stephenson on preclusion grounds remains unavailable unless the legally available constitutional objection is resolved against him under that standard.

### Affirmance on the existing original conflict: Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg

These three would affirm the removal of the settlement bar for Stephenson. Their ground is the original arrangement’s combination of an unrestricted release of future injury claims with a fixed end to direct death-and-disability compensation, represented by symptomatic named claimants without independent protection of the adverse future allocation interest. They do not reason backward from the bare fact that Stephenson received no cash. The initial allocation itself placed the interest in present distribution against the interest in preserving resources or other meaningful protection for injuries outside the payment horizon.

The manufacturers’ admitted account of the named representatives, the fixed program terms reported below, the express refusal of future-claimant counsel in Joint Appendix 129–130, and the reliance on the judge, submissions, and common defenses supply a sufficient basis to reject the asserted preclusion bar in this posture. Individual veterans’ submissions and careful judicial management did not make the court or those speakers representatives of the opposed interest. The assistance program and compensation of some later-manifesting claimants must not be erased; their existence does not by itself answer who protected the interest surrendered when post-program tort claims were released. These Justices do not equate constitutionally adequate representation with a guarantee that every class member ultimately receives money.

The strongest contrary argument is that all asymptomatic veterans originally faced the same uncertain risk, the same serious defenses, and the same compensation rules, and that the precise representation objection was already heard. The response is that shared litigation adversity can justify collective pursuit of recovery without eliminating a conflict over allocation after a fund is obtained. The opposing interest existed before anyone knew which particular veteran would later manifest disease. A prior general finding grounded in the common defense, and Ivy/Hartman’s treatment of persons still eligible for compensation, do not supply the missing protection of that adverse allocation interest. This is an application of the preexisting constitutional representation requirement, not a general reopening rule for unsuccessful settlement beneficiaries.

The judgment leaves Stephenson’s individual action free of this settlement’s preclusion and dependent injunction bar. It preserves the settlement’s operation for persons it validly binds. This affirmation is limited to the original defect; it does not adopt respondents’ asserted continuing scientific-adjustment duty or determine the alleged later reserve diversion.

## D3 — original proceedings, later information, and later conduct

All seven reject an inquiry confined to the instant of initial certification. Adequate representation must persist through the original litigation stages that define and dispose of the affected interest, including settlement approval and allocation. A representative arrangement adequate for litigating a common defense may become inadequate when the interests divide over distributing a recovery. That possibility is not proof that it occurred; it identifies the correct time and subject of the inquiry.

The following distinctions control each commitment:

1. **Later manifestation:** A later diagnosis can identify a claimant affected by a risk and cutoff already present in the original arrangement. Diagnosis does not itself establish original inadequacy or create a new subgroup entitled to reopen a valid settlement.
2. **Later legal decisions:** Amchem and Ortiz may illuminate the application of the preexisting representation requirement to conflicting bargaining interests. Their distinct Rule 23 predominance and limited-fund holdings, or an asserted new certification requirement, cannot independently undo an otherwise binding final judgment. Hansberry and Shutts supplied the constitutional representation foundation before the Agent Orange settlement.
3. **Later science:** Improved evidence of causation does not by itself show that original representatives were constitutionally inadequate. The inquiry concerns what interests and uncertainties the original arrangement had to represent, not an obligation to bargain with knowledge acquired years later.
4. **Postjudgment administration:** Respondents also alleged an unfulfilled adjustment promise and a later disposition of the reserve. Those are genuine arguments made, not findings adopted here. A challenge to an authorized later modification or a breach of an established continuing duty requires its own supported terms, conduct, and available remedy. It cannot silently become proof that the original settlement lacked binding authority. The affirming three do not need that theory; the remanding four do not direct relief on it.

For the four remanding Justices, the appellate court must apply these distinctions while deciding whether an original constitutional defect was established. For the three affirming Justices, the original allocation supplies the sufficient ground, so later diagnoses disclose its operation without supplying a new defect. Neither group adopts automatic reopening based on later law, changed science, or exhaustion of funds.

## Individual grounds, contrary paths, and join limits

### O’Connor

**Commitment and own authority:** Bounded remand on D2. Her authorship of Matsushita separates ordinary judgment recognition in Parts I–III from the independent constitutional requirement in IV-A and expressly preserves the actual scope of collateral review in IV-B. Her Ortiz joins require genuine protection of conflicting allocation interests while retaining the limits of the particular certification holding. Those commitments support review of this concrete constitutional objection but not automatic application of a later certification result to a long-final judgment.

**Strongest alternatives:** Restoration of dismissal has support in the prior express trial and appellate adjudications and the original common defenses; it fails without a valid response to the original-representation objection rather than reliance on the findings’ existence. Affirmance has support in the fixed cutoff and opposed payment interests; it is premature because the court below treated the cutoff and later certification decisions as sufficient while excluding relevant original alignment considerations. Her argument questions about previous adequacy rulings corroborate the existence of this problem, not her answer to it.

**Joins:** Will join D1 and the common D2/D3 rules and the bounded remand application. Will not join a fraud-only collateral limit, unrestricted de novo certification review, automatic nonpreclusion from nonpayment, or a conclusion that the full earlier adequacy question was never raised. No independent notice ruling is part of this commitment.

### Scalia

**Commitment and own authority:** Bounded remand on D2. His Matsushita joins preserve ordinary preclusive effect against assertions of mere approval error, while joining the constitutional limit and the collateral-scope reservation. His Amchem III–IV and Ortiz IV joins enforce actual representative requirements against settlement expediency. His own Kokkonen opinion supplies the related insistence that the existence of a settlement cannot manufacture legal authority, although Kokkonen does not itself decide class preclusion.

**Strongest alternatives:** A finality-based reversal would be justified if the original judgment validly determined the objection for the persons now bound; the fact of certification cannot alone establish the contested representative relationship. Conversely, his direct-review rejection of the classes in Amchem and Ortiz does not convert every feature of their certification analysis into a constitutional collateral rule. This distinction, and the prior judgments’ concrete grounds, makes remand preferable to either categorical endpoint on the appellate rationale presented.

**Joins:** Will join the remand opinion only if it preserves valid final judgments, rejects mere later doctrinal error as an independent ground, and identifies an actual constitutional objection. Will not join an open-ended fairness balancing of the benefits of finality against whatever compensation a later claimant desires, or a newly mandatory architecture for every future-claim class.

### Kennedy

**Commitment and own authority:** Bounded remand on D2. His joins in Matsushita II, III, IV-A, and IV-B preserve both ordinary recognition and the constitutional boundary. His Amchem III and Ortiz IV joins require actual protection for materially conflicting bargaining interests. They support examining this original conflict while distinguishing a challenge to binding authority from a second appeal on settlement value.

**Strongest alternatives:** The earlier litigated adequacy determinations make restoration of dismissal a serious path; they do not establish that these absent persons were represented in deciding the very interest at issue. The structural-affirmance path correctly identifies a possible conflict, but a categorical equation of cutoff and inadequacy does not give the actual original arrangement and the prior decisions their proper significance. His question about the collateral standard identifies this distinction without settling it.

**Joins:** Will join the common constitutional rule and bounded remand. Will not adopt either universal collateral relitigation or a general immunity for adequately administered certification procedures irrespective of the interests actually represented. No special quantitative or heightened evidentiary threshold is supplied by this commitment.

### Souter

**Commitment and own authority:** Affirm on D2. His Matsushita constitutional and collateral-reservation joins, Amchem III join, and Ortiz IV join support the distinction between obtaining a recovery against a common adversary and representing opposed interests in distributing it. His position applies that distinction to the original release and allocation, not to a retrospective demand for better compensation.

**Strongest alternatives:** Prior adjudications and common defenses weigh against casual reopening; their force does not replace representation of the opposed allocation interest. A remand could be appropriate if the dispute were only about the quality of a genuinely representative bargain. Here the original structure and counsel’s acknowledgments sufficiently identify the missing protection, so a remand to revisit that same legal consequence is unnecessary. The bench questions sharpen this allocation issue but are not treated as his vote.

**Joins:** Will join D1, the common D2 constitutional boundary, D3, and affirmance on the original structural ground. Will not join an opinion that treats the court as the absent group’s representative, a shared defense as necessarily resolving an allocation conflict, or every prior ruling as irrelevant. He preserves precedential weight distinct from party binding.

### Thomas

**Commitment and own authority:** Affirm on D2. His authorship of the controlling Amchem and Ortiz representation analyses is the most direct individual support. Those opinions require actual independent protection of materially conflicting bargaining interests; their authority does not rest on the label attached to a class or the economic attraction of settlement. His Matsushita IV-A join preserves that prerequisite constitutionally, and IV-B prevents a claim that the entire collateral boundary was already settled.

**Strongest alternatives:** Direct review and collateral review differ, and ordinary Rule 23 error cannot automatically reopen a final judgment. That objection does not answer an original absence of representative protection for the interest released. Commonly weak claims may support a compromise, but the actual allocation of a finite recovery creates a separate question. A correct-standard remand is a legitimate alternative when that original structure remains undetermined; the supported structure here suffices to reject the preclusion defense without a new general procedural test.

**Joins:** Will join the narrow affirmance, D1, and the common D2/D3 rules. Will not join a holding that all future claims are incapable of settlement, that any particular subclass architecture is constitutionally required in every case, or that Amchem’s predominance and Ortiz’s limited-fund requirements are the constitutional test here. No current-case historical authorship or lineup is imported.

### Ginsburg

**Commitment and own authority:** Affirm on D2. Her Matsushita IV-A and IV-B joins establish the independent constitutional safeguard and retain its collateral question; her withholding of parts of ordinary recognition analysis reinforces the need to identify the actual authorized release rather than begin and end with its wording. Her Amchem III and Ortiz IV joins require protection for the opposed present and future interests.

**Strongest alternatives:** The original judges heard extensive objections and made express findings; that history is real and must not be described as no review. But a judge’s conscientiousness and an objector’s presentation do not themselves establish representative authority over an absent interest. The remand path gives proper attention to original common risks, yet those risks do not remove the distinct allocation conflict established by this structure. Her questions about representation support testing that distinction; her notice and accrual questions do not enlarge the granted issues or create an alternative holding.

**Joins:** Will join D1 and the original-conflict affirmance with the common rules. Will not join a procedure-only bar or a rationale that requires absent claimants to intervene to make representation constitutionally adequate. No independent notice or state-law accrual disposition is supplied.

### Breyer

**Commitment and own authority:** Bounded remand on D2. His Matsushita II–IV joins distinguish finality from the unresolved constitutional binding objection. In Amchem he accepted the governing prerequisites but withheld the actual inadequacy and predominance applications; in Ortiz he likewise accepted the general prerequisites without joining the rejection of the arrangement. Those recorded choices supply a particular reason to demand an examination of the actual arrangement before treating the analogy to those cases as dispositive.

**Strongest alternatives:** Restoration of dismissal protects substantial reliance and avoids endless redefinition of groups after uncertain outcomes become known. It is insufficient if it makes the original adequacy recital conclusive without answering the actual representative objection. Affirmance identifies a serious original conflict but understates the relevance of common risks, the program’s actual protective arrangements, and prior adjudications. Correct-standard remand addresses both errors without requiring a renewed examination of every settlement issue.

**Joins:** Will join D1 and a remand confined to original constitutional representation, with the common D3 distinctions. Will not join a rule that every later claimant may define a new group from the fact of nonpayment, or a mandatory independent lawyer for every possible medical outcome. His conditional remand questions are evidence that the path was presented, not the basis for assigning him to it.

## Operative limits for reconciliation and assembly

The finality path was tested on its strongest actual grounds: express prior adjudications, specifically raised future-interest objections, shared original defenses, uncertainty among then-asymptomatic veterans, and reliance on an executed settlement. It was not reduced to a bare appeal to expediency. The affirmance path was tested against those grounds and against the distinction between a constitutional defect and later Rule 23 doctrine. The remand path is supported by a claimed legal error and a genuinely unresolved application under that corrected standard, not by a research shortfall.

The four remanding Justices do not commit to eventual nonpreclusion or eventual dismissal. The three affirming Justices do not commit to a remand declaring original adequacy undecided. Agreement on the general constitutional and temporal rules is not agreement on that disposition. Assembly must preserve this difference unless a materially revised public formulation actually answers an individual recorded objection, followed by the required reconciliation audit. No coalition may be manufactured by counting both positions as an already agreed disposition.

The following remain specifically limited: the preservation and predicates of independent removal; the binding scope of the original proceedings as applied under the remand position; the actual operative terms and legal effect of any promised later scientific adjustment or reserve protection; and the truth of the alleged later administrative breaches. None is inferred from an unavailable document or from counsel’s statements alone. No production demand, litigant default, or factual-hearing entitlement follows from these limitations.
