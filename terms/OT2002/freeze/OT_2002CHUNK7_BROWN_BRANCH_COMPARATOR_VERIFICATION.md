## Brown v. Legal Foundation of Washington, 538 U.S. 216

**Docket and dates:** No. 01-1325; argued December 9, 2002; decided March 26, 2003. Certiorari to the Ninth Circuit; 271 F.3d 835 affirmed.

**Historical alignment:** Stevens wrote for O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, producing a 5-4 affirmance. Scalia dissented, joined by Rehnquist, Kennedy, and Thomas. Kennedy filed an additional dissent alone and expressly joined Scalia's dissent in full. No concurrence or partial majority join appears.

**Controlling historical reasoning and qualifications:**

- The opinion preserved Phillips's holding that IOLTA interest is the principal owner's property, and Phillips's express reservation of taking and compensation questions. The compelled initial placement of qualifying principal did not confiscate interest and, analyzed under Penn Central, lacked adverse economic impact and interference with investment-backed expectations. The later transfer of earned interest was a distinct step, more appropriately assessed under the per se approach associated with Loretto.
- The majority assumed on the imperfect individual record that Brown and Hayes retained beneficial ownership of some escrow funds, that those funds generated interest, and that their interest was taken when transferred to the Foundation. Its concluding formulation says the transfer could be a per se taking. Section III's shorthand that the Court held a taking occurred should therefore retain this assumption-based treatment of the individual facts, rather than imply an adjudicated amount of interest or a definitive finding on every factual predicate.
- The public use requirement was satisfied by provision of legal services to the needy. Compensation was the owner's pecuniary loss, rather than the recipient's gain; personal objections or other nonpecuniary losses supplied no additional compensation. The Court relied on Boston Chamber of Commerce, Marion & Rye Valley, Olson, and Kimball Laundry for these distinctions.
- Footnote 10 expressly identifies the measure as the net value of interest actually earned. It rejects Scalia's characterization that the measure is simply hypothetical interest from a non-IOLTA account. Transaction, administrative, and bank costs matter to net value. Footnote 11 disclaims a rule permitting confiscation simply because a governmental program created the interest.
- The operative Washington rules, as authoritatively explained by the state court, required client-benefit investment whenever net interest could be earned, including cost-effective subaccounting; technological advances could move additional funds outside IOLTA. The majority treated lawful IOLTA deposits as incapable of producing net client earnings. It separately recited the District Court's factual finding about these depositors; that finding should not be collapsed into the rule's text.
- The Court rejected a remand to explore erroneous placement of net-earning funds. On its reasoning, such a placement violated the rules and any resulting net loss arose from the private LPO's decision, potentially supporting a claim against the LPO but not compensation from the State or these respondents. This was a material ground, not merely a general prediction that most small accounts earn nothing.
- With compensation zero and no Just Compensation Clause violation established, the Court did not reach the certiorari question whether impracticably small compensation claims permit injunctive relief. It did not decide the First Amendment claim.

**Scalia dissent:** Market value at the time of appropriation, ordinarily the face value of the interest already earned, supplied the required compensation. He accepted only the established exceptions where market value is impracticable to ascertain or would produce manifest injustice; he found neither applicable. He distinguished the existence of the pooled interest from the client's inability to earn interest individually. He argued that both the hypothetical non-IOLTA counterfactual and deduction of extraction costs conflicted with property and compensation principles, reading Phillips's rent illustration and Webb's as support while the majority maintained Phillips had reserved compensation. Even under a net-value approach, he argued the costs of extracting interest from the actual pooled account required factual inquiry. He also rejected the majority's private-error ground: the State still appropriated the interest regardless of how the principal entered the account. His footnote 2 disputed the unrequested public-use discussion and its tax analogy.

**Kennedy additional dissent:** The mandatory program could monopolize the use of pooled interest and foreclose alternative funds supporting owners' preferred viewpoints. Abood and Keller suggested a potential compelled-support problem. He expressly described the First Amendment consequences as unaddressed and for possible later litigation; this was no First Amendment merits holding.

## Branch v. Smith, 538 U.S. 254

**Dockets and dates:** No. 01-1437 and cross-appeal No. 01-1596, Smith v. Branch; argued December 10, 2002; decided March 31, 2003. Direct appeals from the three-judge Southern District of Mississippi; the operative judgment reported at 189 F. Supp. 2d 548 was affirmed, while its unnecessary alternative constitutional holding was vacated as a basis for the injunction.

| Historical writing or component | Author and exact joins |
|---|---|
| Principal opinion, Parts I and II, including II-C's constitutional-ground vacatur | Scalia for all nine Justices |
| Principal opinion, Part III-A: section 2c binds courts as well as legislatures | Scalia; Rehnquist, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer |
| Principal opinion, Parts III-B and IV: retained last-resort fallback and responses to the dissent | Scalia; Rehnquist, Kennedy, Ginsburg |
| Kennedy concurrence, Part I | Kennedy alone, explaining his own joins |
| Kennedy concurrence, Part II | Kennedy; Stevens, Souter, Breyer |
| Stevens concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stevens; Souter, Breyer |
| O'Connor concurrence in part and dissent in part | O'Connor; Thomas; joins principal Parts I and II, rejects III-A, III-B, IV |

**Historical components and forks:**

- All nine sustained the injunction against the unprecleared state-court plan. A covered jurisdiction bears the burden of providing sufficient information. DOJ's nonfrivolous, warranted request for missing information postponed the 60-day review period; the period began anew when the requested response arrived. The Court did not finally decide that the state court's jurisdictional ruling itself required preclearance: the reasonableness of the information request sufficed regardless of that ultimate question.
- The state-court judgment was adjudication, not an enactment, so its route to administrative preclearance depended on the State continuing to seek to administer it. After the February 26, 2002 injunction, the State did not appeal and was no longer seeking implementation; the clock ceased running. Private intervenors' appeal did not count as state action or revive the running period. The plan therefore gained no automatic preclearance on either proposed deadline.
- All nine vacated the alternative Article I, section 4 holding as unnecessary. It was not binding on state or federal officials in future efforts to administer a state-court plan. This did not decide the merits of the constitutional objection or affirm an independent-state-legislature theory.
- Seven joined Part III-A's holding that section 2c requires single-member districts and binds state and federal courts fashioning a remedy when legislative redistricting has not occurred. The text's reference to establishment by law includes judicial action, read alongside its historical setting and the courts' uniform treatment of the provision. This supports the federal map, rather than a required at-large election, in this case.
- Four, in Parts III-B and IV, reconciled section 2a(c) with section 2c rather than finding implied repeal. Constitutional portions of section 2a(c), including paragraph (5), remained a last-resort fallback only when an election was so imminent that no competent state or federal actor could complete lawful redistricting without disrupting it. The condition cannot be omitted. The plurality read Mississippi's corresponding fallback the same way. It acknowledged that paragraphs (1)-(4) would ordinarily conflict with population equality, while retaining the unusual possibility of a census requiring no constitutional district changes.
- Stevens, Souter, and Breyer instead concluded that section 2c impliedly repealed section 2a(c) and preempted Mississippi's at-large fallback. Their Posadas analysis invoked both irreconcilable conflict and comprehensive substitution, supported by the 1967 enactment history. The statute's express, expired exception for Hawaii and New Mexico's election to the Ninety-first Congress reinforced their otherwise categorical reading.
- O'Connor and Thomas agreed that no implied repeal occurred but rejected the plurality's imminence condition. Their construction made section 2a(c) govern until the State had completed redistricting through its own legally prescribed process; after that point, section 2c governed remedies for an invalid plan. Because the Mississippi plan lacked preclearance, that process was incomplete and section 2a(c)(5) required an at-large remedy. They did not insist on winner-take-all voting: cumulative voting or another Voting Rights Act-compliant at-large arrangement remained available. Their constitutional-avoidance concerns about commands to state legislatures and their distinction between state process and federal remedial authority were material, not endorsements of the federal map.
- Kennedy's Part II gave a further procedural explanation for the unanimous vacatur: Connor and related preclearance cases made constitutional review premature before a plan could legally be implemented, including a challenge to the state court's authority rather than the plan's substantive districts. Premature review could interfere with the administrative process and create the appearance of Executive revision of a judicial judgment. Stevens, Souter, and Breyer joined this part only.

**Limits on combining historical votes:** The 7-2 result on the federal map is accurate, but neither the plurality's particular last-resort fallback nor Stevens's comprehensive implied repeal rationale received five judgment-supporting joins. Six Justices expressed opposition to implied repeal when the partial dissent is included, but O'Connor and Thomas rejected the plurality's legal operation and judgment on the federal map. Do not present Part III-B or IV as a seven-Justice opinion, turn Kennedy's four-Justice explanation into a unanimous rationale, or infer a controlling narrowest-ground rule without separately testing its actual logical relation to the competing rationales.

**Source discrepancy:** The saved syllabus gives December 21, 2001 as the submission date in its introductory factual account. The full Court opinion repeatedly gives December 26, 2001 for the initial DOJ submission, while December 21 was the Chancery Court's adoption date. Use the full opinion's December 26 recitation for historical comparator chronology; do not silently conflate adoption and submission.
