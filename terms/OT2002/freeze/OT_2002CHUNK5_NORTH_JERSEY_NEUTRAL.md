**Question Presented (summary):** Whether the First Amendment affords the public and press a qualified right of access to deportation hearings designated as matters of special interest to national security, requiring individualized findings rather than categorical closure?

**Question Granted:** The stated questions are the scope of this added review of the identified federal judgment; no historical Supreme Court grant or verbatim petition question is asserted.

**Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Third Circuit reversed a preliminary injunction entered by the District of New Jersey against categorical closure of special-interest removal hearings.

**Relief Requested:** The press organizations seek restoration of access subject to properly justified case-specific closure. Federal officials seek permission to maintain the categorical national-security closure directive.

**Material Record:** After September 11, 2001, the Chief Immigration Judge directed immigration judges to close proceedings designated by the Attorney General as special-interest cases, including restricting public information about their existence. The press sought access to removal hearings. The government offered a national-security explanation that individual identities and procedural information could be combined to expose investigative methods, alert terrorist networks, or reveal what officials knew. The dispute is not limited to a single identified classified document. No finding establishes that every hearing or every portion presents the same concrete risk. The ordinary removal-hearing regulation allowed public hearings with exceptions.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The asserted right belongs to the public and press under the First Amendment, not solely to the detained noncitizen. Preliminary relief requires the appropriate likelihood and equitable findings. The Court must identify the type of proceeding and the claimed access right before assessing closure. Executive national-security expertise is relevant evidence, but the directive’s existence is not itself adjudication of necessity.

**Relevant Dates:** The closure directive followed September 11, 2001. The historical appellate decision is October 8, 2002; the scheduled event is March 4, 2003.

##### Issue 1: Access to removal adjudication

**Controlling Law:** No controlling decision authorizes categorical closure of these hearings. Richmond Newspapers, Globe Newspaper, Press-Enterprise and El Vocero supply the established public-access framework and the distinction between a qualified right and justified closure. El Vocero examines the type of proceeding rather than one jurisdiction’s practice and requires particularized justification after reasonable alternatives are considered.

**Current Reusable Doctrine:** Examine the proceeding’s tradition of openness and the positive role of access; if a qualified right attaches, require a sufficiently demonstrated interest, fitting restrictions, and consideration of alternatives. Distinguish access to adjudication from access to all investigative files.

**Other Governing Law:** The First Amendment and the applicable public-hearing immigration regulation provide the relevant legal setting. The regulation is evidence of institutional practice, not itself the complete constitutional rule.

**Material Other Authority:** The competing Third and Sixth Circuit analyses show disagreement about applying experience and logic to removal proceedings. Neither is a controlling Supreme Court resolution.

**Existing Rule’s Reach:** The criminal-court access precedents do not automatically settle every administrative proceeding. They establish a method that must be applied to adjudication of removal, not to immigration policy at large.

**Open Point:** Whether removal hearings satisfy that method and whether national-security designation can substitute for individualized closure findings.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below:** The Third Circuit found insufficient historical openness and concluded that security-related costs undermined the logic of access. It credited the government’s concern about assembled fragments of information. The district court treated removal adjudication as covered and demanded justification for closure.

**Press:** Liberty-affecting adjudication benefits from public scrutiny, factual accuracy, and accountability. A categorical designation removes judicial evaluation and sweeps harmless information and routine issues into secrecy. The requested rule is qualified access with tailored protective measures.

**Government:** Immigration adjudication historically differs from criminal trials, and publicly explaining each closure can reveal the very information to be protected. National-security assessment may depend on a coordinated investigation beyond an individual judge’s view.

**Limits and alternatives:** Access does not require disclosure of classified evidence, operational details, or protected identities where a sufficient showing supports closure. In camera submissions, redaction, delayed disclosure, and partial closure are possible alternatives rather than predetermined remedies.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may affirm if no First Amendment access right attaches to this proceeding, leaving any regulatory or other preserved claim distinct. It may recognize qualified access and restore or remand the preliminary injunction with instructions to assess demonstrated closure needs. It may uphold some categories only upon evidence showing that their defined characteristics make individualized distinctions impracticable and the restriction sufficiently fitted. A right of access does not decide the noncitizens’ removal cases.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** A qualified-access holding could cover ordinary removal adjudication while allowing security-sensitive portions to close on supported findings. A contrary holding could decline constitutional extension to this administrative setting without approving secrecy in all civil courts. The question supports scrutiny of the categorical directive, not a constitutional right to every immigration file or intelligence source.

**Uncertainty:** The adequacy of the security showing and the feasibility of protected findings require careful record review. The public record cannot be treated as proof that classified concerns are imaginary, nor can their invocation prove necessity for every closed hearing.
