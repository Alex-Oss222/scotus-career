## 59. United States v. Wagoner County Real Estate

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** United States v. Wagoner County Real Estate

* **Citation or Docket:** Tenth Circuit No. 00-7059; listed citation: 278 F.3d 1091

* **October Term:** 2002

* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS. Review of the reported judgment on the preserved federal questions.

* **Simulated Event Date:** 2003-03-04 (organizationally assigned within the OT2002 calendar; not a historical Supreme Court decision date).

* **Question Presented:** Whether federal civil-forfeiture law authorizing forfeiture of real property used to facilitate drug offenses preempts state constitutional and statutory protections exempting homesteads from forfeiture? Whether a court may uphold forfeiture of residential property under the Excessive Fines Clause without specific findings addressing the property’s value, the owner’s culpability, the gravity of the offenses, and the authorized penalties?

* **Question Granted:** The stated federal questions define the counterfactual merits review. They are issue summaries, not a transcription of a historical Supreme Court grant.

* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Tenth Circuit reviewed an Eastern District of Oklahoma decree forfeiting Nanette Lees’s residential property. The district judge had rejected the homestead defense on partial summary judgment, a jury found for the United States, and the judge rejected excessiveness in a short oral ruling. The appellate judgment sustained the homestead and trial rulings but returned the Excessive Fines issue for findings.

* **Relief Requested:** Lees seeks protection of the residence from federal forfeiture under Oklahoma homestead law, or relief from the decree under the Excessive Fines Clause. The United States seeks recognition of federal statutory coverage and a constitutionally permissible forfeiture. Review is confined to the two listed questions.

* **Material Record:** Police investigating marijuana sales by Lees’s resident grandson searched with her consent in June 1999 and later under a warrant. They reported marijuana, scales, suspected steroids, cash, firearms, and other evidence of drug activity, principally associated with the grandson. Valium was found in Lees’s bag. The State prosecuted the grandson but not Lees; her probation on an earlier, separate federal Valium conviction was revoked. The residence’s reported appraisal was $136,000. Lees disputed forfeitable use and asserted innocent ownership. The jury rejected her defense, but the judge’s excessiveness ruling identified no particular offense, valuation, penalty comparison, benefit, or degree of personal culpability supporting the conclusion. These are the reported proceedings, not new findings about every seized substance or its value. Reported opinion, Parts I, III, and IV.

* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Lees’s asserted ownership supplies the property interest at stake. Federal statutory coverage precedes constitutional excessiveness. The reported forfeiture proceeded under the pre-CAFRA framework; later changes to burdens and owner defenses cannot be silently substituted. The original appeal also challenged a jury instruction and impeachment rulings, but those questions are outside this grant. In particular, describing the historical treatment of those objections does not adopt the lower court’s historical reading of Ohler v. United States as governing law. The grant contains no independent tribal-trust-land or treaty question merely because Lees is a Creek Indian.

* **Relevant Dates:** The appellate judgment was entered January 2, 2002. March 4, 2003 is the assigned review date. The reported record does not establish a later completed forfeiture disposition, property sale, or extinguishment of Lees’s interest before this event.

* **Companion or Related Matters:** No additional companion is separately briefed.

* **Participation Matter:** No recusal or nonparticipation is identified.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current-Through Baseline:** Governing decisions through June 28, 2002, together with the relevant December 3, 2002 preemption holding.

* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** Sprietsma v. Mercury Marine (December 3, 2002) distinguishes a failure to impose a federal equipment requirement from an operative federal command or deliberate safety arrangement whose implementation would actually conflict with state law.

* **Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.

* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** Review remains bounded by the preserved federal questions, the lower judgment, and the actual standard of review; appellate inference cannot replace findings on disputed facts.

##### Issue 1: Federal forfeiture and the asserted homestead exemption

* **Controlling Law:** Cipollone v. Liggett Group and Geier v. American Honda Motor Co. require identification of the operative federal command and actual conflict. Neither federal regulatory involvement nor an asserted desire for uniformity alone extinguishes state law. Sprietsma rejects inferring an affirmative federal protection from the agency’s evidence-based decision not to require equipment; an actual conflicting federal command remains material. No controlling decision creates a federal homestead exemption from this forfeiture statute.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** The current preemption standards distinguish an enacted displacement rule or demonstrated conflict from general field occupation. A state-created property interest and a state exemption from enforcement perform different functions.

* **Other Governing Law:** The offense-date 21 U.S.C. § 881(a)(7) reaches all real property used or intended to facilitate specified serious drug violations, subject to its own ownership exception. Section 903 rejects general field occupation but preserves displacement for positive conflict. Oklahoma’s constitutional and statutory homestead protections supply the competing state rules; Article VI supplies federal supremacy when the conflict is established.

* **Material Other Authority:** None beyond the authorities identified here.

* **Existing Rule’s Reach:** A state homestead exemption can define protection against state process without withdrawing property Congress otherwise made forfeitable to the United States.

* **Open Point:** Whether the claimed exemption can coexist with the federal command as applied to this residence, and whether any distinct federal limit protects the actual property interest.

##### Issue 2: Punishment, proportionality, and findings sufficient for review

* **Controlling Law:** Austin v. United States places § 881(a)(7) forfeitures within the Excessive Fines Clause and requires an excessiveness inquiry distinct from statutory connection. United States v. Bajakajian supplies gross disproportionality for punitive criminal forfeiture, legislative respect, independent appellate review of the constitutional conclusion, and clear-error review of supported facts. It does not prescribe an exhaustive checklist. Bennis v. Michigan recognizes a qualified due-process defense for a separately held automobile interest; it is not a historical rule permitting every innocent-owner forfeiture.

* **Current Reusable Doctrine:** The reusable punitive-forfeiture standard requires a real comparison of the exaction with the gravity and circumstances of the wrongdoing. Culpability, harm, statutory and applicable sentencing penalties, property value, and connection to the offense matter where material; neither instrumentality alone nor a maximum-fine comparison is conclusive.

* **Other Governing Law:** The Eighth Amendment directly limits this federal forfeiture. The original statute’s knowledge-or-consent defense and the pre-CAFRA allocation of proof remain separate from the constitutional proportionality question.

* **Material Other Authority:** None beyond the authorities identified here.

* **Existing Rule’s Reach:** Austin v. United States controls coverage of this precise statutory category. Applying the gross-disproportionality framework to this civil residential forfeiture requires explaining the relevant offense and owner relationship, rather than treating an in rem label as an answer.

* **Open Point:** Whether the existing findings permit review, and whether any deficiency calls for findings rather than an appellate declaration that the entire residence is or is not forfeitable.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

* **Reasoning Below:** The district court treated § 881(a)(7) as displacing the homestead exemption. Its final excessiveness ruling invoked severity, harshness, and culpability without stating the factual comparisons. The appellate court agreed on preemption but required findings addressing relevant proportionality considerations. Its additional trial rulings are background only.

* **Petitioner or Appellant:** Lees stresses the special state protection for a home, the absence of a specific federal reference to homesteads, the residence’s value, and the difference between her own conduct and her grandson’s. She argues that a conclusory reference to all the circumstances cannot justify forfeiting the whole property.

* **Respondent or Appellee:** The United States relies on the federal statute’s comprehensive real-property language, its express owner defense, and the conflict created by an extra state immunity. Drug distribution and knowing facilitation are serious; a residence can be an instrument of that activity, and civil forfeiture does not require a separate federal criminal conviction.

* **Alternatives and Limiting Principles:** Rejecting the homestead exemption need not sustain the amount forfeited. Requiring usable findings need not impose a numerical proportionality ratio or a mandatory finding on an immaterial factor. Actual innocent ownership, the property’s connection, and excessiveness remain distinct inquiries. An unrelated prior drug conviction cannot automatically supply the gravity of the present forfeiture offense.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

* **Threshold Paths:** Resolve the identified threshold objections before any merits ruling; if none succeeds, reach only the preserved questions.

* **Merits Paths, Judgment, and Remedy:** A state-exemption holding could terminate forfeiture of the protected interest if the federal statute leaves that exemption operative. A conflict holding permits the federal action but leaves constitutional limits intact. Unlike the absence of a requirement in Sprietsma, §881(a)(7) affirmatively authorizes the federal remedy upon its own conditions; the asserted homestead bar would defeat that remedy rather than supplement an unregulated safety question. If the existing record and findings establish proportionality, the decree may be sustained; if they establish gross disproportionality, relief must reach the excessive exaction. Where material factual predicates remain unresolved, a limited remand for findings and a fresh constitutional conclusion is available without an automatic new jury trial or property-return order.

* **Plausible Holding Scope:** Federal displacement of the asserted Oklahoma exemption in a § 881(a)(7) action, and a record-based excessiveness determination for this residence. No general federal occupation of property law, universal innocent-owner rule, or fixed residential-forfeiture ceiling is necessary.

* **Material Uncertainty:** The reported opinion does not establish the complete trial exhibits, precise contraband value, legislative penalty analysis or any post-remand district disposition. A reported appraisal is not a final constitutional valuation finding.

## 60. Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name:** Connecticut Department of Public Safety v. Doe

**Citation or Docket:** 538 U.S. 1

**October Term:** 2002

**Matter Type:** MERITS

**Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS. Review of the identified lower-court judgment on the listed question.

**Simulated Event Date:** 2003-03-05 (historical).

**Question Presented (summary):** Whether procedural due process requires Connecticut to provide a hearing on current dangerousness before publicly disclosing registration information when the registration and disclosure requirements turn on conviction rather than a finding of current dangerousness.

**Question Granted:** The historical question concerns procedural due process: whether a current-dangerousness hearing must precede public registration based on a qualifying conviction. No substantive-due-process challenge to the statutory classification is granted or resolved; no dangerousness finding is silently made.

**Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Second Circuit affirmed summary judgment and an injunction against public disclosure without a dangerousness hearing.

**Relief Requested:** Connecticut seeks reversal of the procedural injunction. Doe seeks an opportunity to establish that he is not currently dangerous before inclusion in the publicly available registry.

**Material Record:** Connecticut’s registration scheme required persons convicted of specified offenses to supply identifying information, made available through a public registry and the Internet. The website did not claim that the State had individually found every registrant currently dangerous. Doe’s qualifying conviction was not disputed. His proposed hearing would address current dangerousness rather than the statutory predicate. He expressly disavowed a substantive-due-process challenge in the Supreme Court presentation. The record must not be rewritten as containing a risk tier, an individualized dangerousness finding, or a statutory exemption for demonstrably low-risk offenders.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The claim presented is procedural. The existence of a protected interest may be assumed for purposes of testing whether the proposed hearing addresses an authorizing fact. Whether the substantive disclosure rule, collection of nonpublic addresses, or dissemination safeguards independently violates another right is a separate question, not resolved by calling the registry civil.

**Relevant Dates:** The historical appellate judgment preceded the March 5, 2003 event. The operative conviction-based registration provisions control, not later amendments or later empirical risk findings.

**Companion or Related Matters:** Smith v. Doe is separately listed later on the same date and concerns a different statute and ex post facto challenge.

**Participation Matter:** None identified.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

**Current Through:** Law effective through June 28, 2002. Earlier decisions of this term enter only when actually decided.

**Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None is assumed from calendar placement alone.

**Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.

##### Issue 1: Procedure for a conviction-based disclosure rule

**Controlling Law:** W.P. v. Verniero requires a meaningful contest where risk grading and disclosure plans are legally material; its State-persuasion and risk-proof holdings depend on that regime. Paul P. v. Verniero recognizes bounded privacy protection for compiled nonpublic current home information and requires a justified audience-information relationship. Neither creates a universal hearing on an immaterial fact.

**Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify the interest, the authorized deprivation, and the facts that legally permit it. Procedure tests those facts; it does not itself create a substantive exception. Distinguish risk-tier proof from disclosure-safeguard claims.

**Other Governing Law:** The Fourteenth Amendment and the operative Connecticut conviction-based law govern the procedural claim.

**Material Other Authority:** Paul v. Davis supplies the reputation distinction; this case can be resolved without deciding every asserted stigma-plus theory.

**Existing Rule’s Reach:** Entering law protects meaningful process where the governmental entitlement turns on a disputed fact. W.P.’s risk hearing cannot be detached from its risk-based legal predicate and transplanted as a universal requirement.

**Open Point:** Whether a dangerousness hearing provides constitutionally required process when a valid conviction alone triggers disclosure, assuming without deciding a protected interest.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below:** The lower courts treated public sex-offender disclosure as requiring an opportunity to show absence of dangerousness.

**Connecticut:** The legislature selected conviction as the trigger. A hearing about a fact the statute does not use cannot affect lawful inclusion. Any attack on that choice is substantive and was not presented.

**Doe:** Public notification conveys dangerousness in practical effect and causes substantial consequences. The proposed process would correct an inaccurate present implication even if the statute is formally conviction-based.

**Limits:** The State cannot avoid a hearing on a genuinely disputed conviction or other actual inclusion criterion by labeling the claim immaterial. Doe cannot use a procedural claim to obtain an exemption that requires invalidating the substantive trigger.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may reverse because the requested hearing addresses no disputed authorizing fact, assuming an interest. It may affirm only if the law or government’s actual determination makes dangerousness material, or if an adequately preserved independent constitutional ground supports the injunction. Reversal leaves separately preserved substantive privacy or disclosure claims to their proper legal framework; it does not adjudicate them against Doe.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** The predicate-focused rule would apply to administrative disclosure and licensing schemes whose legally operative facts differ from a requested hearing topic. It would not eliminate process where the actual statutory criterion is disputed. A contrary rule would require explaining how procedural due process supplies a dangerousness exception despite the conviction-based scheme.

**Uncertainty:** W.P.’s tailored notification regime and Connecticut’s general dissemination regime differ materially. That difference may matter to a substantive claim, but it does not establish a procedural entitlement to litigate an unused fact.

## 62. Ewing v. California

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name:** Ewing v. California

**Citation or Docket:** 538 U.S. 11

**October Term:** 2002

**Matter Type:** MERITS

**Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS. Review of the identified lower-court judgment on the listed question.

**Simulated Event Date:** 2003-03-05 (historical).

**Question Presented (summary):** Whether California’s sentence of twenty-five years to life for felony theft of three golf clubs, imposed under its three-strikes law in light of the defendant’s prior serious convictions, violates the Eighth Amendment prohibition on grossly disproportionate punishment.

**Question Granted:** The historical question concerns whether this twenty-five-years-to-life grand-theft sentence, imposed after the identified prior serious convictions, violates the Eighth Amendment. It is direct review of this sentence, not the distinct §2254(d) question in Lockyer v. Andrade and not a facial challenge to every application of the three-strikes law.

**Court and Judgment Under Review:** The California Court of Appeal upheld Ewing’s sentence, and the California Supreme Court denied review.

**Relief Requested:** Ewing seeks reversal of the sentence and lawful resentencing. California seeks affirmance of its recidivist sentencing judgment.

**Material Record:** While on parole, Ewing stole three golf clubs priced at $399 each. The approximately $1,197 theft was charged as a felony. His prior record included three residential burglaries and a robbery, qualifying as serious strikes. The sentencing court declined to reduce the current “wobbler” offense to a misdemeanor or dismiss qualifying strikes and imposed twenty-five years to life. The sentence includes a long minimum before parole eligibility, not an assured release at twenty-five years. Prior crimes matter to culpability and recidivism; they do not change the present theft into an assault. The court must evaluate the actual sentence and actual record, not an abstract first-time shoplifting hypothetical.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** This is direct review of the state criminal judgment, not review under the federal habeas deference statute. The constitutional challenge is to the sentence as applied. State-law discretion to reduce an offense or dismiss strikes is relevant to the scheme but does not alone establish that the resulting sentence is constitutional.

**Relevant Dates:** The theft and prior convictions predate the March 5, 2003 event. The legally relevant penalty is the sentence actually imposed for this offense with the established priors.

**Companion or Related Matters:** Lockyer v. Andrade is separately listed and presents an independent federal-habeas limitation issue.

**Participation Matter:** None identified.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

**Current Through:** Law effective through June 28, 2002. Earlier decisions of this term enter only when actually decided.

**Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** None is assumed from calendar placement alone.

**Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** None identified.

##### Issue 1: Proportionality in recidivist imprisonment

**Controlling Law:** Rummel v. Estelle, Solem v. Helm and the fractured decision in Harmelin v. Michigan provide the inherited noncapital proportionality authorities. Harmelin rejects a mandatory individualized-sentencing requirement outside the capital context; the proportionality reasoning must be attributed to the Justices who actually adopted it rather than described as a single majority formula. Henderson v. Norris holds life without ordinary parole grossly disproportionate for the specified first tiny-sale conviction under the surviving narrow proportionality principle. It requires attention to actual offense, offender and release prospects, but does not decide a sentence supported by Ewing’s serious prior convictions.

**Current Reusable Doctrine:** Assess the gravity of the present offense with legally relevant recidivism, the severity and practical operation of the punishment, and genuinely informative sentencing comparisons. Gross disproportionality is a constitutional limit, not ordinary disagreement over the best sentence.

**Other Governing Law:** The Eighth Amendment, applicable to the State, and California’s three-strikes and offense-classification provisions govern.

**Material Other Authority:** The state-court reasoning and sentencing comparisons inform application. Harmelin’s different life-without-parole drug sentence does not establish a categorical rule for every recidivist theft sentence.

**Existing Rule’s Reach:** The State may punish recidivism and need not sentence the present theft as a first offense. That power does not itself answer whether this minimum term is grossly disproportionate.

**Open Point:** How the proportionality limit applies to a long parole minimum for this nonviolent property offense with serious prior crimes.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Reasoning below:** The state court emphasized repeated serious criminal conduct and legislative incapacitation choices, treating the felony theft as the latest offense in a persistent pattern.

**Ewing:** A quarter-century minimum and possible life imprisonment for this theft are grossly excessive even after accounting for recidivism. Prior convictions justify enhancement, not elimination of a relationship between current wrongdoing and punishment.

**California:** The sanction addresses repeated serious offending, failed lesser sanctions, and public protection. Courts should not replace legislative judgments with a free-ranging comparison of preferred terms.

**Limits:** Ewing cannot remove the robbery and burglaries from the analysis. The State cannot establish proportionality merely by repeating the recidivist label. The case does not require invalidating three-strikes laws generally or setting a universal maximum for theft.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may affirm if the sentence falls within constitutional recidivist discretion. It may reverse the sentence as grossly disproportionate and remand for a lawful term, leaving the conviction intact. A rule specifying the proper comparison and weight of recidivism could support remand for a materially deficient analysis, but factual record supplementation is not a reason to evade a fully presented constitutional judgment.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** An affirmance could permit this long minimum for a defendant with these serious strikes without approving every recidivist sentence. A reversal could hold this particular disparity unconstitutional while leaving substantial recidivist enhancements available. A categorical abolition of proportionality review would require an express change to entering law rather than application of it.

**Uncertainty:** Parole eligibility and actual minimum terms must not be confused with guaranteed release or with life without parole.

## 63. Lockyer v. Andrade

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name:** Lockyer v. Andrade

**Citation or Docket:** 538 U.S. 63

**October Term:** 2002

**Matter Type:** MERITS

**Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS. Review of the identified lower-court judgment on the listed question.

**Simulated Event Date:** 2003-03-05 (historical).

**Question Presented:** Whether the California courts unreasonably applied clearly established federal proportionality law, within 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1), by sustaining two consecutive sentences of twenty-five years to life for two petty thefts with a prior conviction under California’s three-strikes law.

**Question Granted:** The historical review issue is stated above in substance; threshold matters and any separable question not necessary to judgment are identified below.

**Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit reversed the federal district court’s denial of habeas relief and concluded that the state adjudication of Andrade’s sentence was an unreasonable application of the Eighth Amendment. The underlying California judgment sustained the two consecutive terms.

**Relief Requested:** The State seeks reversal of the federal writ decision. Andrade seeks relief from the fifty-year minimum aggregate sentence and a lawful resentencing, not acquittal of the thefts.

**Material Record:** Andrade stole videotapes in two separate shoplifting incidents, with a combined value of about $150. California treated each petty theft with a prior conviction as a felony and used qualifying prior serious convictions, including residential burglaries, to impose a separate twenty-five-years-to-life term for each current offense, consecutively. The case therefore combines low-value, nonviolent current conduct, recidivism, separate criminal occasions, and an aggregate period before parole eligibility that must not be mistaken for a single twenty-five-year sentence. The federal district court denied the petition; the court of appeals applied its understanding of gross-disproportionality decisions to grant relief.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Section 2254(d)(1) is a condition on federal habeas relief following a state merits adjudication. An independent conclusion that a punishment is unconstitutional does not by itself establish that the state court acted objectively unreasonably. The relevant clearly established law is determined at the state merits adjudication, not at this Court’s present event. The writ must address the sentence actually imposed and the claims exhausted; it cannot become direct review of California sentencing discretion.

**Relevant Dates:** The thefts occurred in 1995. The state appellate merits adjudication preceded the present federal habeas litigation. The review date is March 5, 2003. That date does not move the § 2254(d)(1) source date forward.

**Participation Matter:** None identified.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

**Current Through:** Governing decisions through June 28, 2002, together with Early v. Packer and Woodford v. Visciotti, effective November 4, 2002.

**Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** Early v. Packer and Woodford v. Visciotti apply §2254(d) to the actual state reasoning and whole supported record. Missing citations or imprecise shorthand alone do not establish unreasonableness; neither formal correctness nor generality of the governing rule immunizes an objectively unreasonable application.

**Unresolved Same-Term Dependencies:** Ewing v. California presents a separate direct-review question on the same date. Its eventual direct-review holding could inform current proportionality analysis, but a new holding would not automatically become clearly established law at Andrade’s earlier state adjudication.

##### Issue 1: Proportionality on federal habeas review

**Controlling Law:** Terry Williams v. Taylor separates contrary-to and unreasonable-application review and rejects both a mere-error standard and a test that counts judges who find an interpretation reasonable. Its governing inquiry remains objective unreasonableness. Early rejects citation-based habeas error and a necessary factual-twin rule while examining the actual constitutional context. Visciotti requires assessment of the whole state opinion and supported sentencing record, not isolated wording or an independent preferred result. Those statutory-review holdings do not add substantive proportionality law to the earlier state-adjudication date.

**Current Reusable Doctrine:** Identify the Supreme Court holding effective at the state merits decision; identify the applicable principle at its proper generality; compare the state’s reasoning and outcome to that principle; then separately establish the underlying constitutional error and appropriate relief.

**Other Governing Law:** The Eighth Amendment and § 2254(d)(1). The general gross-disproportionality principle reflected in Rummel v. Estelle and Solem v. Helm supplies relevant earlier background.

**Material Other Authority:** Harmelin v. Michigan preserves a narrow gross-disproportionality principle while sustaining the punishment for its large drug quantity. Its fractured reasoning must be attributed to the Justices who adopted it; its result does not resolve every recidivist property sentence.

**Existing Rule’s Reach:** A general principle can clearly establish law, but the existing cases do not resolve whether this particular recidivist sentence crosses the principle’s boundary or whether such an error would be objectively indefensible.

**Open Point:** How sharply the preexisting decisions constrained a state court addressing two low-value felonies with serious priors and consecutive mandatory terms.

**Additional controlling authority:** Bell v. Cone (May 28, 2002) retains objective unreasonableness and distinct merits requirements. Henderson v. Norris (May 13, 2002) grants a sentence-only writ under pre-AEDPA review for a first exceptionally small drug sale punished with life without ordinary parole; its holding neither supplies law unavailable at Andrade’s state merits date nor eliminates this case’s AEDPA restriction.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

The State argues that the decisions leave substantial room for legislative recidivism judgments and do not establish a sufficiently close analogue to make the state adjudication unreasonable. It emphasizes the prior burglaries, two new offenses, and the distinction between habeas review and a fresh proportionality judgment. It need not establish that every sentence under the scheme is constitutional. Andrade argues that the sentence’s practical severity, current conduct, and comparison with the earlier proportionality cases make the result an extreme and objectively unreasonable application even without an identical precedent. He challenges any demand for a factual twin or any conversion of legislative discretion into immunity from review. The court of appeals’ use of an independently correct Eighth Amendment analysis must be tested against the additional statutory constraint. Conversely, criticism of a clear-error formulation does not itself establish that the state’s result survives the correct standard.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may reverse the federal relief decision if the state adjudication falls within the range of reasonable applications, without approving the punishment on direct review. It may affirm relief if it identifies a governing principle and explains, under Early and Visciotti’s whole-adjudication review, why sustaining this aggregate sentence was objectively unreasonable, followed by a conditional resentencing remedy. Neither imperfect wording nor the federal court’s preferred punishment can supply that explanation. Any remand should identify whether it concerns the § 2254(d) gateway, the Eighth Amendment merits, or the sentence to be corrected.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** An application of the habeas standard to the identified state adjudication and these consecutive terms. It need not validate every three-strikes sentence, establish a numerical constitutional minimum, or hold that general proportionality principles can never support habeas relief.

**Uncertainty:** Section 2254(d)(1) requires law established at the state merits adjudication. Later proportionality decisions may explain an existing rule but cannot create the necessary earlier constraint.

#### E. SOURCES

Lockyer v. Andrade, 538 U.S. 63 (record and procedural history).
