### Geographic exclusions, including interstate circulation

Seven Associates directly support the Anderson–Burdick explanation: assess the actual burden and the State's precise interests; these exclusions directly prevent otherwise qualified advocates from undertaking the protected candidate-petition activity unless they relocate, imposing a severe burden that requires narrow tailoring to a compelling interest. Lerman establishes the protected candidate-circulation function and invalidity of its reviewed in-state district screen. The interstate application requires its own analysis because Lerman's statewide-process facts do not establish reach over every nonresident.

On the reported record, the independent interstate explanation is sufficient. Qualified signers establish local support; qualified voters select nominees. The candidates' chosen advocates solicit that support and communicate political views. The Board's local-support, boundary-knowledge, valid-signature, election-integrity and restriction-of-outside-influence grounds do not establish a need for either territorial screen. Residence does not establish ability to identify boundaries or obtain valid signatures, while certification and the existing signature-challenge process address the reported verification task. A State's authority to define its electorate does not itself justify preventing its candidates from associating with citizens of other States as political advocates.

The strongest distinct defense is interstate accountability. It cannot be dismissed by assuming every Illinois subpoena reaches every outsider. But the reported litigation expressly did not advance the process-consent and demonstrated nonresident-fraud grounds discussed in footnote 7. The judgment can decide the actual asserted grounds without supplying a different litigation case. Footnote 7's possible administrative registration and jurisdictional consent are neither enacted voter qualifications nor remedies imposed by this decision. The hypothetical fraud correlation is not a finding. The coalition's rule invalidates these actual territorial exclusions on this record; it does not need a conclusion that every differently drawn accountability measure is invalid.

Thomas supports each geographic judgment under traditional strict scrutiny for direct political petition speech. His distinct method reaches the same statutory applications and injunction, without becoming an eighth direct join to the generalized graduated method.

### Enrollment by otherwise eligible residents

Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg directly support invalidation under Buckley's speaker-protection reasoning and the calibrated inquiry. The candidate-petition context does not change the protected task into an office reserved to voters. Exclusion of an otherwise qualified resident advocate remains a substantial restriction even when registration is readily available. Qualified signers, actual electoral support and retained verification address the claimed functions more directly; available enrollment does not itself establish a sufficient need to condition the candidate's choice of advocate on voter status. Thomas reaches invalidity through traditional strict scrutiny.

O'Connor and Breyer acknowledge the same excluded voice but retain their published Buckley assessment where enrollment is actually accessible without relocation. For a person already resident and eligible within the relevant electorate, they view the enrollment step and candidate-nomination connection as a reasonable administrative burden justified by the asserted interests. Their disagreement does not concern the existence of injury, and they do not mistake voter enrollment for registration with an election board. Their premise does not apply to an outsider who must move to become eligible.

The full-affirmance coalition answers their strongest ground directly: ordinary availability of enrollment fails to justify exclusion of an otherwise qualified chosen speaker, and the supported candidate context supplies no administrative function that changes Buckley's conclusion. The majority's invalidation therefore covers the eligible-resident application as well as the territorial barriers. This is a candidate-petition application of the speech rule, not a claim that Buckley's formal initiative holding already adjudicated every issue here.

## Remedy and scrutiny of the partial-disposition alternative

**Six full affirmances.** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg affirm the Seventh Circuit judgment and leave the existing injunction against the reviewed offending portions operative. It covers the actual statewide and district applications, including citizens of other States and otherwise eligible unregistered residents, within the certified controversy and against responsible defendants. The lower appellate judgment itself was an affirmance, not an express remand. The mandate preserves the injunction for ordinary enforcement; the record identifies no unfinished merits finding requiring a substantive remand.

**Two partial reversals.** O'Connor and Breyer would retain the existing injunction against enforcement of the challenged voter-and-residence qualification against otherwise qualified persons outside the candidate's represented electorate. They would reverse only the injunction against enforcement upon persons already resident in and eligible to register within that electorate. Their mandate would direct that legal narrowing of the existing decree. It leaves Illinois enrollment enforceable for otherwise eligible Illinois residents in the Senate application and seventh-district enrollment enforceable for otherwise eligible seventh-district residents in the House application.

This position is an application-specific constitutional judgment about the reach of the existing injunction. It does not depend on a finding of textual severability or a new statute consisting of registration in any State. It authorizes no voter-enrollment requirement for an outsider and no board-registration substitute. Both Justices acknowledge its practical asymmetry: the existing condition would remain enforceable for an eligible insider even though a similarly situated outsider could circulate without voter enrollment. Their answer is that constitutional relief should extend only to the applications they find invalid; relocation is severe while accessible local enrollment is, in their view, reasonable. They do not attribute that asymmetry to a legislative decision or declare it a new general election rule.

The strongest objection to this partial remedy is substantive, not a hidden assumption that the statute has already been severed: it continues excluding qualified resident speakers even though the same verification and support mechanisms suffice without voter status. The six full-affirmance Associates sustain the existing injunction because that residual application is itself unconstitutional on their grounds. No separate equal-protection claim about a later altered system is invented to settle this case.

No Justice requires a new constitutional hearing, new electoral timetable, new administrative form, service waiver or proof of consent. Ordinary implementation of an affirmed or narrowed injunction is distinct from reopening the merits. The source's optional alternatives remain analytical possibilities outside the actual imposed conditions, not mandate instructions.

**Thomas's separate ground, in full substantive scope.** The candidates have a protected interest in selecting political associates to communicate their message and solicit signatures. Direct exclusion of those speakers receives traditional strict scrutiny. Neither voter enrollment nor residence in Illinois or the congressional district establishes the advocate's ability to perform that task. Qualified electors supply support and choose nominees; circulators communicate with them. Meyer protects that conversation, and his Buckley concurrence protects the available speaker pool. His Lerman concurrence confirms that formal candidate nomination does not turn advocacy into an administrative office. The actual support, boundary, valid-signature and integrity interests do not justify the registration or territorial restrictions in light of retained verification, while excluding outside political influence cannot justify the candidates' lost association. He therefore supports invalidation for the Senate, the House and eligible local residents, and full affirmance of the existing injunction. He declines the generalized graduated-scrutiny method; his agreement does not impose the lower court's hypothetical process alternatives.

**O'Connor and Breyer's separate ground, in full substantive scope.** They accept the candidates' own injury and the continuing class controversy. Under the calibrated burden inquiry reflected in Burdick, their published Buckley position and Lerman, a rule requiring an advocate to change residence is materially different from readily available enrollment. They join the invalidation of the Senate-wide and House-district geographic applications, including citizens of other States. The State's authority over its electorate does not establish that the candidate's advocate must be an elector, and the Board's actual interests do not justify those barriers. Interstate service differs from Lerman's facts, but the reported case did not advance the footnote 7 process or demonstrated nonresident-fraud theories.

They dissent from invalidation only where an otherwise qualified person already resides and is eligible to register within the represented electorate. They acknowledge the lost voice but consider that accessible step, in a formal candidate-nomination system, a reasonable burden supported by the asserted administrative interests. Their partial reversal would narrow the existing injunction only to permit that local application. They acknowledge that outsiders would remain free from a voter-enrollment obligation and explain that result as a limit on the constitutional relief they consider justified. They preserve neither a nationwide registration condition nor an obligation for outsiders to register in Illinois, in the district or merely with the Board. Their position is a disagreement with part of the judgment, not concurrence in the entire result on a narrower ground. No author or joinder arrangement for presenting their shared substantive position is selected here.
