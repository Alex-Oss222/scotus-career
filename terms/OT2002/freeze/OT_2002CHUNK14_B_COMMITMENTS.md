## 167. Lawrence v. Texas

### Questions, operative alternatives and judgment

The merits are reachable. The no-contest convictions supply concrete injury and preserve these constitutional questions. Three paths are materially supported: reverse on substantive liberty and expressly displace Bowers; reverse only on the sex-specific criminal classification while retaining Bowers; or affirm under Bowers and ordinary equal-protection review. An opinion adopting the liberty ground cannot call Bowers silently displaced by Casey or Romer.

**Liberty commitment L1:** The liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment includes private consensual sexual intimacy between adults. Texas may not criminally punish the charged conduct solely to enforce moral disapproval of the participants' same-sex intimacy. The State identifies no separate injury or protective feature in this adjudicated setting sufficient to justify that criminal intrusion. Bowers is overruled to the extent it permits that prohibition; the convictions must be reversed and set aside on remand. This is a substantive answer to the criminal prohibition presented, not an instruction that Texas could preserve these convictions by restating the same moral objection.

L1 rests on the individual character of intimate choice reflected in Griswold and Eisenstadt, the protected-choice foundation retained in Casey, and the requirement of real constitutional justification rather than an assumed disability illustrated by Romer. Glucksberg sustains a different prohibition through its actual protection-of-life, error, coercion and professional-responsibility grounds; it adopts no exclusive method requiring every protected liberty to have been historically described in the precise terms of the prohibited act. Casey's six steps are not imported as a universal intimacy test. The operative rule covers the private consensual adult conduct at issue; a rule about coercion, minors, public conduct, commercial sex or marriage status is not part of this commitment.

**Equality commitment L2:** Under ordinary rational-basis review, Texas's selective criminal prohibition fails because its distinction between otherwise comparable private adult conduct rests on disapproval of the same-sex class rather than a legitimate explanation for the additional criminal disadvantage. General authority to regulate conduct does not itself explain why only these participants receive punishment. The convictions must be reversed without deciding that all sex classifications receive a new tier or that Bowers has been overruled.

**Affirmance commitment L3:** Bowers directly permits the specified conduct prohibition, and the prior intimate-choice cases do not displace it. Conduct-based moral judgment supplies a rational reason to prohibit this category while leaving other sexual conduct unpunished. This is not equivalent to recognizing bare hatred as legitimate or treating every moral classification as valid. On that account both preserved constitutional challenges fail and the convictions are affirmed.

### Operative rule alternatives

**Equality E1:** Kansas may punish voluntary sexual conduct involving minors, but it may not deny this otherwise applicable close-in-age mitigation solely because the two participants are of the same sex. On the actual statutory comparison, child protection, maturity and general sexual-health concerns do not rationally explain withholding mitigation from one sex combination while granting it for the same voluntary act, ages, age gap and criminal history. Bare disapproval of the same-sex participants supplies no independent explanation for the added disadvantage. Ordinary rational-basis review suffices; no new orientation tier or fundamental entitlement to sex with minors is adopted.

The strongest contrary path E2 accepts the legislature's judgment that facilitating opposite-sex adolescent development while discouraging same-sex conduct serves a legitimate conduct-based moral policy, particularly where minors are concerned. It would sustain the differential mitigation as an imperfect but rational distinction under Bowers and ordinary review. E1 rejects that account because the classification's operative penalty distinction is precisely what needs justification: a label describing approved development adds no protective connection to the otherwise identical mitigated act. The State remains free to regulate the minor conduct generally.

**Proportionality P1:** The imposed 206-month prison term is grossly disproportionate to this voluntary close-in-age act, even giving substantial aggravating weight to the two prior juvenile adjudications. The present act, the serious history and the actual term must be assessed together; history does not replace the current offense. The radically lower penalty Kansas itself selects for the same act and history with opposite-sex participants is strong comparative evidence, not an automatic ratio rule or a substitute for examining gravity. The ruling concerns the actual term imposed; it does not invent parole eligibility or treat the advocacy about absence of injury as a judicial finding.

The strongest contrary path P2 gives exceptional deference to the legislature's protection of minors and its response to repeated sexual offenses. The current victim's age and the prior aggravated-sodomy adjudications distinguish this case from Ewing's nonviolent property offense, while unequal mitigation is properly corrected through equality rather than converted into a general Eighth Amendment rule. Scalia and Thomas additionally reject the asserted prison-duration proportionality right itself, independently of this application.

**Juvenile-predicate J1:** A valid nonjury juvenile adjudication may remain relevant to lawful sentencing, but its existence does not categorically exempt a historical fact that legally authorizes a higher binding range from the adult prosecution's element protections. McKeiver permits the juvenile procedure within its setting; it does not establish automatic authority for a later aggravated adult range. This narrow rule does not invalidate juvenile judgments, incorporate a state grand-jury requirement, or require relitigation of the underlying conduct when the legal predicate is the existence, identity and qualifying character of an adjudication.

The strongest alternative J2 treats a valid prior adjudication, obtained through the constitutionally permitted juvenile process, as an adjudicated offender-status fact appropriately used in the later sentencing system. J1 answers that Monge deliberately reserved the independent jury and proof issue and that a binding range is actual punishment authority under Angle. ## 169. Stogner v. California

### Two constitutional paths

**Revival-bar S1:** Once the governing criminal limitations period has completely expired, a later legislature may not revive authority to prosecute and punish that completed conduct. The change restores criminal exposure after the existing law has removed it; its effect is qualitatively different from extending a still-running deadline or admitting additional kinds of evidence in an otherwise authorized prosecution. The Ex Post Facto Clause bars applying section 803(g) to both pending indictment counts, and the state judgment must be reversed with directions to terminate those revival-dependent counts.

S1 applies the received Calder protection against retrospective restoration or aggravation of penal liability, informed by Brown v. Walker's account of completed limitations protection. Carmell requires examination of actual legal effect rather than a procedural label and preserves the traditional categories; S1 does not turn Carmell into a general vested-procedure rule. The law does more than alter the manner of trial: without revival the State lacks authority to prosecute either count. The supporting rationale does not depend on innocence, subjective reliance, a finding that evidence was lost, or a prediction about the verdict. The State's reporting and corroboration safeguards address reliability but do not restore power that the Constitution prevents the legislature from reviving.

**Permissible-revival S2:** The Ex Post Facto Clause's received categories concern retrospective criminalization, aggravation of the offense, greater prescribed punishment, or reduction of the legally necessary proof. Section 803(g) changes the time during which the same already-criminal conduct may be prosecuted, without changing the charged acts, prescribed substantive punishment or legally necessary proof of guilt. Completed expiration is substantial statutory protection but does not itself transform revival into one of those prohibited changes. S2 would reject the Ex Post Facto challenge.

The S2 supporters must also dispose of the granted due-process question. They reject the categorical theory that expiry alone creates a constitutionally irrevocable immunity. The conduct was criminal when committed, and the new enactment does not retrospectively remove a substantive offense boundary of the kind Rogers involved. Brown describes the legal force of an expired limit under then-operative law; it does not constitutionalize every such statutory protection. Reliability safeguards and the continuing possibility of a particularized preaccusation-delay objection distinguish permission to prosecute from a finding that every later prosecution is fair. The supplied record establishes neither actual lost evidence nor intentional delay or a trial finding sufficient to decide that distinct factual objection. Affirmance on S2 therefore permits otherwise lawful proceedings on both counts, subject to the statute's actual requirements and the remaining fact-specific inquiry.

### Statutory terms material to both paths

The route concerns designated offenses reported to a California law-enforcement agency by a person of any age alleging victimization before eighteen, with the complaint route ordinarily measured from that report. It requires prior expiry, substantial sexual conduct with nonmutual masturbation excluded, and independent clear-and-convincing corroboration. Otherwise inadmissible evidence cannot satisfy that corroboration condition; mental-health professionals' opinions are not independent evidence. The retrospective, pre/post-January 1, 1997, 180-day transition, refiling, dismissal-effect and nonbinding-prior-ruling provisions remain separate as reproduced in the bounded extract. No commitment converts them into a universal one-year indictment requirement or treats the cross-complainant allegations as established corroboration. The later reproduced section 288(a) penalty supplies no new finding about the offense-time sentence.

## 170. Nike, Inc. v. Kasky

### Threshold alternatives and common commitment

The operative finality question is distinct from the importance of the speech issues and the fact that the state court has settled one legal classification. Jefferson, Florida v. Thomas and the separate-judgment holding in Pierce County preserve all four Cox routes. The present proceedings are not practically complete; factual liability and relief remain. A defense victory could eliminate the need to reach the federal question, effective later review is available if an adverse judgment requires it, and no present disclosure or comparable irreversible legal transfer is ordered.

The strongest immediate-review path invokes Cox's fourth category: California has conclusively classified the communications, the pending suit itself restrains participation in a current public debate, and a First Amendment reversal could end that cause of action. Ritchie prevents treating the bare theoretical availability of later review as conclusive. ASARCO also prevents mechanically importing state-plaintiff standing requirements into a defendant's federal appellate position.

The independent modeling conclusion is that the supplied record does not establish the fourth route. A communication-specific classification correction or a fault-standard correction could leave substantive litigation on the relevant cause of action; a categorical immunity for all the challenged representations is neither established by the entering law nor a premise that may be assumed to create jurisdiction. The actual speech content, asserted deception and potential permissible theory require distinction. Ordinary exposure to continued litigation and a claim that the settled legal rule affects future speech do not alone establish that postponement will destroy the federal protection. The case differs from a completed production determination whose protected information would be irretrievably disclosed.

**Common threshold commitment N1:** Dismiss the writ for want of a final state judgment within section 1257. This disposition leaves the state judgment and ordinary proceedings in place and supplies no endorsement of its commercial-speech rule. The Article III appellate-injury question and the scope/preservation objection to the private-enforcement challenge need not be decided to supply this statutory disposition. That is not a holding that Kasky must satisfy Article III in state court or that Nike can never acquire an injury permitting later review.
