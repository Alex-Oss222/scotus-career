## 18. Yellow Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan, No. 01-270 — November 5, 2002

Michigan formerly waived fees for Yellow's Illinois-plated vehicles under its reciprocity practice. Yellow's principal place of business was Kansas, which lacked the relevant reciprocity arrangement. Michigan announced during 1991 that its 1992 registrations would instead use principal place of business; the announced change took effect February 1, 1992. Yellow paid the disputed 1992 assessment under protest in October 1991. The present requested refunds concern fees under the Single State Registration System, effective January 1, 1994. Advance collection for the 1992 registration year and the legally relevant November 15, 1991 benchmark are distinct matters.

The Michigan Supreme Court reversed refund judgments because it treated the State's generic $10 charge, rather than the effect of reciprocity on particular carriers, as the benchmark. The question presented concerns that generic-fee premise. Whether Yellow's particular informal waiver qualified, the effect of the advance 1992 payment, the legality of a later principal-place-of-business criterion, and the final refund amount remain for the appropriate proceedings on remand; the Court need not decide them to reject or sustain the premise under review.

Former 49 U.S.C. §11506(c)(2)(B)(iv), now §14504, expressly directed the ICC to establish the fee system, linked it to the number of vehicles and States, capped each State's charge at the fee it collected or charged as of November 15, 1991, and imposed a $10 ceiling. Its administration must minimize carrier compliance costs while preserving the relevant State revenues. The ICC's initial proposal questioned continuing reciprocity, but its final May 1993 rule preserved reciprocity in the benchmark after explaining the effects on carrier costs and State revenues. An explained change during rulemaking is not an unexplained abandonment of a settled final rule.

Two lawful readings require consideration: the statutory benchmark itself may account for the fee actually imposed on the carrier, including a qualifying waiver; alternatively, the text may leave the treatment of reciprocity to the expressly delegated fee-system rulemaking. The competing generic-fee reading stresses uniform administration and the distinction between a fee and a waiver. Neither a broad fiscal objective nor deference alone can overcome contrary statutory text. A holding upholding the final rule need not hold that every individualized concession becomes a permanent statutory entitlement.

## 23. Howsam v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc., No. 01-800 — December 10, 2002

The Tenth Circuit reversed dismissal of Dean Witter's suit to prevent NASD arbitration, treating NASD Rule 10304's six-year eligibility requirement as a judicial issue. The Supreme Court issue is allocation of that issue, not whether these claims are actually timely. Purchases in 1986, the 1997 demand, alleged concealment, and continuing reassurances supply the parties' competing timeliness positions; concealment is not an established finding.

The August 26, 1992 client agreement, JA 6–9, provides for construction and enforcement under New York law, excluding its choice-of-law doctrine. Its broad arbitration clause covers controversies concerning the accounts, transactions, or the agreement's construction, performance, or breach, including controversies arising before the agreement. It allows the customer to choose an eligible arbitration organization. The customers selected NASD and signed submission forms, JA 24–38. The record supplies Dean Witter's client agreement, but the customers' separate submission forms do not by themselves establish Dean Witter's signature on a later submission agreement.

NASD Rule 10304, JA 48, makes a claim ineligible after six years from the occurrence or event giving rise to it. It neither extends applicable statutes of limitations nor applies where a court directs arbitration. Rule 10324 assigns arbitrators interpretation and applicability of the Code's provisions and makes their compliance rulings final and binding. Both provisions must be construed together with the agreement.

There are two independently available routes. Under Wiley, Moses H. Cone, and the consent principle retained in First Options, a time requirement governing an existing arbitration agreement may be a presumptively arbitral procedural issue rather than a judicial question whether the parties ever agreed to arbitrate. Alternatively, the chosen New York law can resolve the allocation through the actual incorporation of NASD's interpretive delegation. Smith Barney Shearson, Inc. v. Sacharow, 91 N.Y.2d 39 (1997), treats the NASD eligibility issue as substantive arbitrability, ordinarily judicial, but holds the broad agreement plus Rule 10324 a clear delegation. Do not describe Sacharow as adopting the federal procedural classification. Howsam's agreement expressly includes enforcement, so a distinction based only on the absence of that word is unavailable. Sacharow distinguishes a contractual NASD eligibility rule from the statutory limitations issue in Luckie.

## 24. Abdur'Rahman v. Bell, No. 01-9094 — December 10, 2002

The initial federal petition was filed April 23, 1996, as the JA docket and motion confirm; April 25 is an entry date. The original petition's merits therefore use the applicable pre-AEDPA law under Lindh and Carriger. That does not automatically exempt a distinct later authorization proceeding from §2244 under Felker.

The district court's April 1998 judgment granted sentencing relief for ineffective assistance, rejected other claims on their merits, and rejected other claims for procedural default. Both parties appealed. Abdur did not present the procedural-default ruling as an issue in his cross-appeal. The Sixth Circuit reversed the sentencing relief and affirmed the remaining judgment. The present matter does not reopen that appellate merits ruling merely because its record is discussed in the motion.

Tennessee adopted Rule 39 on June 28, 2001. It states that, for criminal and postconviction appeals from July 1, 1967, rehearing or discretionary permission to appeal to the Tennessee Supreme Court is not required for exhaustion after adverse intermediate appellate review; a claim presented there or to the Supreme Court and denied is exhausted. Its capital-review qualification likewise treats an intermediate-court claim as exhausted without renewal on automatic Supreme Court review. The full text is in JA 163–164. Rule 11 discretionary review still exists. The federal effect and retroactive significance of that declaration remain legal questions: the entering O'Sullivan holding expressly reserved the effect of a State's express opt-out while occasional discretionary review remains available.

The November 2, 2001 Rule 60(b)(6) motion is mixed in its requested relief. JA 158–170, especially paragraphs 20–24 and the prayer, identifies fourteen misconduct claims, seeks removal of default barriers, seeks cumulative reconsideration of claims already considered, and ultimately requests a writ setting aside convictions and sentences. The four enumerated claims previously addressed on their merits are D.1(1) (1972 transcript), D.1(7) (blood evidence), D.6 (publication of inadmissible information), and D.8(4) (sentencing argument about the ministry). JA 49–50, 68–69 and motion paragraphs 6–8 establish their distinct earlier treatment. The other ten enumerated claims fall within the default ruling, JA 56–58. The motion also seeks cumulative consideration with ineffective-assistance and instructional claims. It alleges no new evidentiary discovery and relies on the existing record and Rule 39. It cannot accurately be characterized as seeking only correction of a procedural ruling, or only renewed adjudication of claims already decided on their constitutional merits.

The district court transferred the motion under §1631 on November 27, treating it as successive, and declined a certificate on December 4 for lack of jurisdiction. Its pending-appeal discussion recognized the possibility of an indicative ruling and appellate remand. The January 18 appellate order denied a certificate and treated the motion as successive. The February 11 order separately: (1) denied rehearing/remand relief in the original appellate dockets and directed the mandate; (2) dismissed the transfer appeal, No. 01-6504, as an appeal from a nonappealable transfer; and (3) denied actual authorization, No. 01-6487. These are separate rulings with potentially different channels of Supreme Court review. The complete orders are copied from the primary JA in the authorized `OT_2002CHUNK2_ABDUR_PRIMARY_NEUTRAL.md`.

The threshold disputes are whether the transfer effectively concludes a Rule 60 proceeding or is an ordinary nonfinal transfer; whether review of classification is distinct from review of an actual authorization denial under §2244(b)(3)(E); and whether the appellate court's authority over its original case or as transferee permits corrective remand independently of an appeal from transfer. Hohn, Stewart, Slack, Felker, and Calderon must be applied to the particular order, not used interchangeably. The competing substantive paths distinguish a genuine attack on the federal procedural barrier from renewed claims for relief from the conviction, or treat the effort to reopen a completed default judgment on a later legal argument as successive. Even if the procedural part may proceed under Rule 60, timely invocation, extraordinary circumstances, the omitted original appellate issue, Rule 39's effect, and cumulative reconsideration remain separate questions. No original authorization criterion, certificate, Rule 60 relief, stay, or writ follows automatically from rejecting a categorical classification.

## 26. Borden Ranch Partnership v. United States Army Corps of Engineers, No. 01-1243 — December 16, 2002

The remaining disputed locations are jurisdictional drainages and swales on an approximately 8,400-acre grazing ranch converted toward orchards and vineyards. Jurisdiction over those locations is not contested. The government withdrew the vernal-pool component following SWANCC; the Ninth Circuit reversed that component and ordered penalty recalculation. Do not reinstate it or convert SWANCC into a categorical rule about every remaining wetland.

Deep ripping used shanks reaching roughly five to seven feet to break a restrictive layer. The trial findings describe horizontal and vertical movement of earth, rock, sand, and biological material, and particular filling or mounding of former channels. These are more specific than a finding of ecological injury alone. Whether that relocation is an addition of pollutants from a point source remains the statutory issue. Ordinary movement of a blade, a disturbance that merely changes hydrology, and a proven discharge cannot be equated without analysis.

Sections 1311 and 1362 require an addition of a pollutant from a point source to the relevant waters. Dredged spoil, rock, sand, and biological material are among the listed pollutants; the conveyance definition includes rolling stock but excludes agricultural stormwater runoff. Section 1344(f)(1)(A) exempts normal farming activities including plowing. The applicable plowing regulation protects soil breaking and stirring to prepare crops but excludes specified filling or redistribution that converts waters to dry land or destroys their natural drainage, storage, or filtration functions. Section 1344(f)(2) recaptures a discharge incidental to an activity whose purpose is bringing a water area into a use to which it was not previously subject, where flow or circulation may be impaired or reach reduced. Purpose, a new use of the water area, and the specified physical consequence are separate conditions. A ranch's general agricultural history does not alone decide them; accidental passage alone does not establish the required purpose.

The government relies on actual relocation and deposit, the equipment's conveyance of material, absence of established ongoing cultivation of these wet areas, and recapture for their conversion to intensive cultivation. Petitioners contend that moving soil already within the same water adds nothing, that a plow is not a relevant conveyance merely because it touches soil, and that farming followed by farming does not satisfy recapture. The lower dissent stresses the difference between incidental fallback and addition, as well as accidental passes. National Mining Association, 145 F.3d 1399 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is persuasive authority about incidental fallback, not a controlling holding that every redeposit is exempt. The trial rejected the asserted permission/estoppel defense: guidance to raise the shanks did not authorize fully engaged ripping. Its actual findings must be distinguished from the dissent's characterization.

Section 1319(d)'s then-applicable maximum was $25,000 per day for each violation. The trial counted 348 fully engaged passes in drainages (an average twelve at each of twenty-nine locations) plus ten in the subsequently excluded pool, for 358; it imposed $1.5 million, alternatively $500,000 plus restoration of four acres. Its findings attributed a discharge to each counted pass. The question is whether each distinct discharge is a violation or only a single daily violation of the discharge prohibition may be counted. A pass without a discharge is not a violation under either theory. The statutory factors include seriousness, economic benefit, history, good-faith efforts, economic impact, and justice. The remaining penalty must reflect the pool reversal and the lawful unit of violation; no new amount has been fixed here. The trial did not use the later increased maximum.
