## Source and framing validation

1. **Posture and dates.** The Third Circuit reviewed a preliminary injunction, not a final trial judgment. The district court acted May 28, 2002; the appeal was argued September 17 and decided October 8, 2002. Those dates are lower-court dates. The source does not establish a Supreme Court argument date. The Third Circuit reversed on the absence of a First Amendment access right; it did not adjudicate the alternative strict-scrutiny issue or the injunction's nationwide scope. Its dissent would recognize access while narrowing the decree. The dissent's use of a permanent-injunction review formulation does not change the source's otherwise express preliminary posture. See lower opinion pp. 8–11, 38–39, 50–51.

2. **The proceeding.** The claim concerns adversarial deportation hearings for persons already within the country, now styled removal proceedings. It does not encompass the historically distinct exclusion of persons seeking initial admission, every administrative hearing, all detention information, or general intelligence files. The hearings have pleadings, an adjudicator, chosen counsel, presentation of evidence and cross-examination. The legal right asserted is the public's and press's access right; an alien's opportunity to contest removal does not itself answer it. See pp. 22–24, 28, 38, 42–45.

3. **Directive and designation.** The directive closes designated hearings to visitors, family and press; it also suppresses public confirmation of docket or scheduling information. Special-interest status rests on possible connections to, or information about, terrorist activity. The Department periodically reviewed designations and removed some. That review is material to the government's tailoring argument but is not an independent adjudication that each remaining hearing or portion requires closure. A designation is not a finding that the alien committed terrorism. See pp. 8–10.

4. **History.** The parties dispute what statutory silence and practice prove. Since the late nineteenth century exclusion hearings were expressly closed, while deportation legislation did not impose comparable general closure. A regulation adopted in 1964 expressly presumed public deportation hearings, with public-interest and other exceptions. Hearings also occurred in prisons, hospitals and private homes; the majority inferred limits on public entry, while the press and dissent disputed whether those locations established a tradition of closed adjudication. Abuse-related child and spouse exceptions do not prove that ordinary hearings were closed, and the regulatory presumption does not by itself establish a constitutional right. The record supports a qualified-history inquiry, not a factual assertion of literally uninterrupted openness since the founding. See pp. 22–30, 39–43.

5. **Security showing.** Watson's declaration supplies an actual governmental explanation: assembled information may expose investigative sources, methods, entry routes, compromised cells and gaps in official knowledge; disclosure may accelerate attacks, redirect activity, permit destruction of evidence or intimidation, impair cooperation and stigmatize persons not shown to have committed crimes. The declaration also argues that the selection of a hearing for closure can itself reveal information and that individual adjudicators cannot see the full investigative pattern. These are supported descriptions of the submitted explanation, not judicial findings that every disclosure necessarily causes each harm. No empirical demonstration of an averted attack is required merely to credit predictive expertise. Conversely, the declaration's existence does not resolve the constitutional sufficiency of categorical closure. See pp. 9–10, 32–36.

6. **Functional benefits and their level of generality.** Even the appellate majority acknowledged accuracy, fairness, accountability, performance and public-understanding benefits from open deportation adjudication. The legal dispute is whether the logic inquiry examines the general structural proceeding or a security-designated subset, and where the competing security interest belongs in the analysis. The majority ultimately confined its judgment to the special-interest class despite some broader language. The packet must not convert that qualified conclusion into a judicial holding that all immigration hearings may be secret. See pp. 31–38, 43–45.

7. **Alternatives and their limits.** Protected submissions, sealed initial closure determinations, protection of identities, partial closure, redaction and delayed disclosure remain lawful analytical alternatives. The source supports consideration of them; it does not establish that every option works in every special-interest matter. The directive itself did not prohibit aliens or counsel from disclosing information. A May 28, 2002 regulation newly authorized protective orders and sealing for sensitive law-enforcement or national-security information; that development prevents treating the earlier possibility of disclosure as conclusive proof of futility. The district court did not consider that new regulation. No separate construction or invalidation of it is needed here. See pp. 10–11 n.2, 46–49.

8. **Remedy.** The district court's order allowed government applications for individual closure but was nationwide in reach. A qualified-access ruling does not automatically establish each preliminary-equity finding, a permanent injunction, immediate disclosure of every identity or transcript, or the necessity of relief for every nonparty. The source supports vacating the categorical no-right appellate judgment and returning the preliminary decree, security applications and scope for consideration under the governing rule. The exact decree and all underlying equitable findings are not reproduced; that limit is avoidable through a remand rather than an invented operative decree. No automatic nationwide reinstatement is assumed. See pp. 11, 38, 46–51.

## Law entering March 4, 2003

- **El Vocero de Puerto Rico v. Puerto Rico, May 17, 1993, and the register's Qualified access to adversarial preliminary hearings rule:** all participating members of the present Court who then sat joined the trial-like preliminary-hearing rule. History concerns the kind of proceeding rather than a single jurisdiction's practice. Established access requires particular findings supporting an overriding interest, narrow fit and consideration of reasonable alternatives. Its substantial-probability fair-trial formulation remains specifically identified as such; its transfer to national security requires a stated legal judgment, not a quotation implying that issue was already decided.
- **Richmond Newspapers, Globe Newspaper and Press-Enterprise I and II:** surviving pre-divergence access authorities supply the history-and-function method and distinguish recognition of a qualified right from justification for closure. They do not automatically resolve access to every executive proceeding.
- **California First Amendment Coalition v. Woodford, February 25, 2003:** the controlling six-Justice opinion recognizes observation of integral execution preparations by admitted witnesses and rejects the categorical curtain on the established trial record even under Turner. Its holding does not adopt ordinary closure safeguards as the general prison standard. Breyer authored; Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg joined. O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia and Thomas, rejects extension to prison preparations and independently credits anticipatory security judgments under Turner. Removal adjudication and an ongoing national-security investigation are material differences requiring independent analysis here.
- **United Reporting, December 7, 1999:** the unanimous scrutiny holding preserves the absence of a general right to government records while requiring separate analysis of restrictions on independently protected expression. It does not itself create access to removal proceedings.
- **National Treasury Employees Union, February 22, 1995:** the Court's remedial disposition removes relief for unrepresented senior employees while preserving the certified class and individual relief actually supported. It supplies a relevant remedial caution, not a categorical prohibition on broad relief or a rule deciding this decree's necessary reach.

The lower court discusses later historical Supreme Court decisions, including Ports Authority and Zadvydas. Their discussion is evidence of the parties' arguments and the lower court's reasoning. ## Lawful alternatives preserved

- Recognize qualified access to ordinary adversarial deportation adjudication; reject designation alone as sufficient to close the entire proceeding; remand preliminary relief and scope while permitting supported, protected closure applications.
- Decline constitutional extension because the asserted administrative tradition lacks the required foundation; affirm reversal of the preliminary injunction on the First Amendment ground without approving all executive secrecy or deciding a distinct regulatory claim.
- Assume or recognize qualified access but credit a demonstrated, sufficiently defined security category on an adequate record; distinguish that position from treating all designated hearings as automatically closed without meaningful review.

The decisive uncertainties are legal evaluation of history, choice of the structural proceeding, the showing sufficient for protected closure, and the proper preliminary remedy. They do not require inventing a classified fact or an absent filing. The neutral record is adequate for independent commitments at those bounded levels.
