## Adverse-purpose evidence and reported counterevidence

The LaVines alleged that the school punished the poem's content rather than taking a safety precaution. Paragraph 48 identifies the original expulsion letter, Haney's and Newell's school-board testimony, file references, and other documents as their evidence. That paragraph acknowledges that the poem's content was a factor in the decision. The original letter expressly described violations of the school's discipline policy, identified the act of presenting the paper and its implication of extreme violence, characterized a danger or threat of disruption, and imposed the sanction of emergency expulsion. Its disciplinary wording is part of the record, not displaced by the later letter's safety wording. Paragraphs 23–25 are copied below.

Footnote 8 specifically reports Haney's testimony that he would have emergency expelled anyone who wrote the poem. That testimony must accompany the account of additional individualized circumstances. It is evidence supporting the LaVines' argument about the actual reason for the action; it is not an established policy finding about every student, a finding that every writer was expelled, or proof that Newell shared every implication of Haney's position. Conversely, the source does not permit the testimony to be omitted merely because Newell made the final decision.

The reported counterevidence includes Newell's stated consideration of the poem together with prior suicidal thoughts, disciplinary history, family difficulties, the recent breakup and reported stalking, and the nearby Springfield school shooting. Paragraph 48 calls the evidence that he considered circumstances beyond the poem undisputed. That description establishes what the appellate court said about the evidence; it does not by itself resolve every possible dispute about motive or whether an asserted alternative would actually have produced the same decision.

The actual response also supplies evidence on purpose: the teacher contacted the counselor after becoming concerned; the counselor arranged the Saturday meeting with the vice principal; Haney checked plans for the homecoming dance, alerted security even though James said he would not attend, and consulted the police; personnel followed referrals to child-protection services, the crisis line, and Dr. Dewitt; deputies performed a welfare check; the district later paid for an agreed psychiatric evaluation and allowed return after a favorable assessment. These acts must be considered alongside the disciplinary letter and Haney's testimony, rather than replacing them. Paragraph 49 and footnote 9 infer a predominant safety purpose from these acts. That inference is the lower court's reasoning under review, not a new finding made by this supplement.

## Summary-judgment posture and source limits

Both sides sought partial summary judgment on the First Amendment claim. The district court granted the plaintiffs' motion, denied the defendants' motion, and entered the file injunction. The Ninth Circuit reviewed the interlocutory injunction and its linked legal basis; it affirmed the injunction, reversed the expulsion ruling, and reversed the denial of the defendants' motion on that limited expulsion issue, stating that no genuine material dispute remained even on the plaintiffs' favorable view. The prior validated supplement preserves the precise disposition and remaining-claims limits. No new evidentiary hearing, trial verdict on motive, damages award, immunity ruling, or final disposition of every pleaded claim is established here.

Paragraphs 48–49 and note 8 are therefore not freestanding trial findings that the operator may adopt or discard as conclusive. They identify evidence, competing interpretations, and the appellate court's treatment of that evidence under summary judgment. Whether a further inference or legal consequence follows belongs to the adjudicative stages; this neutral validation supplies neither a vote nor advice to reach a particular result.

The full board testimony, complete school file, and every document invoked in paragraph 48 are not separately reproduced in the supplied lower opinion. The opinion gives the original letter's relevant text, Haney's all-writers testimony, Newell's listed considerations, and the actual response; it does not supply every question and answer or resolve factual propositions beyond that account. A specific claim dependent on omitted testimony must identify that exact proposition and its source limit. This is not evidence of nonproduction, waiver, default, or an absent judicial filing. ### Opinion paragraph 48

```text
The LaVines contend that James was expelled for the content of his poem and that the school's actions were punitive and not meant to protect the safety of James or others. They believe this is evidenced by the original expulsion letter, Haney's and Newell's testimony before the school board, references in James' file and other documents. Even viewing these items of evidence in the perspective most favorable to the LaVines, our conclusion is unchanged. The content of James' poem was admittedly a factor in the decision to emergency expel James. It is because the content of James' speech was a factor in the decision that we analyze the school defendants' actions to determine whether they violated the First Amendment at all. There is undisputed evidence in the record, however, that Principal Newell considered other factors beyond the poem in deciding to emergency expel James.8 Newell stated that he considered James' suicidal ideations, disciplinary history, family situation, recent break-up with his girlfriend and the report of stalking her and the recent school shooting in nearby Springfield in addition to the content of the poem when he made his final decision.
```

### Opinion paragraph 49

```text
The record is also clear that in addition to any potential disciplinary violation James may or may not have committed by writing the poem, the school was foremost concerned about student safety. This is primarily evidenced by the actions the school took immediately after James turned in the poem. As soon as James' teacher read the poem, she became worried about James' well being and called his school counselor. The counselor then read the poem, was also troubled by its content and called the school's vice principal. All three school officials then met on Saturday evening because they were concerned about what they should do to protect the student body's safety. Their first concern was the homecoming dance scheduled for that night. Haney decided to call James' home to see if he planned to attend the dance. Even though James said he would not be attending, Haney decided to warn security at the dance to be on the look-out for James. The three school officials then decided to call the police for help in this situation and were referred to a crisis hotline and eventually a psychiatrist. After discussing the situation with the psychiatrist, they decided that James should be evaluated by a mental health professional. Deputy sheriffs went to James' home for that purpose but, after James refused to be voluntarily examined, concluded that they lacked probable cause under Washington law to commit him. Taken together, the school's actions on the weekend before they emergency expelled him substantiate that it was not trying to discipline James for his speech, but was trying to protect its students from potential violence.9
```

### Opinion paragraph 50

```text
In retrospect, it may appear that, as James' mother predicted, the school overreacted. James very well may have been using his poetry to explore the disturbing topic of school violence and chose to do so through the perspective of a suicidal mass murderer. In fact, James strongly contends this was all he was doing and that he had no intention of hurting himself or others. We have no reason now to disbelieve James, as he did return to Blaine High School without further incident. We review, however, with deference, schools' decisions in connection with the safety of their students even when freedom of expression is involved. At the time the school officials made their determination to emergency expel James, they had facts which might reasonably have led them to forecast a substantial disruption of or material interference with school activities. See Tinker, 393 U.S. at 514. School officials have a difficult task in balancing safety concerns against chilling free expression. This case demonstrates how difficult that task can be.
```

### Footnote 8

```text
There was testimony by Vice Principal Haney that he would have emergency expelled anyone who wrote "Last Words. " Whether the school may have expelled anyone for writing this poem is not what the school must defend here, however. The school must only defend its decision to emergency expel James.
```
