## 36. Norton v. Ashcroft — January 15, 2003

### Components and common sufficient grounds

**N1 — Commerce.** Affirm dismissal of the facial Commerce Clause challenge. American Life League's November 13, 1995 unanimous commerce holding directly controls: the challenged force, threats, physical obstruction and facility destruction intentionally interfere with an actual interstate reproductive-medical-services market. The regulated mechanism is that direct interference, not general economic consequences of violence or a political speaker's commercial motive. Neither an individual interstate journey nor a new jurisdictional element is necessary for the qualifying class. Morrison expressly preserved this service-market distinction.

**N2 — Expression.** Affirm dismissal of the granted facial challenge to the service-related distinction. American Life League's unanimous Parts II and III-A require the prohibited conduct and statutory intent, preserve protected expression, and sustain expressive-conduct restrictions under intermediate scrutiny. The service connection identifies interference with access rather than agreement with a viewpoint. The substantial access and safety interests are unrelated to suppressing advocacy; the defined restrictions leave usable nonobstructive communication and burden no substantially greater speech than necessary.

The statute's elements continue to do real work. Section 248(a)(1) requires force, threat of force or physical obstruction, intentional injury, intimidation, interference or attempt, and the enacted connection to obtaining or providing reproductive services or intimidating persons from doing so. Interference restricts movement; intimidation concerns reasonable apprehension of bodily harm; obstruction makes ingress or egress impassable or passage unreasonably difficult or hazardous. Persuasion that reduces demand, political offense and association alone do not satisfy these predicates. The distinct facility-damage branch and the complete exclusion of FACE penalties and civil remedies for conduct directed exclusively at the actor's own minor retain their existing terms.

The December 10 ACLA rule requiring purpose or knowledge of threatening meaning for the reviewed civil threat category remains effective. It supplies no finding that Norton's words or driveway encounters were threats or obstruction. Its civil holding is not enlarged into a universal criminal-threat mental-state rule.

**N3 — Mandate and scope.** Affirm the judgment insofar as it rejects these two facial challenges. The distinct as-applied ripeness disposition and ungranted issues remain undisturbed. The warning letter's individual directions, the disputed conversations and the district court's discretionary declaratory-relief ground are not independently within the stated review. The mandate makes no new factual attribution or enforcement order.

## 37. Newman v. Sathyavaglswaran — January 15, 2003

### Components and rules adopted by the commitments

**P1 — Entitlement.** Affirm reinstatement of the parents' procedural-due-process claim. At the pleaded facts, California's rights to possess and direct disposition of remains, make anatomical gifts and refuse transfer supply a substantive, legally enforceable interest in the corneas. Federal property protection does not depend on commercial sale value or the State's use of the label quasi-property. The parents claim their own retained interests, not surviving constitutional rights of the deceased.

The corneal-removal statute authorized removal in an autopsy only when the coroner lacked knowledge of an objection. That condition retained the legally effective power to object; it did not transfer all control to the State. The existence of that entitlement is distinct from whether notice was feasible or whether lack of a known objection permitted immediate action in the actual circumstances. California may define the substantive interest, but cannot conclusively define its constitutional procedural protection by the mechanism that deprives its holder of a meaningful opportunity to exercise the retained right. Roth and Loudermill supply that distinction; Sullivan preserves it. Phillips confirms that lack of market value does not decide ownership, without transferring its takings holding to this case.

The rule depends on the actual California entitlements and the pleaded claimant status. Section 7100's decedent-direction and priority rules remain operative: absent contrary directions, spouse, children, parents, next kin and the qualifying public administrator hold the disposition role in order. No superior claimant or conflicting donor direction is established against these allegations. The distinct reasonable-contact rule for other tissue under section 7151.5(a)(2) is not read into former section 27491.47 as its own statutory text. The later consent amendment does not determine the constitutional process owed in 1997. Authority to perform a cause-of-death autopsy does not itself dispose of the separate transfer of tissue for another person's use.

**P2 — Process and state remedies.** Reject the categorical proposition that available state postdeprivation remedies defeat this section 1983 claim. Logan and Hudson distinguish a predictable deprivation carried out under established governmental procedure from a genuinely random, unauthorized act for which advance safeguards are impracticable. Patsy separately rejects an exhaustion prerequisite; absence of an exhaustion rule does not itself establish inadequate process. The former civil-liability shield also prevents assuming that an effective state remedy existed.

**P3 — Required further proceedings.** Affirm the Ninth Circuit's reversal of the pleading dismissal and remand under Mathews and Gilbert. The lower court must assess the actual private interest and finality of removal, the risk of mistaken disregard of an entitled person's objection, the value and feasibility of notice or another meaningful opportunity, and the government's actual time-sensitive retrieval and health interests. Ordinarily a planned irreversible deprivation requires a practicable opportunity before it occurs; an actual urgent need or impracticability may justify action without advance process. Neither advance consent in every case nor adequacy of eventual money relief is established by the pleadings. The factual record must resolve urgency, contact opportunities, any authorized consent and the adequacy of the procedure actually followed, followed by the remaining responsibility and remedial issues under ordinary rules.

### Two supported paths and their limits

**Recognize retained property and remand.** Its strongest counterargument is that the legislature created only a limited burial responsibility and expressly authorized corneal retrieval absent known objection. This path answers that the law retained an enforceable refusal and disposition interest: absence of an opportunity to make the refusal known is not the same as substantive abolition of the refusal. The interest can be limited and still be property. Medical urgency affects what process is due; it does not prove that no interest exists. The path does not constitutionalize every historical rule about remains or prohibit the State from altering substantive rights.

**Treat the removal authorization as the substantive boundary and restore dismissal.** Its strongest support is Roth's state-law source rule and Sullivan's refusal to invent an unconditional entitlement from general eligibility. Its decisive difficulty here is the continuing refusal power together with possession, disposition and gift rights. Unlike an unestablished service-specific payment entitlement, these parents allege a presently vested control interest that the challenged procedure irreversibly defeats. Restoring dismissal would require treating the no-known-objection mechanism as eliminating the very interest it recognizes, or resolving urgency against the complaint. The eight commitments reject that inference. They also reject the lower opinion's unnecessary suggestion that ancient common-law rules can never be legislatively altered; that proposition is not needed or adopted.

## 39. Meyer v. Holley — January 22, 2003

### Corrected posture and common conclusions

This review concerns the Rule 54(b) summary judgment for Meyer on the surviving FHA theory and the Ninth Circuit's reversal, after an earlier pleading disposition of the officer-capacity theory. Triad and Crank claims remained pending. Meyer was president and designated corporate officer/broker; ownership in 1996 was disputed after his asserted 1995 transfer. No allegation charged him with direct participation, and the evidence did not establish direction or approval of the discriminatory act. The reviewed vicarious-liability issue concerns compensatory damages; punitive vicarious damages are outside it.

**M1 — Attribution rule.** The FHA incorporates ordinary agency and corporate principles for this compensatory liability question. An employee's qualifying conduct can be attributed to the corporate principal without separately proving corporate fault, but ownership, office or supervisory control over that corporation does not alone make the individual a second principal. The statute's remedial purpose supplies no special rule erasing corporate separateness or creating a nondelegable personal FHA duty for every controlling officer or designated broker.

Darden supplies the common-law interpretive analogy, not an FHA-specific employee checklist. General Building Contractors distinguishes an ordinary agency duty from an inferred nondelegable duty. Bestfoods preserves corporate separateness while allowing legally supported veil piercing and a genuinely independent direct statutory duty. Faragher and Ellerth require actual agency-based attribution and material aid in their employment setting; they do not make the supervisor an individual employer of all corporate employees.

**M2 — Regulatory and licensing arguments.** Former 24 C.F.R. section 103.20(b) did not impose liability from direction or control alone: its discrimination must be by the controlling person's employee or agent acting within the scope of that authority. The 1999 section 103.202(b) concerns notification of directing or controlling persons, while section 103.203(a) preserves defenses available in court. A notice provision is not a substantive expansion of liability. California's designated-broker supervisory obligations may matter to a properly presented legal theory, but title and a state supervisory duty alone do not establish the federal individual-principal relationship.

**M3 — Judgment and remand.** Vacate the Ninth Circuit judgment and remand for further proceedings under the ordinary attribution rule. Its broader rule cannot remain an instruction governing the return. The lower courts must determine whether a traditional veil-piercing or other ordinary attribution theory was actually raised and preserved and, if so, whether its legal and factual predicates are established. The government's September 2002 brief and footnote 6 establish a real dispute over preservation, not a finding of preservation or liability. The ownership dispute must be resolved only as material to a legally sufficient surviving theory. Sole ownership, inadequate assets or a corporate title alone are not a veil-piercing determination.

The Court does not select state or federal veil-piercing law in advance of the actual presentation and need for that question. Bestfoods left that choice open; this review's special-liability question and unresolved preservation do not require answering it. Ordinary proceedings may resolve a preserved theory without a trial or new discovery entitlement. A general statement that officers remain liable for their own wrongful acts supplies no invented personal-participation claim in this case and revives no unappealed dismissal.

### Material competing paths

**Special personal FHA responsibility.** The strongest argument is that designated brokerage supervision and control over a housing enterprise carry a responsibility necessary to effective antidiscrimination enforcement. Its obstacle is the missing statutory displacement of ordinary agency and the regulation's actual relationship-and-scope qualification. Employer responsibility and the employee's own liability continue to serve enforcement without automatically imposing another person's corporate debts on an officer. All eight reject the special rule.

**Ordinary agency with a bounded return.** The strongest opposing remedial argument is that respondents failed to present or support veil piercing, so rejection of the circuit's theory should end the claim against Meyer. The validated government brief disputes that premise with a citation to the appellate argument; the Court cannot resolve the preservation and evidence questions by assuming either account. Conversely, the government's preferred affirmance of the appellate remand does not justify leaving the circuit's special rule operative. Vacatur and a bounded ordinary-law remit remove that rule while preserving only issues the real proceedings permit.

## 40. Roe v. Texas Department of Protective and Regulatory Services — June 2, 2003

### Review method and component definitions

The legal immunity appeal accepts the supported nonmovant account. Johnson and Behrens prohibit choosing the worker's competing doorstep testimony or resolving factual consent by credibility. The legal significance of the acknowledged scheduling agreement and the legal sufficiency of the plaintiff-favorable examination facts remain reviewable. Elder requires all relevant preconduct authority. Saucier establishes no compulsory merits-first sequence; Hope supplies the current fair-warning method while leaving July 10, 1999 as the notice cutoff.

**R1 — Home entry.** All eight affirm rejection of the mother's separate entry claim. An objectively understood, voluntarily arranged home interview authorized entry for that interview when the identified worker arrived as agreed, absent withdrawal or an expressed limitation. The accepted absence of a second express doorstep invitation does not revoke that earlier authorization. Silence alone would not supply consent, but the result rests on the antecedent affirmative appointment, not silence. The agreement's scope does not extend to intimate examination and photography.

**R2 — Child's examination merits.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer adopt the following rule and application. A nonemergency, intimate visual examination and photography of a child's genital and anal areas in this investigative setting requires voluntary consent or judicial authorization supported by probable cause; genuine exigent circumstances remain an independent exception. The child-protection purpose does not, on this record, supply a special need justifying this operation without those protections. The agency had time for ordinary investigative and judicial steps; the ten-day classification and the worker's acknowledged medical-examination alternative contradict a need for this immediate intrusion. The alleged manipulation and photography exceeded an ordinary interview or superficial observation, and the worker had no training in this type of photography. No categorical rule follows merely from statutory reporting duties or contact with police. Ferguson's actual-operation rule and Chandler's requirement of a supported need, rather than the historical lower opinion's broader mixed-purpose formula, govern.

On the plaintiff-favorable account, assisting with clothing and positioning in response to official directions does not establish voluntary consent as a matter of law. Her questions about necessity and counsel, distress, and lack of advance explanation of the eventual scope must be considered. The Court creates no requirement of a particular warning or verbal formula and invents no threat to remove the child. Genuine consent remains for ordinary proceedings on the actual facts.

Scalia and Thomas do not reach R2's constitutional merits because their R3 immunity disposition resolves this individual federal claim. They do not endorse the search or vote that it was constitutional.

**R3 — July 1999 notice and personal immunity.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reverse the child's immunity judgment. Their sufficient notice ground is the obvious excess of the particular assumed search under already established bodily-privacy, scope and consent rules, even allowing the competing special-needs approach. It is not a claim that the warrant rule for every child-protection examination was settled in July 1999. Scalia and Thomas affirm the child-search immunity judgment because they regard that specific application as still insufficiently clear under the preconduct decisions.

**R4 — Substantive due process.** All eight affirm rejection of duplicative substantive-due-process claims arising from these same intrusions. The mother's home-entry interest and the child's bodily-search interest are directly addressed by the Fourth Amendment. The record supplies no child removal or separate coercive displacement of parental decisionmaking beyond the challenged examination. Graham and the specific-provision rule preserved in Lewis govern these acts. A failure to recover on the specific claim, including personal immunity, does not create an alternative substantive-due-process damages remedy. This conclusion does not erase a parent's distinct family-liberty interest when another supported governmental act infringes it; emotional injury alone is not that additional act.

**R5 — State law and remaining proceedings.** All eight preserve separate consideration of the actual state-law claims and Texas official-immunity defense. Federal qualified immunity does not decide Texas immunity. For the six voting to restore the child's federal claim, the case returns for ordinary consent, participation, causation, injury, liability and remedy proceedings, and any supplemental-jurisdiction decision must use that surviving-claim posture rather than presume all federal claims have ended. For Scalia and Thomas, all reviewed federal claims end and the lower court retains the stated option to evaluate Texas immunity or decline supplemental jurisdiction; a jurisdictional dismissal is without prejudice. Unappealed dismissals of other defendants remain outside the return.

### Adversarial comparison: merits and notice are different questions

**Ordinary judicial protection versus special-needs balancing.** The worker's strongest argument is that detecting abuse serves an independently protective function and that applying criminal-search procedures to every initial inquiry can impair protection. Griffin and the actual Vernonia program show that protective settings can justify modified standards. But this examination is not the ordinary inquiry the argument describes. The actual intrusion, lack of emergency, nonmedical photography and available lawful examination route require a justification corresponding to their scope. Ferguson does not disqualify an entire investigation merely because reporting to police is required; it prevents a benign general purpose from substituting for operational justification. The six reach the presented merits question on these facts. Scalia and Thomas regard deciding this disputed constitutional boundary as unnecessary once immunity is granted.

**Deny immunity on the particular obvious excess.** The strongest contrary argument is that Darryl H., Landstrom and Wildauer permitted protective investigations under a reasonableness approach, while Doe v. Louisiana left the Fourth Amendment issue open and found no clearly established family-privacy violation. The six answer by applying the common limits rather than demanding that every earlier court adopt the same warrant formula. By July 1999, Camara and T.L.O. made civil officials and children's searches subject to the Fourth Amendment; Bell supplied scope, manner, justification and place analysis; the Court's own Vernonia and Chandler decisions required genuine contextual justification and retained bodily protection. Schneckloth and the operative Robinette rule required actual voluntary consent rather than submission to official authority. Good's ordinary Fourth Amendment treatment further demonstrated that child protection was not a universal exemption.

Darryl H. is substantial contrary authority on warrants, not on unlimited scope: it retained reasonableness, questioned unnecessary visual examinations, rejected treating maternal assistance as conclusive consent, and described a protocol directing sexual-abuse examinations to physicians. Landstrom involved a school nurse examining a child's complained-of soreness and evaluated March 1987 notice; it did not approve the materially more intrusive nonmedical photography assumed here. Wildauer's investigative home-visit holding does not decide this intimate examination. Doe's Fourteenth Amendment immunity holding and express Fourth Amendment reservation are taken seriously, but an undecided amendment question is not affirmative permission for a search that exceeds the shared reasonableness and consent limits. These distinctions support notice without converting a state protocol or later decision into the source of the federal right.

**Grant immunity despite a possible violation.** Scalia and Thomas give the preconduct lack of a settled child-examination rule greater weight. Darryl H. rejected a categorical warrant/probable-cause requirement and granted immunity despite substantial doubt about necessity and consent; Landstrom again found insufficient particularity for an intimate examination. Doe's local reservation left the application unsettled. The distinction between examining a complained-of area and photographing it is constitutionally significant, but in their view the authorities had not yet made its legal consequence sufficiently definite for personal damages. General intrusion severity plus the present Court's better interpretation cannot eliminate Anderson's particularized-notice requirement. They accept that a factual twin is unnecessary and that no blanket immunity for child-protection workers exists; they find this particular boundary uncertain. They reject using Ferguson, Wallis, Calabretta, Tenenbaum, Saucier or Hope as new July 1999 notice.

The six likewise exclude those later decisions from the notice sources. Hope explains why an obvious application can suffice; it is not itself the conduct-date warning. No Justice assumes an officer must have read or actually relied on a judicial decision. The disagreement concerns the objective clarity of the existing rule at this particular level of application.

### Opinion compatibility and remedies
