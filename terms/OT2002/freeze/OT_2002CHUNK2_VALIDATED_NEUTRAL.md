### Dow Corning — statutory and precedent additions

The question is permanent nonconsensual elimination of creditors' independent claims against entities that have not become debtors. Consent, estate ownership and temporary restraint remain separate. Section 1123(b)(3) expressly concerns settlement, adjustment, retention and enforcement of claims belonging to the debtor or estate. Section 1123(b)(6) permits other appropriate provisions consistent with applicable Code provisions. The general provisions must be considered together with the debtor-specific discharge structure and §524(e), whose community-property exception in §524(a)(3) remains intact.

Section 524(g) is a particular asbestos trust and injunction mechanism. Its third-party coverage is limited to the statutory relationships and conditions, including the specified trust, findings, class vote and protection of future demands. It is not itself authority for silicone-implant releases. Pub. L. 103-394 §111(b), reproduced in the 2000 Code's §524 notes, preserves other existing authority to issue injunctions with confirmation. Thus the enactment cannot alone establish either that general nondebtor-release power exists or that Congress abolished all other injunction power. A negative construction must establish its independent basis in the general Code; an affirmative construction must identify the authority §111(b) preserves.

*United States v. Energy Resources Co.*, 495 U.S. 545 (1990), is surviving predivergence authority. It approved designation of debtor tax payments toward trust-fund liabilities when necessary to successful reorganization, under §§105 and then-1123(b)(5), now (b)(6), without demanding a provision expressly naming payment designation. Its reasoning emphasized that the IRS retained the right to collect unpaid trust-fund liability from the responsible individuals during and after bankruptcy; those persons benefited through payment of the debt, not judicial extinction of an unpaid independent obligation. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy joined White's opinion. Blackmun dissented; no explanatory dissent accompanies the reported opinion.

Breyer's earlier First Circuit opinion, *In re Energy Resources Co.*, 871 F.2d 223, 228–230 (1989), supplies his own date-eligible evidence. It analyzes the real effect of outside financing and the statutory residual power, while maintaining that the IRS remains legally free to collect an outstanding trust-fund debt from responsible persons. His reasoning therefore supports both serious attention to reorganization necessity and the distinction between allocation of debtor payments and compulsory release of independent unpaid liability. The Supreme Court's affirmance controls the authority; Breyer's opinion is personal-jurisprudence evidence.

Existing Celotex holdings concern actual relatedness, sufficient interlocutory preservation authority and orderly review. They expressly reserve ultimate permanent-injunction validity. Noland and Reorganized CF&I preserve real statutory equity but reject recurring creditor-loss reasons as a replacement for enacted distributions. Hartford Underwriters enforces the allocation of independent remedial power to a named actor while preserving separate authorized remedies. No one of these decisions alone answers the present release question.

**Live alternatives:** reject this permanent nonconsensual release power under the general provisions and remand for conforming plan revision; or recognize exceptional power under §§105 and 1123(b)(6) and retain the lower court's demand for detailed, supported necessity and protection findings. The affirmative route cannot treat plan funding, class voting or a substantial contribution as individual consent. The negative route cannot treat every injunction affecting nondebtors as a discharge or disturb consensual releases and estate-owned claims. A classification-only remand is available for genuinely uncertain claim ownership or consent, but it does not answer the legal question concerning claims established to be independent and nonconsensual.

Affirm if the absence of an acquittal leaves death unresolved and the successful appeal reopens lawful sentencing. ## 18. Yellow Transportation, Inc. v. Michigan, No. 01-270 — November 5, 2002

Michigan formerly waived fees for Yellow's Illinois-plated vehicles under its reciprocity practice. Yellow's principal place of business was Kansas, which lacked the relevant reciprocity arrangement. Michigan announced during 1991 that its 1992 registrations would instead use principal place of business; the announced change took effect February 1, 1992. Yellow paid the disputed 1992 assessment under protest in October 1991. The present requested refunds concern fees under the Single State Registration System, effective January 1, 1994. Advance collection for the 1992 registration year and the legally relevant November 15, 1991 benchmark are distinct matters.

The Michigan Supreme Court reversed refund judgments because it treated the State's generic $10 charge, rather than the effect of reciprocity on particular carriers, as the benchmark. The question presented concerns that generic-fee premise. Whether Yellow's particular informal waiver qualified, the effect of the advance 1992 payment, the legality of a later principal-place-of-business criterion, and the final refund amount remain for the appropriate proceedings on remand; the Court need not decide them to reject or sustain the premise under review.

Former 49 U.S.C. §11506(c)(2)(B)(iv), now §14504, expressly directed the ICC to establish the fee system, linked it to the number of vehicles and States, capped each State's charge at the fee it collected or charged as of November 15, 1991, and imposed a $10 ceiling. Its administration must minimize carrier compliance costs while preserving the relevant State revenues. The ICC's initial proposal questioned continuing reciprocity, but its final May 1993 rule preserved reciprocity in the benchmark after explaining the effects on carrier costs and State revenues. An explained change during rulemaking is not an unexplained abandonment of a settled final rule.

Two lawful readings require consideration: the statutory benchmark itself may account for the fee actually imposed on the carrier, including a qualifying waiver; alternatively, the text may leave the treatment of reciprocity to the expressly delegated fee-system rulemaking. The competing generic-fee reading stresses uniform administration and the distinction between a fee and a waiver. Neither a broad fiscal objective nor deference alone can overcome contrary statutory text. A holding upholding the final rule need not hold that every individualized concession becomes a permanent statutory entitlement.

## 26. Borden Ranch Partnership v. United States Army Corps of Engineers, No. 01-1243 — December 16, 2002

The remaining disputed locations are jurisdictional drainages and swales on an approximately 8,400-acre grazing ranch converted toward orchards and vineyards. Jurisdiction over those locations is not contested. The government withdrew the vernal-pool component following SWANCC; the Ninth Circuit reversed that component and ordered penalty recalculation. Do not reinstate it or convert SWANCC into a categorical rule about every remaining wetland.

Deep ripping used shanks reaching roughly five to seven feet to break a restrictive layer. The trial findings describe horizontal and vertical movement of earth, rock, sand, and biological material, and particular filling or mounding of former channels. These are more specific than a finding of ecological injury alone. Whether that relocation is an addition of pollutants from a point source remains the statutory issue. Ordinary movement of a blade, a disturbance that merely changes hydrology, and a proven discharge cannot be equated without analysis.

Sections 1311 and 1362 require an addition of a pollutant from a point source to the relevant waters. Dredged spoil, rock, sand, and biological material are among the listed pollutants; the conveyance definition includes rolling stock but excludes agricultural stormwater runoff. Section 1344(f)(1)(A) exempts normal farming activities including plowing. The applicable plowing regulation protects soil breaking and stirring to prepare crops but excludes specified filling or redistribution that converts waters to dry land or destroys their natural drainage, storage, or filtration functions. Section 1344(f)(2) recaptures a discharge incidental to an activity whose purpose is bringing a water area into a use to which it was not previously subject, where flow or circulation may be impaired or reach reduced. Purpose, a new use of the water area, and the specified physical consequence are separate conditions. A ranch's general agricultural history does not alone decide them; accidental passage alone does not establish the required purpose.

The government relies on actual relocation and deposit, the equipment's conveyance of material, absence of established ongoing cultivation of these wet areas, and recapture for their conversion to intensive cultivation. Petitioners contend that moving soil already within the same water adds nothing, that a plow is not a relevant conveyance merely because it touches soil, and that farming followed by farming does not satisfy recapture. The lower dissent stresses the difference between incidental fallback and addition, as well as accidental passes. National Mining Association, 145 F.3d 1399 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is persuasive authority about incidental fallback, not a controlling holding that every redeposit is exempt. The trial rejected the asserted permission/estoppel defense: guidance to raise the shanks did not authorize fully engaged ripping. Its actual findings must be distinguished from the dissent's characterization.

Section 1319(d)'s then-applicable maximum was $25,000 per day for each violation. The trial counted 348 fully engaged passes in drainages (an average twelve at each of twenty-nine locations) plus ten in the subsequently excluded pool, for 358; it imposed $1.5 million, alternatively $500,000 plus restoration of four acres. Its findings attributed a discharge to each counted pass. The question is whether each distinct discharge is a violation or only a single daily violation of the discharge prohibition may be counted. A pass without a discharge is not a violation under either theory. The statutory factors include seriousness, economic benefit, history, good-faith efforts, economic impact, and justice. The remaining penalty must reflect the pool reversal and the lawful unit of violation; no new amount has been fixed here. The trial did not use the later increased maximum.

Rule 62. Stay of Proceedings To Enforce a Judgment




(a) Automatic Stay; Exceptions—Injunctions, Receiverships, and Patent Accountings. Except as stated herein, no execution shall issue upon a judgment nor shall proceedings be taken for its enforcement until the expiration of 10 days after its entry. Unless otherwise ordered by the court, an interlocutory or final judgment in an action for an injunction or in a receivership action, or a judgment or order directing an accounting in an action for infringement of letters patent, shall not be stayed during the period after its entry and until an appeal is taken or during the pendency of an appeal. The provisions of subdivision (c) of this rule govern the suspending, modifying, restoring, or granting of an injunction during the pendency of an appeal.


(b) Stay on Motion for New Trial or for Judgment. In its discretion and on such conditions for the security of the adverse party as are proper, the court may stay the execution of or any proceedings to enforce a judgment pending the disposition of a motion for a new trial or to alter or amend a judgment made pursuant to Rule 59, or of a motion for relief from a judgment or order made pursuant to Rule 60, or of a motion for judgment in accordance with a motion for a directed verdict made pursuant to Rule 50, or of a motion for amendment to the findings or for additional findings made pursuant to Rule 52(b).


(c) Injunction Pending Appeal. When an appeal is taken from an interlocutory or final judgment granting, dissolving, or denying an injunction, the court in its discretion may suspend, modify, restore, or grant an injunction during the pendency of the appeal upon such terms as to bond or otherwise as it considers proper for the security of the rights of the adverse party. If the judgment appealed from is rendered by a district court of three judges specially constituted pursuant to a statute of the United States, no such order shall be made except (1) by such court sitting in open court or (2) by the assent of all the judges of such court evidenced by their signatures to the order.


(d) Stay Upon Appeal. When an appeal is taken the appellant by giving a supersedeas bond may obtain a stay subject to the exceptions contained in subdivision (a) of this rule. The bond may be given at or after the time of filing the notice of appeal or of procuring the order allowing the appeal, as the case may be. The stay is effective when the supersedeas bond is approved by the court.


(e) Stay in Favor of the United States or Agency Thereof. When an appeal is taken by the United States or an officer or agency thereof or by direction of any department of the Government of the United States and the operation or enforcement of the judgment is stayed, no bond, obligation, or other security shall be required from the appellant.


(f) Stay According to State Law. In any state in which a judgment is a lien upon the property of the judgment debtor and in which the judgment debtor is entitled to a stay of execution, a judgment debtor is entitled, in the district court held therein, to such stay as would be accorded the judgment debtor had the action been maintained in the courts of that state.


(g) Power of Appellate Court Not Limited. The provisions in this rule do not limit any power of an appellate court or of a judge or justice thereof to stay proceedings during the pendency of an appeal or to suspend, modify, restore, or grant an injunction during the pendency of an appeal or to make any order appropriate to preserve the status quo or the effectiveness of the judgment subsequently to be entered.


(h) Stay of Judgment as to Multiple Claims or Multiple Parties. When a court has ordered a final judgment under the conditions stated in Rule 54(b), the court may stay enforcement of that judgment until the entering of a subsequent judgment or judgments and may prescribe such conditions as are necessary to secure the benefit thereof to the party in whose favor the judgment is entered.

Rule 64. Seizure of Person or Property




At the commencement of and during the course of an action, all remedies providing for seizure of person or property for the purpose of securing satisfaction of the judgment ultimately to be entered in the action are available under the circumstances and in the manner provided by the law of the state in which the district court is held, existing at the time the remedy is sought, subject to the following qualifications: (1) any existing statute of the United States governs to the extent to which it is applicable; (2) the action in which any of the foregoing remedies is used shall be commenced and prosecuted or, if removed from a state court, shall be prosecuted after removal, pursuant to these rules. The remedies thus available include arrest, attachment, garnishment, replevin, sequestration, and other corresponding or equivalent remedies, however designated and regardless of whether by state procedure the remedy is ancillary to an action or must be obtained by an independent action.

Rule 65. Injunctions




(a) Preliminary Injunction.


(1) Notice. No preliminary injunction shall be issued without notice to the adverse party.


(2) Consolidation of Hearing With Trial on Merits. Before or after the commencement of the hearing of an application for a preliminary injunction, the court may order the trial of the action on the merits to be advanced and consolidated with the hearing of the application. Even when this consolidation is not ordered, any evidence received upon an application for a preliminary injunction which would be admissible upon the trial on the merits becomes part of the record on the trial and need not be repeated upon the trial. This subdivision (a)(2) shall be so construed and applied as to save to the parties any rights they may have to trial by jury.



(b) Temporary Restraining Order; Notice; Hearing; Duration. A temporary restraining order may be granted without written or oral notice to the adverse party or that party's attorney only if (1) it clearly appears from specific facts shown by affidavit or by the verified complaint that immediate and irreparable injury, loss, or damage will result to the applicant before the adverse party or that party's attorney can be heard in opposition, and (2) the applicant's attorney certifies to the court in writing the efforts, if any, which have been made to give the notice and the reasons supporting the claim that notice should not be required. Every temporary restraining order granted without notice shall be indorsed with the date and hour of issuance; shall be filed forthwith in the clerk's office and entered of record; shall define the injury and state why it is irreparable and why the order was granted without notice; and shall expire by its terms within such time after entry, not to exceed 10 days, as the court fixes, unless within the time so fixed the order, for good cause shown, is extended for a like period or unless the party against whom the order is directed consents that it may be extended for a longer period. The reasons for the extension shall be entered of record. In case a temporary restraining order is granted without notice, the motion for a preliminary injunction shall be set down for hearing at the earliest possible time and takes precedence of all matters except older matters of the same character; and when the motion comes on for hearing the party who obtained the temporary restraining order shall proceed with the application for a preliminary injunction and, if the party does not do so, the court shall dissolve the temporary restraining order. On 2 days’ notice to the party who obtained the temporary restraining order without notice or on such shorter notice to that party as the court may prescribe, the adverse party may appear and move its dissolution or modification and in that event the court shall proceed to hear and determine such motion as expeditiously as the ends of justice require.


(c) Security. No restraining order or preliminary injunction shall issue except upon the giving of security by the applicant, in such sum as the court deems proper, for the payment of such costs and damages as may be incurred or suffered by any party who is found to have been wrongfully enjoined or restrained. No such security shall be required of the United States or of an officer or agency thereof.


The provisions of Rule 65.1 apply to a surety upon a bond or undertaking under this rule.


(d) Form and Scope of Injunction or Restraining Order. Every order granting an injunction and every restraining order shall set forth the reasons for its issuance; shall be specific in terms; shall describe in reasonable detail, and not by reference to the complaint or other document, the act or acts sought to be restrained; and is binding only upon the parties to the action, their officers, agents, servants, employees, and attorneys, and upon those persons in active concert or participation with them who receive actual notice of the order by personal service or otherwise.


(e) Employer and Employee; Interpleader; Constitutional Cases. These rules do not modify any statute of the United States relating to temporary restraining orders and preliminary injunctions in actions affecting employer and employee; or the provisions of Title 28, U.S.C., §2361, relating to preliminary injunctions in actions of interpleader or in the nature of interpleader; or Title 28, U.S.C., §2284, relating to actions required by Act of Congress to be heard and determined by a district court of three judges.




(As amended Dec. 27, 1946, eff. Mar. 19, 1948; Dec. 29, 1948, eff. Oct. 20, 1949; Feb. 28, 1966, eff. July 1, 1966; Mar. 2, 1987, eff. Aug. 1, 1987.)

Rule 69. Execution




(a) In General. Process to enforce a judgment for the payment of money shall be a writ of execution, unless the court directs otherwise. The procedure on execution, in proceedings supplementary to and in aid of a judgment, and in proceedings on and in aid of execution shall be in accordance with the practice and procedure of the state in which the district court is held, existing at the time the remedy is sought, except that any statute of the United States governs to the extent that it is applicable. In aid of the judgment or execution, the judgment creditor or a successor in interest when that interest appears of record, may obtain discovery from any person, including the judgment debtor, in the manner provided in these rules or in the manner provided by the practice of the state in which the district court is held.


(b) Against Certain Public Officers. When a judgment has been entered against a collector or other officer of revenue under the circumstances stated in Title 28, U.S.C., §2006, or against an officer of Congress in an action mentioned in the Act of March 3, 1875, ch. 130, §8 (18 Stat. 401), U.S.C., Title 2, §118, and when the court has given the certificate of probable cause for the officer's act as provided in those statutes, execution shall not issue against the officer or the officer's property but the final judgment shall be satisfied as provided in such statutes.
