## United States v. White Mountain Apache Tribe — No. 01-1067

### Reconciled propositions and disposition

Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer support affirmance of the Federal Circuit's rejection of jurisdictional dismissal and remand to the Court of Federal Claims. Congress expressly placed the identified property in trust while authorizing federal use, and the United States exercised that authority over the relevant corpus. The relationship carries a duty of reasonable care to preserve the trust property actually occupied or controlled pursuant to the retained use. Mitchell II permits a fair inference of monetary compensation for loss caused by breach of that identified duty. The Indian Tucker Act supplies the waiver and forum; it does not itself create the preservation obligation.

The use reservation qualifies use of the trust property; it does not remove the occupied portions from the trust. The duty follows from this statutory trust and the trustee's exercise of authorized use, not from general guardianship or governmental influence over all tribal affairs. The rule concerning the substantive compensation source is less demanding than the unequivocal-expression requirement for waiver, because the Indian Tucker Act already supplies that waiver. An express damages sentence in the 1960 Act is unnecessary.

Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas support reversal and dismissal of the asserted monetary maintenance claim under this enactment. A right to use the property, even coupled with trust title, is not an enacted undertaking to manage or maintain it for the Tribe comparable to Mitchell II. Common-law duties cannot supply the missing statutory responsibility. Their concern is the nature of legally assigned control, not whether federal occupation is extensive in fact.

### Separate-writing substance, qualifications, and remedy

The source-first concurrence function supported by Ginsburg and Breyer is a full join, not judgment-only agreement or a narrowing refusal. The Mitchell inquiry first identifies a substantive source establishing a particular duty and an allegation of its breach, then asks whether compensation for that breach may fairly be inferred. This Act expressly creates a trust, permits the trustee to occupy the corpus, and leaves preservation to that occupying trustee. The Allotment Act in Mitchell I instead placed use and management with the allottee; that statutory difference, rather than a generic preference for tribal claims, explains the result. Mere approval of another party's commercial bargain is a different legal responsibility. No same-day judgment must be treated as prior law to articulate the distinction.

The dissenting function supported by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas has two connected grounds. First, Testan and Mitchell require a substantive mandate, and the 1960 Act neither sets out preservation responsibilities nor assigns management for the Tribe's benefit. The retained use right can be read as a limitation on the conveyed beneficial estate. Second, Mitchell II's management was assigned by a comprehensive legal scheme; extensive practical occupation is not the same as that legal undertaking. Inferring duties from occupation and general trust principles risks making statutory liability turn on an uncertain parcel-by-parcel account of use. The dissent also challenges the unexamined move from maintenance duty to personal funding responsibility: ordinary trustees may be indemnified from the corpus for proper maintenance expenses. That objection concerns the content and remedy of the claimed duty; it is not an alternative finding that no deterioration occurred.

The affirming ground distinguishes Testan and Sheehan, where the governing nontrust regimes supplied affirmative reasons against the claimed money remedy. It preserves their requirement of a cognizable compensation source while rejecting an express-damages requirement for every established trust duty. A prospective direction to preserve property cannot compensate deterioration already caused; an order requiring repairs would itself impose the economic equivalent of a monetary remedy. This remedial point confirms compensation for the established duty rather than creates the duty.

The Court of Federal Claims must identify the relevant property and adjudicate breach, causation, and the proper measure of loss. The pleaded restoration estimate is not awarded. The historical Court's note 4 is material: no historic-preservation standard is endorsed; the significance of trustee indemnification for proper upkeep and the funding responsibility of a rent-free occupant are not settled by recognizing this claim. Those are limits of the claim and measure now returned for adjudication, not a conclusion that ordinary maintenance expense must automatically be borne personally by the United States. The status of condemned or demolished buildings likewise is not an adjudicated loss calculation.

The concurrence's clarification and the dissent's objection to the compensation formulation must remain distinct; the original description that both sides accept fair interpretation does not erase their dispute about what that standard demands here.

### Complete common explanation and limits

The ordinary collateral-review understanding of finality includes expiration of available certiorari review. Surviving pre-divergence cases such as Griffith v. Kentucky, Barefoot v. Estelle, and United States v. Johnson supply that usage; this is not a universal definition of finality for appeal, preclusion, or every criminal procedure. ## Tenafly Eruv Association v. Borough of Tenafly — March 5, 2003

### Provisional result and rule

All eight Associates affirm the Third Circuit's reversal of the denial of preliminary protection against the selective removal of the eruv attachments. The case returns for implementation of preliminary relief and ordinary merits proceedings under the actual-enforcement rule. The holding is confined to the supported preliminary record; it does not enter a final permanent easement or decide damages.

**Common operative rule.** A municipality cannot invoke Smith's rule for neutral, generally applicable regulation when its actual administration permits substantial comparable secular or other religious pole attachments yet singles out private religious attachments for removal without a harm-related justification. Formal breadth in the ordinance does not cure materially selective administration. Once the restriction is shown to be nonneutral or not generally applicable, the municipality must satisfy compelling-interest scrutiny and narrow tailoring. Here the established tolerated uses and the Borough's religion-based establishment objection support likely success, and the supplied record supplies no adequate justification for the selective removal of these unobtrusive, utility-authorized markers.

Comparability concerns the risks asserted to justify enforcement: safety, property management and visual effects. Duration, dimensions, placement and ownership may matter when actually connected to those risks; the Court does not find every tolerated notice identical in each characteristic. The Borough's general distinction between religious boundary markers and tolerated uses is insufficient on this record. The religious meaning of the eruv neither makes the lechis the Borough's religious message nor itself creates a municipal safety or property injury. Utility permission is relevant to the actual property setting but does not waive municipal regulation.

Private religious use under the same actual permissions given other users does not require the Borough to organize, endorse, certify or fund the religious boundary. Agostini and Good News Club reject an establishment defense based solely on private religion's presence. Pinette and Chabad likewise preserve contextual private attribution. The plaintiffs receive preliminary protection against this selective enforcement; genuinely evenhanded prospective restrictions and supported neutral enforcement remain available. **Affirm preliminary protection.** Lukumi's objective operation and comparable-harm rules apply to a law administered through exceptions as well as to text containing express exceptions. **Sustain enforcement or remand without protection.** This path would be lawful if the Borough had supported an evenhanded duration, attachment, ownership or safety distinction that explained the actual treatment and addressed comparable harms. **Treat accommodation as establishment.** This alternative fails because private creation and use of the eruv are not an official devotional program. Marshfield concerned continuing municipal presentation of a religious centerpiece; no equivalent adopted municipal message is established here. The Borough's concern about public misunderstanding cannot justify treating private religious significance itself as disqualifying. ## Common propositions supported by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer

**Access.** The public and press possess a qualified First Amendment right to attend ordinary adversarial deportation adjudication. The relevant type is the adjudicative hearing, not a new class defined by the Executive's security designation. The regulation's express presumption of openness, the distinction from exclusion proceedings, and the hearing's trial-like function establish the claim together; none is treated as an independently sufficient rule creating access to every administrative process. Exceptions protecting vulnerable persons or particular information are consistent with a qualified right. Neither a founding-era counterpart nor an absolutely exceptionless history is required by the access precedents.

**Closure.** National security can furnish an overriding interest, including protection against harms from assembling individually unremarkable facts. Its existence is not contested merely because the record lacks a completed attack traced to disclosure. But the present designation and declaration do not eliminate the need for supported findings that closure is necessary, sufficiently fitted to the threatened interest, and not reasonably avoidable through available protective means. An adjudicator may receive sensitive reasons in camera and under seal; adequate findings need not publicly reveal the danger they protect against. Consideration of partial closure, redaction, protected identities or delayed disclosure does not require their use when they would be ineffective. A whole hearing may close on a sufficient showing. Common evidence about a connected investigation may support more than one application, but the directive's label cannot itself supply the finding that the hearing falls within a demonstrated need. No new alternative categorical scheme is prospectively approved.

**Security and the logic inquiry.** The general risks and benefits of openness bear on the proceeding's function. Yet a special threat in a subset does not erase a qualified right to the structural proceeding and thereby remove the justification inquiry altogether. Intelligence expertise receives substantial evidentiary weight; it does not decide the constitutional question conclusively. The Court need not find that each immigration judge knows the whole investigation: the government may provide the relevant relationship through a protected submission. Nor must the government expose the identity, scheduling information or investigative link publicly merely to seek closure.

**Remedy and limits.** Vacate the no-right appellate judgment and remand. The lower courts must address the existing preliminary injunction, its equitable basis, protective applications and its necessary reach under the recognized access rule. Do not enter a permanent injunction, order blanket publication of sealed records, release any detainee, decide removal merits, or automatically reinstate relief for every person nationwide. Relief may have effects beyond the named press plaintiffs when necessary to protect their established access; no universal rule either requiring nationwide relief or forbidding incidental nonparty benefit is adopted. The exact decree is not supplied in full, so its final conforming terms remain lower-court work. The ordinary closure safeguards come from the adjudicative-access precedents; Woodford's decision to apply Turner in the prison setting does not transplant that standard into these hearings.
