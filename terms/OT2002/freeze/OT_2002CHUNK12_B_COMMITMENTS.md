## 145. Nguyen v. United States; Phan v. United States

### Components and operative grounds

**N1 — Assignment.** Section 292 does not authorize designation of this Article IV territorial judge to the Ninth Circuit. Its reference to district judges is read in the statutory judicial structure, including §§451 and 132–134; a territorial judge's exercise of district-court jurisdiction does not give that judge Article III status or place the judge in the class Congress authorized for this assignment. The Government concedes this construction. Mookini supplies the distinction between territorial courts and the statutory district courts of the United States. All eight Associates support N1.

**N2 — Late direct challenge and de facto defense.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas would entertain the objection and vacate both appellate judgments. Congress's substantial restriction on who may constitute an appellate tribunal has institutional force distinct from a technical defect in assigning an otherwise eligible federal judge. American Construction and American-Foreign Steamship support enforcing substantial tribunal-composition limits; Glidden permits correction of exceptional adjudicator-qualification defects notwithstanding failure to object earlier. Ryder and Landry establish the importance of actual lawful adjudication but do not themselves dispose of this late objection. The new proposition is the confined extension to this categorical statutory defect on direct review. It does not depend on treating every organizational error as jurisdictional or every structural objection as immune from forfeiture.

**N3 — Quorum and remedy.** Those five reject the suggestion that the participation of two qualified judges converts the actual three-member decision into an independently rendered lawful quorum decision. Section 46's quorum provision permits a lawful panel to act under its conditions; it does not retrospectively authorize an excluded member's deliberation and vote. Both cases should receive fresh Ninth Circuit review by a lawfully constituted panel. The underlying trial judgments remain for that appellate review. This disposition resolves the statutory claim and avoids the broader constitutional claim.

**N4 — Forfeiture alternative.** Scalia, Ginsburg and Breyer would affirm the appellate judgments against the late assignment challenge. They accept the statutory error but apply the ordinary distinction between the existence of error and entitlement to relief. Olano and Johnson retain a demanding correction inquiry after forfeiture; this record does not show outcome prejudice, bias, incompetence, or an exceptional impairment warranting setting aside appellate judgments after no objection or rehearing request. Their votes do not authorize future use of the territorial judge or establish that §46 made the original assignment lawful. The presence of two eligible circuit judges and a validly appointed territorial judge bears on correction and institutional harm, rather than supplying new assignment authority.

### Adversarial comparison

The strongest objection to N2 is that Ryder and Landry expressly preserve defaults, Freytag's separate opinion rejects structural status as a sufficient reason to excuse forfeiture, and even constitutional error can survive Johnson's fourth-stage inquiry. The five answer that the specific statutory qualification and the Court's tribunal-composition precedents make this an exceptional direct-review case: the appellate tribunal itself was constituted with a member Congress excluded, and speculation about a different lawful panel cannot supply the adjudication Congress prescribed. They do not infer intentional waiver from silence.

The strongest objection to N4 is that failure to correct lets appellate administrators defeat an express structural statute and that ordinary outcome prejudice cannot measure the contribution of an unauthorized participant. The three answer that forfeiture preserves a completed judgment without legalizing the original designation; the timely remedy cases remain meaningful, while an unpreserved objection must supply a reason for extraordinary correction beyond the statutory violation itself. They do not treat the unknown influence of the third judge as proof that there was no influence.

Roell is materially different under both paths. Its majority required actual informed voluntary consent before judgment to a type of adjudication Congress authorized. Its dissent required an enacted affirmative election and rejected a de facto substitute for that appellate route. Neither supplied permission for this territorial assignment. The dispute here remains whether direct-review relief follows despite the late objection, not whether silent participation can confer statutory eligibility.

## 146. Desert Palace, Inc. v. Costa

### Operative ground and alternative

**D1 — Proof and instruction.** Section 2000e-2(m) requires proof that race, color, religion, sex, or national origin was a motivating factor for an employment practice, even though other factors also motivated it. Nothing in that text or the definition of “demonstrates” in §2000e(m), which includes production and persuasion, requires direct evidence. A plaintiff seeking the mixed-motive instruction must present evidence sufficient for a reasonable jury to find the prohibited motivating factor by a preponderance; that proof may be direct, circumstantial, or both. All eight Associates would affirm the en banc judgment against the direct-evidence objection.

**D2 — Liability and relief.** Section 2000e-5(g)(2)(B) separately allows the employer to prove it would have taken the same action absent the prohibited factor. That showing restricts relief; it does not erase the violation established under §2000e-2(m). The court may grant declaratory relief, permissible injunctive relief, and attorney's fees and costs shown directly attributable only to pursuit of the motivating-factor claim. It may not award damages or order admission, reinstatement, hiring, promotion, or the payment described in §2000e-5(g)(2)(A), including backpay. The remedial limits follow proof of the employer's same-decision proposition, not merely its production of a nondiscriminatory explanation.

The strongest alternative imports Price Waterhouse's direct-decisional trigger, as used for pre-1991 conduct in Brown, to avoid burden shifts from weak circumstantial proof. It fails because Brown explicitly preserves the original regime, while Congress has now specified a different violation and remedial consequence without specifying an evidentiary type. Hicks and Reeves confirm that the risk of weak evidence is addressed by the plaintiff's burden and ordinary sufficiency, not a favored category of proof. The holding answers the preserved instruction question; it does not rewrite the ordinary pretext framework or decide an unpresented retaliation standard.

## 147. Satellite Broadcasting & Communications Association v. FCC

### Supported common premises

All eight Associates recognize protected satellite editorial selection and consider the claim on the merits within the limited First Amendment grant. Neither the license's voluntary character nor copyright's subject matter eliminates First Amendment scrutiny. The source's other constitutional and agency-rule dispositions remain outside this grant. All use the statutory and record qualifications set out in the preflight, including the different-State exception to the same-network nonduplication rule and the distinction between national capacity and local service election.

### Path S1 — Sustain the condition

Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer would affirm the First Amendment judgment. The statutory trigger is use of the royalty-free local copyright license, not a preferred message; the duty covers requesting eligible local stations without regard to viewpoint or program subject. In this context the distinction protects a distribution system, rather than requiring ideological balance. Intermediate scrutiny under the actual Turner holdings, O'Brien and Ward governs.

The condition directly addresses the new license's supported selective-carriage consequences. The contemporaneous record shows rapid satellite growth and a marked concentration of local retransmission on major-network affiliates. The predicted audience and revenue loss follows from viewers' demonstrated demand for a single programming source and their reduced incentive to retain an antenna once the major networks arrive by satellite. Congress may act preventively without waiting for individual station failures; satellite's smaller current market share and absence of predatory intent do not eliminate a supported threat to independently available local service. This applies Turner's shared preservation-and-diversity ground to a distinct supported mechanism, not cable's physical bottleneck finding by analogy alone.

The duty's fit is sufficient on the presented facial record. It attaches only to markets the carrier selects for licensed local service; request and station-eligibility requirements and duplication limits reduce the required carriage. The carriers' identified burden concerns how to allocate local-service capacity between markets, not compelled dropping of national or regional nonbroadcast channels on this record. Congress accommodated finite nationwide capacity with market-by-market entry, a delayed effective date, and duplication limits. Private clearance remains a lawful alternative but its transaction costs prevent treating it as a complete answer. An unrestricted station-by-station license or reliance on antennas would leave the supported selective-carriage harm in place; intermediate scrutiny does not demand the least restrictive imaginable program.

The lower court's additional advertising-competition rationale is considered separately. License-created asymmetry in audience access is relevant evidence of the mechanism by which independent local service would be weakened. These four do not need to make preservation of equal advertising receipts an independently sufficient ground; free local service and independent sources, supported by that economic mechanism, suffice. A formulation asserting a general power to equalize private media's competitive fortunes would exceed these commitments. That is a rejected broader rationale, not an unanswered element of the granted facial question.

### Path S2 — Reject the condition's present justification

O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg would reverse the First Amendment judgment and remand for appropriate relief and a statutory severability determination. The new license does not by itself authorize government to commandeer editorial capacity; retention of valuable rights on burdensome terms can be unconstitutional even where private clearance is legally available. Their existing Turner objections to preservation arguments that conceal preferences for favored local programming remain pertinent in this distinct satellite setting.

At minimum, even assuming neutral treatment of the license trigger and applying the controlling intermediate standard, the offered connection between the breadth of compulsory carriage and threatened free broadcast service is insufficient. The record supports selective carriage and some audience loss, but the relevant public harm is loss of useful independently available broadcast service, not merely denial of satellite carriage or a less favorable advertising position. Satellite lacks cable's demonstrated predatory incentives; the nationwide capacity constraint makes a channel's opportunity cost extend across local markets. Market election and duplication exemptions mitigate that burden but do not answer whether compulsory carriage of all eligible requesting stations is proportionate to the demonstrated risk. The lower court's suggestion that absence of any carriage condition would be ineffective does not establish that this condition burdens no substantially excessive amount of speech.

The separate advertising rationale also fails for these four on this record. Avoiding a distortion created by a government benefit can be legitimate, but lost audience to a competitor does not alone establish an expressive-market defect that permits compelled selection. The license expands lawful access to programming; Congress must still justify the particular compelled-carriage burden. They do not require evidence of antitrust liability as an indispensable element, reject every preventive regulation, or impose strict least-restrictive-means review as part of intermediate scrutiny. They would not award the carriers an unrestricted royalty-free license by judicial revision: the remedy must address enforcement of the invalid condition and the actual severability of the statutory package.

### Adversarial comparison and join scope

The strongest challenge to S1 is that statutory permission cannot purchase otherwise protected editorial control, the industry's national capacity makes the burden substantial, and accepting “all carriage is beneficial” as the justification would collapse scrutiny into the law's own objective. S1 answers through the independently supported selective-carriage mechanism and its market-bounded response, not through voluntariness alone. It does not adopt the lower opinion's broad proposition that competitors making any significant contribution to a common threat may all be regulated without meaningful distinctions.

The strongest challenge to S2 is that the license, market election, claimed type of displacement, actual selective-carriage experience, and duplication exceptions make this scheme materially more tailored than the compulsory cable obligations criticized in Turner. S2 accepts those differences but concludes that the condition still lacks a sufficient connection between the breadth of compelled carriage and the relevant public-service risk. These are substantive rival applications of the actual record, not predictions from an ideology or a desired margin.

## 148. Fitzgerald v. Racing Association of Central Iowa

### Components, operative rule and remedy

**F1 — Review.** The state judgment's express combined treatment of state and federal equal protection permits review under Michigan v. Long. It contains no clear independent state-law ground supporting the judgment regardless of federal law. All eight Associates reach the federal question. This conclusion does not prescribe the meaning of Iowa's Constitution on remand.

**F2 — Ordinary rational basis.** Iowa may distinguish racetrack and riverboat slot-machine revenue when the distinction rationally serves legitimate economic policies. Identical machines do not make the enterprises identical for every permissible tax purpose. The law can assist racetracks by giving them a new revenue source while also maintaining riverboats' competitive position, protecting associated economic interests, and allocating the public revenue burden. Equal protection does not require every provision to maximize the single purpose of racetrack assistance. Nordlinger and Beach supply the governing latitude, challenger burden and limits; formal legislative findings are unnecessary.

All eight would reverse the federal equal-protection invalidation and remand. A lower court's disagreement with a policy balance is not a constitutional finding of irrationality. The economic purposes here are connected to the two industries and the regulatory package, rather than a naked declaration that the favored class deserves more solely because it was favored. Allegheny Pittsburgh's arbitrary administration under a different equality scheme does not require uniform taxation of every instance of an otherwise identical activity. Independent state constitutional adjudication remains for the Iowa court under its own law.

The strongest contrary path treats the tax differential as self-defeating because rescuing racetracks was the legislation's principal purpose and the same slot activity receives different treatment. That argument does not negate legitimate simultaneous purposes or the material new benefit racetracks obtained. It would incorrectly demand that all means maximize one aim. The alternative independent-state-ground path also fails on the supplied opinion's common analysis, although a future independent Iowa-law ground remains available through the state court's own adjudication.
