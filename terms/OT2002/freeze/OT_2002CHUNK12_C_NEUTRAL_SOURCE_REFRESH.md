## Oakley — the written condition and the state court's characterization

Sources: `sources/chunk12/oakley-lower.txt`, State v. Oakley, 2001 WI 103, paragraphs 1, 6, 15–20, footnotes 21, 23 and 28–30, Bablitch concurrence paragraphs 27–33 and footnote 33, Bradley writing paragraphs 43 and 47–54, Sykes writing paragraphs 65–75; `sources/chunk12/oakley-reconsideration.txt`, 2001 WI 123, paragraph 3 and Abrahamson writing paragraphs 5–17, especially footnote 1. The original official opinion and reconsideration order are the sources recorded in `sources/chunk12/SOURCE_MAP.json`.

The written January 13, 1999 sentencing condition, as reproduced in paragraph 48 of the reported opinion, prohibits additional children during probation unless the court is shown that Oakley is meeting the needs of his existing children and can meet the needs of the additional child. Paragraphs 1 and 6 likewise describe both a present-support requirement and an ability-to-support requirement. The existing packet accurately supplies that wording's financial-capacity feature.

The same opinion's paragraph 20 says that Oakley can satisfy the condition by making efforts to support his children as required by law and by not intentionally refusing to support existing and future children. Footnotes 28–30 likewise describe intentional refusal, rather than income level, as the decisive matter. Footnote 21 disputes the assertion that the sentencing judge found the condition financially impossible and says support would be expected according to earning and payment ability. Bablitch's footnote 33 states his conclusion that the order would be amended if Oakley showed a good-faith effort to support the children. That last statement predicts amendment; it is not an entered amendment supplied by this record.

Other published writings expressly contest whether those descriptions match the written condition. Bradley's paragraph 48 identifies the discrepancy between the original wording and the intentional-refusal characterization. The reconsideration writing's footnote 1 records the claimed internal inconsistency between an ability-to-support prerequisite and compliance by refraining from intentional refusal. Reconsideration paragraph 3 retains reliance on intentional unwillingness and the criminal record; it supplies no separately reproduced amended sentencing order.

The source question is whether the state's controlling decision gives the condition an authoritative narrowing construction, merely predicts a future modification, or leaves a material ambiguity between actual financial capacity and good-faith compliance with support obligations. That question affects what governmental restraint is under review, the strongest argument supporting the condition, the relation between the condition and inability to pay, and the appropriate remedy. Clean review must confront the two formulations together and identify the legally operative one or the consequence of any unresolved ambiguity. It must not silently replace the written order with a hypothetical amendment or disregard the state court's interpretation. Neither interpretation establishes that a particular contraceptive method, sterilization, or abortion was ordered.

The five-year probation term follows incarceration. The sentencing order imposed three years' imprisonment on the first count and imposed and stayed an eight-year term on the other two counts. The review concerns the challenged probation condition, not a freestanding grant of immunity from support enforcement or the preserved criminal convictions.

## Oakley — payment history and source status

Source: `sources/chunk12/oakley-reconsideration.txt`, Abrahamson writing paragraphs 8–15, with reconsideration paragraph 3 and `sources/chunk12/oakley-lower.txt` paragraphs 3–6.

The reconsideration writing reports Oakley's assertion, supported there by reference to record exhibits, that his payments exceeded 70 percent of his support obligations. It reports that the State did not dispute that he had made support payments and had never claimed that he paid none at all. It also identifies the charged period of intentional nonsupport as January 1 through April 30, 1998. The record therefore does not justify describing him as having paid nothing throughout the whole support history.

The reconsideration disposition continues to identify arrears exceeding $25,000, three intentional-nonsupport convictions, and four additional counts read in at sentencing. Those convictions and read-ins remain the supported adjudicative facts. The separate writing's discussion does not vacate them or establish that the convicted conduct was merely inability to pay. Conversely, the convictions do not adjudicate perpetual refusal outside their temporal and count-specific scope. No later compliance finding is supplied: the writing agrees that a post-sentencing compliance contention belongs first in the circuit court.

Clean review should distinguish the convicted intentional refusal and the reported payment history, assess the State's individualized rehabilitation and prevention arguments on that complete basis, and avoid deriving a broader duration or pattern solely from the number of counts. The withdrawn child-abuse characterization remains excluded under reconsideration paragraph 2.

These source issues concern the procreation condition. The later express waiver of the earlier plea-negotiation dispute remains separately supported by original paragraphs 4 and 22–24, including footnote 32. The express undertaking, sentencing concession, and reported consensual character must remain distinct from any general rule about a bare no-contest plea.

## Star Scientific — distinct settlement obligations

Source: `sources/chunk12/star-lower.txt`, Star Scientific, Inc. v. Beales, 278 F.3d 339, official slip-opinion pages 6–8 and 15–19.

The original participating manufacturers accepted conduct restrictions and continuing nonrefundable payments. Qualifying Subsequent Participating Manufacturers accepted conduct restrictions but were not required to make payments while their market share stayed within the applicable grandfathered ceiling. The reported ceiling is the greater of their 1998 market share or 125 percent of their 1997 market share; the opinion describes the preferential eligibility as signing within 90 days. Payments above the ceiling track market share.

Nonparticipating manufacturers do not assume those settlement conduct restrictions. They must place funds in escrow based on cigarettes sold to Virginia consumers, directly or through intermediaries. They receive the interest; principal secures the described judgment or settlement; qualifying annual excess is releasable, and remaining principal is returned after 25 years. The existing packet and preflight already preserve those release conditions.

Star specifically argues that some subsequent participants owe no money while nonparticipants owe per-cigarette escrow, and that its entire escrow secures future liabilities while it attributes only a small part of participants' payments to future harms. The account of Virginia's consent decree describes a 5 percent allocation invoked by Star; that assertion is not itself a constitutional requirement that every group make an identical future-liability payment. The response relies on differences in the full package of conduct restrictions, permanent payment commitments where applicable, retained interest, refundability, and prospective security.

A description of every participant as making equivalent payments would omit the grandfathered exception. A description of different settlement commitments may encompass the exception when it explains that binding conduct restrictions also count as obligations. Clean review should test the actual comparison and preserve the distinction; it must not find Star culpable merely because it sells cigarettes. The pleaded appeal did not pursue the separate Takings Clause count (slip opinion page 9).

## Star Scientific — the actual interstate tracing argument

Source: `sources/chunk12/star-lower.txt`, official slip-opinion pages 19–24, particularly pages 23–24.

Star argues that liability measured by cigarettes ultimately sold in Virginia through independent distributors makes it monitor interstate sales or arrange more burdensome contractual distribution channels. This is an asserted operational burden on interstate commerce, distinct from the loss of access to escrowed capital and from a claim that the statute controls prices outside Virginia.

Virginia responds that its distributors already record the cigarettes receiving Virginia excise stamps and report that information under the state excise-tax provisions. The reported opinion uses that existing reporting obligation to characterize the additional burden of manufacturers obtaining the data. This is a published judicial account of an existing statutory reporting mechanism, not proof of every manufacturer's actual compliance cost or a conceded absence of burden.

Clean review should determine whether the already-frozen examination of the presented incidental burden encompasses this specific tracing contention and its answer. If an additional factual or statutory premise is necessary to the rationale, it must enter through neutral validation and independent modeling before final reconciliation. An economic-classification justification does not itself answer an independent Commerce Clause claim. No new numerical burden threshold or universal approval of all distributor arrangements follows from this source detail.
