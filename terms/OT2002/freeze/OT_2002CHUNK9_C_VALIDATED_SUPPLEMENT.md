## Harry — material entering-law correction and scope validation

Read the exact In re Baby K Holdings in `entering-law/OT_2002CHUNK9_C_BABY_K.md`. On December 12, 1994, Ginsburg for a unanimous Court held that lack of an intended transfer does not excuse the enacted stabilization duty when an acute respiratory emergency and effective stabilizing capability are established. The duty remains examination and treatment within available staff and facilities necessary to prevent likely material deterioration, within reasonable medical probability, resulting from or during transfer, or transfer compliant with subsection (c). This is a controlling earlier Supreme Court decision in this timeline, not merely the Fourth Circuit historical Baby K opinion listed in the supplied packet.

Accordingly, the supplied packet's general alternative of affirming an actual-transfer prerequisite must be tested against this existing holding. A categorical no-duty rule based solely on retention inside the hospital is not an untouched statutory question. Any distinction must rest on a legally material difference—such as the actual diagnosed emergency, stabilizing capability, or the phase after stabilization—rather than forgetting the prior holding. Any proposed limitation or overruling must be identified and justified as such. The present record does not conclusively establish these remaining predicates, and Baby K does not create an indefinite post-stabilization care duty.

The official en banc opinion was downloaded and its full extracted text read: `sources/chunk9-c/harry-en-banc.txt`, from the Eleventh Circuit's May 16, 2002 opinion, No. 99-13205. It confirms the complaint-stage source status, the ICU delay allegations, the internal location of care, the limited en banc question, reinstatement of the panel opinion except its stabilization discussion, and preservation of the separate §1981 reversal. The statutory stabilization claim runs against the hospital. The screening dismissal, separate §1981 proceedings and state claims are not merged into it. The source's characterization of treatment as negligent is not a judicial finding that every statutory prerequisite is satisfied.

The relevant statutory qualifications remain explicit. Transfer is hospital-directed movement outside its facilities, including discharge; it excludes movement after a declaration of death and departure without permission of a hospital-affiliated person. Movement from the emergency department to the hospital's ICU is not that transfer. Treatment refusal under subsection (b)(2) requires an actual offer of the required care, explanation of risks and benefits, refusal by the patient or representative, and all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Transfer refusal under (b)(3) separately requires an actual offer of a subsection-(c)-compliant transfer, risk-and-benefit information, refusal, and all reasonable steps for written informed refusal. Neither exception is established by the allegations.

An unstabilized transfer requires the informed written request or qualified signed medical risk-benefit certification, plus an appropriate transfer meeting each condition copied in Baby K and Roberts: available risk-minimizing treatment; receiving space, personnel, acceptance and treatment; available medical records and the request or certification with required on-call information; qualified transport and equipment including appropriate life support; and any applicable additional regulatory condition. A qualified medical person's certification when no physician is physically present requires consultation, the physician's determination and subsequent countersignature. Transfer remains an alternative permission, not a command to move every patient.

Roberts removes an extra improper-motive requirement but leaves the statutory determination, breach, causal harm, duration and civil-action predicates open. A directly harmed person may seek the State's available personal-injury damages and appropriate equitable relief against the participating hospital within two years after violation. No physician-only private EMTALA action, automatic damages, general federal malpractice claim or required indigence showing follows. State law remains effective except for a direct conflict with an applicable federal duty. No new regulation, including a later inpatient-admission rule, is imported.

The emergency-department delay allegations permit review of the categorical actual-transfer rationale without deciding a complete rule for genuine inpatient admission. What personnel determined, which stabilizing treatment was available, whether delay was justified, and what injury was caused remain ordinary unresolved claim questions. The diagnostic notation does not by itself establish a statutory determination; the death does not by itself establish breach. These factual questions can remain for lawful further proceedings and do not require an operator-created medical premise.
