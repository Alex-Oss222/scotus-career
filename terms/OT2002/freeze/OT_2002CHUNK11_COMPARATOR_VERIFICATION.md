## Chavez v. Martinez, No. 01-1444

**Sources fully read:** `01-1444-ZS.txt`, `01-1444-ZO.txt`, `01-1444-ZC.txt`, `01-1444-ZC1.txt`, `01-1444-ZX.txt`, `01-1444-ZX1.txt`, and `01-1444-ZX2.txt`. These correspond to the [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1444.ZS.html), [Thomas](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1444.ZO.html), [Souter](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1444.ZC.html), [Scalia](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1444.ZC1.html), [Stevens](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1444.ZX.html), [Kennedy](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1444.ZX1.html), and [Ginsburg](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1444.ZX2.html). Souter's [four-page slip-opinion PDF](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/pdf/01-1444P.ZC) was also read in full and saved as `01-1444-ZC.pdf`, with extracted text in `01-1444-ZC-pdf.txt`.

**Material HTML omission recovered:** Souter's PDF page 3 has a dagger footnote absent from the HTML text. It reserves whether absent Miranda warnings may furnish a section 1983 action under any circumstance because that question is not before the Court. His and Breyer's votes therefore do not supply a categorical Miranda-damages holding. Use the PDF for that qualification.

**Published alignment:** Thomas announced the judgment. Rehnquist joined fully; O'Connor joined Parts I and II-A; Scalia joined Parts I and II. Thomas's Fifth Amendment rationale thus had four joins, and his substantive-due-process merits rejection had only three. Do not attribute Part II-B or Part III to O'Connor. Souter's Part I was joined only by Breyer. Souter's Part II was joined by Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, and Breyer and is the Court's due-process remand holding. Scalia, Stevens, Kennedy, and Ginsburg each wrote separately. Stevens joined Kennedy fully; Ginsburg joined Kennedy Parts II and III, not Part I.

**Distinct grounds and limits:** Thomas's group rejected a completed self-incrimination violation absent use in a criminal case; it expressly did not decide precisely when a criminal case commences. The penalty/immunity cases remain protected even on that opinion's prophylactic characterization: government cannot require waiver of immunity as a condition of public employment or contracting. Thomas's group recognized that due process could prohibit torture even when the Self-Incrimination Clause did not apply. Souter and Breyer rejected the requested additional damages protection for failure to show that it was needed to protect the core guarantee, considering the costs and absence of a limiting principle; they did not join Thomas's categorical analysis.

The remand requires consideration of whether a substantive-due-process action is available and, if so, its scope and merits. It does not decide a violation or qualified immunity on that claim. Scalia separately opposed reopening the due-process matter: he viewed the lower court as having decided it and maintained that it was meritless; on the alternative reading that it had not been decided, he regarded it as forfeited. Those are his separate grounds, not the remand majority's premises.

Kennedy and Stevens regarded severe compulsion as an immediate Self-Incrimination Clause violation; Kennedy's separate Part I distinguished mere failure to warn, treating exclusion as sufficient for that issue. Kennedy's due-process analysis permitted legitimate questioning of a person in pain, including exigent questioning and voluntary disclosure, but prohibited prolonging or increasing suffering or using the impression that treatment depended on cooperation. His application rested on intentional exploitation of Martinez's condition. Ginsburg considered the totality sufficiently coercive even without a finding that Martinez believed treatment would be withheld or that Chavez intended that impression. Stevens emphasized the immediate liberty deprivation from the brutal interrogation. Stevens, Kennedy, and Ginsburg joined Souter's remand to supply a controlling judgment while preserving their disagreement. Six historical votes rejecting the Fifth Amendment claim do not make six joins in Thomas's reasoning.

## National Park Hospitality Association v. Department of the Interior, No. 02-196

**Sources fully read:** `02-196-ZS.txt`, `02-196-ZO.txt`, `02-196-ZC.txt`, and `02-196-ZD.txt`: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-196.ZS.html), [Thomas](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-196.ZO.html), [Stevens](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-196.ZC.html), and [Breyer](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-196.ZD.html).

**Correction to the comparator summary:** Stevens's concurrence in the judgment is an Article III standing opinion, not a separate merits analysis. He found no allegation or evidence of a specific injury to the association or a member that favorable relief would redress. He considered the statutory issue fit for decision and would have treated it as ripe if the injury requirement had been met. This verified account controls over the brief comparator's description.

Thomas's ripeness opinion was joined by Rehnquist, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Breyer dissented with O'Connor: both would find standing and ripeness and affirm on the merits principally for the reasons below as supplemented by the Government. Thus the seven votes for the judgment contain a six-Justice ripeness rationale and Stevens's distinct standing rationale.

The six-Justice opinion classifies the challenged CDA statement as a nonbinding general policy statement because NPS neither administers the CDA nor has delegated CDA rulemaking authority. No present legal obligation or alteration of primary conduct followed; concessioners remained able to pursue CDA procedures in a concrete dispute. Mere pricing uncertainty was inadequate hardship, and contract-specific facts could clarify applicability. The judgment vacated only insofar as the appellate decision addressed the challenged portion of section 51.3 and remanded with instructions to dismiss that issue. Footnote 4 declined the distinct challenges to two prospectuses because review of them had not been sought in the petition. The dissent's prospective-bidder analogy under the Tucker Act did not change that scope limitation. The CDA's ultimate application was not decided by the controlling opinion.

## Black & Decker Disability Plan v. Nord, No. 02-469

**Sources fully read:** `02-469-ZS.txt` and `02-469-ZO.txt`: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-469.ZS.html) and [Ginsburg's unanimous opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-469.ZO.html).

All nine joined. ERISA and the regulations supply neither automatic special weight for treating physicians nor a distinct heightened explanation burden for rejecting their evidence in favor of reliable conflicting evidence. This applies to versions labeled procedural or substantive. Ordinary duties to give specific denial reasons and provide full and fair review remain; administrators may not arbitrarily disregard reliable evidence, including treating opinions. The opinion does not prefer plan consultants as a class or resolve ultimate benefit entitlement. It vacates and remands because the court below used the unsupported treating-physician rule. Footnote 1 limits the claim to benefits for up to 30 months; footnote 2 limits review to that rule. The discussion of a possible Labor Department regulation is conditional and does not adopt one.

## Bunkley v. Florida, No. 02-8636

**Sources fully read:** `02-8636-ZPC.txt` and `02-8636-ZD.txt`: [per curiam](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-8636.ZPC.html) and [Rehnquist dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-8636.ZD.html).

This is a May 27, 2003 petition-stage summary disposition: leave to proceed in forma pauperis and certiorari granted, judgment vacated, case remanded. There was no plenary argument. Rehnquist's published dissent was joined by Kennedy and Thomas and expressly would deny certiorari. The per curiam and named dissent establish the published merits/remand disagreement; the familiar six-to-three summary must not be turned into an independently disclosed certiorari poll, conference tally, or signed majority roster.

The per curiam requires Florida to determine whether the pocketknife exception already covered this knife when the conviction became final in 1989. It distinguishes a new statutory interpretation from a later clarification establishing that an element was always missing. Although a clear holding that the later interpretation changed the law would ordinarily resolve the Fiore issue, Florida's description of a century-long evolution left the relevant timing unanswered. The Court did not itself classify the knife under 1989 law or direct release. If the knife remained a weapon then, the question left open in Fiore would remain. The single footnote answers finality/state-ground objections: the earlier attacks preceded L. B.; the new claim depended on that decision; Florida also passed upon the federal due-process issue, so Michigan v. Long did not prevent the remand.

The dissent read Florida as already having decided that L. B. changed the law after the conviction and did not apply retroactively. It objected to expanding Fiore summarily, to revisiting authoritative state-law conclusions, to requiring further clarification contrary to Long, and to defeating the two-year state postconviction limitation after repeated prior challenges. Footnote 1 acknowledges strong arguments for the prisoner's reading of earlier Florida law but regards the state supreme court's contrary answer as controlling. These are substantive and summary-procedure objections, not a finding of missing petition papers.

## Beneficial National Bank v. Anderson, No. 02-306

**Sources fully read:** `02-306-ZS.txt`, `02-306-ZO.txt`, and `02-306-ZD.txt`: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-306.ZS.html), [Stevens](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-306.ZO.html), and [Scalia](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-306.ZD.html).

Stevens wrote for Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Scalia dissented with Thomas. The majority reversed: sections 85 and 86 provide the exclusive cause of action for the pleaded usury claim against a national bank, so it arises under federal law despite its state labels. Section 85 sets substantive interest limits; section 86 supplies the exclusive claim, remedies, and procedures. A federal preemption defense alone remains insufficient for removal. Footnote 5 identifies exclusivity of the federal cause, rather than an independently expressed removal intent, as the relevant congressional intent. Footnote 3 preserves removal of related state claims through supplemental jurisdiction when another claim is removable; footnote 4 treats the Indian possessory-rights precedent as resting on its distinctive historical relationship. The decision resolves jurisdiction, not whether the loans were usurious.

Scalia and Thomas would preserve the well-pleaded-complaint rule and confine the Avco/Metropolitan Life departures to the LMRA and statutes modeled on it. They accept those narrow precedents under stare decisis while denying that exclusivity converts an invalid state claim into a pleaded federal claim. The dissent distinguishes authority to dismiss the state claim from removal jurisdiction; its footnote 1 treats ERISA's parallel language as statutory incorporation of Avco, not as approval of a general exclusivity test.

## Dastar Corp. v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp., No. 02-428

**Sources fully read:** `02-428-ZS.txt` and `02-428-ZO.txt`: [syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-428.ZS.html) and [Scalia's opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-428.ZO.html).

Scalia wrote for every participating Justice; Breyer took no part in consideration or decision. The judgment reverses and remands. For the presented section 43(a)(1)(A) theory, origin identifies the producer of the tangible goods, not the originator of ideas or communications they contain. Dastar produced its tapes rather than merely relabeling purchased copies of respondents' goods. The opinion preserves ordinary reverse passing off of another producer's physical goods and the statute's distinct affiliation, sponsorship, and approval language.

The opinion rejects an attribution right that would recreate expired copyright or patent protection and explains the practical attribution and false-endorsement problems with respondents' reading. A qualifying false advertising claim under section 43(a)(1)(B) would require misrepresentation of nature, characteristics, or qualities in advertising or promotion; the opinion identifies that possibility, not an established alternative liability here. The book-copyright claim remains on remand and was outside the question presented. Footnote 2 preserves the lower court's work-for-hire/renewal issue. Failure of the Lanham Act theory makes the conditional double-profits question unnecessary. Neither the summary nor the opinion authorizes attribution-based perpetual protection for public-domain content.
