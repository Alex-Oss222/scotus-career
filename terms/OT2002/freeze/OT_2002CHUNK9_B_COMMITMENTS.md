## 107. Demore v. Kim — No. 01-1491

### Components, record constraints and two supported paths

**D1 — legal habeas channel.** The legal challenge attacks Congress's categorical prohibition on release without an individual custody assessment, rather than a discretionary bond amount. Section 1226(e)'s first sentence addresses discretionary judgment; its second prohibits setting aside detention or release actions and decisions. The review-preserving construction reads those sentences together and leaves section 2241 available for this challenge to legal authority. The competing construction gives the second sentence independent breadth sufficient to reach the requested order altering detention. Goncalves and St. Cyr establish actual statutory habeas for the provisions they construed; neither automatically decides this different text. The strongest objection to preservation is that relief necessarily sets aside detention. The strongest objection to exclusion is that the asserted defect is the governing statute, not discretionary administration, and the text does not clearly withdraw that distinct legality inquiry. The actual possibility of renewed custody prevents treating release on bond as complete relief from the challenged rule.

**D2 — initial custody and mandatory-category status.** Kim disclaimed a challenge to initial authority to take him into custody. That is not a concession of every removal or mandatory-category issue. His identified intended challenges to his convictions' classifications remain distinct from the legal no-bail challenge. The available category procedure can test statutory inclusion; it cannot decide whether a properly included person needs continued custody for danger or flight. Failure to identify a requested category hearing does not establish waiver of every legal objection. No commitment here decides either conviction's immigration classification.

**D3 — constitutional justification for continued custody.** One path sustains Congress's categorical custody judgment during a reasonably conducted removal process for persons lawfully within the designated categories, while preserving a genuine challenge to erroneous inclusion or arbitrary, unreasonably delayed detention. The strongest support is Carlson's immigration setting, Congress's identified attendance and safety concerns, and the distinction between ongoing removal adjudication and the potentially indefinite post-final-order custody in Zadvydas. Its hardest objection is that a category hearing cannot test individual necessity, and a lawful initial arrest does not justify every ensuing period of incarceration.

The other path requires an opportunity for individualized danger-and-flight assessment on this as-applied challenge after the initial custody and removal-processing opportunity, and sustains the existing conditional hearing remedy. Salerno's preventive purpose was accompanied by individual safeguards; Foucha and Zadvydas preserve attention to the continuing source and purpose of custody; Flores sustained a materially different juvenile-care regime with genuine release avenues. Its hardest objection is that it substitutes judicial assessment for Congress's categorical risk judgment and may wrongly transfer safeguards from another detention regime. This path therefore does not invalidate every application, prohibit prompt initial custody, prescribe a universal burden or numerical ceiling, or copy Zadvydas's six-month implementation rule.

**D4 — disposition and remedy.** Approximately six months of custody, including Kim's requested continuance to obtain criminal records, is the supported account. It supplies neither a finding of government obstruction nor a complete answer to an individual-necessity claim. The lower conditional writ required a bond hearing; the agency later released Kim on $5,000 bond. The record does not establish an intervening hearing. Affirming that conditional remedy is different from ordering unconditional release or finding current danger or flight absent. Reversing it is different from ordering rearrest. Ventura leaves conviction classification and other unperformed agency factual tasks in their assigned forum; it does not defer the constitutional validity of the no-release command to the agency.

## 108. Roell v. Withrow — No. 02-69

### Components and adversarial paths

**R1 — actual consent versus prescribed documentation.** The first sentence of section 636(c)(1) authorizes civil adjudication upon the parties' consent and special district designation. The second sentence expressly requires a specific written request for the identified other part-time magistrates, with its own bar-membership and chief-judge certification requirements. The latter conditions cannot be moved into the former sentence. Section 636(c)(2) nevertheless commands notice, communication to the clerk and protection of voluntary choice; renewed advice must preserve refusal without adverse substantive consequences. Rule 73(b) requires execution and filing of joint or separate forms within the locally specified period and keeps a party's response from the judge unless all consent. These procedures remain mandatory under either construction.

The **actual-consent path** permits knowing, voluntary prejudgment participation to establish the consent required by paragraph (1), while treating failure to record it through the mandated procedure as a real procedural violation that does not itself nullify an otherwise authorized final judgment. The strongest counterargument is that inferring consent after trial dilutes the blind clerk process and invites pressure or tactical silence. Its limiting answer is actual knowledge of the right to refuse plus voluntary conduct manifesting the choice before judgment; silence without notice, a judge's mistaken belief, or a new post-verdict choice is insufficient.

The **advance-express-consent path** reads paragraphs (1) and (2) with Rule 73 as requiring the parties' affirmative, properly communicated choice before a final civil referral. A later inference cannot replace the prescribed act even when it makes an informed choice plausible. Its strongest counterargument is Congress's express-writing language for only one category and the difference between an indispensable condition and its documentation. Its answer is that the generally applicable clerk and rule requirements independently require an express recorded election, while the second sentence imposes its further request and designation conditions on the different magistrate category.

**R2 — Article III and the supported record.** Peretz, 501 U.S. 923, permits consent to magistrate-supervised felony voir dire under the additional-duties provision; it does not directly decide civil final judgment under paragraph (c). Schor, 478 U.S. 833, allows waiver of a personal Article III interest, including an informed choice manifested by conduct, but independently examines structural limits. Neither permits consent to create missing statutory authority. The lower finding that express oral consent did not occur and that the magistrate mistakenly believed forms existed is preserved. The fresh neutral packet establishes prior notice of the right to refuse and continued prejudgment participation by Roell and Garibay. Their later letters can confirm a choice already made; they cannot create a new one retroactively.

**R3 — remedy.** Accepting actual informed consent removes the nullification ground and returns remaining appellate issues to the Fifth Circuit. Requiring advance express consent leaves the challenged magistrate judgment unable to stand through later letters. Neither disposition decides the prison medical-care merits. No fresh factfinding is needed on the packet's established notice and voluntary-participation predicate, and no invented oral consent cures the defect.

## 109. Herring v. Keenan — review of Tenth Circuit No. 99-1263

### Components, assumptions and adversarial alternatives

**H1 — judicial task and damages source.** The presented legal immunity issue is reviewable on the well-pleaded plaintiff-favorable account. Herring's privacy claim seeks individual federal-officer damages; it is not section 1983 and not a direct agency action. Meyer's distinction between waiver and cause of action neither creates this particular Bivens claim nor defeats it merely because the defendant is federal. No independently preserved special-factors or alternative-remedy objection is supplied. The commitments decide the presented immunity ground without manufacturing such an objection; any actually available independent defense retains its ordinary status. Dismissed Eighth Amendment, distinct liberty and declaratory claims and the withdrawn roommate allegation do not return.

**H2 — current intimate-information protection.** The protected-content path applies the narrow current protection for intimate medical information to intentional disclosure outside a sufficient supervisory, treatment or safety function. Sheets protects actual intimate dissemination; Denius protects intimate medical information against unjustified official access; Paul P. and Lawall require a sufficient connection among protected information, audience and purpose. Sterling shows that family exposure is not categorically harmless or outside privacy. Genuine supervisory needs and fitted safeguards can justify a disclosure. Neither a confidential label nor a policy breach creates the constitutional right.

The strongest contrary path emphasizes the probation setting, limited recipients, voluntary initial communication and the gap between this disclosure and Sheets's public dissemination. Its proper version does not assert that probation extinguishes privacy or that every official assertion of safety proves a legitimate need. The complaint alleges an unconfirmed test result, no consent, disclosure to a sister and restaurant employer, and pressure to terminate employment for an asserted legal food-service prohibition. The sister allegation concerns testing and/or positive status; it is not exclusively a verified positive-result disclosure. The officer's adequate purpose, actual knowledge of a positive result and transmission risk are not established findings.

**H3 — December 1993/January 1994 notice.** The denial-of-immunity path uses cumulative preconduct law: Eastwood's unjustified intimate sexual inquiry and harassment, the Tenth Circuit's bounded confidentiality decisions, and the medical-disclosure applications in Woods, Barrington and Plymouth, considered at their actual hierarchy. Griffin recognizes supervisory special needs but does not authorize purpose-free disclosure; Harris and Stine uphold justified intrusions, not status-based extinction of privacy. On the complaint's account, lack of a reasonable relation to the actual DWI supervision distinguishes those upheld uses. The probation guidance reinforces the known difference between authorized safety-related disclosure and unnecessary exposure; it supplies context, not a freestanding constitutional right. The strongest objection is that no controlling 1993 decision defined this limited probation disclosure and Supreme Court confidentiality decisions reserved important applications.

The immunity path accepts that the alleged conduct can be wrongful and currently within privacy protection but finds preconduct constitutional notice insufficiently particular. Eastwood's coerced employee inquiry and harassment, limited lower-court medical cases, and Whalen/Nixon's retained governmental uses did not settle the probation officer's disclosure duties with the required clarity. Griffin's distinct supervision rule and contrary general-right authority add legal uncertainty. The strongest objection is that this demands a factual twin and credits a public-safety account the pleading does not establish. The immunity commitment therefore rests on the incompleteness of the contemporaneous constitutional boundary even on plaintiff-favorable facts, not a finding that the actual disclosures were protective, accurate or lawful.

**H4 — relief.** Reversing immunity leaves proof of the actual acts, responsibility, content, purpose, causation, injury and available independent defenses to ordinary proceedings. It awards neither damages nor an automatic trial or new discovery. Affirming immunity disposes of personal damages through that ground without a new ruling that all probationary medical disclosure is constitutional. Current decisions impose no mandatory merits-first sequence.

**M1 — public action and federal forum.** The United States seeks to enforce confidentiality obligations accepted by the two federal-fund recipients against an actual continuing disclosure policy. **M2 — definition, exclusion and permissions.** Retained internal disciplinary files directly relating to a student satisfy both statutory conditions for education records. These internal student-institution proceedings do not determine criminal guilt for the sovereign, do not prevent criminal prosecution and lack the established tradition of public access to criminal adjudication. Criminal subject matter is already accepted. The Court need not approve a categorical denial of discovery in all confidentiality cases; it affirms this ruling under the applicable discretionary standard. The consequence is affirmance of the judgment and fitted prospective injunction, without ordering disclosure, forbidding lawful redaction or permissions, or creating a new evidentiary hearing.
