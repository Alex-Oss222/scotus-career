## 127. Nevada Department of Human Resources v. Hibbs

### SECTION I: NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

* **Case Name:** Nevada Department of Human Resources v. Hibbs
* **Citation or Docket:** 538 U.S. 721; No. 01-1368
* **October Term:** 2002
* **Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS; review of the identified judgment on the presented questions.
* **Simulated Event Date:** 2003-05-27 (historical calendar placement).
* **Question Presented:** Whether the FMLA family-care provision validly enforces the Fourteenth Amendment and authorizes private actions against States notwithstanding sovereign immunity. The independent Article I ground remains an alternative basis for sustaining the judgment.
* **Question Granted:** The family-care Section 5 and state-immunity question, paraphrased from the contemporaneous merits filing; no ruling on the distinct self-care provision is sought.
* **Court and Judgment Under Review:** The Ninth Circuit reversed the District of Nevada’s judgment barring Hibbs’s FMLA damages action on state sovereign-immunity grounds. The district court also rejected a separate Fourteenth Amendment claim. The federal statutory family-care remedy, rather than a judgment finding an individual equal-protection violation, is the present subject.
* **Relief Requested:** Nevada seeks dismissal of the private damages claim. Hibbs seeks to proceed under the FMLA. Resolution of Congress’s enforcement authority does not determine his eligibility, exhaustion of leave, whether the discharge violated the Act, damages or equitable relief.
* **Material Record:** Hibbs worked for Nevada’s welfare division and requested intermittent leave to care for his wife following a serious injury and surgery. The department approved twelve weeks to be used between May and December 1997. He stopped returning to work after August 5. The department later asserted that available leave had been exhausted and directed him to return by November 12; it terminated him when he did not return. The suit alleges interference with statutory family-care rights. The broader legislative record concerns sex stereotypes in the allocation of family care, unequal leave policies, and discrimination by state as well as private employers; the parties dispute what that evidence proves and how broadly Congress could respond.
* **Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Separate unmistakable statutory authorization, a valid source of congressional power, statutory eligibility and an individual violation. The Act includes public agencies and permits actions against employers in federal or state court. The historical presentation emphasizes § 5, but an independently sufficient source of authority under current law can support the same judgment without expanding the granted dispute. No State waiver need be inferred from employment or participation in commerce. Family-care leave is distinct from the Act’s self-care provision.
* **Relevant Dates:** Leave and discharge occurred in 1997; Ninth Circuit judgment in 2001; argument January 15, 2003; historical-calendar merits event May 27, 2003. Conduct-date law does not replace the event-date rule governing Congress’s authority.
* **Companion or Related Matters:** No separate companion matter is added to this closed list.
* **Participation Matter:** None identified.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

* **Current Through:** Governing law effective before May 27, 2003.

**Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** Popovich v. Cuyahoga County Court of Common Pleas (November 4, 2002).
* **Related Questions:** Sex-linked family-care discrimination and Congress’s preventive enforcement power require their own analysis. Particular statutory-enforcement routes do not displace the independent Article I and Fourteenth Amendment bases recognized in Florida Prepaid and Kimel.

* **Institutional or Procedural Law:** An alternative ground that sustains the judgment remains distinct from imposing a new merits judgment. If statutory damages enforcement is constitutionally authorized, return the case for its unresolved statutory elements and defenses. A State’s constitutional immunity objection is not a decision that the employee necessarily wins his benefits dispute.

##### Issue 1: Express State coverage and Article I enforcement

* **Controlling Law:** Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank independently holds that Congress may unmistakably authorize private suits against States when the substantive duty and enforcement are supported by a valid Article I power. Kimel v. Florida Board of Regents applies that principle to the ADEA’s state-employment duty and private federal remedy. Alden v. Maine sustains the authorized state-court FLSA remedy. 
* **Current Rule:** Require clear congressional authorization and a valid constitutional basis for the substantive regulation and its private enforcement. Do not manufacture State consent from commercial activity. Article I authority and Fourteenth Amendment enforcement are independent routes, not cumulative prerequisites.
* **Other Governing Law:** The Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses support national regulation of employment conditions within their enacted reach. Sections 2611(4), 2612(a)(1)(C), 2615 and 2617 identify public-agency employers, the family-care entitlement, prohibited interference and the express private remedy. The incorporated public-agency definition includes States.
* **Material Other Authority:** Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority remains relevant to regulation of state employment. College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board forecloses inferred commercial-participation waiver, not the separate Article I authorization recognized in the Florida Prepaid holding.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** The existing Article I State-suit rule is controlling. Applying it to the FMLA requires identifying the national employment regulation and unmistakable State remedy, not recreating a sovereign-immunity prohibition absent from current law.
* **Open Point:** Whether this family-care remedy satisfies those statutory and constitutional predicates. The actual leave violation and amount of recovery remain unresolved.

##### Issue 2: Fourteenth Amendment enforcement of sex equality

* **Controlling Law:** City of Boerne v. Flores permits congruent and proportional preventive or remedial enforcement of Fourteenth Amendment rights, including some conduct beyond independently proven constitutional violations, but not congressional redefinition of those rights. Florida Prepaid supplies an independent application of that standard; Kimel expressly reserves the ADEA’s § 5 validity rather than deciding it against Congress. Popovich has since sustained Title II’s express State remedy for the bounded parental-custody access and related-retaliation duties there, including valid statutory obligations beyond separately established constitutional torts. It did not find individual liability, reinstate the $400,000 award, validate every public service or expand M.L.B.’s permanent-termination holding.
* **Current Rule:** Identify the constitutional right, the relevant unconstitutional conduct and the fit of the statutory response, examining scope, burden and workable limits. No fixed quantity of formal findings, separate violation in every State, sunset or geographical restriction is invariably required. Sex stereotypes and sex classifications receive the governing heightened equality analysis; employer status alone is not discrimination.
* **Other Governing Law:** Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment; the FMLA’s sex-neutral twelve-week entitlement, eligibility conditions, notice provisions and limited damages design. The family-care provision can address stereotypes affecting men and women without requiring every statutory claimant to prove personal intentional sex discrimination. Popovich confirms the distinction between a valid statutory enforcement obligation and an independently proved constitutional tort, but its court-access category does not establish the fit of this employment entitlement.
* **Material Other Authority:** United States v. Virginia informs the constitutional treatment of sex-based assumptions. The legislative record and public statute are evidence of congressional response, not prior adjudications of every State’s liability.
* **Existing Rule's Reach:** Article I and Section 5 are independently sufficient routes. The expressly presented Section 5 issue concerns the family-care provision, not every possible leave regulation.
* **Open Point:** The adequacy and fit of this record as a distinct § 5 basis, including whether a neutral entitlement reasonably prevents employers from using family roles as a proxy for sex.

**Further controlling authority:** Board of Trustees v. Garrett (February 21, 2001) sustains expressly authorized state-employment actions independently under Article I and under congruent-and-proportional Section 5 prevention. Its rule separates the powers question from each employee’s statutory elements and remedies.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

Nevada argues that Congress cannot create private State damages exposure on the showing made and that sex-neutral failure to provide family leave is not itself sex discrimination. It attacks the state-specific evidence and the breadth of a universal leave entitlement. Hibbs argues that excluding men from meaningful family leave and assuming women’s caregiving roles are mutually reinforcing sex stereotypes, and that a neutral entitlement is a practical preventive response. He also seeks effect for Congress’s express State coverage and private remedy. Under the controlling law, Nevada must answer the independently sufficient Article I rule rather than rely on the historical immunity premise. The government supports congressional enforcement and the congruent relationship between family-care leave and sex equality. Neither side’s constitutional argument resolves whether this employee exceeded approved leave.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirmance may rest on express statutory authorization within Congress’s commerce authority under Florida Prepaid and Kimel, leaving § 5 undecided. A second path reaches and sustains this family-care provision as congruent and proportional sex-equality enforcement. Reversal based on categorical Article I incapacity would require openly changing controlling law; it cannot be presented as routine application of historical immunity. If a genuine statutory-authorization or commerce-scope defect is identified, it must be adjudicated on its own terms. An immunity ruling in Hibbs’s favor returns the statutory case for adjudication; it awards no automatic leave entitlement or damages.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** The Article I path applies the existing rule to a clearly authorized national employment remedy against States. A separate § 5 holding could reach sex-neutral preventive family-care legislation fitting a demonstrated equality problem. Neither path necessarily resolves self-care leave, every employment statute, State waiver or an individual equal-protection claim.

### Statutory content and qualifications

The exact operative appendix text is copied by script into `terms/OT2002/entering-law/OT_2002CHUNK10_HIBBS_STATUTORY_TEXT.md`. It controls over an incomplete paraphrase in the brief. The qualifications below preserve the distinction between the statute's modest coverage and remedial boundaries and any factual finding that Hibbs satisfies them.

Section 2611(4)(A)(iii) expressly includes a public agency within the employer category, incorporating §203(x), and subsection (4)(B) treats a public agency as engaged in commerce or an industry or activity affecting commerce. Section 2617(a)(2) separately authorizes damages or appropriate equitable relief against any employer, including a public agency, in a competent federal or state court. Express employer coverage and express private remedy therefore have separate texts; neither alone supplies constitutional power or consent. The incorporated public-agency definition includes States and their agencies, as the original authorized packet states. The merits submission's statutory appendix does not itself reproduce §203(x); this validation does not pretend that it does.

Employee eligibility generally requires at least twelve months of employment and at least 1,250 hours of service during the preceding twelve months, with the statutory hours-calculation standards. Section 2611(2)(B) excludes the federal officers and employees covered by the specified Title 5 leave subchapter, and employees at a worksite with fewer than fifty employees when the employer has fewer than fifty within seventy-five miles of that worksite. Public-agency coverage does not erase that employee worksite condition. The incorporated §203(e) definition also controls which State workers are employees; the Government's description of exclusions for high-ranking or sensitive officials is not a new free-floating statutory exception. The appendix's §2611(2)(B)(i) text concerns Title 5 employees, so that particular citation must not be represented as the text of a generalized high-ranking-state-official exclusion.

Section 2612(a)(1)(C), subject to §2613, permits an eligible employee to take up to a total of twelve workweeks of leave during any twelve-month period to care for a spouse, son, daughter or parent with a serious health condition. The twelve weeks are the combined statutory entitlement for the listed categories, not twelve new weeks for each relative or each episode. Serious health condition means an illness, injury, impairment or physical or mental condition involving inpatient care in the specified facilities or continuing treatment by a health-care provider. The parent definition includes a biological parent or an individual who acted as a parent during the employee's childhood; a son or daughter includes the specified biological, adopted, foster, step, ward and parental-role relationships and is under eighteen, or eighteen or older and incapable of self-care because of physical or mental disability. The then-enacted spouse definition is reproduced in the appendix. No broader kinship entitlement is assumed.

Family-care leave may be intermittent or on a reduced schedule when medically necessary, subject to the certification and foreseeable-treatment rules. Only leave actually taken reduces the total entitlement. For foreseeable planned treatment the employee must make a reasonable effort to avoid undue operational disruption, subject to provider approval, and ordinarily give thirty days' notice; if treatment requires earlier leave, notice must be as practicable. An employer may require temporary transfer for foreseeable intermittent treatment to an available alternative job for which the employee is qualified, with equivalent pay and benefits and better accommodation of recurring leave. That permission is not a duty to transfer or authority to cut compensation.

The entitlement may be unpaid, with the distinct paid-leave substitution rules. An employee may elect, or employer may require, accrued vacation, personal or family leave in the specified family categories; the serious-health-condition rule also permits accrued medical or sick leave without requiring paid sick or medical leave in circumstances where the employer would not normally provide it. The same-employer-spouses aggregation permission applies to birth/placement leave or care for a sick parent; it is not a blanket combination of all spousal family-care leave. No such aggregation fact is established here.

An employer may request timely provider certification. The required content includes the condition's commencement and probable duration, appropriate medical facts, the need and expected time for the employee's care, and the applicable treatment dates or intermittent-care necessity and schedule. A doubted certification can lead to an employer-paid second opinion from a provider not regularly employed by that employer, and conflicting opinions to an employer-paid third opinion jointly chosen and binding on both parties. Reasonable subsequent recertification is permitted. These are qualified statutory safeguards, not facts about a deficient Hibbs certification.

Section 2614 preserves return to the former or an equivalent job and accrued preleave benefits, subject to its actual conditions. It creates neither new benefit accrual during leave nor a right the employee would not have retained without leave. The highly compensated employee exception permits denial of restoration only for a salaried employee among the highest-paid ten percent within seventy-five miles when denial is necessary to avoid substantial and grievous economic injury, timely notice is given, and an employee already on leave elects not to return after that notice. It is not a general denial of family-care leave. The health-benefit and recovery rules and other restoration qualifications are copied in full; no exception is found applicable to Hibbs.

Section 2615 forbids interference, restraint or denial of exercising or attempting statutory rights, discrimination for opposing an unlawful practice, and the separately listed discrimination for participation in enforcement or inquiry. Section 2617(a)(1) provides compensation actually lost by reason of the violation; when no wages, salary, benefits or other compensation were denied or lost, it instead permits directly resulting actual monetary losses, such as care costs, capped at twelve weeks of the employee's wages or salary. The twelve-week cap on that alternative must not be described as a general backpay cap. Interest is calculated at the prevailing rate. The additional liquidated sum equals the compensatory amount plus interest. Only if the employer proves both good faith and reasonable grounds for believing the act or omission lawful may the court, in its discretion, reduce liability to principal plus interest. The submission's shorter “if the employer fails to act in good faith” formulation omits this conjunctive proof and judicial discretion and is not the operative rule.

Appropriate equitable relief may include employment, reinstatement or promotion. A judgment for the employee also carries reasonable attorney and expert fees and other costs under the statute. The usual action period is two years after the last event constituting the alleged violation and three years for a willful violation. A qualifying Secretary complaint terminates the private-action right under §2617(a)(4), unless that action is dismissed without prejudice on the Secretary's motion. No such filing is supplied. The Government's discussion of generally unavailable damages includes differing lower-court treatments of emotional-distress damages; no additional categorical damages holding is necessary for this threshold event. Sections 2651–2653 preserve the identified antidiscrimination laws and greater State, local, collective and plan protections while forbidding diminution of the federal floor by a collective agreement or plan.

### Legislative record and the two constitutional routes

The Government offers several related propositions: an extensive history of State restrictions on women's employment; continuing use of sex stereotypes in hiring and advancement; leave policies favoring mothers over fathers; and the effect of discretionary leave decisions in reinforcing assigned domestic roles. Its authorities include legislative testimony and reports, judicial recitations, selected statutes and later date-eligible examples. They are not adjudicated findings that each State violated equal protection. Statements made after the FMLA's 1993 enactment can illuminate conditions but must not be described as evidence Congress received before enactment. Private-employer studies, municipal practices, State-employer practices, medical-pregnancy accommodation, child-rearing leave and care of ill relatives must retain their distinct categories.

The Government cites a Massachusetts public-school teacher's account of sex-specific child-rearing leave and testimony discussing public as well as private employers (brief pp.22–25 and their notes). Those identify more specific public-sector sources than a nationwide private-company percentage alone. Its list of seven women-only leave regimes at pp.37–38 includes pregnancy-disability provisions as well as parenting provisions. That list cannot mechanically be counted as seven unconstitutional denials of sex-neutral family-care leave: pregnancy-related physical incapacity and general caregiving pose different comparisons. Nor does lack of any leave benefit by itself prove intentional discrimination. The strength, relevance and limits of the mixed evidence remain the §5 judgment to be made.

**Article I path.** Florida Prepaid, Kimel and Garrett already sustain an expressly authorized private State action where both the covered substantive obligation and its enforcement fall within valid Article I power. The first task is to identify the actual economic employment relationship and the appropriateness of the enacted private remedy, rather than rely on generalized social costs or statutory labels alone. Garcia and EEOC v. Wyoming are surviving antecedents for federal regulation of covered state employment. If those predicates and clear authorization are met, this route can sustain the unchanged immunity-rejection judgment without reaching §5. Its strongest objection preserves the structural distinction between regulation and a private treasury suit; under current law that is an argument to reconsider controlling doctrine, not an existing categorical bar. A respondent's alternative ground must remain within review and preserve the same judgment; Florida Prepaid's unanimous alternative-ground rule supplies that boundary.

**Section 5 path.** The separate issue is whether the sex-neutral family-care entitlement is congruent and proportional prevention of unconstitutional sex-based State employment practices. Virginia applies demanding intermediate scrutiny to express sex exclusions, without changing the tier to strict scrutiny or denying genuine physical differences. Boerne permits prevention reaching some conduct beyond independently proved constitutional violations, while rejecting replacement of the constitutional right. Garrett and Popovich apply that distinction to different statutory subjects; neither automatically resolves this leave provision.

The strongest supporting argument is that equal family-care availability removes a sex proxy in hiring, advancement and leave administration, addressing mutually reinforcing stereotypes affecting men and women. Eligibility, a finite shared leave total, medical-need and certification conditions, notice and scheduling protections, unpaid status and claim-linked relief support a bounded preventive fit. Its strongest objection is that a uniform minimum benefit also reaches nondiscriminatory employers and denials, while parts of the evidence concern private practices, pregnancy disability or old restrictions instead of unconstitutional State family-care decisions. The model must explain why those differences do or do not undermine the response's fit. The strongest narrowing alternative relies only on Article I and leaves this genuinely distinct §5 inquiry undecided. Rejection of a proposed §5 rationale cannot yield State immunity if the independent controlling Article I ground remains applicable. No new numerical incident quota, formal-findings mandate, violation-in-every-State requirement, geographical restriction or sunset requirement follows from Boerne.
