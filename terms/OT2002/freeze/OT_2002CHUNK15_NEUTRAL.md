#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name / Citation:** Columbia Union College v. Oliver, 254 F.3d 496; Fourth Circuit No. 00-2193; district action No. CA-96-1831-MJG.

**October Term / Entry Stage:** OT2002; assigned counterfactual merits review on the express premise that a timely petition concerning the 2001 judgment was retained for review in this term. **Simulated Event Date:** 2003-04-21. **Questions Presented:** Whether Maryland may deny otherwise eligible higher-education grants because of a college's religious character when the identified grant finances demonstrably secular programs under use restrictions and accounting controls; whether the Constitution requires examination of the actual financed function rather than an institution-wide religious classification; and whether the reported findings and safeguards permit this award.

**Question Granted:** These define the assigned review of the reported federal judgment. No historical Supreme Court docket or verbatim granted question is asserted.

**Judgment Under Review / Relief:** After an earlier appellate remand and a bench trial, the district court determined that the college qualified for Sellinger Program assistance. The Fourth Circuit affirmed. Maryland seeks to sustain its refusal on establishment grounds; the college seeks equal eligibility and the grant treatment required by the statutory program, subject to its ordinary conditions.

**Material Record:** Columbia Union is affiliated with the Seventh-day Adventist Church. Its application sought $806,079 for mathematics, computer science, clinical laboratory science, respiratory care and nursing. Sellinger grants could not fund sectarian purposes; theological enrollment was excluded from the allocation measure, audit authority existed, and annual affidavits identified intended and actual expenditures. The trial court found secular education to be the primary goal of the relevant ordinary courses and found religious references too isolated to establish pervasive religious teaching. Religious governance, hiring preferences and a worship obligation affecting a minority of students remained part of the record. No Sellinger funds had yet been disbursed to the college.

**Threshold and Vehicle Matters:** The institution's denied benefit supplies a concrete injury. The stipulated eligibility criteria, underlying trial findings and constitutional conclusions require their different standards of review. The earlier litigation concerning this institution is part of the same controversy, not a separate award or a second current case. A receipt-based accounting system cannot be presumed both ineffective and effective without examining its actual legal design and the supported program findings. No issue concerning damages, clergy formation funding or an unrestricted operating grant is presented.

#### B. LAW ENTERING THE CASE

**Current Through:** June 28, 2002 baseline, supplemented by the relevant decided events below through March 24, 2003. Only decisions effective before this event enter its law; same-date events do not govern one another.

* **Earlier Same-Term Decisions:** Steele (March 3, 2003) requires financed-function review despite the municipal-project-bond form, preserving lawful secular financing and correcting proved devotional financing on supported uses and practical restrictions. Commack (March 24, 2003) permits civil review of truthful attribution, actual practices and material factual misrepresentation but forbids religious orthodoxy as a civil rule or compulsory religious-office permission. Those holdings bear on the funded work and administration of safeguards here; neither decides this college’s entitlement or makes its secular course labels conclusive.

**Financed Function:** Lemon remains governing as modified by Agostini: purpose, effects and entanglement are evidence within one inquiry, not independent or fallback prongs. A controlling specific prohibition or distinct doctrine governs when it resolves the conduct; an established permissible relationship defeats the objection on that ground; otherwise a legally sufficient unresolved claim receives ordinary attribution-and-function review. In financing, identify what the government purchases or performs rather than attribute all of the recipient’s religion to it. Agostini, Mitchell, Jackson, Strout and Zelman distinguish a genuinely separable secular service from a public purchase of integrated devotional work. Neutral eligibility is not sufficient to validate the latter. Religious identity, possible diversion and incidental institutional savings are not sufficient to establish it. Effective controls and operational separation matter; neither wholly separate staff nor separate corporate governance is categorically required.

**Equal Eligibility:** K.D.M. prohibits a religious-site disability in consideration for an independently controlled secular service where comparable secular locations are eligible. Good News Club and Rosenberger protect private expression in their actual forums; a direct higher-education grant is not automatically the same kind of expressive forum. Lukumi supplies the justification inquiry for an express religious disability. Constitutional permission to fund a service and a statutory or constitutional entitlement to equal consideration remain distinct.

**Earlier Higher-Education Authority:** Roemer concerns the same Maryland program, while Hunt and Tilton address restricted institutional assistance. Their supported separability principles remain relevant; any institution-wide presumption must be read consistently with the later actual-function holdings. The open issue is whether the identified use and safeguards satisfy that rule and whether the State has a valid basis for excluding this otherwise eligible applicant.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Maryland:** Direct cash assistance can support religious operations despite a secular course label, and the college's governance, religious teaching and personnel policies bear on separation. Compliance affidavits cannot by themselves convert integrated religious instruction into secular work. The State argues that the Establishment Clause requires its refusal.

**College:** It satisfies the neutral statutory criteria, seeks funding for specified secular programs, accepts binding use restrictions and accounting, and obtained favorable findings after trial. The State principally offered institution-wide religious evidence rather than proof that the specified programs perform devotional work. Denying eligibility on that basis treats religious institutions differently without the claimed constitutional necessity.

**Material Distinction:** No disbursement means no completed diversion can yet be proved; it does not make a pre-enforcement challenge categorically impossible. Supported evidence that a proposed funded activity is inseparably devotional can establish its constitutional character before payment. Conversely, religious governance alone cannot substitute for that evidence.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

The Court may affirm eligible secular funding on the supported findings and effective restrictions, vacate for an actual-function inquiry if the trial findings answer only an institution-wide label, or sustain refusal if the requested grant actually purchases inseparable devotional work. A decision cannot presume the proposed payment unrestricted when the governing law and application specify otherwise. Any relief must preserve audit, accounting and lawful grant conditions and distinguish prospective eligibility from a fixed treasury award.

**Plausible Holding Scope:** The case permits a rule protecting equal consideration of independently identifiable secular programs while retaining the categorical bar on actual devotional financing. It does not require financing ministerial training, invalidate every state restriction on devotional use or constitutionalize unconditional operating subsidies.

### 3. Lower proceedings, factual findings and competing accounts

In 1998, the Fourth Circuit vacated summary judgment for the State. Motz, joined by Butzner, held that the college's speech interest was impaired by exclusion from this broad program on the asserted religious-viewpoint ground and that Maryland relied on Establishment Clause necessity to justify it. The court treated Roemer as directly applicable, but found the existing record and permissible inferences inadequate for the institution-wide determination. Cross-motions and the parties' agreement that no material fact was disputed did not bind the court to select one side. It required examination of actual practices as well as written institutional materials.

Wilkinson dissented from that remand. He agreed that exclusion on religious viewpoint implicated speech and understood the then-binding higher-education precedents to require refusal if the institution was pervasively sectarian. He regarded the developed documentary record and undisputed facts as sufficient for that conclusion and would affirm. He also objected that increasingly detailed inquiries into institutional religious intensity would pressure colleges to alter their religious character and entangle administrators in religious affairs. His discussion of a different neutrality rule was an argument for doctrinal change, not the governing ground on which he would have affirmed.

After extensive discovery and a bench trial, the district court found the college eligible. The decisive reported findings are more specific than the ultimate institutional classification: secular education was the primary goal; the State's evidence did not show that ordinary liberal-arts courses were taught with religious indoctrination as their primary objective; and religious references in secular-course syllabi were isolated and scattered, rather than pervasive. The trial court also found church dominance of institutional affairs and express religious preferences in faculty hiring and student admission. Mandatory worship applied to a minority of students. It did not find the college irreligious or every aspect of its operation secular.

The 2001 appellate judgment affirmed. Wilkinson's opinion, joined by Williams, gave two grounds. First, it read intervening aid cases to emphasize neutral eligibility, absence of actual diversion, safeguards and the higher-education setting rather than an institution-wide classification. Second, it independently sustained the trial findings under Roemer, Hunt and Tilton as not clearly erroneous. Motz concurred in the judgment solely on the second ground, declining a broader doctrinal holding because directly applicable precedent should continue to control the lower court until this Court changes it. The judgment therefore does not depend solely on the first rationale.

The reviewed facts and the lower courts' legal characterizations must be separated. No funds had been paid, so no completed diversion was established. The majority's further suggestion that no prepayment challenge could succeed is a legal inference to test under this Court's actual-function doctrine. Conversely, the existence of religious governance, worship and hiring preferences cannot silently be substituted for proof that the requested secular instruction is an inseparable devotional activity. Ordinary review of historical trial findings for clear error remains distinct from review of constitutional meaning and the legal sufficiency of the findings under the governing standard.

## Amazon.com, Inc. v. Barnesandnoble.com, Inc.

### Event, question, judgment, and available relief

The reviewed judgment is **239 F.3d 1343 (Fed. Cir. 2001)**, Federal Circuit No. 00-1109, vacating the Western District of Washington's preliminary injunction and remanding. The question is whether the patent-validity presumption requires clear-and-convincing proof of invalidity to resist preliminary relief, or whether a supported substantial validity question that the applicant cannot show lacks substantial merit defeats the applicant's likelihood showing. The assigned question does not seek final invalidation, patent eligibility, or a final infringement judgment.

Amazon seeks restoration of preliminary protection for Patent No. 5,960,411. Barnesandnoble.com seeks preservation of vacatur and continued litigation without the injunction. The Federal Circuit reviewed the injunction through 28 U.S.C. §1292(c)(1). The present assigned merits review concerns that legal dispute and the supported preliminary record. Restoring an injunction would still require satisfaction of the applicable equitable prerequisites; rejecting the injunction does not cancel the patent.

The patent issued September 28, 1999; the action began October 21, 1999. The supplied intake dates the injunction December 1, 1999, effective December 4 upon the undertaking. The appellate decision is February 14, 2001. No later settlement, trial, reexamination, or enforcement development is assumed.

### Material record and lower-court reasoning

The patent concerns single-action ordering in a client/server environment. Amazon asserted claims 1–3, 5–12, 14–17, and 21–24. The four independent claims are 1, 6, 9, and 11. Their limitations differ; claim 11 does not require the purchaser identifier transmitted with each request that claims 1, 6, and 9 require. Claims 1, 2, and 11 exclude the pertinent shopping-cart model; claims 6 and 9 include distinct system components. No claim may be treated as interchangeable with another.

The appellate court construed the single action as occurring after a display of identifying information, without an intervening action at that point. It did not require the first display, every display, or a purchaser's subjective decision to buy. The Express Lane product page offered purchase by one click even though the shopper might previously have clicked from a menu page. On that construction, the court sustained Amazon's showing of likely literal infringement, including the independent claims, while withholding any final infringement determination. It rejected the alternative contention that automatic Express Lane ordering was the excluded shopping-cart model and construed fulfillment in the system claims as the server's order-fulfillment software, rather than physical packing and shipping. Equivalents were unnecessary to the preliminary infringement conclusion.

### Findings, testimony, and competing characterizations

The state evidentiary hearing occurred in 1995. The state habeas trial court credited three jurors and the court clerk who described repeated actual sleep during prosecution questioning and the introduction of evidence. The accepted historical finding is that counsel dozed and actually fell asleep during portions of the trial on the merits, particularly the guilt phase while the State's sole prosecutor questioned witnesses and presented evidence. The Court of Criminal Appeals accepted the supported factual findings but rejected the recommended legal entitlement to presumed-prejudice relief under Strickland.

The testimony is not a judicially fixed sleep log. One juror recalled roughly two to five episodes; another recalled five to ten episodes and an episode of approximately ten minutes. The clerk described numerous incidents and long periods, including an episode of approximately ten minutes. The foreman's shorter-duration account and the other accounts were not identical. The trial judge and prosecutor said they did not observe sleep, and counsel described a habit of closing his eyes while concentrating. The state court nevertheless credited the neutral witnesses as establishing actual sleeping. It did not fix the precise witness, evidence, duration, depth, or percentage for every episode.

The en banc majority treated the credited findings and accounts together as repeated unconsciousness during not insubstantial adverse evidence. It held that an unconscious lawyer cannot listen, object, consult, or exercise judgment, and that functional absence at a critical stage is distinct from imperfect performance. It did not announce presumed prejudice for every momentary doze. It rejected requiring an outcome-changing missed act to prove the absence category and rejected a necessary government-causation condition for counsel's absence.

Higginbotham, joined by King, W. His account treats the ability to receive and respond to adverse evidence as integral to both the guilt defense and a capital penalty presentation. His further observations about the message conveyed to jurors are his reasoning, not an additional state factual finding.

Jolly, joined by Smith, dissented. He stressed the confession, other affirmative representation, the delayed assertion, and absence of demonstrated actual prejudice, and would return other claims to the district court. Barksdale, joined by Jones, Smith, and Garza, separately disputed the new-rule classification, the factual predicate for constructive absence, and the availability of an exception. His principal distinction is between the accepted sleep finding and the legal or inferential characterization of substantial unconscious absence. The substantial-portions language appears in the state court's recommended legal conclusion; it must not silently be treated as a separately quantified historical finding.

The lengthy dissent described counsel's motions, cross-examination, objections, defense presentation, efforts to exclude the confession, and arguments. Those acts support the State's claim that the proceeding retained meaningful advocacy overall. They do not establish that counsel was awake in every intervening interval; the majority noted testimony that he sometimes responded after being awakened. Nor does silence in a transcript prove sleep. The reported total of 12 hours and 51 minutes before the jury over six days is not a measurement of sleeping time; the dissent separately identifies three days of guilt-phase evidence and seven days of voir dire.

Barksdale also sought an adverse inference or further hearing from reports that Burdine nudged counsel and from Burdine's failure to testify at the state hearing.
