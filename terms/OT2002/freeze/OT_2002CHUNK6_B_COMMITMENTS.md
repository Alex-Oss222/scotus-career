## Johnson & Johnston Associates Inc. v. R.E. Service Co., Inc.

### Proposed result and sufficient rule

All eight Associates would affirm the Federal Circuit's judgment removing infringement under the asserted patent, willfulness, damages, attorney fees and expenses. They would adopt a rule confined to an identifiable substitute expressly disclosed as an alternative to the claimed component: when the specification affirmatively identifies that substitute but the issued claims select the other component and omit the substitute, the patentee cannot recover the disclosed substitute under the doctrine of equivalents in that patent. The legal boundary is objective; neither subjective intent to surrender nor a competitor's individualized reliance is required.

Here the claims select aluminum, the specification expressly identifies stainless steel as an alternative substrate, and the accused SC2 and SC3 products use steel. Literal noninfringement is established. RES's acceptance of factual equivalence does not concede the legal availability of equivalents. This is a legal ground for the existing noninfringement result and elimination of its dependent awards, not a new weighing of technical evidence.

The rule is patent-specific. A continuation's existence neither defeats the issued patent's public boundary nor establishes validity or infringement under the continuation. The approved ground does not make all foreseeable substitutes unavailable and does not infer an amendment, deceptive drafting or failure of technical equivalence. Section 112's obligation to disclose the invention and its separate obligation to identify the claimed subject matter perform different functions. The alternative's disclosure remains useful technical information even though the asserted patent does not reserve that disclosed option through its claims.

### Competing lawful paths and limiting grounds

**Express-alternative boundary.** Markman and Vitronics require interpretation of the complete public instrument; Warner-Jenkinson retains element-specific equivalents subject to legal claim limits. The specification's clear contrast between the claimed aluminum and the expressly identified substitute provides stronger public notice than literal omission alone. Miller v. Bridgeport Brass and Mahn v. Harwood supply the earlier claim-and-disclosure principle in their reissue settings; their reissue results are not relabeled as equivalents holdings. Maxwell supplies a persuasive application of that principle. The sufficient rule requires neither proof that the disclosed alternative is independently inventive nor an inquiry into the drafter's private intention.

The strongest objection is that equivalents always reaches beyond literal claims, informative disclosure should not be penalized, and Graver Tank allowed an expressly discussed substitute. The answer must be more precise than saying claims control. Graver Tank's surviving narrower claims supported equivalents after the broader claims encompassing the accused material had been invalidated; the patent had originally claimed that subject matter. Its holding protects contextual equivalents and does not establish that an alternative expressly omitted from every issued claim must remain recoverable. Both the original broad claims and the surviving narrow claims matter to that distinction. The present ground does not depend on Dyk's conjecture that the older Court considered dedication unpreserved.

**Contextual equivalents without a disclosure bar.** The competing route would reverse the categorical dedication ground and remand for remaining preserved issues. It relies on Graver Tank's treatment of an insubstantial substitution, the separate work performed by the equivalence inquiry, and the disclosure obligation; a rule confined to a distinct unclaimed invention would preserve the verdict's possible basis. Its strongest difficulty is that it makes a specifically disclosed but unclaimed choice enforceable through the issued patent despite the public instrument's express distinction. Requiring separate patentability would add a different inquiry without support in the claim-definition requirement. Accepted equivalence cannot itself answer that legal objection. Removing the bar, if selected, would not automatically reinstate every enhancement or fee predicate.

Festo does not supply an amendment estoppel here: its actual-narrowing trigger is absent from the supported record. Its rejection of an irrebuttable bar to all equivalents after every qualifying amendment also does not compel the different conclusion that an expressly identified, wholly unclaimed alternative is within this patent's equivalents protection. Its actual-narrowing rule, separate purpose burden and rebuttable scope presumption remain intact. Rader's general foreseeability route would reach further than this record and would introduce a different technical inquiry; no Associate commits to it.

## Cook County v. United States ex rel. Chandler

### Proposed result and sufficient rule

All eight Associates would affirm the Seventh Circuit's rejection of the County's categorical exclusion and permit the action to continue. A county operating as a municipal corporation is a person subject to the FCA's civil false-claims liability in a private-relator action. The change from double to treble damages did not withdraw counties from that class.

The 1863 Act's broad civil person language, together with the established legal capacity of municipal corporations, supports original coverage. Cowles v. Mercer County, 7 Wall. 118, identifies an Illinois county's corporate character under the quoted 1845 law and sustains federal suit on its obligations. Cowles is evidence of the legal corporate setting and a distinct jurisdictional holding; it did not decide FCA coverage. Reading the FCA's person category to include counties is the statutory conclusion proposed here, not a fact assumed from Cowles. The later remedy change left the defendant category in place.

The multiplier has punitive features and can exceed the Government's immediate out-of-pocket loss. It also serves compensatory and enforcement functions in a scheme involving undetected fraud, delayed recovery and a relator's share of proceeds. Neither exclusively punitive nor exclusively compensatory accurately states the entire remedy. These functions answer the County's attempt to infer categorical noncoverage from the multiplier; they do not establish any award in this case. Chandler's false-report and grant-compliance allegations remain allegations subject to the statute's actual elements and defenses.

### Competing lawful paths

**Coverage with the enacted remedy.** The corporate-person reading preserves the original defendant class while giving effect to the remedial amendment. Vermont Agency's affirmative-authorization rule concerns a sovereign State, not every local corporation. Its reservation of municipalities is resolved here at the County's status. City of Newport v. Fact Concerts establishes municipal protection from punitive damages under section 1983 in its statutory and common-law setting; it does not create a general immunity from every federal statute's multiplied recovery. The FCA prescribed enhanced recovery from its original enactment, and a later increase supplies no sound implied removal of an included class.

The strongest objection is that trebling and fixed penalties punish taxpayers, while municipal punitive-damages immunity has a substantial common-law foundation. That policy has force, but importing it as complete noncoverage would change the defendant language rather than construe the actual remedy. The retained compensatory functions also make the asserted analogy incomplete. The conclusion does not rest on consent inferred from federal grant receipt.

**County exclusion or a municipal remedy limitation.** The competing construction would extend Newport's municipal protection and Vermont Agency's punitive-remedy concern to exclude this action, or separate a compensatory county action from the enhanced statutory remedy. Its principal obstacle is the broad civil class and the absence of a textual withdrawal or municipal substitute remedy. A remedy-only adjustment would not answer the County's categorical person argument on its chosen terms and would require a supported statutory basis for severing the mandated remedial structure. The current record supports neither an arm-of-the-State finding nor an independently contested particular award. Those routes cannot be supplied by labeling a county governmental.

Standing is already supplied by Vermont Agency's partial assignment of the Government's concrete financial claim, subject to ordinary litigation-stage proof. Riley sustains the actual bounded relator mechanism after declination. Those holdings do not establish the County's liability; the present statutory construction performs that separate work. No Associate uses an Article II disagreement with Riley to decide an unpresented constitutional challenge here.

If discussion invokes the statutory cooperation reduction, it must retain its full conditions: disclosure of all known violation information to responsible federal officials within thirty days of first obtaining it; full cooperation; disclosure before the specified prosecution or civil or administrative action begins; and absence of actual knowledge of an investigation. Upon the required findings, the court may assess no less than twice damages; neither entitlement nor satisfaction of those conditions is found here. The after-declination relator share ordinarily is twenty-five to thirty percent of proceeds under section 3730(d)(2), with the statute's other restrictions and expenses separate. The coverage ruling requires no award calculation.

## Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Ayers

### Components, record and alternative paths

The two questions must be committed separately. All six employees have manifested asbestosis. The trial court prohibited an award for cancer or increased risk of cancer because no claimant was shown reasonably certain to develop cancer. The cancer evidence could support the genuineness of claimed fear, and the challenged charge permitted compensation for reasonable fear related to proven physical injury. Norfolk's rejected request demanded both actual likelihood of developing cancer and physical manifestations of the fear. The verdicts were general; no amount attributable to fear can be recovered from them. This does not establish a separate preserved claimant-specific sufficiency challenge.

**Present-fear eligibility and affirmance path.** Emotional injury genuinely and seriously experienced as a consequence of proved occupational disease can be part of ordinary pain-and-suffering damages without proof that the feared cancer probably will occur or that the fear produces a new physical condition. Urie recognizes occupational disease as actual injury. Gottshall and Buckley restrict independent emotional claims and symptomless future-risk exposure; manifested asbestosis presents the distinct physical-injury setting they preserved. Current fear is a different harm from future cancer. The existing charge confined recovery to reasonable fear connected to proved injury and expressly prevented future-disease compensation. The rejected conjunctive request would deny that lawful category. On the categorical issues supplied, affirmance does not decide that diagnosis alone proves serious distress or make an individual severity finding.

The strongest objection is that a physical diagnosis might become a nominal vehicle for substantial compensation for ordinary anxiety over a different possible disease. The answer is proof of actual substantial distress connected to the disease, ordinary evidentiary scrutiny and the charge's exclusion of future cancer, not a requirement that cancer be more likely than not. The charge is evaluated against the preserved categorical demand and the complete supplied directions, not against a newly assumed evidentiary claim. No psychiatric-treatment or expert-testimony prerequisite is adopted.

**Eligibility with a more demanding instruction and remand path.** A competing route accepts that future cancer need not be probable but requires the jury expressly to distinguish serious, substantial emotional harm from the ordinary apprehension anyone aware of a risk may experience. The instruction's entitlement for reasonable fear does not expressly make that distinction. Gottshall's insistence on a compensable-injury boundary independent of negligence and causation, and Buckley's rejection of future-risk contact as a shortcut, support this additional legal insistence. On that view the challenge to the compensable category is sufficient to require a corrected charge, even though Norfolk's proposed conjunctive instruction itself goes too far. This is an objection to the affirmative entitlement instruction, not an invented finding that any employee's proof failed.

The strongest objections are the traditional availability of emotional consequences of bodily injury and the absence of the requested serious-distress formulation. This route must explain why the categorical charge under review remains materially incomplete rather than treating the court's rejection of an erroneous requested instruction as automatic reversible error. The commitments selecting it below rest on the charge's express entitlement for reasonable fear without an independent substantial-injury limitation. They do not demand proof of probable cancer, mandatory psychiatric care or a particular physical manifestation. The resulting new damages trial would follow the asserted instructional defect, not the mere absence of separate figures in the verdict.

**Indivisible injury and full liability.** A negligent railroad whose workplace exposure contributes to one indivisible occupational injury is liable for that injury's damages under section 51; the amount is not reduced merely by assigning exposure shares to other employers. Section 51's in-whole-or-in-part language and the common-law treatment of indivisible harm support this result. Multiple contributors do not by themselves prove divisible injuries. Demonstrably separable harm must be attributed on its actual proof; negligence and causal contribution remain necessary.

The strongest competing route would require allocation to match Norfolk's responsibility, especially for Butler's three months of railroad exposure compared with thirty-three years elsewhere. The disparity makes causation and genuine divisibility material, but does not provide a rational damage division by itself or change one indivisible lung injury into several injuries. Section 53 expressly addresses the employee's own contributory negligence rather than exposure shares among other wrongdoers. Its safety-statute exception remains: employee contributory negligence cannot diminish recovery when a railroad violation of an employee-safety statute contributed to injury or death. No such predicate is established here. The existing smoking and settlement reductions remain distinct from the proposed nonrailroad exposure allocation. Any contribution or indemnity remedy follows its own governing law; this ground creates no universal federal contribution action.

## Woodford v. Garceau

### Competing lawful paths and effects on the existing judgment

**Application-specific pendency.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer would hold that the ordinary chapter 153 merits amendments govern because no application actually submitting habeas claims for merits adjudication preceded April 24, 1996. A qualifying preapplication counsel and stay proceeding is genuine and lawful under McFarland, but its distinct preparatory object does not freeze the review law for a later merits application. Lindh and Carriger involved applications already asking the court to adjudicate habeas claims. Their provisions and limits remain controlling in that category.

Garceau requested counsel and a stay on May 12, 1995. Counsel was appointed and the stay extended on June 26; the State later challenged the missing local issue specification. The cured specification identified constitutional issues that might be presented in a later petition. The court set the petition deadline on October 13. The actual merits application was filed July 2, 1996. The pre-enactment specification is substantive preparatory work; it is not accurately described as no issues having been identified. Its prospective object supplies the distinction without a caption-only test or a finding of default.

This route vacates the Ninth Circuit's existing judgment granting habeas relief and remands for review under the applicable AEDPA standards. Early, Visciotti and Lockyer require examination of the actual state adjudication and complete supported record, while keeping the statutory defect distinct from the constitutional merits. Their current explanations do not create new substantive constitutional law at the earlier state adjudication. No ultimate merits result follows from selecting the review law.

The strongest contrary argument is that McFarland recognizes an actual habeas proceeding and Lindh's chapter comparison concerns pending cases, so a federal court exercising stay and appointment authority already has the case. The response is the statutory object: preparation and preservation can be pending while the merits request regulated by amended section 2254 has not yet been submitted. McFarland's rule gives useful effect to statutory representation and discretionary protection without determining all later temporal rules. No reliance on an appointed lawyer or preparation deadline can change the substantive object of the filed papers.

**Proceeding-wide pendency.** Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg would reject the State's temporal challenge and retain former-law treatment. A qualifying McFarland proceeding had begun, involved actual judicial supervision and a stay, and continued through preparation into the application. Lindh's statutory contrast between ordinary chapter 153 and expressly pending chapter 154 cases supports treating that ongoing federal habeas undertaking as pending at enactment, rather than restarting its temporal identity when the final pleading arrives. The issue specification strengthens the conclusion that the proceeding was concrete without needing to mischaracterize it as a merits application.

The strongest objection is that the amendments govern merits applications and the earlier decisions deliberately distinguish preparation from submitted claims. These three accept the distinction for the immediate relief available but regard it as insufficient to divide the same qualifying case for the ordinary chapter 153 transition. Their rule does not grant a constitutional right to preserve all earlier law, apply to an unrelated request for assistance, or govern every AEDPA provision. It rests on the statutory inference and the authorized ongoing proceeding.

On this route the existing Ninth Circuit writ judgment remains unaffected by the rejected temporal challenge. The State additionally requested a merits ruling, but the supplied neutral record identifies neither the specific ground nor the necessary merits record; the granted question is the transition question. These Justices would not vacate an existing grant of relief merely because deciding the temporal issue supplies no new merits entitlement. They would affirm against the temporal challenge and decline the additional unprovided merits request at this review scope. That disposition preserves the existing appellate relief, rather than replacing it with an instruction to conduct an initial undecided application.

Both routes preserve McFarland's appointment and preparatory stay authority, Lindh and Carriger's separately specified temporal coverage, chapter 154 qualification requirements and other provisions with separate temporal commands. Capital status alone establishes no chapter 154 qualification. Clay supplies contextual treatment of its different finality trigger, not a universal definition of every temporal term. Abdur'Rahman's separation of procedural and merits objects informs the comparison without deciding when this first merits application became pending.
