## 141. Entergy Louisiana, Inc. v. Louisiana Public Service Commission

### Historical evidence and premise comparison

The official report expressly identifies a unanimous Court, with Thomas writing. Thus Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer historically joined both reversal and the reported rationale. The frozen independent commitments match that alignment for reasons grounded in Nantahala and Mississippi Power, rather than because Thomas historically authored the opinion.

The operative predicate remains an actual FERC tariff allocation and a retail disallowance that defeats its effect. The simulated actual-conflict rules in Freightliner, Geier, and Sprietsma require the operative federal predicate; they do not displace the earlier electricity-allocation decisions. The official opinion confirms that the agreement determines both who makes the classification and how that decision is made, and that MSS-1 is an automatic adjustment clause permitted by 16 U.S.C. §824d(f). These details confirm the packet's distinction between an operative allocation and bare federal regulatory silence. They do not create a new ground or enlarge the frozen commitments to every private choice allowed by federal law.

The full record account also prevents two inaccurate inferences. FERC found a violation under the earlier agreement, denied a refund on the grounds it stated, and amended the agreement on August 5, 1997. That earlier violation does not establish a violation under the amended agreement. The challenged state order did not rest on such a violation finding. Although the prudence analysis concerned treatment after August 5, footnote 4 explains that the actual disallowed MSS-1 payment figures were made in 1996 and used to calculate 1997–1998 retail rates. Public text should describe the challenged cost treatment, not incorrectly assert that every disallowed payment was made after August 5.

### Final common commitments

All eight Associates reach the preserved question, reverse the Louisiana Supreme Court, and support conforming further proceedings. All eight directly support the following sufficient ground and limits:

1. A state retail-rate authority must give binding effect to interstate costs allocated by an operative FERC tariff. It cannot trap those costs by treating them as unavailable for recovery on a prudence ground that substitutes a different allocation. Nantahala supplies the allocation and cost-trapping rule; Mississippi Power applies it to state prudence review without requiring FERC first to adjudicate the exact prudence objection.
2. Discretion assigned by the federal arrangement does not alone remove that binding effect. The federal tariff's method remains operative when it assigns the classification decision to the utility's committee under specified criteria. Here the disallowance rejects the allocated cost consequences of that classification. State review cannot become independent merely by describing its target as an antecedent managerial choice.
3. The result rests on that actual conflict. Federal regulation's existence, agency silence, general uniformity, or presumed approval of every managerial decision would not suffice. Genuinely independent retail regulation and prudence inquiries that leave the binding allocation effective retain their own scope.
4. An appellate tariff-violation theory cannot save this order, because the order contains no such finding and instead rests on the conflicting prudence rationale. The Court does not certify universal tariff compliance. Whether FERC has exclusive initial authority over a distinct tariff-compliance controversy is outside the basis of this order and remains undecided. No retail rate, refund, or damages amount is fixed.

The strongest affirmance argument separates the utility's optional classification from the mandatory allocation formula and assigns the cost of imprudence to shareholders. Its defect is practical: the state remedy defeats the federally allocated costs just as the earlier allocation cases forbid. Nantahala's assumed alternative-source-purchase reservation does not establish an exception for every discretionary choice. The frozen common ground answers this objection without abolishing all state prudence review.

### Final component rules and remedies

**A — Congressional authorization.** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer join a holding that section 144 does not exempt the challenged pricing and pooling rules from Commerce Clause review. Congress may authorize a state interstate burden, but the permission must clearly cover that burden. Section 144 preserves California's authority regarding the percentage of milk solids or solids not fat in fluid milk sold at retail or marketed in California, and labeling concerning those characteristics. Its introductory protection against direct or indirect limits remains tied to those listed subjects. The integrated economic relationship among the State's laws does not add pricing and pooling to that enacted scope. South-Central Timber supplies the clear-authorization rule; New York and Pic-A-State preserve Congress's power to provide real authorization.

**C — Commerce remedy.** Those seven Associates vacate the Commerce dismissal and remand for ordinary adjudication. They do not find discrimination, reject a developed justification, order refunds, or redesign the milk program. West Lynn Creamery requires attention to the actual assessment/benefit relationship if established; Tracy preserves the need for a sound practical comparison. The categorical authorization ground fails before the unresolved merits can be adjudicated.

**P — Article IV.** All eight Associates vacate the dismissal of the individuals' claims that rested solely on absence of express residence or citizenship language. Chalker establishes that the constitutional inquiry cannot stop at the regulation's face. Whether it reaches only classifications functioning as proxies for citizenship or every classification with a discriminatory practical effect need not be decided to reject that categorical ground, and is not resolved here. The individuals must establish their own covered discrimination in a protected pursuit; if they do, Lunding's substantial independent reason and close relationship requirements govern justification at their established scope. This is not an automatic finding that every cross-border economic disadvantage is citizenship discrimination. The corporations' separate Article IV exclusion was not challenged and is not reopened. The merits, appropriate comparison, justification, and lawful relief remain for remand.

The strongest integrated-program alternative relies on the saving provision's broad introductory language and the economic interaction of the milk laws. It fails because that language does not erase the specified subjects. The strongest facial-neutrality alternative correctly observes that effects on goods and firms need not equal discrimination against citizens, but incorrectly treats the absence of citizenship words as dispositive. The remand preserves that genuine merits distinction without sustaining categorical dismissal.
