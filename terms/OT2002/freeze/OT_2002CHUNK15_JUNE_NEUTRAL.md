## Amazon.com, Inc. v. Barnesandnoble.com, Inc.

### Event, question, judgment, and available relief

The reviewed judgment is **239 F.3d 1343 (Fed. Cir. 2001)**, Federal Circuit No. 00-1109, vacating the Western District of Washington's preliminary injunction and remanding. The question is whether the patent-validity presumption requires clear-and-convincing proof of invalidity to resist preliminary relief, or whether a supported substantial validity question that the applicant cannot show lacks substantial merit defeats the applicant's likelihood showing. The assigned question does not seek final invalidation, patent eligibility, or a final infringement judgment.

Amazon seeks restoration of preliminary protection for Patent No. 5,960,411. Barnesandnoble.com seeks preservation of vacatur and continued litigation without the injunction. The Federal Circuit reviewed the injunction through 28 U.S.C. §1292(c)(1). The present assigned merits review concerns that legal dispute and the supported preliminary record. Restoring an injunction would still require satisfaction of the applicable equitable prerequisites; rejecting the injunction does not cancel the patent.

The patent issued September 28, 1999; the action began October 21, 1999. The supplied intake dates the injunction December 1, 1999, effective December 4 upon the undertaking. The appellate decision is February 14, 2001. No later settlement, trial, reexamination, or enforcement development is assumed.

### Material record and lower-court reasoning

The patent concerns single-action ordering in a client/server environment. Amazon asserted claims 1–3, 5–12, 14–17, and 21–24. The four independent claims are 1, 6, 9, and 11. Their limitations differ; claim 11 does not require the purchaser identifier transmitted with each request that claims 1, 6, and 9 require. Claims 1, 2, and 11 exclude the pertinent shopping-cart model; claims 6 and 9 include distinct system components. No claim may be treated as interchangeable with another.

The appellate court construed the single action as occurring after a display of identifying information, without an intervening action at that point. It did not require the first display, every display, or a purchaser's subjective decision to buy. The Express Lane product page offered purchase by one click even though the shopper might previously have clicked from a menu page. On that construction, the court sustained Amazon's showing of likely literal infringement, including the independent claims, while withholding any final infringement determination. It rejected the alternative contention that automatic Express Lane ordering was the excluded shopping-cart model and construed fulfillment in the system claims as the server's order-fulfillment software, rather than physical packing and shipping. Equivalents were unnecessary to the preliminary infringement conclusion.

The court nevertheless found a substantial validity challenge. The principal references perform different work:

The appellate court treated what a reference teaches as factual and reviewed that determination for clear error, while addressing claim construction and the injunction standard as legal questions. It found that the district court had used materially inconsistent claim breadth in examining the accused process and the references. It also rejected treating the expert's personal failure to conceive the modification as dispositive of what the hypothetical ordinarily skilled artisan would have understood. The cited copying evidence required a demonstrated connection to the actual claims. The preliminary record did not establish that commercial success or reduced shopping-cart abandonment resulted from the claimed feature.

The lower court had considered irreparable harm, hardships, and the public interest. The Federal Circuit's failure-of-likelihood ground independently supported vacatur. Its recital of a circuit presumption of irreparable patent harm is background circuit doctrine; the present question does not automatically adopt that presumption as Supreme Court law or decide it unnecessary to the selected disposition.

### Entering law and operative distinctions

Section 282 preserves the validity of each claim independently and assigns the invalidity burden to the challenger. Its text does not itself recite the clear-and-convincing phrase. The dispute takes the ordinary final invalidity burden as its premise; it asks what showing is necessary to resist provisional relief, not whether that final burden should be abolished. Section 283 permits injunctions according to equitable principles on reasonable terms; it does not command preliminary exclusion whenever a patent issues.

Section 102 and §103 retain the governing pre-1999 patentability rules relevant to this patent. The 1995 text is supplied for those provisions. Section 103(a) concerns the subject matter as a whole, the relevant time, and the hypothetical ordinarily skilled person; neither the actual expert's ingenuity nor an unreasoned combination of missing limitations settles obviousness. The later text of §102(e) concerning published applications and the 1999 expansion of §103(c) are not mechanically applied to this already-issued patent. ### Strongest supported alternatives

**Preserve vacatur.** A supported, substantial validity challenge can defeat the applicant's forecast of likely success even when it would not yet sustain a final invalidity judgment. The forecast must account for the burdens and presumptions at trial. Amazon's contrary argument is strongest when a substantial-question formula becomes free-floating doubt or shifts the ultimate statutory burden to the patentee. A lawful version must therefore require a concrete claim-specific defense, retain the final burden, and ask whether the applicant has answered the substantial challenge.

**Require a stronger interim invalidity showing or remand for a properly calibrated forecast.** Congress's claim-specific presumption and final burden are relevant to the likelihood of success; denying relief merely because some possibility of invalidity exists may undervalue them. The strongest response is that the applicant seeks extraordinary provisional relief and must demonstrate likely entitlement without first demanding a complete trial of the defense. A clear-and-convincing requirement for resistance to provisional relief must explain how the applicant's separate burden survives. It would not alone justify immediate restoration without the other equitable prerequisites.

**Apply the substantial-question standard but correct its application.** If a particular relied-on reference cannot support a material limitation, the court must distinguish unsupported anticipation from a supported obviousness combination and identify the claim on which an injunction could rest. The same claim must be both likely infringed and likely to survive the actual challenge. This may justify remand if a legal error infects the preliminary assessment; it does not authorize new appellate technical findings or final invalidation.

## Burdine v. Johnson (Cockrell v. Burdine)

### Event, question, judgment, and available relief

The reviewed judgment is **262 F.3d 336 (5th Cir. 2001) (en banc)**, affirming the federal grant of habeas relief on the sleeping-counsel claim. The reported en banc disposition vacated the capital conviction and allowed the State to retry Burdine. Its underlying district judgment granted conditional new-trial relief; the intake identifies a 120-day condition in the original order. The assigned review is of the merits judgment on the counsel claim. It does not adjudicate a separate enforcement controversy, revive an expired historical deadline, establish current custody events, or import a later stay.

The two questions are whether the established sleeping episodes constitute critical-stage denial of counsel for which prejudice is presumed, and whether the requested rule was dictated at 1987 finality or is barred on collateral review by Teague. The State seeks reversal; Burdine seeks preservation of new-trial relief. If the presumption ground fails, other properly pending habeas claims remain for adjudication. Reversal on this claim alone does not resolve all those claims or itself direct an execution.

The trial occurred in January 1984. Direct review became final in 1987. Cronic and Strickland preceded finality. The federal application was filed in **April 1995**, before AEDPA. The en banc opinion issued August 13, 2001, revised August 29. No historical post-divergence Supreme Court treatment of this matter enters the packet.

### Findings, testimony, and competing characterizations

The state evidentiary hearing occurred in 1995. The state habeas trial court credited three jurors and the court clerk who described repeated actual sleep during prosecution questioning and the introduction of evidence. The accepted historical finding is that counsel dozed and actually fell asleep during portions of the trial on the merits, particularly the guilt phase while the State's sole prosecutor questioned witnesses and presented evidence. The Court of Criminal Appeals accepted the supported factual findings but rejected the recommended legal entitlement to presumed-prejudice relief under Strickland.

The testimony is not a judicially fixed sleep log. One juror recalled roughly two to five episodes; another recalled five to ten episodes and an episode of approximately ten minutes. The clerk described numerous incidents and long periods, including an episode of approximately ten minutes. The foreman's shorter-duration account and the other accounts were not identical. The trial judge and prosecutor said they did not observe sleep, and counsel described a habit of closing his eyes while concentrating. The state court nevertheless credited the neutral witnesses as establishing actual sleeping. It did not fix the precise witness, evidence, duration, depth, or percentage for every episode.

The en banc majority treated the credited findings and accounts together as repeated unconsciousness during not insubstantial adverse evidence. It held that an unconscious lawyer cannot listen, object, consult, or exercise judgment, and that functional absence at a critical stage is distinct from imperfect performance. It did not announce presumed prejudice for every momentary doze. It rejected requiring an outcome-changing missed act to prove the absence category and rejected a necessary government-causation condition for counsel's absence.

Higginbotham, joined by King, W. Eugene Davis, and Wiener, concurred fully and separately stressed that Hamilton and the critical-stage cases supplied an old rule, while the particular facts supplied its application. His account treats the ability to receive and respond to adverse evidence as integral to both the guilt defense and a capital penalty presentation. His further observations about the message conveyed to jurors are his reasoning, not an additional state factual finding.

Jolly, joined by Smith, dissented. He stressed the confession, other affirmative representation, the delayed assertion, and absence of demonstrated actual prejudice, and would return other claims to the district court. Barksdale, joined by Jones, Smith, and Garza, separately disputed the new-rule classification, the factual predicate for constructive absence, and the availability of an exception. His principal distinction is between the accepted sleep finding and the legal or inferential characterization of substantial unconscious absence. The substantial-portions language appears in the state court's recommended legal conclusion; it must not silently be treated as a separately quantified historical finding.

The lengthy dissent described counsel's motions, cross-examination, objections, defense presentation, efforts to exclude the confession, and arguments. Those acts support the State's claim that the proceeding retained meaningful advocacy overall. They do not establish that counsel was awake in every intervening interval; the majority noted testimony that he sometimes responded after being awakened. Nor does silence in a transcript prove sleep. The reported total of 12 hours and 51 minutes before the jury over six days is not a measurement of sleeping time; the dissent separately identifies three days of guilt-phase evidence and seven days of voir dire.

Barksdale also sought an adverse inference or further hearing from reports that Burdine nudged counsel and from Burdine's failure to testify at the state hearing. The majority rejected the asserted chain of mandatory inferences. Those disputed extrajudicial accounts are not findings that sleep occurred only during irrelevant evidence, and the packet adopts no litigation misconduct, waiver, forfeiture, or default conclusion from them. The lateness of the sleeping claim is historical background, not an independently established federal procedural bar.

### Entering law and distinct temporal inquiries

The Standards and Tests entry **Temporal reach of ordinary AEDPA habeas amendments**, present operation effective Carriger, April 16, 1998, extends Lindh's application-date construction to this pending capital application absent established chapter 154 qualification. Former §2254(d), not amended §2254(d)(1) or replacement §2254(e)(1), governs qualifying state factual findings. The exact former text, including all eight exceptions, the whole-record qualification, and record-production provisions, is supplied. Capital status alone supplies no chapter 154 qualification; none is established here.

The Sixth Amendment, applied to the State through the Fourteenth, requires assistance of counsel. Cronic's actual or constructive critical-stage absence ground is distinct from the complete-failure-of-adversarial-testing ground. Strickland ordinarily requires deficient performance and a reasonable probability of a different outcome. Counsel's impairment or inattention does not itself establish the absence category; a proven missing function cannot be rejected simply because the defendant has not already proved the outcome prejudice that the category replaces.

Bell v. Cone, May 28, 2002, applies Strickland to particular tactical omissions amid substantive defense advocacy and expressly preserves Cronic's other recognized categories. Its eight-Justice application is not a rule that any affirmative act at trial defeats every denial-of-counsel claim. Roe v. Flores-Ortega separately distinguishes a missing appeal from errors within an appeal and requires proof of causal deprivation before dispensing with probable-success proof. Neither case makes every lapse presumptively prejudicial.

For the preserved Teague question, the **Late assertion of Teague nonretroactivity** entry, present operation effective Schiro, January 19, 1994, retains Caspari's distinct preserved-defense treatment. The defense is expressly within the supplied questions; no new presentation obstacle is inferred. Caspari requires the finality-specific inquiry and preserves both contemporary exceptions: substantive limits on punishment and watershed procedure essential to accuracy and fundamental fairness. Graham v. Collins and Lambrix, in their actual controlling forms here, require substantive comparison with the prefinality rule; factual novelty or judicial disagreement alone does not settle novelty. The lower dissent's quotations from their different historical formulations cannot substitute for those holdings.

The current Bell, Roe, Early, Woodford, and Wiggins decisions can explain present doctrine or methodological distinctions, but cannot manufacture a constitutional rule available in 1987. Wiggins, June 26, 2003, distinguishes investigation from presentation, requires both constitutional elements where Strickland applies, and preserves the limits of an actual state adjudication; it does not resolve the critical-stage absence question.

The relevant prefinality sources include Cronic, Strickland, Powell, Gideon, Hamilton, White v. Maryland, Herring, Geders, and their particular absence or interference settings. Javor, 724 F.2d 831 (9th Cir. 1984), is prefinality lower-court support involving extensive sleeping; it is not a controlling Supreme Court holding. ### Strongest supported alternatives

**Affirm on the established absence category.** The credited repeated sleep during adverse proof in a short capital trial can establish functional unavailability at a critical stage. Counsel's other acts do not supply the missing assistance during those episodes. The strongest contrary argument is that the accepted finding lacks sufficient qualitative and temporal detail to separate absence from intermittent deficient performance, and that the requested application was not dictated in 1987. The affirmative path must identify why the actual credited evidence establishes a meaningful absence without inventing a sleep percentage or a particular missed objection; it must independently explain old-rule treatment.

**Require ordinary Strickland analysis.** The accepted facts may be considered insufficient to establish the narrow absence category in a proceeding containing extensive actual advocacy. This path can set aside the presumption-based writ and remand the sleeping claim for ordinary performance and prejudice analysis while preserving other pending claims. Its strongest counterargument is that aggregating awake advocacy over the entire trial collapses the independent critical-stage category and improperly makes absence harmless merely because counsel sometimes participated.

**Find a new rule and decide the actual exceptions.** The Court could conclude that the identified application extends rather than applies prefinality denial-of-counsel law. It must assess the strongest old-rule answer based on functional absence, not rest on judicial head counts. If new, a watershed claim requires its own demonstrated accuracy-and-fairness basis; the importance of counsel or the capital setting alone does not establish it. The substantive-punishment exception is analytically different and does not become applicable merely because the claim attacks a capital trial. Denial of this habeas ground would not necessarily reject the proposed rule for direct-review litigation.

**Remand a specifically material factual issue.** If a selected ground cannot lawfully proceed without identifying an unresolved predicate of actual sleep or its substantiality, the Court may return that bounded issue under the governing former-law framework. It must respect the accepted actual-sleep finding, identify the legal significance of the missing fact, and not treat disagreement about a legal label as authority to discard the entire state determination. No automatic new hearing follows. A path seeking to displace a qualifying finding as unsupported requires the pertinent whole state record; that record has not been independently inspected in this preflight.

## Holland ex rel. Overdorff v. Harrington

### Event, questions, judgment, and available relief

The reviewed judgment is **268 F.3d 1179 (10th Cir. 2001)**, No. 99-1373, an interlocutory qualified-immunity appeal. It granted immunity on the decision to deploy the SWAT team, directed dismissal of Sheriff Schirard and Undersheriff Harrington because that was their only surviving liability ground, and otherwise affirmed the denial of summary judgment, leaving claims against supervisor Davis for further proceedings.

The assigned questions concern the Fourth Amendment reviewability and reasonableness of the planned degree of force and the continued direct gunpoint restraint of compliant, nonthreatening children. The residents seek restoration of the deployment claim; the officials seek immunity. The separate knock-and-announce claim and other dispositions are outside the supplied questions. Henry's alternative substantive-due-process analysis must be identified as part of the lower-court disagreement but is not silently added as a distinct granted question.

The operation occurred **April 16, 1996**. The district court's relevant denial was August 3, 1999; the circuit opinion issued October 19, 2001. Conduct-date notice is fixed to April 16, 1996. Review of the supported plaintiff-favorable account does not award damages, establish every officer's conduct, or adjudicate institutional liability.

### Supported record and lower-court disagreement

The sheriff authorized a ten-deputy SWAT team, led by Davis, to serve arrest and search warrants at Heflin's property following an alleged restaurant assault. The arrest charges were misdemeanor assault and reckless endangerment. The search sought items such as a black hat, cigarette packages, a bloody shirt, and receipts. The search warrant permitted execution day or night but contained no no-knock authorization. Its validity does not decide the permitted manner of execution.

Around 8:30 p.m., armed deputies in hooded camouflage approached. Some residents, including children, were outside; others were in the home. On the supported resident account, deputies ordered an eight-year-old and older companions to the ground at gunpoint, held a fourteen-year-old prone, and pursued a screaming four-year-old into the house while a laser-sighted weapon was trained at her back. Residents inside were restrained, and all were gathered in the living room while checks and the search proceeded. Except for Heflin, who was arrested, they were then released. These details describe the summary-judgment account, not trial findings.

The planners asserted violent histories, multiple possible occupants, firearms, uncertainty, preservation of evidence, and a desire to control the scene safely. The residents disputed the known violent histories and resistance premise; Henry's separate account specifies that the one resident with a known violent history had already been removed. They relied on Heflin's prior cooperation, the misdemeanor charges, the known presence of children, and the nature of the evidence sought. Weapons actually discovered and the absence of shots or wounds do not by themselves prove what officers reasonably knew before entry.

The majority held that deciding to use overwhelming force was immediately connected with the seizure and therefore reviewable under the Fourth Amendment. It nonetheless found the deployment justified. It separately found no showing that the planners intended or knew of later excessive conduct or directed it. Those are distinct issues: objective review of the initial force selected must not be collapsed into a requirement that the planners subjectively intended an unrelated later violation.

On the gunpoint claim, the majority distinguished initial display and ready carriage from continued direct aiming after submission and complete control. It relied on the supported absence of threat and rejected the absence of physical wounds as a categorical constitutional bar. It denied Davis immunity on this assumed account, identifying his presence and supervision and the continuation of gunpoint restraint until his direction stopped it as the asserted affirmative personal link. That record does not authorize damages solely because he held supervisory office.

Henry joined the opinion except for its treatment of deployment. He would preserve the claim against the planners at summary judgment on the residents' version, citing the absence of a supported violent-history or resistance premise, known children, the tactical policy, and alleged punitive animosity. He invoked both Fourth Amendment review and alternative substantive due process if the Fourth Amendment did not govern. He expressly did not award judgment to the residents. Department policy and an angry remark are evidence in the supported context, not independent federal constitutional commands.

### Entering law and current review method

The Standards and Tests entry **Qualified-immunity legal appeals and factual sufficiency**, present operation effective Behrens, February 21, 1996, and current wording authority Clanton, December 29, 1997, preserves Johnson's unanimous distinction. Immediate review may decide a separable legal defense using the supported plaintiff-favorable facts; it may not choose disputed intelligence, participation, causation, or historical events for the officials. If assumptions require identification, that identification must not become truth-finding.

The entry **Relevant precedent in appellate qualified-immunity review**, effective Elder, February 23, 1994, requires all relevant conduct-date precedent. Harlow and Anderson retain objective, particularized notice; officials need not prove actual reliance on a binding authorization. Denius preserves that defense. Wilson distinguishes warrant authority from the permissible manner of execution and preserves the independent immunity inquiry. Saucier's binding mandate applies the existing force and immunity rules separately; it establishes no mandatory merits-first sequence and no replacement defense. The different historical Saucier passages in the lower opinion are not current law.

Graham v. Connor and Tennessee v. Garner require objective reasonableness of the actual manner and degree of force in context, including offense severity, immediate threat, resistance, and the circumstances confronting officers. Brower supplies intentional acquisition of control; submission to the intentional gunpoint restraint is material even for residents not named in an arrest warrant. Summers permits reasonable control in execution of a search; it does not authorize any degree or duration of force. A tactical label neither exempts the conduct nor establishes a violation.

Hope, June 27, 2002, and McClendon, March 4, 2003, retain particularized conduct-date notice while allowing sufficiently obvious applications without a factual replica. Neither supplies new notice in April 1996. McDonald v. Haskins, 966 F.2d 292 (7th Cir. 1992), concerns a gun threat against a nonthreatening nine-year-old; Baker v. Monroe Township, 50 F.3d 1186 (3d Cir. 1995), concerns continued gunpoint restraint of nonresisting people, including minors, during a raid. Their actual settings can support the fair-warning comparison, but they remain lower-court authorities; their use does not make every initial display unconstitutional. Later Lanier or Hope formulations cannot themselves constitute preconduct notice.

### Strongest supported alternatives

**Affirm deployment immunity on a sufficiently supported ground.** The decision to use a specialized team may fall within Fourth Amendment review yet be reasonable because the actual supported contemporary circumstances justify the initial degree of force. Alternatively, the particular deployment restriction may have lacked sufficient April 1996 clarity even if the assumed facts permit a constitutional violation. These grounds must remain separate. The strongest contrary argument is that the majority relied on disputed violent-history assertions, hindsight about discovered weapons and the operation's success, or an improper subjective-intent limitation rather than the supported nonmovant account.

**Vacate the deployment disposition and remand the legal analysis.** If the appellate court used an impermissible factual premise or treated the planned initial force as categorically remote, proper review may require renewed force and distinct notice analysis on the resident-favorable account. This restores consideration; it does not prove liability or remove conduct-date immunity. The strongest contrary argument is that even the resident account leaves a lawful basis for swift armed control or insufficiently specific notice, making remand unnecessary. A particularized immunity ground remains available without importing the officials' disputed facts.

**Preserve denial of Davis's immunity on continued gunpoint restraint.** A reasonable initial display does not justify continuing direct threats against compliant children after complete control and without a supported danger. The fair-warning argument rests on the pre-1996 constitutional principles and particular lower-court cases, not on a new 2002 rule. The strongest contrary argument is that an unsettled, active search still justified readiness and officers could reasonably perceive interference or danger. The record requires differentiating holding a weapon ready from directly aiming it, and genuine uncertainty from a factual account rejected for this appeal. A conclusion on notice still leaves personal participation, causation, injury, damages, and materially different supported facts for ordinary adjudication.

**Grant bounded immunity on an identified notice or responsibility ground.** That is legally possible if the particular assumed conduct did not receive sufficient preconduct warning, or if a separable legal responsibility defect exists. It cannot rest solely on lack of physical injury, automatic immunity for warrant execution, or appellate rejection of supported participation facts. An evidentiary-sufficiency dispute falls outside immediate review under Johnson; dismissing that part of an appeal leaves the district order in place without creating a merits liability finding.
