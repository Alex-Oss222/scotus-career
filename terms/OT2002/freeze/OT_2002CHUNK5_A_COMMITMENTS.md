## Boeing Co. v. United States — Nos. 01-1209 and 01-1382

### Questions and operative alternatives

The two tax periods present the same substantive allocation issue: whether Treasury may allocate currently deducted research expense to the broad product category rather than honor Boeing's airplane-program assignment as the exclusive allocation. The DISC and FSC grouping texts remain distinct; their differences do not supply a separate result on this record. The cross-petition presents only the corresponding computational consequence if the principal judgment is reversed.

**Affirmance ground.** Sections 994 and 925 leave room for a lawful definition of combined taxable income. The source-allocation structure and Treasury's implementing authority permit a reasonable research-specific allocation rule that preserves the connection between the expense and the relevant product category. The regulation's treatment of speculative research and cross-product benefits reasonably supports the two-digit transportation-equipment category for these years. The accepted transaction grouping governs the choice and application of pricing methods; it does not displace the separately incorporated expense-allocation rule when combined taxable income is calculated. Haggar and Mead require actual delegated authority and statutory fit before deference, and those conditions are satisfied at this limited scope.

**Strongest contrary ground.** The statute requires income attributable to the export property and the export regulations accept a taxpayer's product grouping. Broad-category allocation can charge a productive export line with costs directed to another identifiable product. If that relationship requirement fixes the relevant attribution unit, the agency cannot replace it with an irrebuttable industry-category assumption. United Dominion's protection of the actual computation and MCI's protection of an operative statutory limit support reversal without rejecting deference generally.

**Answer adopted by the affirming commitments.** Attribution does not require exclusive project tracing. The special research provision defines the relationship for this expense, while the grouping provisions retain substantial independent work in applying the pricing choices. A company accounting designation is evidence of its organization, not a statutory election to make research legally unrelated to other products. No finding of sham expenditure or tax abuse is needed. The narrower direct-tracing construction is therefore permissible advocacy but not compelled by these statutory and regulatory terms.

**Answer adopted by the reversing commitments.** A broad regulatory choice remains subordinate to the export-specific attribution requirement; making grouping relevant only after cost allocation does not answer the textual objection. They would require the expense relationship supplied by the governing export computation rather than uphold the challenged categorical rule merely because research can sometimes confer incidental benefits. They do not find every claimed refund proved or invalidate an adequately grounded allocation of genuinely shared research.

## United States v. White Mountain Apache Tribe

### Questions and operative alternatives

**Affirmance ground.** Where Congress expressly places identified property in trust for the Tribe and the United States retains and exercises occupancy or control over that property, the statute carries a duty to exercise reasonable care to preserve the trust corpus subject to that control. Under Mitchell II, that identified fiduciary responsibility fairly supports compensation for loss caused by its breach. The Indian Tucker Act supplies the forum and waiver; the substantive duty comes from the 1960 enactment construed in its property relationship.

**Reversal ground.** The enactment creates title in trust subject to a reserved federal use, but states no maintenance undertaking comparable to the management provisions in Mitchell II. Actual occupancy cannot supply an unexpressed statutory duty; common-law duties may elaborate an established undertaking but cannot create one. On that reading the particular monetary maintenance claim must be dismissed.

**Principal conflict and responses.** The affirmative path's difficulty is the absence of an express repair command. Its answer is that the duty arises from an express statutory trust whose trustee actually controls the corpus, not a general guardianship label, and the use reservation does not make the beneficiary's remainder disposable. The negative path's difficulty is that it reduces the trust to protection of nominal title despite the trustee's own use. Its answer is that Congress can convey a restricted beneficial interest and reserve use without undertaking preservation at public expense. Both paths accept Mitchell II's governing fair-interpretation rule; the disagreement concerns what duty this statute establishes.

## United States v. Navajo Nation

### Questions and operative alternatives

**Reversal ground.** The Indian Mineral Leasing Act's approval and regulatory structure, as presented for this lease and amendment, does not assign the United States the comprehensive beneficial management of the Nation's commercial bargaining or a particular compensable duty to secure the asserted higher royalty. The Nation must identify an enacted or regulatory fiduciary duty; alleged secret intervention and actual influence cannot alone supply that substantive source. Mitchell I and II require that source before common-law trust principles can define liability. A coal provision governing a different lease category cannot cure the omission.

**Affirmance ground.** The legally assigned authority over the royalty adjustment, administrative appeal, and final approval imposes a fiduciary obligation to administer that controlled process loyally for the tribal lessor, without secretly using it to favor the lessee. This is a source-based duty in the specific mineral-lease relationship, not an obligation to obtain the highest possible price. Mitchell II permits a fair inference of compensation for the loss caused by breach of that duty. Whether the Nation would have secured a different royalty remains for proof.

**Principal conflict and responses.** The reversal path faces the strongest objection that it characterizes a claim of disloyal intervention as mere dissatisfaction with bargaining. Its supporters accept the factual distinction but find that wrongdoing still cannot create a compensation obligation absent the necessary substantive source. The affirmance path faces the strongest objection that federal approval is not comprehensive resource management and that tribal bargaining autonomy is a feature of the statute. Its supporters answer that a duty of loyalty in the federally controlled process protects tribal bargaining rather than displaces it, and is narrower than a general managerial obligation. Neither path uses the same-day Fort Apache case as precedent.

## Clay v. United States

### Question, alternative, and governing formulation

**Common commitment.** When a federal defendant's conviction is affirmed on direct appeal and he does not seek certiorari, the judgment becomes final for §2255's ordinary one-year trigger when the time to seek certiorari expires. An appellate mandate may return authority to the lower court while direct Supreme Court review remains available; the mandate therefore does not start this statutory clock in the ordinary case presented.

**Alternative tested.** Congress expressly referred to expiration of review time in §2244(d)(1)(A), but omitted that phrase in §2255. The strongest mandate argument draws an inference of different finality from that contrast. The common answer is contextual: §2255 uses a legal term with an established direct-review meaning and contains no mandate trigger. Section 2244's fuller formulation states that concept for its provision; the textual difference alone does not replace it with an administrative step that precedes the end of available review. Lindh's use of a meaningful contextual contrast does not make every omission controlling. The government's agreement is not the Court's legal ground.
