## Reachability and issue structure

The authorized inventory and handoff place the preserved federal question properly before the Court. The supplied roster identifies all eight Associates as seated, with no case-specific participation obstacle. Each reaches the merits. The single judgment concerns the state disallowance of specified MSS-1 costs after August 5, 1997, based on the continued capacity treatment of units in extended reserve shutdown. There is no separate adjudicated compliance claim or additional judgment component to reconstruct.

The material question is whether the State may reject costs allocated under the operative interstate agreement because it considers the utility's generation-capacity classification imprudent, although the agreement leaves a utility some discretion. The existence of discretion and the binding legal effect of the allocation are distinct propositions.

## Common sufficient ground and scope

All eight Associates support these propositions together, not merely the judgment:

1. **Binding interstate allocation.** When an operative FERC-regulated agreement allocates interstate system costs, a state retail-rate order may not substitute a different allocation by disallowing the resulting costs as imprudent. The filed-rate rule reaches allocation terms that determine wholesale costs as well as a stated price. Nantahala, 476 U.S. at 962–973, supplies that rule and its prohibition on trapping costs through inconsistent retail treatment; Mississippi Power, 487 U.S. at 369–376, applies it to state prudence review of costs assigned within an interstate system.

2. **Utility discretion does not itself remove the conflict.** An allocation remains federally binding when its operation includes choices left to a utility. A State cannot avoid the allocation rule merely by directing its objection to the prudence of a choice that produced the allocated charge. The required comparison is between the operative agreement and the legal effect of the challenged order. Here the order rejects the payment consequences of the capacity treatment under that agreement; it does not enforce a finding that the agreement was violated. Treating those assigned costs as unrecoverable defeats the federal allocation even without a federal command to make the particular classification choice. This applies the allocation precedents to the identified formula-and-discretion distinction; it does not treat approval of a formula as an affirmative finding that every business decision is prudent.

3. **Actual conflict, not regulatory silence.** The ground is an operative allocation, not the mere existence of federal regulation, an agency's failure to condemn a business decision, or an abstract preference for uniformity. Mississippi Power rejects a requirement that FERC separately have decided the very prudence objection before its binding allocation receives effect. The entering-law rules in Freightliner, Geier Parts I–II, and Sprietsma remain intact: overlap, compliance, or silence alone does not establish a conflict. AT&T, Part I, supplies an analogous distinction between binding tariff terms and genuinely independent duties; its communications provisions do not themselves determine the Federal Power Act's reach.

4. **The actual order and remedy.** Because the challenged disallowance rests on the conflicting prudence rationale, the state judgment must be reversed and the matter returned for rate proceedings that recognize the costs assigned by the governing federal arrangement. A possible violation of that arrangement cannot sustain this order without the finding and adjudicated basis the record lacks. The absence of such a finding is not a judicial certification of universal compliance. The remand implements the federal allocation rule within the State's retail-rate proceeding.

The retained-state-authority boundary is substantive: this rule does not displace retail regulation or a genuinely independent prudence inquiry that leaves the federal allocation effective. Nantahala's discussion of a purchaser's ability to obtain lower-cost power elsewhere was expressly assumed without decision; Mississippi Power likewise distinguished an unnecessary purchase the utility had a legal right to refuse. Neither passage establishes a general exception whenever some managerial discretion exists. A fresh controversy over compliance with a tariff, including whether FERC has exclusive initial authority over every such controversy, is outside the question presented by this order and is left open on that ground. No blanket immunity for discretionary conduct is part of any Associate's commitment.

## Alternative-path comparison

**Preemption path and its strongest objection.** Reversal gives binding effect to the allocation actually operating here. The strongest objection is that the State regulates an antecedent business choice, while leaving the formula and intercompany payment obligation formally unchanged. On that account, disallowance merely places the cost of managerial imprudence on shareholders. The answer is that Nantahala and Mississippi Power look to the effect of the retail order, not whether it formally rewrites the interstate agreement. On this record the State's rejected capacity treatment changes which costs the federally governed allocation can carry into retail rates. The asserted independence therefore does not separate the order from the very allocation it defeats. A federal agency's express approval of this particular choice is unnecessary; an operative federal allocation is necessary and is supplied here.

**Independent-prudence path and its strongest objection.** Affirmance would preserve a state inquiry if the challenged duty genuinely could be separated from the federal allocation. The retained discretion, the statutory division between wholesale and retail authority, and the assumed alternative-purchase reservation in Nantahala give this path a legal basis. Brennan's dissent in Mississippi Power, 487 U.S. at 383–391, states its strongest version: distinguish the allocation of incurred costs from the prudence of incurring them, and distinguish distribution among system members from distribution between ratepayers and shareholders. That dissent is considered as an argument, not controlling law or a position attributed to a present Justice. The controlling opinion rejected that distinction where the prudence inquiry traps allocated system costs. The present order identifies no independent tariff violation or other separable adjudicated ground that avoids that problem. Discretion alone supplies no such ground. Consequently, affirmance on the stated theory does not survive the allocation precedents as applied to the supplied record.
