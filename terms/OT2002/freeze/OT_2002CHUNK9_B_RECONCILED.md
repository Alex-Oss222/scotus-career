## 107. Demore v. Kim — No. 01-1491

### Historical comparison and changed premises

### Breyer's complete narrower path and compatibility test

For Breyer, section 1226(c)'s command concerning an alien who is deportable leaves a statutory question when the alien presents a legal or factual deportability objection that is not insubstantial and is not interposed solely for delay. Serious constitutional doubt favors a construction permitting an individualized bail assessment in that situation, rather than treating the stricter Joseph criterion—substantial unlikelihood that the government will establish inclusion—as the sole route out of no-bail custody. This concerns entry into the statutory category; it does not turn section 1226(c)(2)'s witness-protection exception into an ordinary bail clause.

The complete proposed rule uses the section 3143(b) analogy stated in his own writing: the deportability challenge must not be solely for delay and must raise a question of law or fact that is not insubstantial; release additionally requires the alien to show by clear and convincing evidence that he is not likely to flee or pose a danger to the community. The right to seek the assessment is different from satisfaction of that release showing. The rule does not award release or declare every covered detention unconstitutional.

His mandate is a remand for the Ninth Circuit to determine whether Kim raises the qualifying challenge. Because the current judgment supplies an unconditional constitutional hearing requirement, the formal disposition is vacatur of that judgment and remand for the narrower inquiry, preserving jurisdiction. His historical text expressly requested remand; this handoff states the operative treatment of the existing judgment rather than leaving its force ambiguous. Ordinary agency responsibility for conviction classification remains under Ventura. The Court does not resolve the burglary or theft classifications, find delay, prescribe a new universal hearing calendar or order rearrest.

If Kim had actually conceded deportability, Breyer's historical separate position would permit the ordinary short processing interval, subject to removal-linked duration limits of the kind discussed in Zadvydas. The validated packet supplies no such comprehensive concession. This observation is not an additional six-month holding under section 1226(c), an automatic entitlement at that date, or a substitute for the actual threshold remand.

The strongest objection to this construction is that Congress mandated custody of the charged category and expressly specified the only release exception, while Joseph already tests inclusion. Breyer's answer is the uncertainty whether the person is within the category at all, the serious liberty doubt in prolonged custody during a substantial challenge, and a workable, government-protective release standard. The custody supporters reject that extension; category review and the bounded removal setting suffice for the present general challenge. The direct constitutional supporters regard individual necessity as necessary even if the statutory inclusion is sound, so the statutory threshold does not fully remedy their constitutional objection.

No source establishes Stevens, Souter or Ginsburg as a full join in Breyer's complete package. Their Zadvydas joins support avoidance in a suitable statute, but do not establish agreement with this threshold or the alien's clear-and-convincing proof burden. Their historical joinder in Souter's separate writing, rather than Breyer's, is strong evidence of the surviving difference. They retain affirmance. A judgment-only accommodation cannot be inferred from supposed lesser-included relief: a remand that could remove all hearing relief upon failure of Breyer's threshold is not their existing disposition. Nor does Breyer support unconditional hearing affirmance on another established ground. A future formulation would need a genuinely supported response to these objections; assembly may not count one as already accepted.

All eight preserve section 1226(c)(2)'s actual exception: the Attorney General must determine under 18 U.S.C. 3521 that release is necessary to protect a witness, potential witness, cooperating person investigating major criminal activity, or the specified immediate family member or close associate; the alien must satisfy the nondanger and appearance conditions, and the procedure must consider offense severity. That exception supplies no general discretion to release upon nondanger alone.

## 110. United States v. Miami University

### Historical anchor and retained sufficient grounds

There is no historical Supreme Court merits lineup. The Sixth Circuit affirmed coverage, the federal enforcement action, independent equitable relief, rejection of the claimed access right and the discovery ruling. All eight initial commitments remain affirmance of those components on their carefully bounded grounds. Agreement with the lower judgment does not adopt every sentence of its reasoning.

M1 requires an actual public enforcement source: accepted federal funding obligations and the preserved traditional means of enforcing them, supported by Pennhurst, King and Wheeler. Section 1345 supplies jurisdiction rather than the claim; section 1232g(f) directs appropriate enforcement and section 1234c(a)(4) preserves otherwise authorized action rather than creating an unlimited action from its own words. Newport News's route-specific requirement remains controlling. Gonzaga's rejection of the particular private section 1983 entitlement does not displace the distinct United States claim.

M2 applies the education-record definition to institutionally retained internal disciplinary files directly concerning a student. Owasso's separate maintenance requirement is satisfied. Criminal subject matter does not convert these into records created and maintained by a law-enforcement unit for law-enforcement purposes. A unit may perform disciplinary functions without losing its unit status; its exclusively disciplinary files do not become excluded police files. A school-maintained disciplinary copy differs from the original qualifying police record, and transfer of an education record to police does not extinguish its protection. The statute supplies the sufficient ground, with properly promulgated regulations confirming the distinction; no universal deference to advisory correspondence is adopted.

M3 preserves ordinary equity, independently of coverage and authority to sue. Established identifiable releases and the announced continuing policy threaten loss of confidentiality that publication cannot undo. Damages and the described administrative alternatives do not adequately avert that harm on this record. Hecht and Weinberger preserve equitable judgment; no automatic injury presumption follows from FERPA. The decree permits statutory disclosure and proper redaction, and it remains tied to the recipients' funding obligations. A legally material future change may support modification rather than perpetual federal supervision.

M4 rejects the claimed constitutional access right to these substantially unredacted files under the actual experience-and-function inquiry. Internal discipline determines the student-institution relationship, does not determine criminal guilt for the sovereign and does not preclude prosecution. The public value of campus oversight does not alone establish a criminal-adjudication access right. El Vocero requires examination of the kind and actual function of the proceeding, not reliance on its local label. United Reporting subjects speech-selected access conditions to scrutiny but creates no general entitlement to government files. No universal finding that openness never helps discipline, or that every campus safety proceeding lacks access protection, is adopted.

M5 affirms the actual discovery ruling and fitted prospective decree. The identified discovery would not change the conceded criminal overlap, actual internal records requested or established continuing policy. This is not a categorical bar on discovery in confidentiality cases. The federal action independently construes FERPA and does not review the state mandamus as an appellate court, find that mandamus satisfied, bind the different requester or replace authoritative state-law meanings. No broad preemption ruling is required.
