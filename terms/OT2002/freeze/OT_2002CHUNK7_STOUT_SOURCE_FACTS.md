# Stout: source facts for neutral validation

## Source and boundary

Source: the official Fifth Circuit opinion, *Stout v. Baxter Healthcare Corp.*, 282 F.3d 856, No. 00-60542 (February 19, 2002), reproduced in the authorized `runtime/chunk7-comparator-sources/stout.txt` and corresponding PDF. The full ten-page opinion text was read. This handoff contains the lower court's objective record recitations and its description of the evidentiary issue, without a Supreme Court outcome or proposed commitment. The event cutoff is May 19, 2003.

## Objective record clarification

Pages 2–3 report that Stout was hired as a material handler in May 1998, received positive performance reviews and maintained perfect attendance during her first two months. Beginning August 14, early labor followed by miscarriage made her medically unable to work for over two weeks. She immediately notified her supervisor and provided a medical excuse a week later. Baxter terminated her on August 21 because her absence exceeded its probationary limit.

These are the appellate court's recitation of the summary-judgment record. They establish the reported individual absence and medical excuse; they do not establish every pregnant worker's condition, prove comparative incidence of absence, or convert an individual fact into a classwide finding.

The original neutral packet already identifies miscarriage-related absence, the ninety-day probation, the three-day cutoff, and the absence of medical leave. The duration of this particular medically required absence and the supplied medical excuse are additional specificity material to distinguishing individual injury from group impact. The expert's childbirth proposition must still not automatically be recast as a proposition about every miscarriage.

## Evidence and appellate treatment

Page 8 reports expert testimony that no pregnant woman who gives birth can work for at least two weeks. The Fifth Circuit accepted that this is evidence that all or substantially all pregnant women giving birth during probation will be terminated. This is the court's characterization of the testimony's implications, not a trial finding of ultimate statutory liability.

Pages 8–10 reject extending the circuit's *Garcia* nonstatistical route to this attendance policy, reasoning that using the inevitable temporary inability to work would effectively require more generous pregnancy leave despite the PDA's same-treatment language. The court did not categorically exclude all pregnancy disparate-impact claims. Its identified reason for rejecting this evidence was the legal effect it assigned to the comparison clause and an attendance requirement, rather than a determination that the testimony was factually incredible.

The opinion supplies no comparative statistics concerning the rate at which other probationary employees exceeded three days, no class-certification judgment, and no established classwide remedy. Those limits do not establish that missing sources reflect a filing failure. The source supports addressing the adequacy of nonstatistical evidence and the law governing the two summary-judgment motions separately.
