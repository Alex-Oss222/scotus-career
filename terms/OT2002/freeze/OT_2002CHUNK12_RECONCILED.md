## 146. Desert Palace, Inc. v. Costa

## 147. Satellite Broadcasting & Communications Association v. FCC

### Scope, shared premises and statutory qualifications

All eight Associates reach the limited First Amendment question. Satellite carriers' editorial selection is protected speech. Neither the optional license nor copyright's subject matter automatically exempts the carriage condition from scrutiny. The current Eldred holding rejects categorical speech immunity without supplying a universal scrutiny tier. The separate Copyright Clause, takings, due-process and a-la-carte implementing-rule issues are outside this grant and acquire no Supreme Court merits disposition through this event.

The challenged duty attaches when a carrier uses §122's royalty-free license in a selected local market and a qualifying station requests carriage. It does not impose carriage of all stations nationwide. Section 338(c)(1) excludes a commercial signal substantially duplicating another carried local signal and generally does not require more than one affiliate of a network; the latter limit has an exception for same-network affiliates licensed to communities in different States within the market. Section 338(c)(2) directs the FCC to limit multiple noncommercial carriage. The scheme has no imported cable one-third-capacity cap.

Finite nationwide channel capacity is a real burden. The source's estimates of approximately 450–500 channels per principal carrier and roughly 1,600 local stations describe the record's architecture, not unlimited future capacity. Spot-beam development was prospective. The carriers' presented displacement claim concerned local affiliates in smaller markets; it did not claim compelled removal of national or regional nonbroadcast channels. Private copyright clearances remain legally possible, but the record describes substantial transaction costs. These limits affect the facial fit analysis without eliminating protected choice.

### Final rival grounds and remedies

**S1 — Affirm on preservation and diversity.** Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer would affirm the First Amendment judgment. The scheme's trigger and eligible-station treatment regulate the means of obtaining licensed local distribution without selecting a message or viewpoint. The general condition is content neutral and subject to the controlling Turner intermediate standard, with O'Brien's substantial nonsuppressive interest and Ward's prohibition on substantially excessive speech burdens.

The actual satellite record supports a preventive judgment. Rapid growth and the carriers' selective carriage of major-network affiliates support a prediction of reduced access to independents. Viewers receiving the networks through satellite have a reduced incentive to obtain or retain separate antennas. Lost audience affects advertising support for independently available free local service. Congress need not await individual station failures. These are supported predictive mechanisms, not findings that named stations already failed; they do not depend on importing cable's physical monopoly or predatory incentives.

Market-by-market election, requests, eligibility and nonduplication rules connect the condition to the activity the new license enables. The delayed effective date and actual local-service scope accommodate the national capacity constraint. Neither a station-by-station license without carriage safeguards nor reliance on antennas answers the supported selective-carriage risk as effectively. Intermediate review does not demand the least restrictive imaginable arrangement, but the actual capacity burden and relevant alternatives remain part of the fit inquiry. On this facial record the condition burdens no substantially greater amount of speech than necessary for the accepted preservation interest.

The lower court's independent advertising-competition rationale has been tested separately. Audience and advertising effects support the preservation mechanism. These four do not need an independently sufficient power to equalize competitors' receipts or to prevent every financial disadvantage between media. Nor do they adopt the lower court's broad proposition that all significant contributors to a common threat may be regulated without meaningful attention to their differences. Their affirmative ground remains this supported satellite mechanism and this bounded carriage condition.

**S2 — Reverse and remand for relief and severability.** O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg would reverse the First Amendment judgment. Their prior Turner objections to content favoritism and inadequate fit remain material. Even assuming neutrality and applying the controlling intermediate standard, they find insufficient justification for the breadth of the compulsory carriage duty. Evidence of selective carriage and some audience loss does not itself establish a proportionate connection between carrying all eligible requesting stations and preventing loss of useful independently available free service.

The national architecture imposes an opportunity cost across local markets; absence of cable's demonstrated predatory incentives matters to the claimed threat. Market election and duplication limits mitigate the burden but do not establish its proportion. A conclusion that no carriage safeguard would be ineffective does not prove that this safeguard burdens no substantially excessive speech. Neither an optional government benefit nor a description of compelled carriage as speech enhancing answers the editorial objection.

These four also reject the independent advertising ground on this record. Avoiding a government-created distortion can be legitimate, but a competitor's audience disadvantage alone does not establish the expressive-market harm needed to justify this compelled choice. They do not require a completed antitrust violation, formal legislative findings or a strict least-restrictive-means showing. Their remedy is reversal with remand for appropriate relief against the invalid condition and determination of the actual statutory severability consequences. They would not judicially award an unrestricted royalty-free license.

## D2 — the common legal boundary and the genuine disagreement

### Bounded constitutional inquiry

Each Associate supports the following narrow proposition: an absent claimant who identifies a material conflict in the original representation and an asserted absence of protection for that interest is not foreclosed merely by the original judgment’s general statement of adequacy, the existence of notice, or the opportunity to object or opt out. The court must determine whether the asserted binding effect rests on constitutionally adequate representation. A claim that a settlement was poor, a certification ruling erroneous, or a payment disappointing does not establish that defect.

This conclusion applies Matsushita Part IV-A’s independent constitutional prerequisite and resolves only the concrete form of collateral objection presented here. It does not turn the reservation in Part IV-B into a general right to retry certification from the beginning. Ordinary finality continues to govern a person’s own adjudication and a genuinely binding representative adjudication. The earlier Agent Orange judgments are neither nonexistent nor irrelevant: their actual findings, grounds, scope, and precedential force must be addressed. The fact that an objector raised an argument does not itself make that objector the authorized representative of everyone later advancing it. Conversely, the use of a different claimant’s name or a newly described category does not itself defeat an otherwise binding adjudication.

The seven reject petitioners’ broadest procedure-only proposition: good-faith use of Rule 23, coupled with no demonstrated fraud or collusion, does not invariably end the constitutional inquiry. That would permit an unprotected material conflict to be cured by the very certification finding being challenged, contrary to Matsushita’s separation of actual representation from procedural opportunities. They also reject respondents’ broadest proposition if understood to make every adequacy ruling perpetually provisional and open to wholesale de novo retrial. Their disagreement is whether the supported original structure already resolves this particular objection.

### Correct-standard remand: O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Breyer

These four would vacate the appellate determination that the settlement cannot bind Stephenson and remand for application of the constitutional standard to the original proceedings. They would not reinstate Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal by treating the earlier adequacy findings as conclusive merely because they exist.

The remand rests on an identifiable defect in the appellate analysis. The Second Circuit treated the absence of post-1994 direct payments, together with Amchem and Ortiz, as establishing inadequate representation and stated that the ultimate merits had no bearing on adequacy. The merits of Stephenson’s tort claim are not to be decided as a substitute for representation. But common defenses, the contemporaneously understood risks, and the actual distribution arrangement can bear on whether the represented interests were materially opposed and whether that opposition was protected. They cannot be categorically excluded from that inquiry. Nor can an individual’s eventual placement on the losing side of an uncertain compensation arrangement alone prove that the arrangement lacked representative authority when adopted.

The lower courts must examine the original constitutional objection with the earlier decisions’ actual holdings and grounds in view: the common-defense basis of the 1987 ruling, the compensation-eligibility basis of Ivy/Hartman, and the asserted conflict in allocating a finite fund while releasing later claims. Any determination of a binding prior adjudication must address the relationship through which it purported to bind Stephenson; any determination of a constitutional defect must identify the unprotected material interest, not simply the date of diagnosis. Actual representation during allocation matters even though the manufacturers negotiated the lump sum before the court adopted the allocation.

The original refusal to appoint future-claimant counsel is established in Joint Appendix 129–130; the remand does not ask whether that refusal occurred or presume that some separate lawyer might have existed. These four distinguish the absence of that particular safeguard from the ultimate constitutional conclusion. They require an assessment whether the original differences were materially adverse in this arrangement and whether the protection actually supplied was constitutionally sufficient, considering the serious common risks, direct-payment program, assistance program, and adjudicated grounds together. Cost alone cannot excuse a material unprotected conflict. Conversely, the absence of a separately appointed attorney does not establish a universal constitutional subclass requirement. This is the precise application on which they disagree with the affirming three.

This does not prescribe new discovery, an evidentiary hearing, a numerical deference measure, or a new burden of proof. It requires reconsideration under the correct legal distinction through the ordinary procedures appropriate to the Rule 12(b)(6) posture and the properly considered settlement record. Whether the existing record permits disposition without further fact development remains a judicial application question. The original settlement is not vacated wholesale. Dismissal of Stephenson on preclusion grounds remains unavailable unless the legally available constitutional objection is resolved against him under that standard.

### Affirmance on the existing original conflict: Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg

These three would affirm the removal of the settlement bar for Stephenson. Their ground is the original arrangement’s combination of an unrestricted release of future injury claims with a fixed end to direct death-and-disability compensation, represented by symptomatic named claimants without independent protection of the adverse future allocation interest. They do not reason backward from the bare fact that Stephenson received no cash. The initial allocation itself placed the interest in present distribution against the interest in preserving resources or other meaningful protection for injuries outside the payment horizon.

The manufacturers’ admitted account of the named representatives, the fixed program terms reported below, the express refusal of future-claimant counsel in Joint Appendix 129–130, and the reliance on the judge, submissions, and common defenses supply a sufficient basis to reject the asserted preclusion bar in this posture. Individual veterans’ submissions and careful judicial management did not make the court or those speakers representatives of the opposed interest. The assistance program and compensation of some later-manifesting claimants must not be erased; their existence does not by itself answer who protected the interest surrendered when post-program tort claims were released. These Justices do not equate constitutionally adequate representation with a guarantee that every class member ultimately receives money.

The strongest contrary argument is that all asymptomatic veterans originally faced the same uncertain risk, the same serious defenses, and the same compensation rules, and that the precise representation objection was already heard. The response is that shared litigation adversity can justify collective pursuit of recovery without eliminating a conflict over allocation after a fund is obtained. The opposing interest existed before anyone knew which particular veteran would later manifest disease. A prior general finding grounded in the common defense, and Ivy/Hartman’s treatment of persons still eligible for compensation, do not supply the missing protection of that adverse allocation interest. This is an application of the preexisting constitutional representation requirement, not a general reopening rule for unsuccessful settlement beneficiaries.

The judgment leaves Stephenson’s individual action free of this settlement’s preclusion and dependent injunction bar. It preserves the settlement’s operation for persons it validly binds. This affirmation is limited to the original defect; it does not adopt respondents’ asserted continuing scientific-adjustment duty or determine the alleged later reserve diversion.

## D3 — original proceedings, later information, and later conduct

All seven reject an inquiry confined to the instant of initial certification. Adequate representation must persist through the original litigation stages that define and dispose of the affected interest, including settlement approval and allocation. A representative arrangement adequate for litigating a common defense may become inadequate when the interests divide over distributing a recovery. That possibility is not proof that it occurred; it identifies the correct time and subject of the inquiry.

The following distinctions control each commitment:

1. **Later manifestation:** A later diagnosis can identify a claimant affected by a risk and cutoff already present in the original arrangement. Diagnosis does not itself establish original inadequacy or create a new subgroup entitled to reopen a valid settlement.
2. **Later legal decisions:** Amchem and Ortiz may illuminate the application of the preexisting representation requirement to conflicting bargaining interests. Their distinct Rule 23 predominance and limited-fund holdings, or an asserted new certification requirement, cannot independently undo an otherwise binding final judgment. Hansberry and Shutts supplied the constitutional representation foundation before the Agent Orange settlement.
3. **Later science:** Improved evidence of causation does not by itself show that original representatives were constitutionally inadequate. The inquiry concerns what interests and uncertainties the original arrangement had to represent, not an obligation to bargain with knowledge acquired years later.
4. **Postjudgment administration:** Respondents also alleged an unfulfilled adjustment promise and a later disposition of the reserve. Those are genuine arguments made, not findings adopted here. A challenge to an authorized later modification or a breach of an established continuing duty requires its own supported terms, conduct, and available remedy. It cannot silently become proof that the original settlement lacked binding authority. The affirming three do not need that theory; the remanding four do not direct relief on it.

For the four remanding Justices, the appellate court must apply these distinctions while deciding whether an original constitutional defect was established. For the three affirming Justices, the original allocation supplies the sufficient ground, so later diagnoses disclose its operation without supplying a new defect. Neither group adopts automatic reopening based on later law, changed science, or exhaustion of funds.

## O1 — Oakley's procreation condition

### Operative source and law

The condition must be reviewed with the Wisconsin Supreme Court's construction. Its majority holds that good-faith effort and compliance with support duties according to ability satisfy the substantive condition; poverty or inability alone does not defeat compliance. That is an authoritative state construction, not a proposed federal amendment. The written sentence nevertheless makes additional parenthood conditional on a showing to the court concerning support of existing children and the additional child's needs. No supplied order deletes that showing or substitutes an ordinary payment condition. Sources: 2001 WI 103, paragraphs 1, 6, 15 note 21, 20 and notes 28–30, 48; 2001 WI 123, paragraph 3 and paragraph 5 note 1.

The six commitments favoring relief below accept the good-faith construction and address the residual judicial permission requirement on that basis. They do not hold that the majority preserved a categorical wealth qualification. The two commitments favoring affirmance treat the same showing as a limited compliance-verification mechanism: ability-sensitive present support and good-faith observance of the lawful support obligation for an additional child, not proof of wealth, full satisfaction of all arrears, or a discretionary family-fitness license. Neither position assumes a later amendment to the sentence.

Skinner establishes the fundamental character of procreation even in the setting of criminal punishment; its sterilization holding is not an automatic rule invalidating every temporary restriction. Turner v. Safley requires a justified relationship between retained family rights and correctional interests and rejects a universal forfeiture premise. Bearden distinguishes willful nonpayment from inability despite bona fide efforts. Griffin v. Wisconsin recognizes probation's conditional liberty and justified supervision but concerns the particular search setting; it does not itself decide this condition. Zablocki supplies the closely related example of a direct family-status permission requirement tied to support, with direct collection and punishment available. Its nonprobation setting is a material distinction.

Gerber, March 25, 2003, forecloses reliance on the proposition that imprisonment necessarily extinguishes all procreation protection. Its Turner holding concerns a pleaded no-contact, privately arranged proposal in prison and does not prescribe the scrutiny level for probation. Scalia and Thomas's published dissent distinguishes retained liberty from a claim to institutional assistance; no institutional assistance is requested here. Troxel's controlling proposition concerns fit parents and does not decide the rights of a person convicted of nonsupport. Its individual grounds remain relevant evidence of the Justices' treatment of direct family interference, with those limits preserved. Glucksberg's particular assistance holding supplies no exclusive historical methodology for all liberties. Owens and Newman concern their own reproductive-care framework; none of these commitments imports Casey's abortion framework into probation.

### Competing legal paths and disposition

**Relief path.** Even accepting the ability-sensitive construction, the separate bar on additional parenthood pending a judicial showing unnecessarily makes a retained fundamental family decision the means of enforcing support. The State can supervise earnings, require lawful payments and punish deliberate refusal; that refusal remains unlawful whether or not another child is born. Conviction supplies the basis for those restraints, but does not by itself establish why a separate reproductive permission requirement is a properly fitted means of rehabilitation. The relationship to future support is real, but the condition reaches conception or birth before a new intentional failure has occurred and places an additional family decision under judicial clearance. The remedy is reversal of the judgment sustaining this condition and remand to remove that condition, with lawful support enforcement and otherwise unaffected convictions preserved. This conclusion does not require a finding that Oakley is unable to pay or a categorical rule against all conditions affecting family life.

**Strongest objection to relief.** The condition addresses the very conduct of conviction, protects existing and future children, lasts only during probation and can be satisfied by genuine compliance rather than wealth. Probation need not be the least restrictive means; forcing renewed intentional nonsupport before intervention could defeat preventive rehabilitation. The reply adopted by the six relief commitments is specific: the surviving prior-court-showing term adds a direct reproductive restraint whose justification is not supplied by the authority to enforce the underlying payment duty. The demonstrated intentional violations support stronger direct enforcement, but the payment record and actual construction do not establish that prospective reproductive clearance is needed to accomplish it. The available-prison comparison cannot supply the missing justification under Turner and Gerber.

**Affirmance path.** Under the authoritative good-faith construction, a finite, offense-related condition can require this convicted parent to demonstrate observance of existing legal duties before incurring additional support responsibilities. It is a supervisory restraint directed at deliberate evasion, not permanent incapacity or a rule rationing parenthood by income. Its operative showing verifies lawful efforts within ability and an undertaking to meet the additional lawful obligation; it cannot demand financial sufficiency that the majority expressly disclaimed. The convictions, substantial arrears and supported disregard of court obligations supply a reasonable rehabilitative and protective connection. Affirmance leaves the condition effective at that construed scope.

**Strongest objection to affirmance.** The written permission term is distinct from intentional nonsupport, and its future-support language could otherwise put a childbearing decision behind an unattainable financial forecast. Gerber removes categorical extinction through imprisonment as a sufficient answer, and Skinner protects procreation in the criminal setting. Scalia and Thomas answer that the controlling construction supplies the ability-sensitive limit now; judicial verification of that compliance is itself a reasonable part of this probation, not an unresolved power to demand prosperity. They reject affirmance resting only on the abstract greater power to imprison. If assembly instead treats the operative showing as proof of sufficient resources to meet every need or as discretionary permission despite bona fide lawful effort, neither affirmative commitment below supports that different condition.

**Clarification alternative.** A remand merely to ask the State to repeat or implement its good-faith construction is unnecessary to the frozen positions. The six relief positions conclude that the condition fails even under that construction. The two affirmative positions regard its compliance standard as sufficiently authoritative to sustain the surviving verification requirement at that limited scope. No vote rests on an invented amended order. A newly established materially different operative term would require fresh neutral treatment.

## T3 — Star Scientific's Commerce Clause theories

### Separate inquiries and source status

The territorial theory, a geographic-discrimination theory, and the actual incidental tracing burden are distinct. A sale by Star to an independent distributor outside Virginia can lead to a covered sale to a Virginia consumer; that does not turn a cigarette never sold into Virginia into a covered unit. The supplied measure contains no outside price-parity requirement. Healy and Brown-Forman require examination of actual external control, not a rule that any upstream commercial effect is invalid. BMW's territorial punishment rule concerns a different governmental act and does not exempt local consumer sales from security requirements.

The relevant register is **Discriminatory routing, charges and linked subsidies in interstate commerce**. Its existing origin-discrimination, private-processing and integrated-local-subsidy components are supplied by Oregon Waste (April 4, 1994), Carbone (May 16, 1994) and West Lynn Creamery (June 17, 1994). Its specified present-operation additions are SSC (March 11, 1996) and Waste Management (May 27, 1998); Tracy (February 18, 1997) and Waste Management supply the stated current-wording limits. Those later public-service and purchasing rules do not supply a new tobacco exception. West Lynn requires examination of the whole competitive arrangement. Here settlement status is not a geographic classification; the supplied operation does not reserve the benefit to Virginia producers or burden products according to outside origin. That conclusion rests on the actual supplied scheme rather than an immunity for facially equal wording. Walsh, May 19, 2003, reinforces the actual transaction and competitor inquiries.

For the incidental-burden claim, Pike asks whether the interstate burden is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits, taking account of their nature and whether they can be served as well with less interstate impact. Sorrell, March 17, 2003, independently preserves that inquiry despite local reach and evenhandedness. Star's claim that it must police distribution or change contracts must be addressed as such, not converted into only a loss-of-capital objection.

Virginia's existing excise-stamp system requires distributors to record and report the Virginia units. That statutory mechanism is established by the published recitation. The lower court's further conclusion that acquiring the information adds only a minimal burden is an inference at dismissal, not a stipulation or trial-tested finding. The source supplies neither each manufacturer's data-access entitlement nor an actual tracing-cost calculation. Sources: Star slip opinion pages 19–24.

### Frozen result and adversarial alternatives

All eight Associates reject the supplied extraterritorial and discrimination theories and support affirmance of the Commerce dismissal, with the differing Pike grounds recorded below. The six conventional Pike positions accept that obtaining distribution information and arranging contracts can impose real commercial burdens; they do not adopt a factual finding that every such burden is minimal. In the challenged statutory operation, those costs identify where the manufacturer's products entered the very consumer market that creates the security obligation. The strongest remand path is that a court deciding Rule 12(b)(6) cannot resolve an adverse factual inference about access to independent distributors' information merely because records exist.
