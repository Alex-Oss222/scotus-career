## Record and scope

The asserted disclosures concern December 1993 and January 1994. Herring was on federal probation for driving while intoxicated on federal property. The complaint alleges that he told Keenan he had taken an HIV test and thought he might be infected but had not received the result. He never told her that the result was positive and authorized no disclosure. The employer communications allegedly asserted positive status and pressed for termination. The sister communication was alleged to concern his having been tested and/or being positive; it must not be converted into an unequivocal allegation of only the latter. The separate roommate allegation was withdrawn at the April 2, 1997 hearing and is outside this review.

The original claim began December 21, 1995. Herring died July 23, 1996, and his sister continued as estate representative. On the second amended complaint the district court dismissed the Eighth Amendment, distinct liberty and declaratory claims, but refused to dismiss the informational-privacy damages claim. Review is of a legal qualified-immunity dismissal issue on the complaint's assumptions, not a trial verdict, a proven infection, or a damages award. No factfinding resolves motive or actual public-health risk here.

The lower majority characterized Keenan's actions as limited, protective disclosure of voluntarily supplied information. The dissent disputed the adequacy of that characterization under the alleged facts, stressing the absence of a confirmed result, ordinary food handling and family contact, the actual probation offense and contrary official guidance. Each is a lower judicial characterization. Neither a punitive motive nor an adequate supervisory purpose is supplied as an established fact. The government's legal misunderstanding is alleged; whether that establishes objective constitutional notice is a separate legal question.

The source appendix supplies the complete quoted probation guidance, copied with this handoff. It does not impose absolute secrecy. The HIV provisions address informed written consent, state-law-permitted notice to public-health officials, defined custody transfers, legitimate professional staff access, relevant presentence information, and confidential advice to the court. The distinct third-party-risk provisions require a reasonably foreseeable risk linked to the actual relationship and criminal background, permit different responses to that risk, require specified supervisory approval, and provide court involvement when employment exclusion or disclosure is opposed. The precise provisions retain their separate triggers and exceptions. No administrative violation itself creates the federal constitutional right or defeats immunity.

## Date-eligible notice sources and competing grounds

The lower opinion's complete majority, dissent and appendix are preserved in `entering-law/chunk9-a-sources/herring-218-f3d-1171.txt`; Eastwood is also preserved there. They are primary opinion reproductions. Lower citations establish research leads and each court's actual characterization, not a new Supreme Court rule.

Eastwood, 846 F.2d 627 (1988), denied immunity for an intrusive sexual-history inquiry and associated harassment lacking a sufficient governmental relation; it expressly required applying established law to analogous factual settings. Mangels, 789 F.2d 836 (1986), rejected confidentiality for accurate information about unlawful drug activity and cannot be treated as invalidating every disclosure. Slayton, 726 F.2d 631 (1984), and Flanagan, 890 F.2d 1557 (1989), supply additional circuit privacy context at their actual scopes. Whalen and Nixon preserve legitimate safeguarded governmental information uses rather than absolute confidentiality.

The probation-specific counterargument rests on Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868 (1987), whose Fourth Amendment holding permitted a search under a valid probation regulation serving supervision's special needs. The estate's answer emphasizes Griffin's express limit on permissible intrusion: probation is not the loss of all constitutional protection, and an assumed illegitimate disclosure is different from a properly justified search. Neither argument may silently turn a Fourth Amendment search holding into a fully resolved medical-disclosure rule.

Other preconduct sources identified and substantively discussed below include Harris v. Thigpen, 941 F.2d 1495 (1991), which upheld HIV segregation in light of the prison's supported transmission risks; United States v. Stine, 675 F.2d 69 (1982), which upheld a related counseling condition; Woods v. White, 689 F. Supp. 874 (1988), addressing unjustified inmate medical disclosure; Doe v. Borough of Barrington, 729 F. DeSanti, 653 F.2d 1080 (1981), rejects a general constitutional nondisclosure right. Those sources must be weighed at their actual hierarchy and factual scope rather than counted.
