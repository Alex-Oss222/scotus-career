## Specific continuity findings

### Threats, protected expression and publication

**Black and ACLA.** ACLA's December 10 holding requires attributable contextual threatening meaning and, for its reviewed public-expression civil liability, purpose or knowledge of that meaning. Its six-vote culpability rule remains controlling in that setting; Stone's recklessness proposal is not the rule. Black's five-Justice permission concerns Virginia's express intent-to-intimidate offense and requires an actual serious threat and the statutory intent proved beyond reasonable doubt. It neither reduces ACLA's civil floor nor selects a minimum culpability for a criminal statute lacking that express element. Black's rejection of its particular by-itself charge preserves ACLA's separation of contextual threat, personal attribution and the findings actually made by the relevant adjudicator. The fractured facial theories in Black create no broader controlling invalidation rule.

American Amusement protects violent expressive games and rejects a new unprotected category based on violence or interactivity alone. Black addresses an actual intentional threatening communication, not violent imagery or ideological hostility alone; the two rules are consistent. The later Center for Reproductive Law and Policy decision concerns independently financed lawful advocacy and collaboration, not threats. Its viewpoint-disqualification holding therefore remains intact.

**Peavy.** The settlement disposition adds only mootness and equitable-vacatur law as applied to the settled review. It leaves the Fifth Circuit judgment undisturbed without adopting its interception, participant-publisher or First Amendment grounds as Supreme Court law. It changes neither ACLA nor Black, and creates no merits exception to Konop or the existing interception/disclosure framework. Specht likewise adjudicates arbitration and assent, leaving the underlying statutory privacy claims for further proceedings. Neither case's procedural result establishes a privacy violation or defense in the other.

### Searches, confidential information and conduct-date defenses

**Hambrick and Lawall.** Hambrick applies Guest to the claimant's actual restricted-account relationship and governmental acquisition, followed by the separate constitutional and exclusion inquiries. Stone's proposed general ordinary-account extension remains noncontrolling. Lawall concerns authorized outside-agency access to intimate bypass information and requires a sufficient information-recipient-purpose connection with safeguards. That disclosure rule is not a decision about a subscriber's Fourth Amendment interest or criminal exclusion. Neither holding displaces the other, and neither turns mere confidentiality into a universal legal rule.

**Nassau and Roe.** Nassau applies Bell to the identified jail-intake strip and visual body-cavity search of misdemeanor detainees, requiring individualized reasonable suspicion. Roe concerns a nonurgent intimate investigative examination and photography of a child outside that jail-security setting. Its consent, probable-cause/judicial-authorization and exigency requirements remain consistent with Nassau's contextual rule. Both preserve personal consent or suspicion facts, actual search scope and the distinct question of official immunity. Nassau's current fair-warning methodology is compatible with Roe's: all relevant preconduct authority and obvious applications count, without an identical-facts requirement. Neither new 2003 search decision can create notice for Nassau's July 1995 conduct or Roe's July 1999 conduct. Roe's accepted no-consent account and its ordinary factual remand remain undisturbed.

**Gerber, Benson and Lawall.** Gerber preserves a Turner inquiry for the alleged privately financed no-contact proposal. It adopts no heightened general reproductive-liberty standard, universal prison-service entitlement or automatic accommodation. Benson's AEDPA and medication inquiry therefore remains governed by its eligible state-merits-date holdings and supported findings; Gerber cannot supply an earlier clearly established command. Woodford v. Garceau's application-date temporal rule also remains intact. Lawall continues to apply its independent information-protection rule and the existing Casey framework to bypass access. Gerber's confined prison rule neither substitutes for Casey nor enlarges Lawall's remedy.

### Redistricting and preclearance

**Branch and Georgia.** Branch's later-period administrative holding depends on the State ceasing to seek administration of an adjudicative plan; private appellants did not continue the State's administrative submission. Georgia expressly finds that the State still seeks authority to use its original Senate plan. Its live controversy and Rule 24 intervention holdings concern a judicial declaratory action, not revival of an abandoned administrative clock. The different predicates preserve both results.

Branch's Connor ground concerns constitutional examination of an unenforceable state map, including the state court's authority to adopt it. Georgia's constitutional component concerns Congress's authority to impose the federal preclearance requirement itself. It is not a premature merits judgment about the constitutionality of an uncleared map. Georgia's independent effects burden and denial of original-plan clearance are also consistent with Branch's actual-approval prerequisite; a replacement's existence alone does not approve the original change.

**Branch and Old Person.** Branch's section 2c rule concerns a feasible congressional single-member remedy and preserves conditions on a necessary judicial map. Old Person adjudicates section 2 opportunity under an existing Montana legislative plan and rejects anticipated replacement as a substitute for liability analysis. It does not require an at-large congressional election or settle either nonmajority account of section 2a(c)'s remaining operation. Both decisions distinguish actual lawful replacement from an expected future plan and preserve independently justified remedial timing. No five-vote emergency exception is imported from Branch's separate writings.

### Insurance, employment, bankruptcy and agreement

**Miller.** Its eight-Justice two-condition insurance-saving rule replaces the former guidepost method at the stated ERISA scope, while preserving actual exemptions, the deemer boundary and separate federal enforcement requirements. Stone's preference for the earlier framework remains noncontrolling. None of the six previously completed later-date Records decides ERISA insurance saving or relies on the superseded method. Okpalobi's inquiry into particular officers' authority over malpractice protections is a jurisdictional and official-function inquiry, not an ERISA classification or saved-insurance determination. Miller changes no predicate of that remand.

**Stout.** The actual existing later-date decisions after May 19 are Benson, Roe, Center for Reproductive Law and Policy, Georgia and Old Person. None adjudicates Title VII pregnancy-related disparate impact or requires employment statistics contrary to Stout's bounded medical/operational-evidence holding. Stout preserves the comparative causal showing, individual nexus, employer necessity defense, refused-alternative inquiry, class predicates and eventual remedy. It does not establish an unconditional leave entitlement or constitutional reproductive-accommodation rule. Lawall, already effective April 28, deals with governmental bypass-information access and supplies no different rule for Baxter's private attendance practice. Miller's saving clause does not preempt or replace this federal employment claim.

**Archer and Specht.** No previously completed later-date Record resolves the same bankruptcy characterization or electronic-assent issue. That surviving-note setting does not conflict with Peavy's complete settlement of the actions under review: Peavy establishes no surviving debt-characterization controversy.
