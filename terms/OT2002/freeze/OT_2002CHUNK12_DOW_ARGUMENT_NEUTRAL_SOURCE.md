## The collateral-review distinction actually presented

Manufacturers argue that the existing final adequacy adjudications and the procedures used to test the conflict prevent renewed determination from the beginning. Their alternatives are a highly deferential collateral standard and actual substantive adequacy based on the original common defenses, uncertain future claims and distribution process. Claimants respond that a court's approval finding cannot itself establish representative authority over persons who lacked adequate representation; they distinguish the binding effect of a judgment from the precedential weight of another claimant's unsuccessful challenge. Both sides contest whether the prior judicial process actually adjudicated the relevant future-interest conflict.

O'Connor's exchanges at approximately 2381–2452 seconds ask whether adequacy and notice had been litigated before and require clarification of the difference between broad adequacy adjudication and the specific future-claim argument. Kennedy's exchange at approximately 2738–2832 seconds asks what collateral standard applies after prior adjudications and how it differs from direct review. These questions do not establish their answers.

Souter's exchanges at approximately 545–604 and 908–1150 seconds distinguish obtaining the lump-sum settlement from allocating its proceeds, and ask who represented the asymptomatic interests when the allocation was made. His exchange at approximately 2870–2890 seconds distinguishes binding adjudication from the precedential weight of the earlier litigation. Ginsburg's exchanges at approximately 727–835 and 1706–1760 seconds likewise ask who represented absent future claimants and distinguish those persons from parties who litigated jurisdiction in the ordinary finality cases. These inquiries do not themselves find inadequate representation.

Breyer's questions examine both sides. At approximately 1420–1521 seconds, he asks why collateral deference follows and whether the proper course, if the Second Circuit treated Amchem as dispositive, would be further review under the appropriate standard. At approximately 2065–2133 seconds, he stresses the difficulty of achieving final settlement if every later claimant may redescribe a group and demand another representative. At approximately 2187–2266 and 2497–2614 seconds, he asks what weight the earlier judicial treatment deserves and distinguishes the earlier compensable claimants from the present claims. At approximately 2999–3046 seconds and 3202–3216 seconds, he tests a position allowing a later claimant to raise representation while giving earlier adjudications precedential force, and asks what disposition follows if Amchem and Ortiz are relevant but not determinative. These are conditional inquiries rather than an announced conclusion. They make both a finality disposition and a correct-standard remand analytically relevant, without supplying a vote for either.
