## Controlling holdings

### I. The agreed interview authorized entry within its own scope

**Holding and operative rule:** An objectively understood, voluntarily arranged home interview permits the identified worker to enter for that interview when she arrives as agreed, absent withdrawal or an expressed limitation. The absence of a renewed express doorstep invitation does not revoke that antecedent permission, but the interview agreement does not authorize intimate bodily examination or photography.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court accepts the mother's account that she did not expressly invite Strickland inside again at the doorway. Her acknowledged earlier agreement to an appointment at home, after learning the worker's identity and welfare purpose, nevertheless supplies affirmative authorization for the interview. The result rests on that agreement and its objective scope, not on silence as consent. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte supplies voluntary consent, and Ohio v. Robinette requires attention to the actual circumstances rather than an indispensable verbal formula. Johnson v. Jones and Behrens v. Pelletier permit assessment of the legal effect of the accepted account, not a credibility choice in the worker's favor. The mother's separate entry claim therefore fails, while the child's distinct search claim requires its own justification.

**Precedent treatment:** Schneckloth and Robinette are applied to voluntary, scope-limited authorization; Johnson and Behrens preserve the assumed-fact boundary of interlocutory legal review.

### II. This nonurgent intimate examination required consent or judicial authorization

**Holding and operative rule:** A nonemergency investigative visual examination and photography of a child's genital and anal areas in these circumstances requires voluntary consent or appropriate judicial authorization supported by probable cause; genuine exigency is an independent exception. The claimant must establish the governmental search, and the official must substantiate consent or another invoked exception; cooperation with official directions does not conclusively establish voluntary consent.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Scalia and Thomas do not reach the constitutional merits; they neither join this rule nor vote that the search was constitutional. Seven direct joins control.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines the actual operation, as Ferguson v. City of Charleston requires, and the demonstrated need, as Chandler v. Miller requires. A protective purpose is substantial, but the challenged operation was intimate exposure, manipulation and photography by a worker without training in that photography, rather than an ordinary interview or superficial observation. The ten-day response classification, absence of claimed urgency or probable cause, and acknowledged medical-examination route supply no necessity for immediate intrusion without ordinary authorization. Griffin and Vernonia allow justified protective arrangements; they do not make a general protective mission sufficient for every investigative method. Statutory reporting duties or possible police contact do not categorically defeat special needs, and Ferguson's coordinated prosecution program is not a fact of this case. Good v. Dauphin County Social Services applies the Fourth Amendment in child-protection investigation; Darryl H. v. Coler likewise retains limits on scope and necessity under its different approach. Consent remains a distinct question. Under Schneckloth and Robinette, the mother's assistance in removing clothing and positioning the child, in response to official direction, must be considered with her questions about necessity and counsel, distress and lack of advance explanation of the eventual scope. These facts permit a finding of submission rather than permission; the Court requires no particular warning or verbal formula and assumes no threat to remove the child. On the supported plaintiff-favorable account of no voluntary consent, this search lacked the required justification. Actual consent remains for ordinary proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:** Ferguson and Chandler are applied to current merits, with their actual-operation and demonstrated-need limits. Griffin and Vernonia retain their justified protective settings; Good supplies child-investigation protection; Darryl H. is distinguished in scope and safeguards; Schneckloth and Robinette require actual voluntary consent.

### III. Pre-July 10, 1999 law supplied fair warning on the accepted account

**Holding and operative rule:** Qualified immunity does not protect the assumed unconsented, highly intimate and nonurgent search because preconduct law made its lack of justification apparent under either plausible search framework. The legal inquiry uses supported plaintiff-favorable facts and law effective before July 10, 1999; materially different established facts remain subject to their own defenses, and later decisions supply no earlier notice.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Scalia and Thomas dissent from reversal of immunity. Seven direct joins control; no fractured-rationale synthesis is used.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton require objective, particularized conduct-date notice. Wilson v. Layne distinguishes violation from personal immunity without demanding a factual twin; Elder v. Holloway requires all relevant preconduct law. Hope v. Pelzer explains how obvious application can give warning, but is not itself July 1999 notice. Before the search, Camara and New Jersey v. T.L.O. subjected civil officials and children's searches to the Fourth Amendment. Bell v. Wolfish required examination of scope, manner, justification and place. Vernonia and Chandler required real contextual justification, while Schneckloth and Robinette required voluntary consent rather than submission. Good specifically rejected a general child-protection exemption. The contrary authorities do not disappear: Darryl H. rejected a categorical warrant requirement and granted immunity despite substantial necessity and consent doubts. Yet it retained reasonableness limits, declined to treat maternal assistance as conclusive consent and identified medical examination for suspected sexual abuse. Landstrom involved a nurse's examination of complained-of soreness and a March 1987 notice date; Wildauer concerned an investigative home visit. Doe v. Louisiana granted Fourteenth Amendment immunity and expressly reserved the Fourth Amendment question. That reservation warrants care but does not affirmatively authorize this distinct nonmedical photography beyond the shared scope, necessity and consent limits. The Court's conclusion therefore does not depend on a settled warrant rule for every protective examination. It rests on the particular intrusion's obvious excess under either approach. Scalia and Thomas regard the immunity outcomes and unresolved local question as dispositive uncertainty; the Court instead finds the shared limits sufficiently concrete for the accepted lack of consent, probable cause and urgency. Ferguson, Wallis, Calabretta, Tenenbaum, Saucier and Hope create no July 1999 warning. Neither actual reading of precedent nor proof of actual reliance is required. No compulsory merits-first sequence is imposed.

**Precedent treatment:** Harlow, Anderson, Wilson and Elder retain objective, context-specific notice. Hope supplies the present method only. The listed preconduct scope and consent rules provide the warning; contrary child-protection cases are considered in their actual settings, not treated as a universal exemption.

### IV. The same entry and search do not create duplicate substantive-due-process claims

**Holding and operative rule:** The mother's home-entry claim and the child's bodily-search claim are governed by the specific Fourth Amendment protection applicable to those acts, not a duplicate substantive-due-process damages theory. Failure to recover on a specific claim, including because of personal immunity, does not supply a separate substantive-due-process remedy for the same intrusion.

**Authority:** Part IV of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham v. Connor and the specific-provision rule preserved in County of Sacramento v. Lewis direct the identified intrusions to their express constitutional source. The record contains neither removal of the child nor a separate coercive displacement of a parental decision beyond the challenged examination. The mother's emotional injury does not itself establish that additional governmental act. This conclusion does not erase a parent's distinct family-liberty interest when a different supported act infringes it. It rejects these duplicative theories concerning the entry and examination, whose actual scope and justification are addressed by the Fourth Amendment.

**Precedent treatment:** Graham and Lewis are applied to the particular acts and claimants; distinct family-liberty claims based on different governmental conduct are not extinguished.

### V. The restored federal claim and state-law claims return in their actual posture

**Holding and operative rule:** Texas official immunity must be assessed under its own law, independently of federal qualified immunity. With the child's federal search claim restored, any decision about supplemental jurisdiction must take account of that surviving claim; the unappealed dismissals of other defendants remain intact.

**Authority:** Part V-A, the independence of Texas immunity, is joined by all nine. Part V-B, the restored-federal-claim remit and its supplemental-jurisdiction consequence, is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer; Scalia and Thomas would instead leave the federal dismissal in place and preserve the independent state-law return.

**Controlling explanation:** The federal notice determination does not decide a Texas defense. The Fifth Circuit's state-law return must be conformed to the federal claim's restored posture, rather than implemented on an assumption that all federal claims have ended. Ordinary proceedings determine actual consent, personal responsibility, causation, injury, liability and remedy. Strickland must substantiate an invoked consent justification on the actual circumstances; the child must prove the actionable search and resulting compensable injury by a preponderance. If the proved facts materially differ from those assumed for the legal appeal, the court applies the governing defenses to those facts. A lawful discretionary refusal of supplemental jurisdiction remains governed by its actual predicates, and a jurisdictional dismissal is without prejudice. The department and other officials whose dismissals were not appealed are not restored by this mandate.

**Precedent treatment:** Johnson and Behrens preserve the difference between legal assumed-fact review and actual liability; the existing objective federal defense does not decide Texas official immunity.

### Review method and component definitions

The legal immunity appeal accepts the supported nonmovant account. Johnson and Behrens prohibit choosing the worker's competing doorstep testimony or resolving factual consent by credibility. The legal significance of the acknowledged scheduling agreement and the legal sufficiency of the plaintiff-favorable examination facts remain reviewable. **R1 — Home entry.** All eight affirm rejection of the mother's separate entry claim. An objectively understood, voluntarily arranged home interview authorized entry for that interview when the identified worker arrived as agreed, absent withdrawal or an expressed limitation. The accepted absence of a second express doorstep invitation does not revoke that earlier authorization. Silence alone would not supply consent, but the result rests on the antecedent affirmative appointment, not silence. The agreement's scope does not extend to intimate examination and photography.

**R2 — Child's examination merits.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer adopt the following rule and application. A nonemergency, intimate visual examination and photography of a child's genital and anal areas in this investigative setting requires voluntary consent or judicial authorization supported by probable cause; genuine exigent circumstances remain an independent exception. The child-protection purpose does not, on this record, supply a special need justifying this operation without those protections. The agency had time for ordinary investigative and judicial steps; the ten-day classification and the worker's acknowledged medical-examination alternative contradict a need for this immediate intrusion. The alleged manipulation and photography exceeded an ordinary interview or superficial observation, and the worker had no training in this type of photography. No categorical rule follows merely from statutory reporting duties or contact with police. Ferguson's actual-operation rule and Chandler's requirement of a supported need, rather than the historical lower opinion's broader mixed-purpose formula, govern.

On the plaintiff-favorable account, assisting with clothing and positioning in response to official directions does not establish voluntary consent as a matter of law. Her questions about necessity and counsel, distress, and lack of advance explanation of the eventual scope must be considered. The Court creates no requirement of a particular warning or verbal formula and invents no threat to remove the child. Genuine consent remains for ordinary proceedings on the actual facts.

Scalia and Thomas do not reach R2's constitutional merits because their R3 immunity disposition resolves this individual federal claim. They do not endorse the search or vote that it was constitutional.

**R3 — July 1999 notice and personal immunity.** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reverse the child's immunity judgment. Their sufficient notice ground is the obvious excess of the particular assumed search under already established bodily-privacy, scope and consent rules, even allowing the competing special-needs approach. It is not a claim that the warrant rule for every child-protection examination was settled in July 1999. Scalia and Thomas affirm the child-search immunity judgment because they regard that specific application as still insufficiently clear under the preconduct decisions.

**R4 — Substantive due process.** All eight affirm rejection of duplicative substantive-due-process claims arising from these same intrusions. The mother's home-entry interest and the child's bodily-search interest are directly addressed by the Fourth Amendment. The record supplies no child removal or separate coercive displacement of parental decisionmaking beyond the challenged examination. Graham and the specific-provision rule preserved in Lewis govern these acts. A failure to recover on the specific claim, including personal immunity, does not create an alternative substantive-due-process damages remedy. This conclusion does not erase a parent's distinct family-liberty interest when another supported governmental act infringes it; emotional injury alone is not that additional act.

**R5 — State law and remaining proceedings.** All eight preserve separate consideration of the actual state-law claims and Texas official-immunity defense. Federal qualified immunity does not decide Texas immunity. For the six voting to restore the child's federal claim, the case returns for ordinary consent, participation, causation, injury, liability and remedy proceedings, and any supplemental-jurisdiction decision must use that surviving-claim posture rather than presume all federal claims have ended. For Scalia and Thomas, all reviewed federal claims end and the lower court retains the stated option to evaluate Texas immunity or decline supplemental jurisdiction; a jurisdictional dismissal is without prejudice. Unappealed dismissals of other defendants remain outside the return.

### Adversarial comparison: merits and notice are different questions

**Ordinary judicial protection versus special-needs balancing.** The worker's strongest argument is that detecting abuse serves an independently protective function and that applying criminal-search procedures to every initial inquiry can impair protection. Griffin and the actual Vernonia program show that protective settings can justify modified standards. But this examination is not the ordinary inquiry the argument describes. The actual intrusion, lack of emergency, nonmedical photography and available lawful examination route require a justification corresponding to their scope. Ferguson does not disqualify an entire investigation merely because reporting to police is required; it prevents a benign general purpose from substituting for operational justification. The six reach the presented merits question on these facts. Scalia and Thomas regard deciding this disputed constitutional boundary as unnecessary once immunity is granted.

**Deny immunity on the particular obvious excess.** The strongest contrary argument is that Darryl H., Landstrom and Wildauer permitted protective investigations under a reasonableness approach, while Doe v. Louisiana left the Fourth Amendment issue open and found no clearly established family-privacy violation. The six answer by applying the common limits rather than demanding that every earlier court adopt the same warrant formula. By July 1999, Camara and T.L.O. made civil officials and children's searches subject to the Fourth Amendment; Bell supplied scope, manner, justification and place analysis; the Court's own Vernonia and Chandler decisions required genuine contextual justification and retained bodily protection. Schneckloth and the operative Robinette rule required actual voluntary consent rather than submission to official authority. Good's ordinary Fourth Amendment treatment further demonstrated that child protection was not a universal exemption.

Darryl H. is substantial contrary authority on warrants, not on unlimited scope: it retained reasonableness, questioned unnecessary visual examinations, rejected treating maternal assistance as conclusive consent, and described a protocol directing sexual-abuse examinations to physicians. Landstrom involved a school nurse examining a child's complained-of soreness and evaluated March 1987 notice; it did not approve the materially more intrusive nonmedical photography assumed here. Wildauer's investigative home-visit holding does not decide this intimate examination. Doe's Fourteenth Amendment immunity holding and express Fourth Amendment reservation are taken seriously, but an undecided amendment question is not affirmative permission for a search that exceeds the shared reasonableness and consent limits. These distinctions support notice without converting a state protocol or later decision into the source of the federal right.

**Grant immunity despite a possible violation.** Scalia and Thomas give the preconduct lack of a settled child-examination rule greater weight. Darryl H. rejected a categorical warrant/probable-cause requirement and granted immunity despite substantial doubt about necessity and consent; Landstrom again found insufficient particularity for an intimate examination. Doe's local reservation left the application unsettled. The distinction between examining a complained-of area and photographing it is constitutionally significant, but in their view the authorities had not yet made its legal consequence sufficiently definite for personal damages. General intrusion severity plus the present Court's better interpretation cannot eliminate Anderson's particularized-notice requirement. They accept that a factual twin is unnecessary and that no blanket immunity for child-protection workers exists; they find this particular boundary uncertain. They reject using Ferguson, Wallis, Calabretta, Tenenbaum, Saucier or Hope as new July 1999 notice.

The six likewise exclude those later decisions from the notice sources. Hope explains why an obvious application can suffice; it is not itself the conduct-date warning. No Justice assumes an officer must have read or actually relied on a judicial decision. The disagreement concerns the objective clarity of the existing rule at this particular level of application.

## Holdings

### I. The agreed interview authorized entry within its own scope

**Holding and operative rule:** An objectively understood, voluntarily arranged home interview permits the identified worker to enter for that interview when she arrives as agreed, absent withdrawal or an expressed limitation. The absence of a renewed express doorstep invitation does not revoke that antecedent permission, but the interview agreement does not authorize intimate bodily examination or photography.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court accepts the mother's account that she did not expressly invite Strickland inside again at the doorway. Her acknowledged earlier agreement to an appointment at home, after learning the worker's identity and welfare purpose, nevertheless supplies affirmative authorization for the interview. The result rests on that agreement and its objective scope, not on silence as consent. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte supplies voluntary consent, and Ohio v. Robinette requires attention to the actual circumstances rather than an indispensable verbal formula. Johnson v. Jones and Behrens v. Pelletier permit assessment of the legal effect of the accepted account, not a credibility choice in the worker's favor. The mother's separate entry claim therefore fails, while the child's distinct search claim requires its own justification.

**Precedent treatment:** Schneckloth and Robinette are applied to voluntary, scope-limited authorization; Johnson and Behrens preserve the assumed-fact boundary of interlocutory legal review.

### II. This nonurgent intimate examination required consent or judicial authorization

**Holding and operative rule:** A nonemergency investigative visual examination and photography of a child's genital and anal areas in these circumstances requires voluntary consent or appropriate judicial authorization supported by probable cause; genuine exigency is an independent exception. The claimant must establish the governmental search, and the official must substantiate consent or another invoked exception; cooperation with official directions does not conclusively establish voluntary consent.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Scalia and Thomas do not reach the constitutional merits; they neither join this rule nor vote that the search was constitutional. Seven direct joins control.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines the actual operation, as Ferguson v. City of Charleston requires, and the demonstrated need, as Chandler v. Miller requires. A protective purpose is substantial, but the challenged operation was intimate exposure, manipulation and photography by a worker without training in that photography, rather than an ordinary interview or superficial observation. The ten-day response classification, absence of claimed urgency or probable cause, and acknowledged medical-examination route supply no necessity for immediate intrusion without ordinary authorization. Griffin and Vernonia allow justified protective arrangements; they do not make a general protective mission sufficient for every investigative method. Statutory reporting duties or possible police contact do not categorically defeat special needs, and Ferguson's coordinated prosecution program is not a fact of this case. Good v. Dauphin County Social Services applies the Fourth Amendment in child-protection investigation; Darryl H. v. Coler likewise retains limits on scope and necessity under its different approach. Consent remains a distinct question. Under Schneckloth and Robinette, the mother's assistance in removing clothing and positioning the child, in response to official direction, must be considered with her questions about necessity and counsel, distress and lack of advance explanation of the eventual scope. These facts permit a finding of submission rather than permission; the Court requires no particular warning or verbal formula and assumes no threat to remove the child. On the supported plaintiff-favorable account of no voluntary consent, this search lacked the required justification. Actual consent remains for ordinary proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:** Ferguson and Chandler are applied to current merits, with their actual-operation and demonstrated-need limits. Griffin and Vernonia retain their justified protective settings; Good supplies child-investigation protection; Darryl H. is distinguished in scope and safeguards; Schneckloth and Robinette require actual voluntary consent.

### III. Pre-July 10, 1999 law supplied fair warning on the accepted account

**Holding and operative rule:** Qualified immunity does not protect the assumed unconsented, highly intimate and nonurgent search because preconduct law made its lack of justification apparent under either plausible search framework. The legal inquiry uses supported plaintiff-favorable facts and law effective before July 10, 1999; materially different established facts remain subject to their own defenses, and later decisions supply no earlier notice.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Scalia and Thomas dissent from reversal of immunity. Seven direct joins control; no fractured-rationale synthesis is used.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton require objective, particularized conduct-date notice. Wilson v. Layne distinguishes violation from personal immunity without demanding a factual twin; Elder v. Holloway requires all relevant preconduct law. Hope v. Pelzer explains how obvious application can give warning, but is not itself July 1999 notice. Before the search, Camara and New Jersey v. T.L.O. subjected civil officials and children's searches to the Fourth Amendment. Bell v. Wolfish required examination of scope, manner, justification and place. Vernonia and Chandler required real contextual justification, while Schneckloth and Robinette required voluntary consent rather than submission. Good specifically rejected a general child-protection exemption. The contrary authorities do not disappear: Darryl H. rejected a categorical warrant requirement and granted immunity despite substantial necessity and consent doubts. Yet it retained reasonableness limits, declined to treat maternal assistance as conclusive consent and identified medical examination for suspected sexual abuse. Landstrom involved a nurse's examination of complained-of soreness and a March 1987 notice date; Wildauer concerned an investigative home visit. Doe v. Louisiana granted Fourteenth Amendment immunity and expressly reserved the Fourth Amendment question. That reservation warrants care but does not affirmatively authorize this distinct nonmedical photography beyond the shared scope, necessity and consent limits. The Court's conclusion therefore does not depend on a settled warrant rule for every protective examination. It rests on the particular intrusion's obvious excess under either approach. Scalia and Thomas regard the immunity outcomes and unresolved local question as dispositive uncertainty; the Court instead finds the shared limits sufficiently concrete for the accepted lack of consent, probable cause and urgency. Ferguson, Wallis, Calabretta, Tenenbaum, Saucier and Hope create no July 1999 warning. Neither actual reading of precedent nor proof of actual reliance is required. No compulsory merits-first sequence is imposed.

**Precedent treatment:** Harlow, Anderson, Wilson and Elder retain objective, context-specific notice. Hope supplies the present method only. The listed preconduct scope and consent rules provide the warning; contrary child-protection cases are considered in their actual settings, not treated as a universal exemption.

### IV. The same entry and search do not create duplicate substantive-due-process claims

**Holding and operative rule:** The mother's home-entry claim and the child's bodily-search claim are governed by the specific Fourth Amendment protection applicable to those acts, not a duplicate substantive-due-process damages theory. Failure to recover on a specific claim, including because of personal immunity, does not supply a separate substantive-due-process remedy for the same intrusion.

**Authority:** Part IV of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham v. Connor and the specific-provision rule preserved in County of Sacramento v. Lewis direct the identified intrusions to their express constitutional source. The record contains neither removal of the child nor a separate coercive displacement of a parental decision beyond the challenged examination. The mother's emotional injury does not itself establish that additional governmental act. This conclusion does not erase a parent's distinct family-liberty interest when a different supported act infringes it. It rejects these duplicative theories concerning the entry and examination, whose actual scope and justification are addressed by the Fourth Amendment.

**Precedent treatment:** Graham and Lewis are applied to the particular acts and claimants; distinct family-liberty claims based on different governmental conduct are not extinguished.

### V. The restored federal claim and state-law claims return in their actual posture

**Holding and operative rule:** Texas official immunity must be assessed under its own law, independently of federal qualified immunity. With the child's federal search claim restored, any decision about supplemental jurisdiction must take account of that surviving claim; the unappealed dismissals of other defendants remain intact.

**Authority:** Part V-A, the independence of Texas immunity, is joined by all nine. Part V-B, the restored-federal-claim remit and its supplemental-jurisdiction consequence, is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer; Scalia and Thomas would instead leave the federal dismissal in place and preserve the independent state-law return.

**Controlling explanation:** The federal notice determination does not decide a Texas defense. The Fifth Circuit's state-law return must be conformed to the federal claim's restored posture, rather than implemented on an assumption that all federal claims have ended. Ordinary proceedings determine actual consent, personal responsibility, causation, injury, liability and remedy. Strickland must substantiate an invoked consent justification on the actual circumstances; the child must prove the actionable search and resulting compensable injury by a preponderance. If the proved facts materially differ from those assumed for the legal appeal, the court applies the governing defenses to those facts. A lawful discretionary refusal of supplemental jurisdiction remains governed by its actual predicates, and a jurisdictional dismissal is without prejudice. The department and other officials whose dismissals were not appealed are not restored by this mandate.

**Precedent treatment:** Johnson and Behrens preserve the difference between legal assumed-fact review and actual liability; the existing objective federal defense does not decide Texas official immunity.

## Law After Decision

The bodily-search holding requires voluntary consent, probable cause with appropriate judicial authorization, or genuine exigency for the nonurgent intimate investigative operation addressed here. It does not impose one procedure on every protective inquiry or make police reporting alone decisive. The scheduled interview authorizes only its objective scope. On the accepted no-consent, no-probable-cause and no-urgency account, pre-July 10, 1999 law gave sufficient warning for the child-search claim to proceed. Actual liability remains subject to the proved facts and ordinary defenses. The same intrusions do not support duplicate substantive-due-process damages theories. Texas official immunity remains independent, and jurisdictional treatment must reflect the restored federal claim.

## Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs in Parts I, IV and V-A and dissents from the reversal of child-search immunity and the consequential federal-claim remit. They affirm the interview-entry and duplicative-due-process rulings and preserve the independence of Texas immunity. They would affirm dismissal of all reviewed federal claims and return the state-law claims for evaluation of Texas immunity or a lawful decision declining supplemental jurisdiction, with a jurisdictional dismissal without prejudice. They do not decide the child's Fourth Amendment merits. In their view Anderson's particularized-notice requirement is not satisfied by the general gravity of this intrusion. Darryl H. rejected a categorical warrant-and-probable-cause requirement and granted immunity despite serious questions about necessity and consent; Landstrom likewise found insufficient clarity for an intimate examination. Doe v. Louisiana's local Fourth Amendment reservation left the application unsettled. Nonmedical photography is a material distinction, but the preconduct authorities had not made its personal-damages consequence sufficiently definite by July 10, 1999. A factual twin is unnecessary, all relevant other-circuit authority remains relevant, and child-protection workers have no blanket immunity. Nevertheless, the present Court's stronger merits interpretation cannot cure the earlier uncertainty. Ferguson, Wallis, Calabretta, Tenenbaum, Saucier and Hope cannot supply retrospective notice. Their position finds neither voluntary consent nor constitutional approval of the search.
