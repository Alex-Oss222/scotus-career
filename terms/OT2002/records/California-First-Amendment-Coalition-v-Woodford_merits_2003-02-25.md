## Holdings

### Qualified access covers the continuous execution

**Holding and operative rule:** Public and press witnesses admitted to an execution have a qualified First Amendment right to observe the continuous proceeding from chamber entry through death, including integral restraint and intravenous preparation. The right concerns observation by admitted witnesses, subject to lawful admission, conduct and anonymity controls.

**Authority:** Breyer's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg; six complete joins control both holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the initial procedures are part of the execution the witnesses attend. Richmond Newspapers and Press-Enterprise connect access with historical openness and informed public oversight. El Vocero directs attention to the type of proceeding and its function, rather than one jurisdiction's practice alone. The tradition of witnessed executions, including California's observation of corresponding preparations under earlier methods, supports continuity. Intravenous technology does not turn the restraint and invasive preparation into a different closed administrative event. Observation informs the public about pain, difficulties and the actual administration of the sentence; an official account is not an equivalent. Pell and Houchins reject general prison-access demands, but these witnesses seek to observe the particular governmental act to which they are already admitted. United Reporting preserves that distinction between general information and an independently protected activity. The continuing policy supplies prospective injury, and the earlier remand permitted the developed factual showing.

### The categorical curtain fails even contextual prison review

**Holding and operative rule:** Procedure 770's categorical concealment of those stages is an exaggerated response on the supported trial findings and fails Turner: it lacks an adequate connection to the asserted justification, supplies no equivalent alternative observation, and disregards workable anonymity measures whose supported operational costs do not justify the blackout. Turner requires contextual review of connection, alternatives, accommodation effects and ready alternatives; it does not require the least restrictive means.

**Authority:** The same six-Justice opinion controls.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deference requires consideration of the record, not acceptance of an untested assertion. Shaw preserves meaningful application of Turner's factors. Here live testimony and demonstrations supported the finding that caps, masks and gloves could protect anonymity. Officials' concerns about struggles, communication and recruitment were considered; disagreement with the credited evidence establishes no clear error. The absence of demonstrated identification followed by retaliation is relevant but does not require officials to wait for an attack. Avoiding an appearance of brutality is not a legitimate reason to conceal the process. Witnesses have no equivalent means to observe the hidden acts, and the supported accommodation costs do not justify this blanket response. This contextual conclusion answers the dissent's security argument without making protective garb mandatory in every setting. Because the restriction fails the officials' preferred standard, choosing a stronger general closure standard is unnecessary. A materially changed threat would require a supported, fitted response on its own facts.
