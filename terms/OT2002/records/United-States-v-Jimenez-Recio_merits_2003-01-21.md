## Controlling holdings

### I. Unknown government frustration does not itself terminate a conspiracy

**Holding and operative rule:** A drug conspiracy under 21 U.S.C. §846 does not automatically terminate when government action unknown to its members makes the intended transaction impossible; pre-seizure joinder is therefore not a categorical statutory element. The prosecution must still prove beyond a reasonable doubt an actual unlawful agreement and the defendant’s knowing participation: mere presence, association, awareness, or an arrangement solely with government agents is insufficient, and actual withdrawal, abandonment, termination, and entrapment retain their independent requirements.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court, joined by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Direct unanimous agreement on this proposition does not establish unanimous agreement on the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Shabani construes §846 as an agreement-based offense requiring no additional overt act. Salinas supplies the bounded distinction between agreeing to advance an unlawful undertaking and personally completing its acts, within its different RICO setting. Secret seizure changes the plan’s prospects without necessarily changing the private agreement or a participant’s knowing commitment. The Ninth Circuit’s Cruz rule incorrectly makes objective attainability a continuing legal condition and pre-seizure joinder an additional requirement. The defendants’ concern about manufactured liability identifies an actual evidentiary question: a police-only arrangement is not a genuine private conspiracy. The answer is proof of the unlawful agreement and knowing joinder, together with applicable defenses, rather than legal extinction of every agreement when police secretly remove its object. Actual abandonment or withdrawal must be distinguished from an unknown external obstacle. The Court rejects the categorical rule without supplying the factual agreement necessary for these convictions.

### II. Correction of the appellate legal rule preserves the consequences of the trial proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court may correct the court of appeals’ automatic-termination rule in deciding the granted statutory question without thereby granting relief for an unobjected-to trial instruction or extinguishing the legal consequences of the Government’s accepted trial burden and procedural choices. Those consequences remain for resolution on properly maintained appellate grounds, rather than being treated as irrelevant or conclusively resolved by the substantive correction.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stone-Zsela does not join this additional procedural rationale, and Stevens dissents from it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Government did not object before jury retirement to the second-trial instruction, accepted pre-seizure joinder as its burden in closing, did not initially attack the circuit rule on appeal, and first challenged it on rehearing. The then-applicable Rule 30 objection requirement and the consequence of that accepted burden are real procedural questions. Stevens would treat them as sufficient to preserve the existing insufficiency reversal. The Court instead distinguishes the granted correction of a court of appeals’ statutory rule from a ruling authorizing relief for instructional error. Shabani’s limited appellate return and Quality King’s separation of a legal correction from unadjudicated grounds support leaving distinct questions to their proper disposition. This correction neither assumes government plain-error authority nor decides that nonobjection establishes intentional relinquishment. It also does not select a sufficiency benchmark that disregards the Government’s accepted instruction. The court of appeals must give the procedural history its proper effect on genuinely remaining claims.

### III. The limited reversal requires resolution of remaining proper appellate questions

**Holding and operative rule:** The appellate judgment is reversed only insofar as it rests on automatic termination, and the court of appeals must address remaining properly maintained sufficiency, instructional, and other trial objections from the existing trial record before reinstating the convictions or directing further proceedings. If legally required proof was insufficient, the remedy must respect the bar on another opportunity to supply missing proof; any other trial remedy requires its own lawful predicate.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stevens dissents from this remedial disposition while joining Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Shabani’s remand preserves actual appellate objections after rejection of an added statutory element. Here the legal error concerns automatic termination; the convictions’ ultimate treatment also depends on claims with distinct preservation and remedial requirements. The remand preserves only arguments properly maintained, not a new right to revive forfeited issues. Both actual statutory elements and the legal consequences of the trial and appellate history remain relevant within those claims. Correcting the termination rule supplies no missing evidence and does not itself authorize a third trial. The court of appeals must resolve the remaining proper grounds and apply the corresponding sufficiency, instruction, and double-jeopardy consequences. That is ordinary appellate work under the governing rules, rather than an unresolved definition of the §846 agreement offense.

The court of appeals must resolve the remaining proper grounds and apply the corresponding sufficiency, instruction, and double-jeopardy consequences. That is ordinary appellate work under the governing rules, rather than an unresolved definition of the §846 agreement offense.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Shabani, November 1, 1994: applied to preserve actual agreement, knowing participation, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the absence of an additional overt-act requirement. Its limited remand supplies the model for resolving remaining appellate grounds.
- Salinas v. United States, December 2, 1997: applied only by analogy for agreement rather than personal completion; its distinct RICO elements are not substituted for §846.
- Quality King Distributors, Inc. v. L’anza Research International, Inc., March 9, 1998: the seven-Justice procedural portion applies the distinction between correcting a categorical legal premise and deciding remaining independent grounds; its copyright rule is not extended to conspiracy liability.
- United States v. Cruz, 127 F.3d 791: the lower court’s automatic-termination rule is rejected. Unknown police frustration no longer supplies its categorical pre-seizure-joinder requirement.

## Law After Decision

Effective January 21, 2003, unknown governmental frustration of the unlawful object does not automatically terminate a §846 agreement. Genuine agreement and knowing joinder remain indispensable, with their ordinary proof and defense requirements. The Court adds that legal application to Shabani without changing the offense to punish mere presence or an agreement only with agents. Correction of the appellate rule leaves the independent legal consequences of accepted instructions, preservation, sufficiency, and double jeopardy for proper appellate treatment. The statute’s rule is settled; the remaining work concerns these convictions under that rule and the applicable procedural protections.

## Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He agrees that §846 does not make a conspiracy end automatically upon secret government seizure. He would nevertheless preserve the defendants’ appellate victory. Rule 30 required a timely instructional objection before jury retirement, but the Government made none, affirmatively accepted pre-seizure joinder as its closing burden, and first attacked the circuit rule on rehearing after not challenging it in its initial appellate presentation. For Stevens, characterizing the requested relief as correction of appellate doctrine does not justify removing an insufficiency reversal reached under the very trial standard the Government accepted. His own procedural objection, also reflected in his Williams dissent, supplies the ground for leaving that judgment in place now; it neither endorses automatic termination nor finds that these defendants could never join a real conspiracy. He rejects the Court’s limited reversal because he regards the established procedural history as already sufficient to preserve the result.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit after reversal only of the automatic-termination basis. That court must resolve remaining properly maintained appellate grounds from the trial record, including the proper consequences of the instruction and the Government’s procedural conduct, and then determine the lawful treatment of the convictions. Neither reinstatement nor another trial follows automatically from this mandate; a remedy for insufficient legally required proof must preserve the applicable bar against a second opportunity to supply it.
