## Holdings

### Effective electoral opportunity under Section 5

**Holding and operative rule:** A covered jurisdiction must prove by a preponderance that its voting change does not worsen protected voters' effective electoral opportunity relative to the lawful operative benchmark. Reliable crossover and coalition support can preserve that opportunity without a fixed demographic threshold, but predicted influence, sympathetic officeholders, committee positions, or hoped-for policy gains cannot compensate for a demonstrated loss of practical ability to elect preferred representatives; a statewide increase in nominal districts does not itself establish preservation.

**Authority:** Part II's full rule and application are adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All nine separately adopt the benchmark, ordinary burden, and rejection of numerical shortcuts, but the influence boundary has five direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Beer protects effective exercise of the franchise against deterioration. Voters' dependable ability to choose representatives differs from an expectation that officials they cannot choose will attend to their interests. A plan may rearrange districts or replace one opportunity with multiple genuine coalition opportunities when evidence establishes preserved or strengthened effective opportunity for affected voters. The inquiry examines actual electoral conditions and reliable cross-group support, without a mandatory statistical threshold or a guarantee of victory. Georgia's 1997 plan is the benchmark. Under Georgia's counting convention and current census data, Black voting-age shares in Districts 2, 12, and 26 fell from about 60.58%, 55.43%, and 62.45% to 50.31%, 50.66%, and 50.80%; registration fell below half in each. Those figures matter with the local polarization and crossover evidence, not as a racial floor. The statewide 44.3% estimate does not resolve contrary local conditions. Legislators' support deserves weight but cannot waive voters' protection or establish missing electoral capacity. The lower court considered the competing counting conventions, registration, political testimony, and local operation; its finding of inadequate proof is supported under ordinary factual review. The separately cleared plans' percentage reductions confirm that the rule does not freeze concentrations. Georgia has not proved preserved opportunity in the affected districts or commensurate effective gains elsewhere. Material uncertainty therefore remains with the State, which bears the ordinary burden. Clearance of the original plan is properly denied.

### Preventive enforcement and the separate purpose inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may require covered jurisdictions to prove nonretrogression in effective racial voting opportunity without a new constitutional violation being established for each proposed change. That preventive requirement is valid as applied here because it preserves opportunity without mandating racial maximization, a fixed majority, or permanent boundaries.

**Authority:** Part III, adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, controls. The other four Justices reserve the constitutional question rather than vote to invalidate §5.

**Controlling explanation:** South Carolina v. Katzenbach and City of Rome sustain preventive preclearance, including an effects inquiry reaching conduct not independently unconstitutional. Boerne preserves those authorities. Georgia's contention that this application freezes racial districting mistakes the rule: reliable coalition opportunity can satisfy it, and other plans with reduced racial percentages were cleared. Section 5's benchmark and burden remain independent of constitutional injury and of §2's prerequisites. Denial on effects neither finds nor excuses unlawful purpose. The Bossier decisions' independent prohibition of unconstitutional racial dilution purpose remains governing law; a fresh purpose finding is unnecessary because failure on effects independently defeats the requested declaration.

### Live controversy and intervention

**Holding and operative rule:** A covered jurisdiction's continuing request to use an original plan preserves a live preclearance controversy despite clearance of a different plan. Section 5 does not categorically exclude private-voter intervention in the judicial declaratory action; Rule 24 governs, and the intervention ordered here is lawful on the voters' concrete interests, common issues, and interests not adequately represented by the existing parties.

**Authority:** Part I is adopted by all nine Justices and directly controls the party-status component.

**Controlling explanation:** Georgia still seeks legal authority to use the original plan. Excluding the four voters would alter their continuing party status, so the United States' present agreement with them does not make intervention review advisory. NAACP v. New York applies Rule 24 in judicial preclearance proceedings. The statute's identification of the Attorney General does not displace ordinary civil intervention, and Morris's administrative-review restriction concerns a different route. The record identifies no untimeliness, undue delay, or prejudice defeating this order. The Court sustains the intervention actually granted, without creating an unconditional right for every proposed intervenor. Party status permits defense of the Senate denial; without a cross-appeal it does not permit reversal of the favorable House, congressional, or revised-Senate clearances.

## Law After Decision

Section 5 protects effective electoral choice rather than a fixed racial percentage. Proven coalition opportunity and actual commensurate electoral gains remain available to justify a changed plan; diffuse legislative influence cannot substitute for reliable opportunity removed from affected voters. The State retains the ordinary preclearance burden, and the independent purpose rule remains intact. This holding governs proposed changes generally, while the present judgment leaves only the original Senate plan uncleared. Ordinary Rule 24 intervention remains available in judicial preclearance proceedings.

## Separate Writings

O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, concurs in the live-controversy, intervention, benchmark, burden, and common evidentiary propositions, but dissents from the original-plan affirmance. She reads the statute in force's effective-franchise protection more broadly. Drawing on her Gingles concurrence, she would permit Georgia to show that distributing voters across the plan preserves effective political opportunity through meaningful electoral influence and coalition activity, even without preserving or replacing every existing ability-to-elect opportunity. Legislators' informed judgments and practical political conditions therefore warrant first-instance assessment under that broader standard.

The dissent accepts that the State must prove its asserted opportunity: nominal influence labels, an expected partisan majority, or legislators' approval alone are insufficient. Relevant evidence must persuasively connect the proposed arrangements to actual voter behavior, responsiveness or coalition operation, and practical durability; those considerations are not a compulsory research checklist or numerical exchange formula. The dissent would vacate only the original-Senate denial and remand for the proper statutory comparison under ordinary procedure. It would order neither automatic clearance nor an automatic new trial, and clearance would again be denied if Georgia failed its burden. The revised-plan clearance would remain operative. The dissent would reserve the as-applied constitutional attack until that statutory inquiry was resolved. Its §5 position neither narrows the governing Bossier purpose rule nor adopts a §2 district-allocation holding.
