## Holdings

### I. Relevant persuasive law and obvious applications can provide fair warning

**Holding and operative rule:** Qualified immunity depends on whether relevant law existing when the official acted made the particular federal unlawfulness apparent to a reasonable official. Neither a Supreme-Court-or-controlling-circuit-only rule nor a factual-twin prerequisite is permissible; actual authority, consistency, context, contrary decisions and obvious applications govern, rather than a numerical circuit tally.

**Authority:** Breyer's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source and application inquiries must both be performed. Harlow and Anderson require objective, particularized notice. Lanier permits relevant pre-existing lower-court authority and obviously covered applications; Hope preserves that principle while requiring the specific context to matter. Pinder and Uhlrig likewise require all relevant contemporaneous law rather than a search for identical facts. Later opinions may explain this method but cannot supply warning absent in July 1993. The reviewed en banc court allowed sufficiently strong persuasive authority; the Court does not attribute a contrary categorical rule to it.

### II. The particular gun transfer was not clearly established as unconstitutional in July 1993

**Holding and operative rule:** On the supported plaintiff-favorable account, pre-July-1993 law did not make sufficiently apparent that Carney's affirmative transfer into this escalating dispute constituted his own substantive-due-process deprivation. He therefore receives personal qualified immunity on this damages claim without a ruling adopting the lower court's alternative constitutional merits ground.

**Authority:** Breyer's Part II, joined by all seven other Associate Justices; eight direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the transfer's affirmative character matters but does not by itself establish the required historical clarity. DeShaney rejects a general duty to protect against private violence while leaving distinct official creation of vulnerability for its proper analysis. Reed involved removal of a sober driver while leaving an intoxicated person with the keys; Freeman permitted an amended claim of official interference with otherwise available protection. Their particular control and causal settings do not establish a general rule resolving this gun transfer. Other contemporary cases differed in official conduct and culpability, including deliberate-indifference and gross-negligence formulations.

Stone-Zsela regards supplying the instrumentality of the known danger as an obvious application despite those differences. The Court considers the escalating conflict and affirmative transfer favorably to McClendon, but finds the causal and culpability application insufficiently settled at the time. This record does not establish the more definite premise of knowing instrumental aid to an intended assault. That distinction does not impose a universal purpose-to-harm standard or resolve disputed knowledge against McClendon. The asserted protective explanation is not adopted as a finding of reasonable conduct. Later Lewis, Saenz and custody cases retain their own boundaries and supply no retroactive warning. Because immunity independently resolves the individual claim and Saucier imposes no controlling merits-first sequence, the broader substantive theory need not be decided.

### III. Municipal responsibility remains separate

**Holding and operative rule:** Carney's personal immunity neither establishes nor defeats municipal liability; the City requires its own federal-deprivation, policy or custom, attribution and causation predicates. Its independent judgment remains because no preserved policy, final-policymaker or other demonstrated basis for disturbing that judgment is established in this review.

**Authority:** Breyer's Part III, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an employee's individual transfer is not itself municipal responsibility. Monell requires the City's own attributable policy or custom, and Collins keeps that inquiry distinct from the underlying deprivation. Gibson preserves the same source-specific separation. McClendon's challenge supplies no independent preserved basis for disturbing the City's judgment, so it stands on its own ground rather than borrowing Carney's immunity.
