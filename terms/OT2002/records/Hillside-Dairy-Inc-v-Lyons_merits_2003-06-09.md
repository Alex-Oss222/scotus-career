## Holdings

### Part I — Congressional authorization covers the enacted subjects

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 144 of the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 does not exempt California's challenged milk-pricing and pooling rules from Commerce Clause review. Congress may authorize a particular interstate burden, but permission must clearly encompass that burden; protection of composition and labeling authority does not extend to pricing and pooling merely because those measures operate within one regulatory program.

**Authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: eight direct joins. Thomas separately agrees that the lower court's statutory interpretation was erroneous, while dissenting from the Commerce disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 144 protects California's authority, against direct or indirect limitation under that Act or other law, to establish or continue requirements concerning the percentage of milk solids or solids not fat in fluid milk sold at retail or marketed in California, and labeling of such products concerning those characteristics. The broad introductory protection remains tied to those two enacted subjects. Neither pricing nor contributions to an equalization pool is among them.

South-Central Timber Development v. Wunnicke requires clear congressional permission for the state burden assertedly immunized. New York v. United States and Pic-A-State preserve Congress's authority to give such permission; they do not extend it past Congress's terms. California's strongest argument relies on the integrated operation of its marketing system and the statute's reference to indirect limits. Integration cannot erase the specified subjects. The composition statute and the separate pricing and pooling statutes reinforce that textual distinction.

The Commerce dismissal must therefore be vacated. West Lynn Creamery requires attention to a linked assessment and local distribution when the facts establish that connection; Tracy requires an actual competitive comparison rather than equivalence inferred from a label. Those principles govern ordinary adjudication of the remaining claim. The Court's retained Commerce doctrine supplies the basis for that adjudication despite Thomas's objection to its constitutional foundation.

### Part II — Express citizenship language is not required for Article IV review

**Holding and operative rule:** An individual's Privileges and Immunities Clause claim cannot be dismissed solely because the challenged rule does not expressly classify by residence or citizenship. The individual must establish covered discrimination against nonresident citizens in a protected pursuit; when that predicate is established, the State must show a substantial reason beyond noncitizenship and a close relationship between that reason and the discriminatory treatment.

**Authority:** Part II's affirmative rule and rejection of the categorical dismissal have nine direct joins. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer additionally join Part II's reservation below; Stone-Zsela does not join that reservation and states his broader practical-operation rule separately.

**Controlling explanation:** Chalker v. Birmingham & Northwestern Railway Co. rejected a higher tax tied to an individual's out-of-state principal office because its operation discriminated against nonresident citizens, despite its lack of citizenship terminology. California's facial wording therefore cannot alone defeat the individuals' claims. Lunding supplies the substantial-independent-reason and close-relationship requirements once covered unequal treatment is proved; it preserves genuine relevant distinctions rather than requiring identical treatment in all respects.

California correctly observes that an interstate disadvantage to goods or firms need not establish discrimination against citizens. That point calls for the proper personal and substantive inquiry; it does not restore a categorical facial-language prerequisite. The individuals' covered status, practical disadvantage, appropriate comparison, and any justification require ordinary adjudication on remand. The corporations' separate Article IV exclusion was not challenged and remains undisturbed.

**Question reserved:** Whether Chalker reaches only classifications functioning as proxies for citizenship or every classification producing discriminatory practical effects lies beyond the categorical facial-ground question properly presented at this stage and is not resolved by the eight Justices joining this reservation.

## Precedent Treatment

- South-Central Timber Development, Inc. v. Wunnicke, 467 U.S. 82 (1984): applies the clear-congressional-authorization requirement to the particular interstate burden.
- New York v. United States, June 19, 1992: preserves Congress's power to authorize specified interstate burdens under its actual enacted conditions.
- Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno, April 28, 1997: preserves bounded express congressional permission, without extending it beyond the category Congress authorized.
- West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy, June 17, 1994: preserves examination of a linked assessment and local competitive benefit; its established discriminatory operation is not presumed from this dismissal record.
- General Motors Corp. v. Tracy, February 18, 1997: preserves the need for an actual competitive comparison and its holding concerning materially different regulated service obligations.
- Chalker v. Birmingham & Northwestern Railway Co., 249 U.S. 522 (1919): applies its rejection of a facial-citizenship prerequisite. The broader proxy-versus-practical-effects question remains reserved at this stage by the stated eight-Justice coalition.
- Lunding v. New York Tax Appeals Tribunal, January 21, 1998: applies its substantial-independent-reason and close-relationship requirements at their established scope.

## Law After Decision

Section 144's protection is confined to its composition and labeling subjects; California's pricing and pooling operation remains subject to the established Commerce Clause rules. Eligible individuals may pursue Article IV claims based on actual citizen discrimination despite neutral wording, subject to proof of a protected pursuit, the relevant disadvantage, and the existing justification standard. The eight-Justice reservation preserves the broader proxy-versus-effects question. The remaining discrimination, comparison, justification, and lawful relief require adjudication under the applicable rules.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The Chief Justice joins Part I, Part II's rejection of a facial-citizenship prerequisite and its established justification requirement, and both vacatur-and-remand judgments. He withholds assent to the proxy-versus-effects reservation. His rule requires an individual to establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, covered citizenship, a protected pursuit, a practical disadvantage to nonresident citizens, and its connection to the rule; the State must then establish a substantial reason beyond noncitizenship and a close relationship to the discrimination. Chalker makes operation decisive, and Lunding requires examination of the full treatment and the reasons offered. Interstate business activity alone establishes neither the individual's protected status nor the necessary disadvantage. Corporate Commerce claims remain distinct.

He would give further express instructions for remand. The court should identify each plaintiff's standing and claim, trace the transactions bearing the assessment and the competing producers receiving its linked benefit, and apply West Lynn Creamery if discrimination is proved. California then must establish a legitimate local purpose that adequate reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot serve; protecting local producers from competition is insufficient. An evenhanded measure instead receives Pike's inquiry into whether the actual incidental interstate burden is clearly excessive in relation to putative local benefits. Existing adequate evidence should be used, with development confined to material unresolved facts; ordinary motions may resolve a claim without trial. Any decree must remove the proved unlawful operation consistently with lawful severability. A monetary claim requires payment or loss, an authorized remedial route, and consideration of preserved defenses. His fuller instructions and specified proof formulation are separate positions.

**Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Thomas joins Part II in full and the remand of the individuals' Article IV claims. He agrees independently that §144 does not support the Ninth Circuit's statutory analysis. He nevertheless would affirm dismissal of the Commerce claim because, for the reasons stated in his Camps Newfound/Owatonna dissent, the negative Commerce Clause has no basis in the Constitution's text and provides no lawful ground for invalidating state legislation. That foundational disagreement, not approval of blanket statutory immunity, explains his different Commerce disposition.
