## Controlling law

### I. The Organic Act supplies the federal free-exercise analogue

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 1421b(a) and (u) of Guam’s Organic Act provide the governing federal constitutional free-exercise protection and do not authorize a territorial court to substitute an independently broader test. On the reported neutral, generally applicable character of the importation law, the claimed broader protection must come from a valid separate source such as RFRA.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela’s unanimous opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Organic Act is a federal enactment, so its interpretation presents a federal question rather than an adequate independent local ground. Its borrowed constitutional language and same-force provision carry the federal constitutional analogue. Smith governs genuinely neutral, generally applicable restrictions; Lukumi requires scrutiny of objective targeting and substantial comparable secular underinclusion. Tenafly confirms that actual administration matters to neutrality. The present defense establishes no such separate defect. A territorial charter’s local constitutional function cannot itself change these federal clauses’ meaning. Congress’s ability to provide additional statutory protection is a different question, answered here through RFRA rather than a substituted construction of the Organic Act.

### II. Territorial power sustains RFRA’s express application to Guam

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress’s substantive authority over Guam under Article IV, §3 supports RFRA’s express territorial coverage notwithstanding Boerne’s invalidation of RFRA’s universal State and local command under Fourteenth Amendment §5. Guerrero may invoke that statutory defense, subject to RFRA’s substantive requirements and independent constitutional limits.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Boerne distinguishes enforcement of the Constitution from replacing its substantive standard. Its §5 holding leaves independent powers open. Congress’s legislative power over this territory supplies a substantive source for the additional statutory accommodation, and RFRA expressly includes Guam. Earlier statutory terminology calling a territory a State cannot alter its constitutional status. Dickerson forbids legislative reduction of a constitutional minimum; an additional protection within an independent power presents the converse situation. The Establishment Clause and other independent constitutional limits remain applicable. Other federal applications of RFRA and an independent Establishment Clause objection are outside this adjudication.

### III. Actual religious burden precedes application-specific justification

**Holding and operative rule:** A restriction may substantially burden religious exercise by materially obstructing its practical means even when those means are not doctrinally commanded. Guerrero must prove actual exercise and a substantial burden from this importation restriction; if he does, Guam bears production and persuasion that applying the burden to him furthers a compelling interest through the least restrictive means.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Scientology and Swanner prescribe separate claimant and governmental burdens within RFRA’s valid field. The 2000 exercise definition rejects compulsion or centrality as a condition, and received religious-exercise principles independently reject judicial ranking of theological necessity. Sacramental use may require practical acquisition even when the act of importation is not a prescribed rite. Conversely, sincere adherence and religious use do not establish the purpose of every part of this shipment or the burden of restricting this means of acquisition.

The territorial courts must determine intended use, quantity and seeds, the actual connection to practice, and feasible lawful acquisition alternatives. A practical source matters if established, not if imagined. If substantial burden is proved, the general importance of drug control cannot substitute for Guam’s proof concerning this application and possible accommodations. The trial court’s earlier rejection of justification did not resolve the acquisition distinction. Its usable findings remain available, and any material preserved temporal objection to the 2000 definition must be resolved under the enacted temporal provisions and prosecution posture; the rejection of theological necessity does not depend solely on retroactive application of that amendment. If the burden is proved and Guam fails its justification, the statutory defense defeats the unjustified application; otherwise prosecution may proceed.
