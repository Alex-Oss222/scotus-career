## Event and chronology

Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Ayers, No. 01-963. Argued November 6, 2002; decided March 10, 2003. On certiorari to the Circuit Court of Kanawha County, West Virginia, after the Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia declined review. Six former railroad employees obtained judgments on their asbestosis claims. Norfolk challenges compensation for fear of cancer without probable future cancer and separate physical manifestations of fear, and seeks a deduction for asbestos exposure attributable to nonrailroad employers. The employees seek to preserve their judgments.

All six workers had manifested asbestosis. The trial judge found no claimant reasonably certain to develop cancer and prohibited damages for cancer or increased cancer risk. Medical testimony supplied context for the genuineness of claimed fear, including significantly increased lung-cancer risk with smoking and approximately one-in-ten mesothelioma risk. Five claimants had smoking histories and two continued after diagnosis. The charge permitted compensation for reasonable cancer fear related to proven physical injury from asbestos. Norfolk sought conjunctive requirements of probable cancer and physical manifestations of fear, not a distinct genuine-and-serious instruction or the significantly-impaired-everyday-life-and-work formulation.

The negligence instruction required workplace asbestos exposure contributing, however slightly, to the injury and barred a deduction simply for other employers' exposure. Butler had three months of railroad exposure and thirty-three years as a pipefitter elsewhere; Ayers had several years of exposure in auto-body work. Those different exposures did not establish separable lung injuries. General verdicts ranged from $770,000 to $1.2 million; comparative-negligence reductions for three claimants' smoking and settlement credits yielded final judgments of approximately $4.9 million. Norfolk sought neither a special verdict nor an interrogatory isolating fear damages. The reviewed record supplies no preserved plaintiff-specific sufficiency challenge of the asserted genuine-and-serious character. These preservation facts are affirmative published recitations, not assumptions from missing papers.

## Controlling law

### I. Genuine and serious present fear may accompany manifested disease

**Holding and operative rule:** An employee with actionable asbestosis caused in whole or in part by railroad negligence may recover for genuine and serious cancer fear as present pain and suffering associated with the physical disease, without proving probable future cancer or a separate physical manifestation of fear. Each claimant must prove the disease, actual compensable fear and its connection to the bodily injury or the negligent conduct causing it by a preponderance; diagnosis alone does not establish fear, and the recovery compensates present distress rather than unaccrued cancer or increased disease risk.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Six Justices adopt the same rule, reasoning and affirmance directly.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that present emotional harm accompanying an existing occupational disease differs from distress caused by symptomless exposure. Urie recognizes occupational disease as injury under FELA. Gottshall limits independent negligent emotional-distress claims, and Buckley applies that limit to asbestos contact that has caused no disease. Neither excludes the ordinary emotional consequences of manifested bodily injury. The common-law bodily-injury rule reflected in Restatement §456 includes emotional disturbance resulting from the physical harm or the negligent conduct producing it.

All six employees have asbestosis, and the testimony connects their exposure and disease to heightened cancer risks. The direct-relationship objection is answered by the connection to the negligent conduct producing the bodily injury; a biological progression from asbestosis to cancer is unnecessary. The separate prospect of compensation if cancer later occurs cannot compensate the present fear of a worker who never develops it; duplicate compensation remains impermissible.

The genuine-and-serious requirement and ordinary evidence review constrain speculative recovery. The actual charge tied fear to proven physical injury, and surrounding instructions prohibited damages for cancer or increased risk. Norfolk requested the additional probability and physical-manifestation prerequisites, not a distinct genuine-and-serious charge, an itemized fear verdict or plaintiff-specific sufficiency review of that character. The significantly-impaired-life-and-work formulation likewise was not presented. The Court rejects the preserved categorical demands; the unpresented alternative instruction and individual-sufficiency questions supply no basis to retry these judgments. This affirmance does not equate diagnosis with proof of fear.

### II. Several contributors do not divide an indivisible injury

**Holding and operative rule:** When railroad negligence contributes to an indivisible injury covered by FELA §51, the railroad is liable to the employee for that injury's damages without a deduction merely corresponding to other employers' shares of asbestos exposure. The employee must prove negligence and the required causal contribution by a preponderance; a party seeking divisibility-based allocation must identify a supported basis for separating the injury rather than exposure percentages alone.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion has all nine Justices' direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that FELA's causal language and retained common-law treatment of indivisible injury require full recovery from a railroad whose negligence contributed to that injury. Section 51 addresses injury resulting in whole or in part from railroad negligence. Rogers supplies the causal-contribution rule; it does not dispense with negligence or actual injury. The common law distinguishes several causes of one injury from injuries that can be separately attributed. Monessen requires attention to that background without treating every tort rule as immutable.

Norfolk's proposed exposure-share reduction would divide damages without establishing divisible harm. Butler's and Ayers's outside exposures may bear on causation, but their different durations do not by themselves partition lung injury. Section 53 expressly adjusts for the employee's own contributory negligence; it supplies no corresponding discount for each outside tortfeasor. The existing smoking-related reductions and settlement credits therefore remain distinct from the deduction Norfolk seeks.

Genuinely divisible injury retains its separate attribution rules. Employee negligence remains subject to §53, which prohibits diminution on that ground when a contributing railroad violation of an employee-safety statute is established. Contribution or indemnity may separately allocate responsibility under the law governing such a claim; that possible recourse does not postpone or reduce the employee's recovery merely because other exposure occurred. This holding creates no general federal contribution action.

## Published noncontrolling positions

Kennedy's partial dissent, joined by O'Connor and qualifiedly by Breyer, in Ayers on March 10, 2003, requires a direct relation between physical injury and emotional harm before ordinary bodily-injury damages encompass the fear. A common exposure and correlation between asbestosis and possible later cancer are insufficient for contemplative future-disease fear on that account. The independent Gottshall/Buckley restrictions and uncertainty of valuation would require a damages remand, preserving the underlying asbestosis-liability finding and full indivisible-injury responsibility. This is a proposed injury-category limit, not a claim that Norfolk preserved a seriousness-only instruction. It is noncontrolling.

Breyer's further partial dissent, on the same date, adds a generally applicable exclusion only when three conditions coincide: the feared disease's development can neither be expected nor ruled out for many years; fear remains separately compensable if that disease occurs; and the risks feared are not significantly different in kind from background risks everyone faces. Absence of any condition prevents that exclusion. Even when all coincide, unusually severe fear is compensable if it significantly and detrimentally impairs everyday life and work; physical manifestations, psychiatric treatment and professional testimony are not universal requirements. The rule would govern the recoverable category on his proposed damages remand, without a claimant-specific sufficiency finding or guaranteed new award. This complete qualification, as well as his valuation and compensation-preservation grounds, is noncontrolling.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy dissents from the cancer-fear holding and damages affirmance, joined by O'Connor; Breyer joins the direct-relationship analysis subject to his further limitation. They read the traditional bodily-injury exception to require a direct relationship between the physical harm and emotional injury. Immediate fright and distress from the physical condition differ from reflection on an uncertain correlated disease. Common exposure and heightened cancer risk do not by themselves supply that relationship. Gottshall and Buckley require an independent boundary, and sincerity or seriousness alone does not answer the category objection. The Court's rule, they conclude, risks speculative valuation and diminishes resources for later serious disease. Their objection concerns the category the charge allowed, not an unrequested adjective. They would vacate damages and remand for proceedings under the narrower injury rule while preserving liability for proved asbestosis and its otherwise compensable consequences. They join the Court's entire indivisible-injury holding.

Breyer writes further because his position does not exclude every serious cancer fear. In addition to genuineness and seriousness, he would generally deny recovery only when all three conditions hold: development of the feared disease can neither be expected nor ruled out for many years; fear remains separately compensable if the disease occurs; and the feared risks are not significantly different in kind from the background risks everyone faces. If any condition is absent, that exclusion does not apply. Even when all three are present, unusually severe fear remains compensable if it significantly and detrimentally affects everyday life and work. Physical manifestations, psychiatric treatment and professional testimony are not universal conditions. He regards this boundary as responsive to the difficulty of valuing incremental future-risk fear and preserving compensation for later disease, notwithstanding the unpresented exact formulation. He seeks a damages remand under the complete rule, without deciding any worker's individual sufficiency or guaranteeing renewed recovery, and joins the indivisible-injury holding.
