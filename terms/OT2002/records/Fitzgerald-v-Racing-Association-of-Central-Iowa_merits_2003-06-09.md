## Event, chronology and posture

Fitzgerald v. Racing Association of Central Iowa, No. 02-695. Argued April 29, 2003; decided June 9, 2003. On certiorari to the Iowa Supreme Court, which reversed the trial court and invalidated the racetrack/riverboat slot-revenue tax difference under common state-and-federal equal-protection reasoning. Iowa authorized racetrack slot machines with a graduated revenue-tax rate rising to 36 percent while retaining the 20-percent riverboat rate. The questions are whether an independent state ground bars review and whether the rate distinction violates federal equal protection. The treasurer seeks reversal of the federal invalidation; the racetracks seek preservation of the judgment and equal treatment.

## Controlling law and reasoning

**Authority.** Both holdings are adopted by all nine Justices in the opinion of the Court: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

### I. The interwoven state and federal judgment is reviewable

**Holding and operative rule.** When a state court expressly applies common state-and-federal constitutional reasoning and identifies no clear state ground independently sustaining its judgment, this Court may review the federal ground under 28 U.S.C. §1257. The Iowa Supreme Court's interwoven analysis permits that review here.

**Controlling explanation.** Michigan v. Long supplies the rule for identifying an independent state ground. The Iowa opinion treats the two equal-protection guarantees together; its reasoning does not establish that the same judgment would follow regardless of federal law. Review therefore answers the federal issue while leaving the Iowa court free on remand to decide an independently grounded state constitutional claim.

### II. Multiple legitimate economic purposes rationally support the different rates

**Holding and operative rule.** In ordinary economic taxation involving neither a suspect class nor a fundamental right, a classification satisfies equal protection when a reasonably conceivable legitimate purpose rationally supports it, and the challengers bear the burden of negating that basis. Iowa may authorize new slot-machine revenue for racetracks while taxing it at a higher rate than riverboat revenue to balance distinct legitimate industry and public-revenue objectives; every provision need not maximize aid to racetracks.

**Controlling explanation.** Nordlinger v. Hahn permits rational though imperfect tax classifications, and Beach Communications permits reasonably conceivable legitimate grounds without formal legislative findings or proof that each ground was the legislature's exclusive actual motive. The law gives racetracks a new revenue opportunity even at a rate rising to 36 percent. Keeping the riverboat rate at 20 percent can simultaneously protect a different industry and balance public revenues. Those aims concern the actual licensing-and-tax package, rather than the self-justifying claim that favored businesses deserve favor.

The racetracks argue that identical machines should produce identical taxes and that the disparity contradicts the legislation's assistance to them. But enterprises operating the same machines may stand differently within a lawful industry policy. A partial benefit remains a benefit; the Constitution does not require that every part of a compromise supply the largest possible advantage to one beneficiary. Allegheny Pittsburgh concerned arbitrary administration under a different state equality arrangement, not an enacted classification with the rational policy grounds present here. The federal invalidation therefore cannot stand.

The Iowa classification survives the federal challenge; independent Iowa constitutional meaning and available state-law remedies remain for the Iowa courts.
