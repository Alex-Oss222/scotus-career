## Controlling holdings

### I. FACE applies to clinic personnel under its complete conduct and service-related predicates

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 248(a)(1) reaches any person, including clinic personnel, who by force, threat of force or physical obstruction intentionally injures, intimidates or interferes with a person, or attempts to do so, because that person is or has been obtaining or providing reproductive health services, or to intimidate that person, another person or a class from obtaining or providing them. Occupation and support for abortion supply no exemption; the prohibited means, intentional conduct and alternative service-related nexus must each be established.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** American Life League applies the enacted access protection to defined conduct and preserves its intent, service connection and attribution requirements. Nothing in those provisions limits liability to outsiders or opponents of abortion. A patient seeking hospital treatment for reproductive injuries is seeking a covered kind of care, and intentionally restraining her may restrict access to it if the statutory nexus is present. The alleged request to stop the procedure and summon an ambulance thus matters to the statutory inquiry without establishing why the defendants restrained her.

The defendants' treatment-quality objection is answered by the statute's actual conditions. A medical error, unwanted touch or restraint alone does not establish each element. If the actor's sole actual purpose is protective medical action and the required service-access cause or intimidating purpose is absent, the statutory claim fails. A protective label is not a separate immunity when the required intent and nexus are proved. The reported conditional concession about a solely protective purpose supplies no finding of the actual motive. Scheidler's refusal to add an economic motive absent from its statute cannot justify deleting FACE's express service nexus.

**Precedent treatment:** American Life League is applied to the clinic-personnel setting with its definitions, attribution and statutory exclusions intact. National Organization for Women v. Scheidler, decided January 24, 1994, supplies the distinct rule against inventing an omitted statutory motive; FACE's expressed alternatives remain necessary.

**Operative qualifications:** Interference means restriction of a person's freedom of movement; intimidation means reasonable apprehension of bodily harm to oneself or another. Physical obstruction makes ingress or egress impassable or passage to or from the facility unreasonably difficult or hazardous. Reproductive services encompass medical, surgical, counseling and referral services concerning reproduction, pregnancy or termination, including carrying pregnancy to term, in hospitals, clinics, physicians' offices or other facilities. A facility includes the reproductive-service establishment and its building or structure. Requested treatment elsewhere for the alleged reproductive injury can fall within this definition.

For a private action under §248(a)(1), the claimant must be aggrieved by the prohibited conduct and involved in providing or seeking to provide, or obtaining or seeking to obtain, services in a reproductive-health facility. Each defendant's violation must be legally attributable to that defendant; shared belief, membership or another's independent act is insufficient. A parent or legal guardian is exempt from every FACE penalty and civil remedy for the described activity insofar as it is directed exclusively at that person's own minor. Section (d)(1) preserves First Amendment expression, including protected peaceful picketing and demonstrations. Section (d)(2) separately creates no new remedy for outside-facility interference with protected speech or religion, regardless of viewpoint, and limits no existing remedy. FACE remedies are nonexclusive, other state and local remedies are not preempted, and state and local regulation of reproductive services remains enforceable. Those independent provisions retain their distinct terms.

**Holding and operative rule:** Roe receives the reasonable opportunity to amend directed below, with Rule 8 requiring fair notice of the intentional acts and their connection to obtaining or seeking the covered care rather than evidentiary proof in the complaint. Because this review is on the defendants' petition and no cross-petition seeks improved pleading relief, the renewed-dismissal baseline with leave to amend remains effective.

**Controlling explanation:** Swierkiewicz distinguishes substantive proof from pleading, and Leatherman rejects a judge-made heightened pleading requirement beyond the governing Rules. Those decisions preserve the actual statutory elements. An amendment must give fair notice of the service-related connection instead of relying solely on the asserted injury, while the ultimate conduct, purpose, personal attribution and causal injury must be proved by a preponderance in ordinary merits proceedings.

The lower judgment directed renewed dismissal with a reasonable amendment opportunity. Roe seeks preservation of that judgment on the clinic defendants' petition; without a cross-petition the Court cannot improve her pleading disposition by declaring the present complaint sufficient. The statutory private-action and relief conditions remain operative. Section 248(c)(1)(B) permits appropriate injunctive relief, compensatory and punitive damages, and costs and reasonable attorney and expert fees on their applicable conditions. Before final judgment an eligible plaintiff may elect five thousand dollars per attributable violation in place of actual compensatory damages; the election is permissive and replaces that category rather than every other remedy. The complete own-minor exception in Part I applies to all FACE relief.

### III. Public pseudonymity must protect substantial intimate privacy while preserving a fair defense

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Rules 10(a) and 26(c), a court may permit public pseudonymity when the substantial privacy interest in the intimate subject of a particular action outweighs the need for public identification and a tailored order preserves meaningful defense access. Roe's abortion and reproductive-injury allegations, coupled with her offer of necessary confidential identification to the defendants, warrant that limited protection while the proceedings and legal issues remain public.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the procedural rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 10(a) makes public naming the ordinary practice, and openness must receive weight independently of the defendants' need to investigate. Rule 26(c) permits fitted protection for sensitive information. This action necessarily exposes exceptionally intimate treatment and alleged injuries to reproductive organs. The plaintiff's identity contributes little to evaluating the statutory issue, while her offer of confidential identification permits access to medical records, investigation and a meaningful response.

Private-party status and a demand for damages do not categorically defeat protection. Nor does the subject of abortion establish automatic anonymity. The decision rests on the particular disclosures and a practical means of protecting adversarial fairness. Necessary defense identification can be provided confidentially without requiring public identification. The district court must specify necessary recipients and filing protections, preserve access to nonidentifying legal and evidentiary material, and adjust disclosure upon a concrete showing of defense or public-access need. The governing basis is the Rules' procedural accommodation.

**Precedent treatment:** The protective-order framework under Rules 10(a) and 26(c) is applied to the particular intimate disclosure and defense needs.

### Component P1 — Clinic personnel and the statute's actual coverage

## Holdings

### I. FACE applies to clinic personnel under its complete conduct and service-related predicates

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 248(a)(1) reaches any person, including clinic personnel, who by force, threat of force or physical obstruction intentionally injures, intimidates or interferes with a person, or attempts to do so, because that person is or has been obtaining or providing reproductive health services, or to intimidate that person, another person or a class from obtaining or providing them. Occupation and support for abortion supply no exemption; the prohibited means, intentional conduct and alternative service-related nexus must each be established.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** American Life League applies the enacted access protection to defined conduct and preserves its intent, service connection and attribution requirements. Nothing in those provisions limits liability to outsiders or opponents of abortion. A patient seeking hospital treatment for reproductive injuries is seeking a covered kind of care, and intentionally restraining her may restrict access to it if the statutory nexus is present. The alleged request to stop the procedure and summon an ambulance thus matters to the statutory inquiry without establishing why the defendants restrained her.

The defendants' treatment-quality objection is answered by the statute's actual conditions. A medical error, unwanted touch or restraint alone does not establish each element. If the actor's sole actual purpose is protective medical action and the required service-access cause or intimidating purpose is absent, the statutory claim fails. A protective label is not a separate immunity when the required intent and nexus are proved. The reported conditional concession about a solely protective purpose supplies no finding of the actual motive. Scheidler's refusal to add an economic motive absent from its statute cannot justify deleting FACE's express service nexus.

**Precedent treatment:** American Life League is applied to the clinic-personnel setting with its definitions, attribution and statutory exclusions intact. National Organization for Women v. Scheidler, decided January 24, 1994, supplies the distinct rule against inventing an omitted statutory motive; FACE's expressed alternatives remain necessary.

**Operative qualifications:** Interference means restriction of a person's freedom of movement; intimidation means reasonable apprehension of bodily harm to oneself or another. Physical obstruction makes ingress or egress impassable or passage to or from the facility unreasonably difficult or hazardous. Reproductive services encompass medical, surgical, counseling and referral services concerning reproduction, pregnancy or termination, including carrying pregnancy to term, in hospitals, clinics, physicians' offices or other facilities. A facility includes the reproductive-service establishment and its building or structure. Requested treatment elsewhere for the alleged reproductive injury can fall within this definition.

For a private action under §248(a)(1), the claimant must be aggrieved by the prohibited conduct and involved in providing or seeking to provide, or obtaining or seeking to obtain, services in a reproductive-health facility. Each defendant's violation must be legally attributable to that defendant; shared belief, membership or another's independent act is insufficient. A parent or legal guardian is exempt from every FACE penalty and civil remedy for the described activity insofar as it is directed exclusively at that person's own minor. Section (d)(1) preserves First Amendment expression, including protected peaceful picketing and demonstrations. Section (d)(2) separately creates no new remedy for outside-facility interference with protected speech or religion, regardless of viewpoint, and limits no existing remedy. FACE remedies are nonexclusive, other state and local remedies are not preempted, and state and local regulation of reproductive services remains enforceable. Those independent provisions retain their distinct terms.

### II. The amendment opportunity proceeds under ordinary pleading and the private-action conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** Roe receives the reasonable opportunity to amend directed below, with Rule 8 requiring fair notice of the intentional acts and their connection to obtaining or seeking the covered care rather than evidentiary proof in the complaint. Because this review is on the defendants' petition and no cross-petition seeks improved pleading relief, the renewed-dismissal baseline with leave to amend remains effective.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join.

**Controlling explanation:** Swierkiewicz distinguishes substantive proof from pleading, and Leatherman rejects a judge-made heightened pleading requirement beyond the governing Rules. Those decisions preserve the actual statutory elements. An amendment must give fair notice of the service-related connection instead of relying solely on the asserted injury, while the ultimate conduct, purpose, personal attribution and causal injury must be proved by a preponderance in ordinary merits proceedings.

The lower judgment directed renewed dismissal with a reasonable amendment opportunity. Roe seeks preservation of that judgment on the clinic defendants' petition; without a cross-petition the Court cannot improve her pleading disposition by declaring the present complaint sufficient. The statutory private-action and relief conditions remain operative. Section 248(c)(1)(B) permits appropriate injunctive relief, compensatory and punitive damages, and costs and reasonable attorney and expert fees on their applicable conditions. Before final judgment an eligible plaintiff may elect five thousand dollars per attributable violation in place of actual compensatory damages; the election is permissive and replaces that category rather than every other remedy. The complete own-minor exception in Part I applies to all FACE relief.

**Precedent treatment:** Swierkiewicz and Leatherman govern the distinction between notice pleading and proof. American Life League preserves attribution, private-action eligibility, the permissive statutory-damages election and the complete own-minor exception.

### III. Public pseudonymity must protect substantial intimate privacy while preserving a fair defense

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Rules 10(a) and 26(c), a court may permit public pseudonymity when the substantial privacy interest in the intimate subject of a particular action outweighs the need for public identification and a tailored order preserves meaningful defense access. Roe's abortion and reproductive-injury allegations, coupled with her offer of necessary confidential identification to the defendants, warrant that limited protection while the proceedings and legal issues remain public.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the procedural rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 10(a) makes public naming the ordinary practice, and openness must receive weight independently of the defendants' need to investigate. Rule 26(c) permits fitted protection for sensitive information. This action necessarily exposes exceptionally intimate treatment and alleged injuries to reproductive organs. The plaintiff's identity contributes little to evaluating the statutory issue, while her offer of confidential identification permits access to medical records, investigation and a meaningful response.

Private-party status and a demand for damages do not categorically defeat protection. Nor does the subject of abortion establish automatic anonymity. The decision rests on the particular disclosures and a practical means of protecting adversarial fairness. Necessary defense identification can be provided confidentially without requiring public identification. The district court must specify necessary recipients and filing protections, preserve access to nonidentifying legal and evidentiary material, and adjust disclosure upon a concrete showing of defense or public-access need. The governing basis is the Rules' procedural accommodation.

**Precedent treatment:** The protective-order framework under Rules 10(a) and 26(c) is applied to the particular intimate disclosure and defense needs.

## Precedent Treatment

American Life League governs FACE's complete means, intentional conduct, alternative reproductive-service nexus, definitions, personal attribution, private-action conditions and separate statutory exclusions. Its rule applies to covered clinic-personnel conduct. Scheidler's omitted-motive rule does not erase FACE's express nexus. Swierkiewicz and Leatherman preserve ordinary notice pleading alongside ultimate substantive proof.

## Law After Decision

FACE protects the defined access to reproductive services against covered acts by clinic personnel as well as outsiders. The actual statutory cause or intimidating purpose distinguishes such interference from treatment error. Public pseudonymity is available on an individualized showing of substantial intimate privacy with meaningful defense access and continued public adjudication of the legal dispute.
