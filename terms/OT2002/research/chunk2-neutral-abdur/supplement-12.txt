I. THE SIXTH CIRCUIT DID NOT HAVE APPELLATE JURISDICTION TO 


REVIEW THE DISTRICT COURT’S ORDER TRANSFERRING PETITIONER’S 
MOTION TO THE SIXTH CIRCUIT PURSUANT TO 28 U.S.C. § 1631. 


On November 27, 2001, the district court determined that petitioner's Rule 60(b) 
motion constituted a second or successive application under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b). 
Because § 2244(b)(3)(A) requires that, “[bJefore a second or successive application 
permitted by this section is filed in the district court, the applicant shall move in the 
appropriate court of appeals for an order authorizing the district court to consider the 
application,” the district court further concluded that it lacked jurisdiction to entertain 
the application absent authorization from the court of appeals. Accordingly, the district 
court transferred the action to the Sixth Circuit under 28 U.S.C. §1631.' Pectioner filed

Petitioner could not sppeal as of right from the transfer order. As a general 
principle, it is well settled that transfer orders are not appealable final orders under 28 
U.S.C. § 1291. Ukiah Adventist Hospital v. Federal Trade Commission, 981 F.2d 543, 546 
(D.C. Cir. 1993). This settled rule against appealability applies to transfer orders issued 
pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1631. Jd. (wansfer from district court to court of appeals for 
lack of jurisdiction); Persyn v. United States, 935 F.2d 69, 72-73 (Sth Cir. 1991) (transfer 
from district court to claims court); Alimenta (USA), Inc. v. Lyng, 872 F.2d 382, 383-85 
(11th Cir. 1989) (same); Raines v. Block, 798 F.2d 377, 379-80 (10th Cir. 1986) (same); 
Middlebrooks v. Smith, 735 F.2d 431, 432-33 (11th Cir. 1984) (transfer between district 
courts). "The decisions in Persyn, Alimenta, Raines and Middlebrooks reflect the common 
sense view that section 1631 orders are interlocutory for the same reason that venue 
transfers are: the very purpose of transfer provisions is to allow the case to continue, 
rather than terminating it." Ukiah, 981 F.2d at 546. 

Nor is the order here reviewable as a final judgment under the collateral order 


doctrine, which requires that the order satisfy at least three conditions: "it must

separate from the merits of the action, and be effectively unreviewable on appeal from 
a final judgment." Lauro Lines S.R.L. v. Chasser, 490 U.S. 495, 498 (1989) (citing 
Richardson-Merrell Inc. v. Koller, 474 U.S. 424, 431 (1985), quoting Coopers & Lybrand v. 
Livesay, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978) (internal quotation marks omitted)). First, the 
collateral order doctrine is wholly inapplicable in this context. Clearly inherent in the 
doctrine is the notion that there should be an effective appeal from any “final” judgment. 
But, under § 2244's statutory scheme, Congress has determined that there should be no 
appeal from the final judgment at all. See 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(3)(E). Indeed, Congress 
has vested the court of appeals itself with primary and exclusive jurisdiction over the 
screening of second or successive petitions. 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(3)(A). 

Furthermore, even if the collateral order doctrine were applicable, “[t)he courts 
have alrnost universally agreed that transfer orders fal] outside the scope of the collateral 
order exception.” F.D.I.C. v. McGlamery, 74 F.3d 218, 221 (10 Cir. 1996). Here, the 
transfer order fails to satisfy even the first requirement, that it "must conclusively 
determine the disputed question." It did not, because, as this Court noted in Christianson 
v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800 (1988), the transferee court, i.¢., the Sith 
Circuit, had the power to revisit the decision. For the same reason, the transfer order 
lacks the third characteristic of an appealable collateral order. The collateral order 


doctrine is reserved for those situations in which a non-final order could not otherwise

Il. THIS COURT DOES NOT HAVE CERTIORARI JURISDICTION TO REVIEW 
THE SIXTH CIRCUIT'S ORDER DENYING PETITIONER LEAVE TO FILE A 
SECOND HABEAS CORPUS PETITION. 


As demonstrated above, the court of appeals lacked any independent basis of 
appellate jurisdiction to review the district court's transfer order—under 28 U.S.C. 
§1291, the collateral order doctrine, or 28 U.S.C. § 1292. It follows, therefore, that the 
sole basis upon which the case could have proceeded to judgment in the court of appeals 
was on the terms specified in the district court's transfer order, namely, as a motion for 
leave to file a second or successive habeas application, over which the courts of appeals 
enjoy original, exclusive jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(3). The transfer statute, | 
28 U.S.C. §1631, dictates that a transferred “action .. . proceed as if it had been filed 
in .. . the court to which it is transferred .. .." Plainly, the case could not have been 
filed in oe Sixth Circuit as a motion for relief from the district court's prior judgment 
in the original habeas case under Rule 60(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. 
The courts of appeals have no original jurisdiction to dispose of Rule 60(b) motions. 

"The grant or denial of an authorization by a court of appeals to file a second or 
successive application shall not be appealable and shall not be the subject of a petition 
for rehearing or for a watt of certiorari." 28 U.S.C. §2244(b)(3)(E). Accordingly, this 
Court lacks jurisdiction to review the Sixth Circuit's February 11, 2002, judgment 
denying petitioner's motion. Felker v. Turpin, 518 U.S. 651, 661 (1996)("2244(b)(3)(E) 
precludes us from reviewing, by appeal or petition for certiorari, a judgment on an 


application for leave to file a second habeas petition in district court”). 


Petitioner may not evade this jurisdictional restriction on the theory that the 
Sixth Circuit's judgment embodies a "predicate determination" concerning the true 
nature of his Rule 60(b) motion that this Court may somehow review, independent of 
the judgment itself. In the first place, it was the district court's transfer order, not the 
Sixth Circuit, which made the "predicate determination” characterizing petitioner's 
motion as one for leave to file a second habeas application. And, as previously 
demonstrated, the court of appeals lacked jurisdiction to consider petitioner's attempted 
appeal of the transfer order. While the court of appeals did endorse the district court's 
characterization of petitioner's Rule 60(b) motion (J.A 36), the fact remains that the 
court of appeals could only have accepted and decided the case on the terms of the 
transfer—as a motion for leave to file a second or successive application. 

Finally, even if the judgment below decided more than what was transferred from 
the district court and encompassed, in addition, an independent adjudication by the 
court of appeals concerning the true nature of the motion filed by petitioner in the 
district court, that adjudication could orly have been made in the exercise of the Sixth 
Circuit's original, exclusive “gatekeeping” jurisdiction under the provisions of 28 U.S.C. 
§ 2244(b)(3), as much so as when it ultimately denied the motion. The decision 
whether a petitioner is in fact seeking to file a second or successive application subject 
to the restrictions of §2244(b) cannot logically be separated from the decision whether 
to grant him leave to do so, and the provisions of §2244(b)(3)(E) expressly forbid review 
of such decisions by certiorari. In Stewart y. Martinez- Villareal, 523 U.S. 637, 641-2
