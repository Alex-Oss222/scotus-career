# Scheidler: United States amicus position

Scope: objective, date-eligible advocacy facts only, supplied for fresh neutral validation. This receipt states no Supreme Court disposition, holding, vote, author, or non-Stone commitment.

The United States requested reversal of the judgment upholding the RICO injunction and affirmance of the holding that the conduct came within the Hobbs Act. Its requested relief therefore must be stated by component. Original source: United States amicus merits brief, July 2002, page 30; see also its statement of questions at page I.

The Government maintained that the defendants committed extortion under a theory that obtaining control over the use of business assets obtains the business's intangible right of exclusive control. Its support for reversing the injunction must not be described as agreement with the defendants' obtaining-property objection. The brief distinguishes obtaining a business's control rights from a bare liberty interest and argues that coercion and extortion can overlap when property is obtained. Original source: United States amicus brief, pages 15 n.6 and 21–29, particularly page 22. The theory remains an advocate's position.

The Government separately opposed private RICO injunctive authority. It read §1964(a) as defining judicial remedies, §1964(b) as authorizing the Attorney General to seek them, and §1964(c) as authorizing a private damages action. It relied on the antitrust remedial comparison and the absence of a counterpart to Clayton Act §16. Original source: United States amicus brief, pages 6–15 and 30. This is the Government's proposed construction, not an adopted rule.

At argument, the Solicitor General agreed that the case would have to return to the Seventh Circuit to examine the generalized instruction's inclusion of the woman's right to seek services, which he characterized as a liberty interest. When asked whether this meant a new trial, he said that might be the ultimate result and noted that the Government had not briefed the remedial question. He continued to seek a ruling that obtaining sufficient business control to stop services constituted extortion. Original source: December 4, 2002 argument transcript, pages 29–31. The established concession is remand to examine the instruction, not an unqualified concession that every alternative necessarily requires a new trial.

Respondents did not adopt that concession. Their counsel defended the instruction by invoking the clinics' control of assets, the women's ability to spend money, and actual appointments characterized as contractual rights. She disputed the suggestion that respondents had conceded two of the three property categories were invalid. Original source: argument transcript, pages 44–47. Her characterization of appointments and contracts remains advocacy, not a newly verified finding concerning every claimant.

## Contemporaneous verdict reference and its limit

The Government's statement of the case reports four acts or threats of physical violence among the jury's findings and cites the petition appendix at pages 159a–160a. Petitioners' rebuttal independently refers to four findings under special interrogatory 4(e). Original sources: United States amicus brief, page 3; argument transcript, page 62. This establishes that the four findings were mentioned in materials available in this review, rather than introduced only through later litigation.

The petition appendix has now been recovered from the Internet Archive Records and Briefs collection, item `micro_IA40385014_0538`. Its complete reproduced jury-instruction and verdict portion, printed pages 146a–163a, was read. Appendix F expressly identifies itself as excerpts from jury instructions; it is not the full trial transcript. Appendix G contains the special interrogatories and verdict form. A mechanically copied bounded OCR extract is saved as `research/chunk4-control/SCHEIDLER_JURY_APPENDIX_EXTRACT.txt`.

The fifth-element instruction lists multiple categories of acts at pages 149a–150a: extortion; murder threats; attempts or conspiracies concerning those categories; physical violence or threats to persons or property; interstate travel, mail or telephone use with intent to promote, manage or carry out extortion, arson or crimes of violence followed by furtherance; related attempts; and causing such travel or communications. It expressly permits the two or more acts to come from the same or different categories and requires connection among them. The physical-violence category is separately listed. That particular list does not expressly limit the physical-violence category to acts furthering robbery or extortion.

The federal-extortion instruction at page 150a requires knowingly, willfully and wrongfully causing victims to give up a property right through actual or threatened force, violence or fear. Its property definition includes the woman's service-access right, employees' right to perform their jobs and the clinics' service-provision right. It does not instruct the jury to find acquisition of that right by a defendant or a third party. The state-extortion instruction at pages 151a–152a likewise uses intentional surrender of those defined rights, with its stated force/fear and specified state-value conditions. These are descriptions of the instructions, not independent findings that every legally valid element was established.

The threat definition at page 151a uses what a reasonable person would foresee the audience understanding from the contextual statement or conduct; it does not require intent or ability to carry out the threat. The attribution instruction at page 153a separately forbids liability solely from membership, requires enterprise authorization or ratification of unlawful activity, and requires defendants' specific intent to further illegal objectives. These different instructions must not be merged into a finding that the jury necessarily found subjective awareness of threatening meaning for every act.

Special interrogatory 4 separately reports 21 federal-extortion acts or threats, 25 state-extortion acts or threats, no murder threats, 25 attempts or conspiracies in the specified preceding categories, and four physical-violence acts or threats under 4(e). The four finding is visible in the recovered OCR and independently confirmed by the Government's brief and petitioners' argument. Question 6 asks whether the extortion answers in 4(a) or 4(b) rested solely on blockades or sit-ins without more; the answer is no. That response expressly concerns the two extortion categories, not an independent legal validation of 4(e).

Questions 5 and 7 address at least two acts within ten years and a pattern; question 8 addresses interstate commerce; question 9 asks whether any two of the listed acts proximately caused injury to the named categories of plaintiffs. Those are general answers tied to the combined list, not separate causal and pattern findings for each of the four 4(e) acts. The form does not identify those four acts, their actors or separate damages. Question 10 awards Summit $54,471.28 and Delaware $31,455.64 for RICO injuries without apportionment by predicate category. The form directs jurors who find all defendants liable on Claim I to bypass Claim II's conspiracy questions.

The recovered instruction and verdict therefore establish a separately listed violence category and four findings. They do not by themselves decide whether that category is a legally valid independent Hobbs Act predicate, whether an independent theory was preserved for affirmance, or what relief could survive removal of legally deficient predicates. Those are actual legal and remedial issues for fresh neutral analysis, not grounds to describe the four findings as absent or to presume that they sustain the judgment. No later judicial action supplies their meaning.
