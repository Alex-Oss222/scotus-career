STATEMENT 


California has strong interests in caring for its resi- 
dents and in protecting them from actual or potential 
abusive practices by companies licensed to do business in 
the state. These interests are particularly compelling 
where the regulated companies are insurers, engaged in a 
quasi-public business and clothed with a public trust. Cal. 
Auto. Ass’n v. Maloney, 341 U.S. 105, 109-110 (1951); 
German Alliance Ins. Co. v. Lewis, 233 U.S. 389, 414-415 
(1914). Members of the public have a right to expect their 
government to assure that insurers deal truthfully and 
disclose basic insurance policy information to them. 


The HVIRA (California Insurance Code §§ 13800-13807) 
is not a foreign policy initiative. The statute makes no 
political statement, expresses no criticism or hostility toward 
any foreign government, and indeed is not aimed at foreign 
governments at all. It is an insurance regulation that seeks 
disclosure of withheld insurance policy information from 
private companies doing business in California. That infor- 
mation will benefit California residents who have been 
repeatedly and systematically denied access to it for decades. 


In addition to benefiting victims of insurer stonewall- 
ing, the HVIRA furthers a number of legitimate consumer 
protection interests. It protects California against insur- 
ance companies that are engaging in, or might be influ- 
enced by companies that are engaging in, the widespread 
practice of wrongfully withholding insurance information 
from insureds and beneficiaries. It accomplishes this by 
requiring all California licensees to disclose such informa- 
tion if they or their affiliates possess it, or have their 
licenses suspended. This satisfies the legislative goal of 
ensuring that no insurance company or family of compa- 
nies persisting in these practices will have access to the 
California market, and serves notice to all insurance 
companies that the state will take swift and strong meas- 
ures to protect the interests of insureds — all of which 
results in a more secure marketplace for California insur- 
ance consumers. In addition, the information sought - 
information establishing the existence of policies — will 


2 


provide the state with at least a first step in evaluating 
whether families of insurers have engaged in a pattern 
and practice of falsely denying the existence or record of 
those policies to inquiring policyholders and beneficiaries. 


In today’s global society, with substantial business 
being conducted in the United States by multinational 
corporations, state laws often have international effects. 
State regulators routinely request information from 
licensees about foreign affiliates and foreign transactions. 
In the present case, petitioner Generali is a multinational 
that itself does business in California and abroad. All of 
the other insurance company petitioners are members of 
international insurance holding company systems - 
multinational conglomerates whose members are linked 
together through various control relationships. Jnt. App. 
40.’ These relationships provide substantial benefits to the 
system’s members, as the record in this case amply re- 
flects. See 1-ER 697-699, 703-710, 722-747, 879-900, 903, 
1026-1069. As amicus Chamber of Commerce has pointed 
out in its brief (p. 17), “[clompanies depend on complex, 
far-flung networks of corporate affiliations to remain 
competitive in the global marketplace.” 


Those relationships also carry obligations. Under the 
laws of California and virtually every other state,’ mem- 
bership in such holding company systems obligates licen- 
sees to provide information about the conduct of other

Under this Court’s precedents, a state’s commercial 
regulations are not unconstitutional because they have 
adverse consequences for foreign companies, are strongly 
protested by foreign governments, and are expressly 
disapproved by the Executive Branch, so long as they are 
not disapproved by Congress. Barclays Bank PLC uv. 
Franchise Tax Bd., 512 U.S. 298, 320-331 (1994). Nor may 
the Executive Branch, through an expressly non- 
preemptive executive agreement or by endorsing a private, 
voluntary claims organization, unilaterally supersede such 
state regulations. If the Executive perceives that the 
HVIRA interferes with the Nation’s ability to speak with 
one voice, it may seek a remedy from Congress. 


Under petitioners’ expansive (and _ standardless) 
reading of the foreign affairs power, however, generally 
accepted reporting statutes would be vulnerable to attack 
on the ground that they reflect state “foreign policy” or 
interfere with Executive Branch foreign relations. Peti- 
tioners’ theory would invalidate many state regulatory 
regimes whose constitutionality has never before been 
doubted, eviscerate this Court’s Foreign Commerce Clause 
jurisprudence, and call upon courts to determine the 
“foreign affairs” implications of state regulations on a case- 
by-case basis. State regulatory authority, if subordinated 
to the views and preferences of the Executive Branch and 
foreign governments, will become uncertain. 


Similarly, if petitioners’ due process and Commerce 
Clause arguments are successful, then out-of-state conduct 
in which the states have legitimate regulatory interests 
could become immunized from regulatory scrutiny. A 
regulated company could assert that it is not required to 
disclose fraudulent conduct committed by its parent com- 
pany in Europe because the “subject matter” of that conduct 
was European transactions involving only Europeans. 


4 


Regulatory disclosure could be thwarted by locating 
critical documents in the hands of off-shore affiliates. 
Petitioners’ arguments, if accepted, would threaten the 
entire system of holding company regulation. 


Finally, petitioners’ assertion that the “actual pur- 
pose” of an economic regulation controls legislative juris- 
diction is both unprecedented and unwise. If accepted, 
such a rule would undermine decades of economic due 
process law and create doubts about existing and future 
statutes that serve state regulatory interests but lack 
whatever magic terms or legislative history are deemed 
necessary to evidence the “correct” purpose. 


A. The HVIRA And The State Interests It 
Serves. 


1. The Plight Of Holocaust Survivors. 


Thousands of Holocaust survivors live in California. 
Jnt. App. 29-30; Resp. App. 24; Cal. Ins. Code § 13801(d). 
These survivors are elderly and many live in poverty. Jnt. 
App. 29-30. Although life and other insurance was com- 
mon in Europe in the 1920s and 1930s (Jnt. App. 30), very 
few Holocaust survivors have proof of insurance or even 
know which company issued insurance to their family 
when they were children. Jnt. App. 25, 31, 33, 34; Resp. 
App. 23-25, 31; 1-ER 400-489; 2-ER 570-580. In most 
cases, insurance company records are the only existing 
proof of the policies. Cal. Ins. Code § 13801(c). 


Yet, many insurers have refused to make that infor- 
mation available, while at the same time denying numer- 
ous claims because the claimants cannot provide proof of 
insurance. Jnt. App. 33-37; Resp. App. 23-24. Substantial 
evidence exists that insurance companies have repeatedly 
deceived Holocaust survivors, lied to survivors about the 
existence of policies or policy records, and demanded that 
survivors produce documents that the companies knew 
survivors did not have, such as the actual policies, 


5 


cancelled checks, and death certificates.’ The head of the 
International Commission On Holocaust Era Insurance 
Claims (“ICHEIC”), former Secretary of State Lawrence 
Eagleburger, has publicly accused one of the ICHEIC 
members, Allianz, of failing to pay any of thousands of 
claims submitted to ICHEIC. Jnt. App. 69. In response, 
Allianz pointed out that 92% of the claims did not name 
any insurance company, because survivors did not recall 
the companies that sold the policies. Jd. Allianz has thus 
far agreed to publish only a handful of policyholder names 
through ICHEIC, and is expected to publish only a limited 
number of additional names in the future. Resp. App. 26, 
27. 


One of the petitioners, Generali, was sued in 1997 for 
failing to disclose policy information to the family of a 
Holocaust victim. 1-ER 751-781. Generali is an Italian 
company licensed to transact insurance business in Cali- 
fornia since 1935 and subject to jurisdiction in California. 
1-ER 873. Generali repeatedly denied that it ever issued a 
policy to the family and claimed that all its files on its 
Eastern European policies were lost. 1-ER 751-781; 1998 
Hearing at 114. In fact, Generali has copies of its Eastern 
European policies, including the family’s, stored in its 
warehouse in Trieste, Italy (1-ER 767; 2458; 1998 Hearing 
at 114) and has computerized information on approxi- 
mately 340,000 policies issued between 1920 and 1945. 
Jnt. App. 38. Yet, Generali has refused to make most of 
that information available, publishing only the barest of 
policyholder lists through ICHEIC. Jnt. App. 38.

2. The California Legislature’s Response 
To Decades Of Insurer Stonewalling 
And Bad Faith. 


In 1999, the California legislature unanimously 
passed the HVIRA, a reporting statute that responds to 
the persistent refusal of insurance companies to disclose 
essential policy information. Disclosure of this information 
will assist survivors in deciding whether and against 
whom they may have an insurance claim. Survivors may 
then decide whether they wish to pursue a claim, and if so, 
in what manner (e.g., informally with the insurer, or 
through the ICHEIC, or through litigation). 


The HVIRA imposes the obligation to obtain this 
policy information on insurers doing business in California 
if the insurer or a related company sold insurance policies 
in Europe from 1920 through 1945 and if the information 
is in the possession, custody or control of the licensee or 
any of its related companies. App. 22a, n.11. The legisla- 
ture expressly recognized the persistent and present 
refusal by insurance companies to disclose necessary 
information, and it determined that insurers doing busi- 
ness in California should take responsibility to obtain that 
information to prevent further concealment from survivors 
and their families. Cal. Ins. Code § 13801(a)-(f); App. 25a. 


As the Court of Appeals concluded, the HVIRA reflects 
a number of state regulatory interests. App. at 16a, 22a, 
23a, 25a. The Court of Appeals also found that the HVIRA 
is rationally related to these legitimate government 
interests. App. at 22a-27a.* 


* The HVIRA is broadly drafted for a number of legitimate reasons.

foreign law defenses does not violate due process. App. 22a-27a.

3. Petitioners Mischaracterize The HVIRA. 


Petitioners repeatedly mischaracterize the HVIRA, 
claiming that its goal is to affect foreign policy; that its 
purpose is to interfere with international means for 
resolving Holocaust claims; that it “regulates” foreign 
insurance transactions and foreign insurers; that it 
“fosters” and “encourages” litigation in California courts; 
and that it forces payment of claims. See, e.g., AIA Brief at 
11, 26. As the Court of Appeals has twice ruled, however, 
such contentions “mischaracterize HVIRA as a matter of 
law.” App. at 9a, 43a. The HVIRA does not regulate either 
foreign transactions or foreign companies. App. at 9a, 15a, 
16a, 43a-45a. 


It does not demand, require or force the payment of 
any claims, and it makes no judgment as to whether 
policies have been properly paid. Nor does it encourage 
private litigation in California or elsewhere. Quite the 
contrary, the statute seeks only the disclosure of insurance 
policy information, which Members of Congress and others 
have recognized is essential to any resolution of Holocaust- 
era insurance claims, including resolution through 
ICHEIC. See, e.g., 2-ER 1955; 1998 Hearing at 100-102, 
105-107 (Statements of Congressmen Foley and Engel) 
(describing the necessity of compelling public disclosure of 
policy information by insurance companies through 
legislation). 

Petitioners and amici also improperly resort to exten- 
sive citation and discussion of other California statutes 
that are not part of this litigation and that are not before

ARGUMENT 


I. THE HVIRA DOES NOT INVADE AN AREA OF 
EXCLUSIVE FEDERAL POWER OVER FOR- 
EIGN RELATIONS 


Insurance is a quasi-public industry that, with Con- 
gress’ express approval, has long been regulated by the 


16 


states. See McCarran-Ferguson Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1011, 
1012(a). The HVIRA, a reporting statute that requires 
disclosure of information by private insurance companies 
doing business in California, furthers important state 
interests and is not directed at any foreign government. 
Yet, petitioners claim that the HVIRA unconstitutionally 
encroaches on a matter of foreign relations reserved to the 
federal government. No authority supports this remark- 
able claim of federal displacement of state power over a 
traditional area of commercial regulation. 

The Constitution’s text proscribes state activities that 
may affect foreign relations in three principal ways. 
Article I, Section 10 bars states from certain specific 
activities. Article VI makes federal treaties and statutes 
supreme over state law. And certain grants of power to 
Congress in Article I, Section 8 exclude states from some 
fields by negative implication — in particular, the dormant 
Commerce Clause precludes certain state commercial 
regulations. Petitioners do not rely on any of these textual 
provisions aside from the dormant Commerce Clause (an 
argument addressed in Section I.B.3 below). 


Beyond the Constitution’s express language, this 
Court has identified only two ways that federal foreign 
relations power may override state regulations, neither of 
which is applicable here. A single decision, Zschernig v. 
Miller, 389 U.S. 429 (1968), recognized a “dormant” federal 
foreign relations power that excludes states from certain 
foreign relations activity even in the absence of inconsis- 
tent federal activity. Though petitioners seek to make 
much of Zschernig, that case involved the distinct circum- 
stance of a state law that, as applied, was a vehicle for 
criticism and hostility toward foreign governments. The 
HVIRA does not do this. Indeed, the HVIRA is not ad- 
dressed to foreign governments at all, and does not reflect 
any attempt by the state to interact with any foreign 
government. It is simply a regulation of private business. 


This Court has also held that under some circum- 
stances, the President may enter into executive agreements 


17 


with foreign nations that supersede state legislation in 
much the same manner as treaties. See, e.g., United States 
v. Belmont, 301 U.S. 324 (1937). Petitioners discuss 
certain executive branch initiatives in an attempt to 
invoke this idea of “executive preemption.” But (1) the 
executive agreements relied upon by petitioners are 
expressly not preemptive (and in any event do not affect 
all of the petitioners); (2) Congress has not authorized the 
President to address Holocaust-era insurance claims to the 
exclusion of the states; and (3) this Court has rejected the 
suggestion that executive branch initiatives, unsupported 
by Congress and not reflected in preemptive executive 
agreements, can override state commercial regulation. See 
Barclays, 512 U.S. at 328-331. 


A. The HVIRA Does Not Violate The Consti- 
tution’s Allocation Of Powers Between 
The States And The Federal Government. 


Petitioners do not contend that the HVIRA is inconsis- 
tent with any provision of the Constitution’s text, or is 
preempted by any federal statute or treaty. Rather, peti- 
tioners claim a much broader principle, supposedly de- 
rived from the Constitution’s history and structure, that 
state commercial regulations must give way, even in the 
absence of the ordinary means of preemption, when the 
Executive Branch says they affect foreign relations. No 
such principle exists. 


First, the Constitution’s text, history and structure do 
not support this principle. Petitioners make much of the 
fact that state activities under the Articles of Confedera- 
tion interfered with the Continental Congress’ ability to 
conduct foreign relations, and that the Constitution was 
drafted in part to remedy that problem. But they draw the 
wrong lesson from that history. 


The Constitution could have adopted a general rule

gress broad powers over foreign commerce (which Con-

carefully crafted provisions balancing federal and state 
power belies the sweeping but unstated principle petition- 


ers suggest.”

C. The HVIRA Is Constitutional Even If It Is

1. The HVIRA Does Not Regulate Extra- 
territorially. 


Even if the McCarran-Ferguson Act does not shield 
the HVIRA from Commerce Clause attack, the statute is 
constitutional because it is not an extraterritorial law. The 
HVIRA is a California regulation of California insurance 
companies. App. 45a. It is not discriminatory and is not 
protectionist or anti-competitive in purpose or effect. It 
applies to all insurers doing business in California, foreign 
or domestic. It does not affect the manner in which any of 
the regulated companies or their affiliates do business 
outside California. 


Its only effect outside the state is informational; that 
is, it requires the reporting of historical information about 
foreign insurance policies. It does not even require out-of- 
state insurers to gather and compile the information, 
since, as far as the statute is concerned, performance of 
those tasks might be assumed by the California companies 
whose licenses are at issue. 


Petitioners’ extraterritorial regulation argument relies 
upon three decisions of this Court: Edgar v. MITE Corp., 
457 US. 624 (1982); Healy v. Beer Institute, 491 U.S. 324 
(1989); and Brown-Forman Distillers Corp. v. N.Y. State 
Liquor Auth., 476 U.S. 573 (1986). In Edgar, 457 U.S. at 
644-647, the Court held that an Illinois statute restricting 
business takeovers violated the Commerce Clause because 
the burden on interstate commerce exceeded its local 
benefits, a theory petitioners cannot seriously advance 
here. A plurality in Edgar also believed the statute was an 
extraterritorial regulation, but the plurality expressly 
approved this Court’s holding in Hall v. Geiger-Jones Co., 
242 U.S. 539 (1917), that a state blue sky law requirement 
that companies report on out-of-state matters as a condition 
of state licensing does not violate the dormant Commerce

2. Minimum Contacts Analysis Depends 
On Justifying Interests, Not Motivat- 


ing Purposes. 


The language and method of analysis in legislative 
jurisdiction decisions involving limits on both state choice 
of law and taxation make clear that what is important is 
state justifying interests, not motivating purposes. In 
Shutts, this Court explained that the point of the inquiry 
into “contacts” in the choice of law setting is to determine 
whether the contacts create “state interests, such that 
choice of its [forum state] law is neither arbitrary nor 
fundamentally unfair.” 472 U.S. at 818 (quoting Allstate 
Ins. Co. v. Hague, 449 U.S. at 312-313). See also Hoopeston 
Canning Co. v. Cullen, 318 U.S. 313, 316-317 (1943). The 
minimum contacts test, in other words, tests for justifica- 
tion, not for motivating purposes. See, e.g., Allstate, 449 
U.S. at 315, 319 (contacts with Minnesota created state 
interests justifying “stacking” of uninsured motor vehicle 
coverage even in absence of legislative purpose to protect 
those interests).

C. Making The Minimum Contacts Test De- 
pendent On Purposes Rather Than Inter- 
ests Would Be Inconsistent With The 
Principles That Underlie Substantive Due 
Process Jurisprudence And With The 
Concept Of Fundamental Fairness That 
Underlies The Minimum Contacts Test. 


The substantive constitutionality of economic legisla- 
tion under the Due Process Clause depends on an analysis 
(the rational basis test) based on justifying interests, not 
motive or purpose. Daniel, 336 U.S. at 222-224. Cf. FCC v. 
Beach Communications, 508 U.S. at 315; Metro. Life Ins. 
Co. v. Ward, 470 U.S. 869, 875 n.5 (1985). Under the 
rational basis test, a court must consider all conceivable 
state interests served by the legislation, regardless of 
whether they were expressed in the legislation or actually 
motivated the legislature. E.g., Beach Communications,

508 U.S. at 315. All of the reasons why this Court has 
evolved the rational basis test in this manner are equally 
applicable to the test for legislative jurisdiction. Moreover, 
injection into minimum contacts analysis of a “real pur- 
pose” issue is inconsistent with the concept of fundamental 
fairness that underlies the test. 


1. The Reasons That Inquiries Into Ac- 
tual Purpose Are Excluded From Sub- 
stantive Due Process Law Are Equally 
Applicable In The Legislative Jurisdic- 
tion Context. 


The watershed case for modern substantive due 
process law was United States v. Carolene Products Co., 
where this Court announced that because of the deference 
courts owe legislative decisionmaking, substantive due 
process review of legislation “must be restricted to the 
issue whether any state of facts either known or which 
could reasonably be assumed affords support for it.” 304 
U.S. 144, 154 (1938). There is no apparent reason why the 
same deference is not appropriate in the subspecies of 
substantive due process law relating to legislative jurisdic- 
tion, and petitioners have mentioned none. 


Substantive due process law’s focus on interests 
rather than purpose is based not only on the principle of 
deference, but also on three problems that would result if 
purpose were determinative. Two of these problems were 
discussed in United States v. O’Brien, a leading decision 
holding that legislative purpose or motive is normally 
irrelevant to a statute’s constitutionality. 391 U.S. 367, 
382-385 (1968). There, this Court refused to invalidate, on 
the ground that Congress had a speech-repressive pur- 
pose, a statutory prohibition on draft card destruction, 
citing both the difficulty of accurately determining legisla- 
tive purpose and the “repassage problem,” i.e., that the 
same statute “could be reenacted in its exact form if the 
same or another legislator made a ‘wiser’ speech about it.” 
Id. at 384. The third problem, not present in O’Brien, is 
that if purpose were constitutionally determinative,
