RELEVANT STATUTORY PROVISIONS 


The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), 9 US.C. §§ 1-16, 
mandates enforcement of the terms of arbitration agreements 
contained in contracts evidencing transactions in interstate 
commerce. In particular, Section 2 of the FAA, 9 USC. § 2, 
provides that such agreements “shall be valid, irrevocable, 
and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in 
equity for the revocation of any contract.” /d. 


STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


In its decision below, the Supreme Court of South Carolina 
rejected Green Tree’s challenge under the FAA to two 
interrelated class-action arbitration awards. Those two 
awards require Green Tree to pay nearly $27 million in 
statutory damages, attorney’s fees and costs to two classes 
consisting of a total of more than 3,700 individuals. Green 
Tree challenged those arbitral awards in the Supreme Court of 
South Carolina because the arbitration agreement underlying 
them does not provide for class-action arbitration, and the 
FAA does not permit class-action procedures to be 
superimposed onto that arbitration agreement. In affirming 
these awards, the Supreme Court of South Carolina rejected 
that showing and issued a decision that deepened an already 


3 


mature judicial conflict on the question whether the FAA 
permits class-action procedures to be engrafted onto an 
arbitration agreement that does not, by its terms, provide for 
class-action arbitration. 


The Supreme Court of South Carolina acknowledged that, 
on one side of this conflict, a majority of the federal courts of 
appeals have held that class-wide or consolidated arbitration 
proceedings cannot be imposed on an arbitration agreement 
that is “silent” as to those issues. Pet. App 12a-I3a 
(analyzing Champ v. Siegel Trading Co., 55 F.3d 269, 275 
(7th Cir. 1995)). The decision below also recognized that, on 
the other side of the conflict, the California Supreme Court 
and its appellate courts have ruled that class-action 
procedures can be imposed on arbitration agreements that are 
“silent” regarding the availability of class-action arbitration. 
Pet. App. 13a-16a (discussing Keating v. Superior Ct., 645 
P.2d 1192, 1208-09 (Cal. 1982), rev'd on other grounds sub 
nom. 465 U.S. 1 (1984); Blue Cross of Cal. v. Superior Ct., 
67 Cal. App. 4th 42, 62-66 (1998)) After analyzing these 
competing lines of precedent, the Supreme Court of South 
Carolina highlighted that “[t}he United States Supreme Court 
has not addressed this issue and the precedent set by the 
federal circuit courts is not binding on this Court.” Pet. App. 
22a. 


Thereafter, the court rejected the approach taken by the 
Champ line of cases, adopted the “approach taken by the 
California courts in Keating and Blue Cross,” and held that 
“class-wide arbitration may be ordered when the arbitration 
agreement is silent if it would serve efficiency and equity, and 
would not result in prejudice.” Pet. App. 22a (emphasis 
added) (footnote omitted), The South Carolina Supreme 
Court’s holding was based expressly upon its prior 
determination that “a state court may order consolidation of 
claims subject to mandatory arbitration without any 
contractual . . . directive to do so.” Pet. App. 21a. Because it 
had permitted “consolidation of appropriate claims where the 


4 


arbitration agreement is silent,” the court held that it “would 
permit class-wide arbitration, as ordering class-wide 
arbitration calls for considerably less intrusion upon the 
contractual aspects of the relationship” Pet. App 2la 
(internal quotation marks omitted) 


As shown below, this case squarely presents a recurring 
issue of paramount importance to the congressional scheme 
governing arbitration under the FAA that has generated a 
substantial conflict among the federal courts of appeals and 
state appellate courts and courts of last resort. As a result, 
Green Tree respectfully submits that its petition for a writ of 
certiorari should be granted to allow this Court to address and 
resolve this important decisional conflict 


Statutory Background 


Section 2 of the FAA provides that arbitration agreements 
subject to the FAA “shall be valid, irrevocable, and 
enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in 
equity for the revocation of any contract.” 9US.C.§2. As 
this Court has explained, Congress’s goal in enacting the 
FAA was to overcome deep-seated judicial hostility to 
arbitration and thereby allow private parties to choose to 
resolve their disputes through arbitration rather than litigation. 
See, e.g., Volt Info. Scis. Inc. vy. Board of Trs. of Leland 
Stanford Junior Univ., 489 U.S. 468, 478 (1989), Dean Witter 
Reynolds, Inc. v. Byrd, 470 US. 213, 219-20 (1985) 
Specifically, the FAA permits private parties to “‘trade[ | the 
procedures _..—~.«sOoff:«~ the courtroom for the simplicity, 
informality, and expedition of arbitration’” Gilmer v. 
InterstateJohnson Lane Corp., 500 US. 20, 31 (1991) 
(quoting Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler- 
Plymouth, Inc., 473 US. 614, 628 (1985)) Moreover, 
because judicial hostility to arbitration had existed in both 
federal and state courts, this Court concluded almost 20 years 
ago, and more recently has reaffirmed, that § 2 of the FAA 
applies both in state and federal courts. Southland Corp. v. 
Keating, 465 U.S. 1, 12-15 (1984); see Allied-Bruce Terminix

Essential to Congress’s goal of ensuring the enforcement of 
parties’ agreements to arbitrate is the principle that such 
agreements may not be rewritten by courts. Rather, state and 
federal courts must “‘rigorously enforce’ such agreements 
according to their terms.” Volt, 489 US. at 479 (quoting 
Dean Witter, 470 U.S at 221) Indeed, this Court repeatedly 
has explained that “the central purpose of the Federal 
Arbitration Act [is] to ensure ‘that private agreements to 
arbitrate are enforced according to their terms’” 
Mastrobuono v. Shearson Lehman Hutton, Inc., 514 US. §2.

Options of Chi., Inc. v. Kaplan, 514 U.S 938, 947 (1995) 
Nevertheless, the “basic objective” under the FAA “is not to 
resolve disputes in the quickest manner possible, no matter 
what the parties’ wishes, but to ensure that commercial

parties.” 514 US. at 947 (citations and internal quotation

The Supreme Court of South Carolina assumed jurisdiction 
over the Bazzle and Lackey appeals and consolidated the 
proceedings Pet App 2a The court below first ruled that 
both arbitration agreements were “governed by the FAA” ld 
at |la&n9 The court then addressed “the FAA's impact on

“{t}hus, there is no binding precedent that this Court 1s 
obligated [to] follow.” /d The Supreme Court of South 
Carolina recognized, however, that “[s]everal federal circuits 
have precluded class-wide arbitration when the arbitration 
agreement is silent,” whereas “the California courts have 
permitted class-wide arbitration on a case by case basis when 
the arbitration agreement is silent.” /d at 11a, 12a. 


Although the arbitration agreements in these cases, by their 
terms, limit arbitration to “‘disputes, claims, or controversies 
arising from or relating to this contract, or relationships 
which result from this contract,” the court below concluded 
that “this language does not limit the arbitration to non-class 
arbitration” Pet. App. 19a (emphasis added by court) 
Specifically, the court ruled that this language “creates an 
ambiguity” that the court would construe against Green Tree 
to conclude that “Green Tree’s arbitration clause was silent 
regarding class-wide arbitration ” /d. 

Given its determination that the agreement was “silent” as 
to the availability of class-action arbitration, the court 


recognized that this case implicated the conflict between the 
rule in the (hamp line of cases decided by the federal courts 
of appeals and the rule adopted by the California state courts 
The court below rejected the Champ line of cases, explaining 
that the “United States Supreme Court has not addressed this 
issue and the precedent set by the federal circuit courts is not 
binding on this Court.” Pet App 19a-20a The court below 
explained that it previously had “held that a state court may 
order consolidation of claims subject to mandatory arbitration

REASONS FOR GRANTING THE PETITION 


Further review by this Court of the decision of the Supreme 
Court of South Carolina is necessary to ensure the proper and 
uniform. resolution of a recurring and important issue under 
the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) that has generated a 
substantial conflict among federal courts of appeals and state 
appellate courts and courts of last resort. Specifically, this 
case presents the question whether the FAA permits class- 
action procedures to be imposed on an arbitration agreement 
that is “silent” as to class-action arbitration 


The clear majority of courts that have addressed this 
question and the related issue of consolidation of arbitration 
proceedings have answered no These courts have held that, 
under the FAA, the parties’ silence on the question of class- 
action or consolidated arbitration precludes imposition of 
class-action or consolidated arbitration proceedings onto the 
parties’ agreement. See, e.g., Champ v. Siegel Trading Co., 
$5 F 3d 269, 274-75 (7th Cir. 1995) (citing cases). In doing 
so, these courts have relied upon this Court’s decisions, which 
hold that the FAA was designed to overcome judicial hostility 
to arbitration by mandating that arbitration agreements be 
enforced rigorously in accordance with their terms. See, e.g., 
Volt, 489 US. at 478. 


The Supreme Court of South Carolina acknowledged the 
clear rule of these cases, which prohibit class-action 
procedures from being superimposed on a “silent” arbitration 
agreement, but expressly rejected that rule in favor of the 
minority approach first embraced by the California courts 
See, e.g., Keating v. Superior Ct., 645 P.2d 1192, 1208-10 
(1982), rev'd on other grounds sub nom. 465 US. | (1984), 
Blue Cross of Cal. v. Superior Ct., 67 Cal. App. 4th 42, 62-66 
(1998) (following Keating and rejecting Champ line of cases) 


13 


This minority approach holds that the FAA does not prohibit 
a court from imposing class-action or other procedures onto 
an arbitration agreement, rather, class-action procedures may 
be imposed, in the court’s discretion, if they further the 
court's notions of judicial efficiency and equity Pet App 
13a-16a Put another way, the minority approach permits 
courts to frustrate private agreements to arbitrate by allowing 
those agreements to be modified whenever they do not 
unambiguously preclude a procedure or result that a court, in 
its discretion, believes will “serve efficiency and equity, and 
would not result in prejudice” /d at 22a 


This minority approach ignores that the FAA is designed to 
allow parties to choose arbitration rather than litigation by 
mandating that courts enforce such agreements in accordance 
with their terms. Moreover, the minority approach cannot be 
reconciled with this Court’s decisions which make clear that 
concerns regarding efficiency and economy are subsidiary to 
enforcement of the parties’ agreement according to its terms 
Indeed, under the minority approach adopted by the court 
below, parties’ agreements to arbitrate would be subject to the 
same judicial hostility that the FAA was designed to combat 
when it was enacted more than 75 years ago That result 
frustrates congressional intent that arbitration agreements 
must be enforced according to their terms Further, adoption 
of this approach would increase the costs of private 
arbitration by obligating parties to draft long and unwieldy 
arbitration agreements that seek to anticipate and address 
every possible procedural contingency to prevent additional 
procedures from being imposed upon the parties’ private 
agreement in the putative interests of judicial economy and 
efficiency 


Finally, the conflict implicated by the decision of the South 
Carolina Supreme Court is one of paramount importance 
because, without a uniform nationwide standard, the 
determination whether class-action arbitration can be 
compelled will turn on the happenstance of geography, rather 


14 


than the intent of parties as expressed in the terms of their 
arbitration agreements. A uniform national rule is vital 
because the same arbitration agreements often may apply to 
agreements entered across the country Moreover, the 
absence of a uniform standard fosters not only geographic 
forum shopping but also forum shopping between federal and 
state courts See Southland, 465 US at 15 (rejecting 
interpretation of FAA that would “encourage and reward 
forum shopping’) 


In sum, certiorari should be granted to resolve this deep and 
recurring conflict and to adopt the majority view that the FAA 
mandates that arbitration agreements be enforced according to 
their terms and not on the basis of ambiguous policy choices 
of a court’s making 


I. THE DECISION BELOW IMPLICATES A DEEP 
AND MATURE CONFLICT ON THE QUESTION 
WHETHER THE FEDERAL ARBITRATION ACT 
PERMITS CLASS-ACTION PROCEDURES TO BE 
IMPOSED ON AN ARBITRATION AGREEMENT 
THAT DOES NOT PROVIDE FOR CLASS- 
ACTION ARBITRATION. 


The decision of the Supreme Court of South Carolina 
deepens a conflict among both state and federal courts on the 
recurring and important question whether the FAA permits 
class-wide arbitration where the parties’ arbitration agreement 
is “silent,” i.e., it does not expressly provide for or against 
class-action arbitration. As shown below, the majority of 
courts, including the Seventh Circuit, Eighth Circuit and 
Alabama Supreme Court, have held that the FAA requires 
enforcement of private arbitration agreements according to 
their terms, and that courts therefore have no authority to 
order class-action arbitration where an arbitration agreement 
does not expressly provide for class-action arbitration. These 
decisions, in turn, are built on the decisions of the Second, 
Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, and Eleventh Circuits, which hold 


15 


that a court has no authority to order consolidated arbitration 
if the parties’ agreement does not so provide 


In stark contrast, the minority position, adopted by the court 
below and other state courts, holds that if the parties’ 
agreement is “silent” regarding class actions—because it does 
not expressly preclude them—a court may, in its discretion, 
superimpose class-action arbitration if consistent with its 
notions of efficiency and equity. The minority position relies, 
among other things, on a decision of the First Circuit that 
permits consolidated arbitration even if the parties’ agreement 
does not provide for it. The minority courts invoke the 
proposition that the FAA leaves them free to order arbitration 
upon terms as they see fit, so long as they do not directly 
contravene any provision of an agreement and do not require 
resort to a judicial rather than arbitral forum.

B. Courts Applying The Minority Approach, 
Including The Court Below, Have Held That The 
FAA Permits Discretionary Judgments To 
Justify Class-Wide Or Consolidated Arbitration 
Even When An Arbitration Agreement Does Not

As the Supreme Court of South Carolina acknowledged, 
there are “two different approaches” on the question 
presented here. Pet. App. at lla. The court below expressly 
rejected the Champ line of cases, and instead adopted the 
minority position of the state courts in California and 
Pennsylvania (as supported by the First Circuit) Those 
courts hold that even if the parties to an arbitration agreement 
have not provided for class arbitration, the FAA does not 
prohibit such procedures from being superimposed on an 
arbitration agreement as a matter of “discretion.” /d. at 15a. 


In the decision below, the Supreme Court of South Carolina 
criticized Champ and United Kingdom for making “strict 
enforcement of the terms of the agreement” the paramount 
policy under the FAA. Pet. App. 13a __ Instead of 
implementing the parties’ intentions as expressed in the terms 
of their agreement, the court adopted precisely the reasoning 
that Champ, United Kingdom, Med Center, and other courts 
expressly have rejected. It held that, faced with an arbitration 
agreement that is “silent” regarding class arbitrations, class 
arbitration could be ordered, as a matter of discretion, if doing 
so “would serve efficiency and equity, and would not result in 
prejudice.” /d at 22a. Although the class members in each 
of these arbitrations were awarded at least $5,000, plus 
attorneys’ fees, the court below expressed concern that, under 
the majority position, “parties with nominal individual claims, 
but significant collective claims, would be left with no avenue 
for relief,” and that arbitrating numerous identical cases in 
multiple arbitrations would not “serve the interest of judicial 
economy.” /d.

The ruling of the court below is built directly and expressly 
on “the approach taken by the California courts,” Pet. App. 
22a, particularly Keating v. Superior Court, 645 P.2d 1192 
(1982), and Blue Cross of California v. Superior Court, 67 
Cal. App. 4th 42 (1998). In Keating, the California Supreme 
Court held, in a case under the FAA, that state law permitted 
a court to order class arbitration even where an arbitration 
agreement did not provide for it. 645 P.2d at 1209-10. The 
California Supreme Court acknowledged that “a judicially 
ordered classwide arbitration would entail a greater degree of 
judicial involvement than is normally associated with 
arbitration.” /d. at 1209 (explaining that court “would have to 
make initial determinations regarding certification and notice 
to the class, and... to exercise a measure of external 
supervision in order to safeguard the rights of absent class 
members to adequate representation”). Nevertheless, the 
California Supreme Court concluded that the availability of 
class arbitration must be determined not based solely with 
regard to the parties’ intent, but rather on an analysis of which 


procedure offers “a better, more efficient, and fairer solution.” 
Id. 


California appellate courts have built upon the analysis in 
Keating to hold that the FAA does not prohibit class-action 
procedures from being superimposed on an arbitration 
agreement that does not provide for class-wide arbitration. 
Specifically, the court in Blue Cross squarely held that the 
FAA does not “preempt[] California decisional authority 
authorizing classwide arbitration.” 67 Cal. App. 4th at 46.

concluding that if an order of class arbitration would not 
expressly contradict any terms of the arbitration agreement, a 
court was free to issue such an order under state law 
authorizing it. See id. at 60, 65. Whether to do so was a 
question for the discretion of the trial court. Jd at 64. The 
Blue Cross court concluded that the purpose of the FAA was 
simply “to abolish antiarbitration laws and to make

agreements to arbitrate specifically enforceable” /d at 63

arbitration, the court in Blue Cross reasoned that this 
approach did not violate this narrow goal of the FAA and 
therefore was not pre-empted. /d at 64 (“[E]ven if a conflict

trial court may resolve the conflict. It is unlikely the AAA

arbitration.”) (citations omitted) Indeed, the Blue Cross

agreements by making classwide arbitration available in

federal circuit courts and state courts that prohibit class-action

There is a well developed and persistent conflict among the 
federal courts of appeals and state courts regarding the 
requirements of the FAA. On the one hand, the Champ line 
of cases makes clear that class-actions and consolidation

rigorously in accordance with their terms. On the other hand,

LAW/ANALYSIS 


l. Waiver 


The Bazzle and Lackey plaintiffs argue this Court can 
avoid addressing the broad issue of whether class-wide arbi- 
trations were permissible by affirming on the alternate ground 
that Green Tree manifested consent to class-wide arbitration 
and may not now object to it. We are not convinced by this 
waiver argument. 


The courts of this state have recognized that a party cannot 
complain when it receives the relief for which it has asked. 
See McKissick v. J.F. Cleckley & Company, 325 S.C. 327, 
479 S.E.2d 67 (Ct. App. 1996); Estes v. Gray, 319 S.C. 551, 
462 S.E.2d 561 (Ct. App. 1995). Specifically, a party that by 
its conduct consents to arbitration of a dispute waives any 
subsequent judicial challenge to its arbitrability. See Rock- 
Tenn Co. v. United Paperworkers Int'l Union, 184 F.3d 330 
(4th Cir. 1999). Although this is an accurate statement of the 
law, Green Tree did not consent to class-wide arbitration by 
defending itself in the arbitration. As discussed, Green Tree 
protested class-wide arbitration vigorously in both cases, 
receiving adverse rulings every time, before defending itself 
on the merits. 


In Bazzle, Green Tree requested arbitration and received it 
after the motion to certify the class was granted by the trial 
court. Even after the trial court certified the Bazz/e class, 
Green Tree continued to push for arbitration, and the trial 
court granted Green Tree’s Motion to Compel arbitration. 
The Bazzles therefore contend that Green Tree got what it 
asked for and cannot now complain that the court abused its 
discretion by granting the relief it requested. Although Green 
Tree did object to the class-wide arbitration before the arbi- 
trator, the Bazzles contend it waived its ability to object by 


10a 


manifesting consent to proceed through some of its subse- 
quent actions. For example, the Bazzles point to Green Tree’s 
Motion to Decertify the class before the arbitrator on sub- 
stantive grounds as evidence that Green Tree manifested 
consent to the arbitrator’s authority to certify or decertify the 
class. The Bazzles claim that by placing the decertifica- 
tion issue squarely before the arbitrator, Green Tree mani- 
fested acceptance of the arbitral forum as sufficient to resolve 
the issue. 

In Lackey, the plaintiffs argue similarly that Green Tree 
waived its right to object to class-wide arbitration by moving 
to compel arbitration when it knew of the class allegations 
being asserted. They argue that moving to compel arbitration 
without insisting that the trial court address the class certi- 
fication motion manifested consent for the arbitrator to decide 
the certification issue. The Lackey plaintiffs assert that Green 
Tree did not object with motions and stays until after the 
arbitrator ruled against Green Tree on the general issue of 
whether this action could possibly proceed as a class-wide 
arbitration. The Lackey plaintiffs claim Green Tree was obli- 
gated to force the trial court to decide the Lackey plaintiffs’ 
motion before it granted Green Tree’s Motion to Compel 
because Green Tree was aware of the class allegations and 
knew the arbitrator would decide the issue if the trial court 
did not. 

We are not persuaded by the plaintiffs arguments for 
waiver in Bazzle or Lackey. In Bazzle, Green Tree argued 
against the Motion to Certify before the trial court, lost, and 
then filed a Motion for Reconsideration of the issue. The 
Motion for Reconsideration was denied and Green Tree 
appealed the denial all the way to this Court. In Lackey, 
although Green Tree did not insist the trial court decide the 
issue before arbitration was compelled, it objected vigorously 
to the possibility of class certification at the first hearing on 
the matter before the arbitrator and filed actions to enjoin 
class certification in federal and state court. Green Tree’s 


lla 


motions in both cases were entirely unsuccessful, but, none- 
theless, manifested Green Tree’s objection to class-wide arbi- 
tration sufficiently to counter the plaintiffs’ waiver argument.

III. Due Process Rights of Absent Class Members 


Green Tree and the Amici argue that the absent class 
members’ due process rights were violated by the class-wide 
arbitration of their claims. The Bazzle and the Lackey 
plaintiffs, on the other hand, claim the rights of the absent 
class members were adequately protected through proper 
notice. Regardless, both classes of plaintiffs argue that this 
issue is not preserved for review because Green Tree failed to 
raise it in a timely fashion. We agree that the issue was not 
preserved for review, and, further, find no evidence that the 
rights of the class members were not protected in this case. 


It is well-settled that an issue may not be raised for the first 
time on appeal. In order to preserve an issue for appellate 
review, the issue must be (1) raised to and ruled upon by the 
lower court, (2) by the appellant, (3) in a timely manner, and 
(4) with sufficient specificity. See /’On, L.L.C. v. Town of Mt. 
Pleasant, 338 S.C. 406, 526 S.E.2d 716 (2000); Wilder Corp. 
v. Wilke, 330 S.C. 71, 497 S.E.2d 731 (1998); Toal, Vafai, & 
Muckenfuss, Appellate Practice in South Carolina at 66 (S.C. 
Bar 1999). 


In Bazzle, Green Tree first raised the issue of the absent 
class members’ due process rights in its motion to vacate the 
arbitrator’s final award. Green Tree argued vaguely that “the 
Arbitrator violated certain due process protections afforded 
by the Fourteenth Amendment and the South Carolina 
Constitution.” To be preserved, the issue must have been 
raised and ruled upon by the trial court at the time the class 


was certified. / ‘On, L.L.C. v. Town of Mt. Pleasant. As Green 
Tree gave the trial court no opportunity to address this issue, 
it is procedurally barred. /d. 


Similarly, in Lackey, Green Tree did not raise the due 
process rights of the absent class members when it moved to 
decertify the class before the arbitrator. Green Tree raised this 


26a 


issue for the first time when it argued that the arbitrator's 
final award should be vacated. Because it did not give 
the arbitrator an opportunity to rule upon this issue, Green 
Tree failed to preserve it for review before this Court. 
1’On; Wilder. 


Green Tree has not articulated precisely how it believes the 
class members’ due process rights have been violated. In any 
case, the class members’ rights appear to have been properly 
protected by the notice given to all of them.” Green Tree was 
given the opportunity to review and comment on the notice 
sent and did not voice an objection to it at that time. Although 
protection of the due process rights of absent class members 
is an essential component in all class actions, and one which 
may necessitate particular attention in class-wide arbitrations, 
Green Tree has not presented the Court with the means to 


address it properly.”

COMBINED FINDINGS OF FACT AND 
CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 


This case was filed as a class action on May 28, 1996. 
From the beginning, Green Tree demanded that the case be 
arbitrated pursuant to the Arbitration clause included in its 
form contracts with consumers. After the Circuit Court found 
its arbitration clause to be unconscionable and denied Green 
Tree’s motion to compel arbitration, Green Tree appealed to 
the South Carolina Court of Appeals. On March 16, 1998 the 
Court of Appeals reversed this Court and found that while the 
contracts were contracts of adhesion, that finding did not 
support a conclusion that the contracts were unconscionable. 
Lackey, et al. v. Green Tree Financial Corp., 330 S.C. 388, 


498 S.E.2d 898 (S.C. App. 1998). 


Green Tree raised no issue to the Court of Appeals that the 
Plaintiffs class action allegations could not be addressed in 
the arbitration proceeding it consistently demanded. The 
Court of Appeals noted that the case was brought as a class 
action suit. Id. at 901. It is noteworthy that the Court of 
Appeals concluded that the Plaintiffs “failed to demonstrate 
that they are unable to get the same relief in arbitration which 
is available in a judicial setting.” Id. at 905. 


The Court of Appeals remanded this case to this court “for 
further proceedings.” Up to that point, Green Tree had never 
stated its position that the Arbitrator could not consider the 
class allegations contained in the complaint filed by the 
Plaintiffs almost two years earlier. Green Tree did not take 
any action, including filing a Motion to Strike Class Alle- 
gation from the Plaintiffs complaint, prior to consenting to the 
selection of the Arbitrator on July 23, 1998. 


Green Tree now claims that the Arbitrator was without 
authority to determine or decide whether the matter could 
proceed as a class action in arbitration. Green Tree has 
waived this argument. The Consent Order signed on July 23, 


45a 


1998 reserves no claims or issues for the Court to decide prior 
to submitting the matter to binding arbitration. The Consent 
Order is attached as Exhibit “A”. Green Tree filed no reser- 
vation of rights on any issues, including potential class 
certification, for a ruling by the Court prior to providing its 
written consent to be bound by the decision of the Arbitrator 
selected by both parties. This Court has no authority to 
consider the issue of class certification which was never 
raised by Green Tree below prior to the binding arbitration 
proceeding it repeatedly demanded. Green Tree was afforded 
exactly the proceeding in binding arbitration that it sought. 
Green Tree cannot seek to correct its failure to raise this issue 
after the Court of Appeals issued its Order and prior to Green 
Tree’s written agreement contained in the July 23, 1998 
Consent Order giving the Arbitrator jurisdiction to handle all 
matters, including ali pending motions. 


A recent case from the Supreme Court of the United States 
is instructive here, and indeed, it is dispositive of Green 
Tree’s arguments concerning the standard of review applic- 
able to the Arbitrator’s decision on the arbitrability of class 
treatment. In First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan, 514 
U.S. 938, 115 S. Ct. 1920, 131 L.Ed.2d 985 (1995). The 
Court held that the questions of whether a Court or an 
Arbitrator hold the primary power to decide arbitrability turns 
upon the agreement of the parties. If the parties agree to 
submit the questions of arbitrability itself to arbitration, then 
the standard of review applicable to the arbitrator’s decision 
on arbitrability is the same as that applicable to the arbi- 
trator’s decision on any other issue which the parties agreed 
to arbitrate. First Options, 514 U.S. at 943. 


Similarly, the Fourth Circuit has stated: 


It would be odd if having consented to have the 
arbitrator decide what its legal rights . . . were, [appellee] 
could then have the same questions redetermined by two 
courts— the district court and this court. . . Since 


46a 


appellee, by submitting without a peep to the arbitrator's 
jurisdiction, admitted the competence of the arbitrator to 
determine its “legal rights,” it cannot complain that the 
arbitrator exceeded his authority by determining them. 


Richmond, Fredericksburg & Potomac R.R. Co. v. Transp. 
Communications Int'l Union, 973 F.2d at 280 (citations 


omitted). 

Thus, in this case, in which the parties willingly submitted 
to arbitration the questions of whether the Arbitrator could 
adjudicate a class action, the Arbitrator’s decision is not 
subject to review except to the extent that Green Tree can 
present grounds for vacatur. Green Tree is simply unable to 
make the showing required to justify this extraordinary relief. 

A review of the record in arbitration reflects that Green 
Tree did not argue to the Arbitrator that only a Court could 
consider whether or not the case could proceed as a class 
action in arbitration. Green Tree argued to the Arbitrator that 
its arbitration clause did not permit or authorize class actions 
and urged the Arbitrator to rule that the arbitration clause 
would not permit the case to proceed as a class action. The 
Arbitrator, after considering all the arguments and materials 
submitted by the parties, ruled against Green Tree's position. 
Only after the Arbitrator decided that the arbitration clause 
permitted class arbitration and that class treatment was 
appropriate in this case did Green Tree object. Green Tree's 
failure to argue to the Arbitrator that only a Court could grant 
class certifiction cannot now be cured by seeking to vacate 
the Arbitrator’s Order. 

The Courts have recognized that parties can manifest their 
agreement to arbitrate issues by their conduct. Rock-Tenn 
Company v. United Paperworkers International Union, 184 
F.3d 330 (4th Cir. 1999). Plaintiffs argue that Green Tree’s 
failure to reserve an argument with the Circuit Court that 
the Arbitrator could not decide class arbitration prior to

D. FINDINGS OF FACT 
1. The Transactions 


The Plaintiffs and all others similarly situated in this case 
entered into standard form consumer Retail Installment 
Contracts and Security Agreements (hereinafter “HI-RIC”) 
with Green Tree secured by real property that are subject to 
the South Carolina Consumer Protection Code. (Notebook 1, 
Items 9 & 10). The HI-RIC is identified by the alphanumeric 
Code GT-15-41-010 (11/92). The contracts and agreements 
entered into by the Plaintiffs and Green Tree were prepared 
by Green Tree and obligated each consumer to make monthly 
payments. The HI-RIC makes specific reference to two 
provisions of the South Carolina Consumer Protection Code. 
Furthermore, the HI-RIC unequivocally requires the 
consumer to acquire insurance on the secured realty and list 
the creditor as a lost payee. 


In each of the transactions, the consumer completed a 
Green Tree application with the assistance of a Green Tree 
dealer. Green Tree’s dealer then submitted the application to 
Green Tree for approval. (/d.). The Green Tree application 
form contained no attorney preference and insurance pref- 
erence notice. No separate attorney preference form was 
provided to the consumer at the time of credit application. 
Green Tree verified the consumer’s employment and income 
and determined the creditworthiness of the consumer. (/d). 
Green Tree would then notify its dealer if credit was granted. 
If credit was not approved, Green Tree would notify the 
consumer directly in writing of the denial of credit. (Note- 
book 2, Item 23). 


The record shows that Green Tree was the “creditor” from 
the beginning of the transaction. Green Tree was the creditor 


62a 


before any assignment and before it could claim the status of 
assignee. If credit was granted, Green Tree alone set the terms 
of the credit. Green Tree determined the rate of interest. 
Green Tree determined if the consumer was required to meet 
any special conditions. Green Tree obtained an appraisal on 
the real property. Green Tree obtained a title search. Green 
Tree prepared the HI-RIC. Green Tree prepared the mort- 
gage identified by alphanumeric designation GT-15-41-050 
(8/90). Green Tree prepared the HUD-1 Statement which 
identified Green Tree as the creditor. Green Tree specified on

closing. (Notebook 2, Items 11 & 23). No attorney was

Green Tree would then issue checks to its dealers.

E. CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 


1. Violations of and Liability under the Attorney 
Preference Statute. 


Based upon the ruling of the South Carolina Supreme 
Court in Tilley v. Pacesetter, 333 S.C. 33, 508 S.E.2d 16 
(1998) and the facts shown on the record in this case, Plain- 
tiffs are entitled, as a matter of law, to a decision that Green 
Tree is liable for violating S.C. Code Ann. § 37-10-102(a). 


As a major corporation doing business in South Carolina 
for an extended period: of time, Green Tree, based on its 
continuing non-compliance with the preference statute, has 
demonstrated a complete indifference to its legal obligations 
to adhere to South Carolina’s statutory requirement. Despite 
Green Tree’s vast financial resources, it took no affirmative 
action to educate itself on the requirements of South Caro- 
lina’s attorney preference statute. Green Tree’s former chief 
counsel, Mr. Evans, testified that he was responsible for 
Green Tree’s legal compliance. Mr. Evans testified that he 
was “not aware of the attorney and insurance agent pref- 
erence statute prior to 1995.” 


All the testimony and evidence presented at the arbitration 
hearing clearly shows an ongoing pattern and practice of a 
disregard for the applicable South Carolina law. The pattern 
and practice began as a careless or negligent attitude and 
graduated into wilful non-compliance and a disregard for 
South Carolina law. While Plaintiffs as a class did not 
attempt to show actual damages, certainly the plaintiffs 
suffered injury and loss of their state law right to be advised 
of their attorney and insurance agent preference. Plaintiffs 
were denied any knowledge of this important provision of 
South Carolina law, which the Legislature chose to enact and 
reenact for many years. I find that Plaintiffs in this class were 
harmed even to the extent that it is difficult to put a dollar 
figure on their injury. One of the factors that the Arbitrator 
must pursue and examine is the attitude and conduct of the 


70a 


defendant in determining the appropriate penalty in the range 
provided by S.C. Code Ann. §37-10-105. With regard to an 
appropriate penalty, the arbitrator should look at the totality 
of the circumstances. That decision can not be made in a 
vacuum. The case law says that the requirements of a penal 
statute must be applied in a manner which results in fairness 
and justice to the parties. The record reflects that the 
consumer lending industry is becoming more and more 
significant in South Carolina. As a matter of public policy, 
the General Assembly intended to protect consumers in 
secured transactions of this nature when it placed the 
preference statute in the South Carolina Consumer Protection 
Code. The record in this case reflects that Green Tree did 
have corporate legal counsel and yet no reasonable or direct 
efforts were taken by Green Tree or his staff to: 


(1) Learn of the South Carolina attorney and insurance 
preference statutory provision; and 


(2) More importantly, once it came to Green Tree’s 
attention, to take some affirmative action to bring 
Green Tree into compliance with the provisions of 


the statute. 


The real question becomes: What did Green Tree do once it 
was on notice of the preference statute? The answer to that 
question is one of the most disturbing and shocking aspect 
[sic] of this case. Green Tree did nothing. Green Tree did 
nothing after repeatedly being directly made aware of the 
statute. 


Contrary to the position of Green Tree, this case is far 
worse than the related case of Lackey. Green Tree’s Home 
Improvement Division learned of the preference statute by

at no time issued a policy statement. Green Tree did nothing 


Tla 


to educate its employees or dealers. Green Tree’s Home 
Improvement Division merely went about its business, unlike 
the Manufactured Housing Division, which at least issued a 
memo about compliance to some of its personnel. 


After hearing all the testimony and reviewing all the 
evidence and considering all the relevant factors in accessing 
an appropriate penalty, I find and conclude as a matter of law 
that Green Tree’s behavior up to September 25, 1995 was 
careless and reckless and that the appropriate penalty in this 
case, as in the Lackey case, is $5,000.00 per transaction for 
transactions occurring prior to September 25, 1995. Based

Attorney’s Fees and Costs 


Having determined that the Defendant is liable to the

202(8) of the South Carolina Consumer Protection Code 


72a 


provides for the awarding of reasonable attorney’s fees and 
costs to prevailing plaintiffs, to be assessed against the 
defendants. Freeman v. A&M Mobile Home Sales, Inc., 293 
S.C. 255, 359 S.E.2d 532 (Ct. App. 1987). Accordingly, the 
Plaintiff Class herein is entitled to recover reasonable 
attorney’s fees and costs to be assessed against Green Tree in 
addition to the penalty to be paid to class members. 


Plaintiffs Class Counsel have submitted a petition for fees 
and costs requesting that the Arbitrator award attorney’s fees 
amounting to one-third the gross recovery generated for the 
benefit of the Plaintiffs Class. They have also submitted their 
hourly rates for non-contingent legal work paid on a prompt 
and regular basis. The Arbitrator has carefully reviewed the 
Plaintiffs Class’ fee petition and supporting documentation, 
including the affidavit of Professor John P. Freeman, who 
also testified at this hearing as an expert witness in the Lackey 
case and in this case in the field of attorney’s fees in the 
specific context of class actions. Professor Freeman was 
designated an expert in this field without objection. After this 
hearing and cross examination by Green Tree of Professor 
Freeman, Green Tree filed with the Arbitrator certain docu- 
ments in opposition to the Plaintiff Class’s fee petition 
including the affidavit of attorney William L. Pope opposing 
the requested attorney’s fees. The Arbitrator has carefully and 
thoroughly reviewed these materials submitted by Green Tree 
and the hourly rate information provided by Class counsel. 


South Carolina has no reported appellate cases in which 
attorney’s fees sought by prevailing plaintiffs in a class action 
were contested. Nonetheless, the South Carolina Supreme 
Court has established certain factors which should be 
considered in awarding attorneys fees. 


The general rule is that attorney’s fees are not recoverable 
unless authorized by contract or statute. Hegler v. Gulf Ins. 
Co., 270 S.C. 548, 243 S.E.2d 443 (1978). In the instant case, 
S.C. Code Ann. §37-5-202(8) authorizes an award of reason- 


73a 


able attomey’s fees and costs to the Plaintiffs’ class, who 
prevailed in this arbitration proceeding. In deciding the 
amounts of attorney’s fees and costs to be awarded, the 
Arbitrator should consider each of the following six factors: 
the nature, extent and difficulty of the services rendered; the 
time and labor necessarily devoted to the case; the pro- 
fessional standing of counsel; the contingency of compen- 
sation; the fee customarily charged in the locality for similar 
legal services; and the beneficial results obtained. Numerous 
recent cases decided by the South Carolina Supreme Court 
confirm that each of these factors must be reviewed and 
considered in determining the reasonableness of an attorney’s 
fee to be awarded to a prevailing plaintiff pursuant to contract 
or statute. See Global Protection Corp. v. Halbersberg, 332 
S.C. 149, 160, 503 S.E.2d 483, 489 (1998) and Taylor v. 
Medenica, 331 S.C. 575, 503 S.E.2d 458 (1998) (recovery of 
fees provided under South Carolina Unfair Trade Practices 
Act, §39-5-10 et seq.); Jackson v. Speed, 326 S.C. 289, 308 

486 S.E.2d 750, 760 (1997) (recovery of fees provided under 
Motor Vehicle Dealers Unfair Trade Practices Act, §56-15-10 
et seq.); Rice v. Multimedia, Inc., 318 S.C. 95, 456 S.E.2d 
381 (1995) (recovery of fees provided under South Carolina 
Payment of Wages Act, §41-10-10, ef seg.); Blumberg v. 

Nealco, 310 S.C. 492, 427 S.E.2d 659 (1993) and Baron Data 
Systems, Inc. v. Loter, 297 $.C. 382, 377 S.E.2d 296 (1989) 
(recovery of fees provided by express terms of contract). See 
also Freeman v. A&M Mobile Home Sales, Inc., 293 S.C. 

255, 359 S.E.2d 532 (Ct. App. 1987) (pre-Baron Consumer 
Protecton Code case in which fees were awarded under §37- 

5-202(8) after similar reasonableness analysis consisting of 
professional standing of plaintiff's attorneys, nature and 

extent of services rendered, complexity of issues, and 

beneficial results obtained). The Baron Data Systems case 

instructs that consideration of all six factors is required, but 

none of the six is controlling in determining the reasonable 

fee to be awarded. Baron Data Systems, supra. As Judge 


74a 


Goolsby observed in Freeman v. A&M Mobile Home Sales, 
Inc.. 293 S.C. 255, 359 S.E.2d 532 (Ct. App. 1987), a 
Consumer Protection Code case in which the Court of 
Appeals affirmed a fee award pursuant to the same statute 
relied upon by these Plaintiffs, “the measure of proper 
attorney's fees to be assessed against a party is not always the 
time spent.” 293 S.C. at 266, 359 S.E.2d at 538-39. Where a 
fee award is supported by any competent evidence, an appeal 
will not prevail. Baron /ua Systems, supra, citing Singeton 
v. Collins, 251 S.C. 268%. 151 S.E.2d 246 (1968).

G. ATTORNEY’S FEES AND COSTS 


Having determined that the Defendant is liable to the 
Plaintiff Class for its class-wide violations of the South 
Carolina attorney and  imsurance agent preference 
requirement, | now turn to the issue of attorney’s fees. 
Section 37-5-202(8) of the South Carolina Consumer 
Protection Code provides for the awarding of reasonable 
attorney’s fees and costs to prevailing plaintiffs, to be 
assessed against the defendants. Freeman v. A&M Mobile 


99a 


Home Sales, Inc., 293 $.C. 255, 359 S.E.2d 532 (Ct. App. 
1987). Accordingly, the Plaintiff Class herein is entitled to 
recover reasonable attorney’s fees and costs to be assessed 
against Green Tree in addition to the penalty to be paid to 
class members. 


Plaintiff Class Counsel have submitted a petition for fees 
and costs requesting that the Arbitrator award attorney’s fees 
amounting to one-third the gross recovery generated for the 
benefit of the Plaintiff Class. They have also submitted their 
hourly rates for non-contingent legal work paid on a prompt 
and regular basis. The Arbitrator has carefully reviewed the 
Plaintiff Class’s fee petition and supporting documentation, 
including the affidavit of Professor John P. Freeman, who 
also testified after the Bazzle hearing before me as an expert 
witness for the Lackey and Bazzle cases in the field of 
attorney’s fees in the specific context of class actions. 
Professor Freeman was designated an expert in this field 
without objection. Professor Freeman is a noted expert in 
attorney fee awards in class actions. He has testified in courts 
around the country on the subject. After the Bazzle hearing 
and Green Tree’s cross examination of Professor Freeman, 
Green Tree filed certain documents in opposition to the 
Plaintiff Class’s fee petition including the affidavit of 
attorney William L. Pope opposing the requested attorney’s 
fees. The Arbitrator has carefully and thoroughly reviewed 
the materials submitted by Green Tree and the hourly rate 
information provided by Class Counsel. 


South Carolina has no reported appellate cases in which 
attorney’s fees sought by prevailing plaintiffs in a class action 
were contested. Nonetheless, the South Carolina Supreme 
Court has established certain factors which should be 
considered in awarding attorneys fees. The general rule is that 
attorney’s fees are not recoverable unless authorized by 
contract or statute. Hegler v. Gulf Ins. Co., 270 S.C. 548, 243 
S.E.2d 443 (1978). In the instant case, S.C. Code Ann. §37-5- 


100a 


202(8) specifically authorizes an award of reasonable 
attorney’s fees and costs to the Plaintiff class, who prevailed 
in this arbitration proceeding. In deciding the amounts of 
attorney’s fees and costs to be awarded, the Arbitrator should 
consider each of the following six factors: the nature, extent 
and difficulty of the services rendered; the time and labor 
necessarily devoted to the case; the professional standing of 
counsel; the contingency of compensation; the | fee 
customarily charged in the locality for similar legal services; 
and the beneficial results obtained. Numerous recent cases 
decided by the South Carolina Supreme Court confirm that 
each of these factors must be reviewed and considered in 
determining the reasonableness of an attorney's fee to be 
awarded to a prevailing plaintiff pursuant to contract or 
statute. See Global Protection Corp. v. Halbersberg, 332 S.C. 
149, 160, 503 S.E.2d 483, 489 (1998) and Taylor v. 
Medenica, 331 S.C. 575, 503 S.E.2d 458 (1998) (recovery of 
fees provided under South Carolina Unfair Trade Practices 
Act, §39-5-10 et seq.); Jackson v. Speed, 326 S.C. 289, 308, 
486 S.E.2d 750, 760 (1997) (recovery of fees provided under 
Motor Vehicle Dealers Unfair Trade Practices Act, §56-15-10 
et seq.); Rice v. Multimedia, Inc., 318 S.C. 95, 456 S.E.2d 
381 (1995) (recovery of fees provided under South Carolina 
Payment of Wages Act, §41-10-10, et seqg.); Blumberg v. 
Nealco, 310 S.C. 492, 427 S.E.2d 659 (1993) and Baron Data 
Systems, Inc. v. Loter, 297 S.C. 382, 377 S.E.2d 296 (1989) 
(recovery of fees provided by express terms of contract). See 
also Freeman v. A&M Mobile Home Sales, Inc., 293 S.C. 
255, 359 S.E.2d 532 (Ct. App. 1987) (pre-Baron Consumer 
Protection Code case in which fees were awarded under §37- 
§-202(8) after similar reasonableness analysis consisting of 
professional standing of plaintiffs attorneys, nature and 
extent of services rendered, complexity of issues, and 
beneficial results obtained). The Baron Data Systems case 
instructs that consideration of all six factors is required, but 
none of the six is controlling in determining the reasonable 


10la 


fee to be awarded. Baron Data Systems, supra. As Judge 
Goolsby observed in Freeman v. A&M Mobile Home Sales, 
Inc., 293 S.C. 255, 359 S.E.2d 532 (Ct. App. 1987), a 
Consumer Protection Code case in which the Court of 
Appeals affirmed a fee award pursuant to the same statute 
relied upon by Plaintiffs, “the measure of proper attorney's 
fees to be assessed against a party is not always the time 
spent.” 293 S.C. at 266, 359 S.E.2d at 538-39. Where a fee 
award is supported by any competent evidence, an appeal will 
not prevail. Baron Data Systems, supra, citing Singleton v. 
Collins, 251 S.C. 208, 161 S.E.2d 246 (1968). 


After consideration of each of the prescribed six factors to 
establish a reasonable fee award, | find and conclude as 
follows: 


|. The Nature, Extent, And Difficulty of The Case 


The record in this case clearly reflects that this plaintiff 
class action has been one of extraordinary difficulty from the 
outset. One must consider the complexity inherent in all class 
actions; the fact that the attorney preference law is unique to 
the South Carolina Consumer Protection Code; the issues 
surrounding the validity and effect of the arbitration clause; 
the appeal of the initial Circuit Court order finding the 
arbitration clause unconscionable and unenforceable; the 
unique issues raised in the case relating to class arbitration; 
and the ongoing refusal by Green Tree to cooperate with 
Plaintiffs’ Counsel on discovery and other matters. 


This class action was commenced in May 1996, seeking 
class-wide relief on behalf of certain customers of Green Tree 
Financial Corporation and related entities for Green Tree’s 
violation of the attorney and insurance agent preference 
provisions of the South Carolina Consumer Protection Code. 
Green Tree moved to compel arbitration pursuant to the 
arbitration clause in its retail installment contracts. The 
Circuit Court issued its order finding that the arbitration

clause was unconscionable and therefore unenforceable. 
Green Tree appealed, and the South Carolina Court of 
Appeals, in Lackey v. Green Tree Financial Corp., 330 S.C.

action to the Circuit Court for referral to arbitration. At that 
time, the parties consented to Arbitration pursuant to the 
Court of Appeals Order and chose this Arbitrator to hear the 
case (Notebook 5, Item 15). After the case was referred to 
arbitration, the Plaintiffs continued to move for class 
certification. The Arbitrator, after reviewing the broad 
language in the arbitration clause and considering all matters 
raised by Green Tree, certified the class in the arbitration 
proceeding. Green Tree contested the Arbitrator’s power to 
certify and adjudicate the class. In September 1998, Green 
Tree filed an action in the United States District Court for the 
District of South Carolina seeking a temporary injunction 
halting the adjudication of this lawsuit as a class action. The 
Plaintiffs successfully defended against the injunction in the 
federal forum on the ground of lack of subject-matter 
jurisdiction. Thereafter, in December 1998, Green Tree sued 
the Plaintiffs in the Circuit Court, again seeking to enjoin the 
Arbitrator from adjudicating this case as a class action. The 
Plaintiffs again were successful in defending against Green 
Tree’s procedural attempts to thwart their efforts on behalf of 
the class, and the case proceeded to trial before the Arbitrator 
in March of 2000. 


The brief procedural history of the case only outlines part 
of the story about the difficulty of this case. The consumer 
finance industry collectively engaged numerous lobbyists 
during the 1997 legislative session in an all-out effort aimed 
at retroactively eliminating all class actions brought to 
enforce the attorney and insurance agent preference law. 
Counsel was forced to forego undertaking of [sic] other fee-

6. Customary Legal Fees for Similar Services 


In Blum v. Stensen, 465 U.S. 886, 104 S.Ct. 1541 (1984), 
the U.S. Supreme Court held that customary fees for like 
work are “to be calculated according to the prevailing market 
rates in the relevant community.” 104 S.Ct. at 1547 (emphasis 
added). 


As the South Carolina Supreme Court found in Global 
Protection Corp. v. Halbersberg, 332 S.C. 149, 503 S.E.2d 
483 (1998), the customary fee in South Carolina for complex 
cases accepted on a contingent-fee basis ranges from one- 
third to one-half of the gross recovery. While Global 
Protection Corp. was not a class action, its difficulty and 
complexity make it relevant here. 


In the class action context, courts have likewise awarded 
substantial fees based upon a percentage of recovery. The 
“relevant community” for like work in this case is that 
community of class action litigation lawyers involved in 
complex civil cases. Professor Newberg, cited supra, has 
written that “the relevant community may potentially be the 
forum, out-of-state forums, or a uniform national rate.” 
Newberg, supra, note 23 §414 at 142. 


Citing such circumstances as the complexity and expense 
of class action litigation, multiple parties, and the 
involvement of “national defendants who normally will be 
represented by national, as well as local counsel,” Professor 


Newberg states: 


Courts have recognized that these and other 
circumstances make a local forum rate for compensation 
. .. a inappropriate and illogical standard, in contrast to 


106a 


. . . the growing adoption by courts of national rate 
standards. 


Newberg, supra, note 23 §415 at 143. 


Professor Freeman attached to his affidavit in support of 
the fee request in this case copies of two other recent class 
action cases in which he testified about fee reasonableness 
and other issues. In one of these cases, Jn re State of Florida 
v. American Tobacco Co., (Arbitration Award dated 
September 11, 1998), the arbitrators awarded plaintiff's 
counsel a fee of in excess of $3.4 Billion. In the other, Ramos 
v. Philip Morris Companies, Inc., 743 So.2d 24 (Fl. App. 
1999), the Florida Court of Appeals affirmed a fee award of 
over $46 Million. These cases illustrate the fact that class 
counsel are very well compensated as a rule when they 
provide a benefit to class members. The reasonableness of 
attorney fee awards in class actions should not be confined to 
any single element such as the number of hours spent in 
achieving a beneficial result for the class. 


The class action cases cited by Professor Freeman and by 
Class Counsel were virtually all settled, rather than tried. 
Because this case required the Plaintiffs to brief and argue an 
appeal and fight off two efforts by Green Tree to stop the 
litigation with an injunction; engage in an expensive, tiime- 
consuming legislative battle; independently gather statewide 
information without the aid of traditional discovery; and try 
the case before an arbitrator, they are entitled to a reasonable 
attor.ieys fee of $3,066,666.00 in addition to the judgment of 
$9,200,000.00 obtained on behalf of the Plaintiff Class.
