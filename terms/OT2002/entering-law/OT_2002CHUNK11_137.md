## Standards and Tests selection: Federal claim preclusion as a removal defense

### Federal claim preclusion as a removal defense

**Governing question:** Whether an earlier federal judgment makes a state-law complaint removable under §§1331 and 1441.

**Current rule:** A prior federal judgment’s claim-preclusive effect is a defense and does not convert a complaint presenting only state-law claims into a federal cause. Complete statutory preemption instead requires Congress to displace the state claim with an exclusive federal cause supplying its legal character. A conclusive judgment defense or an artful-pleading label supplies no such cause. Without original jurisdiction or an actual later cure, dependent federal merits judgments must be vacated and the action returned to state court.

**Authority by component:**

- Federal claim preclusion remains a defense; dependent judgment correction: Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana, 1998-02-24; Scalia for all nine Justices.

**Present operation effective:** 1998-02-24.

**Exceptions and limits:** The rule does not decide actual preclusion or foreclose otherwise authorized enforcement proceedings. Moitie’s claim-preclusion rule remains; its footnote supplies no general removal exception.

**Related current rules:** Diversity cured before trial after erroneous removal requires actual jurisdiction before trial, not efficiency or finality alone.

**Holdings navigation:** Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana — Federal-question removal and claim preclusion.

## Standards and Tests selection: Federal interest classification and national-bank home-state charges

### Federal interest classification and national-bank home-state charges

**Governing question:** Classification of §85 interest and the charges a national bank may collect from local and interstate borrowers.

**Current rule:** Federal law defines §85 interest; home-state law then supplies the allowed rate within the statute. Effective §7.4001(a) includes compensation for extending credit, making a line available, or borrower default/breach of credit conditions. Examples include periodic rates, late, insufficient-funds, overlimit, annual, cash-advance and credit-related membership fees. It ordinarily excludes appraisal, repayment-insurance premiums/commissions, finder, document-preparation, notarization and credit-report fees. Subsection (b) permits the highest rate allowed a home-state chartered or licensed lender, retaining provisions material to the loan class, including loan-size restrictions without requiring that state license. Subsection (c) preserves definitions/calculations used solely for state law; home-state-permitted late fees may be federally classified interest charged interstate even if excluded from the state’s own interest calculation. Subsection (d) permits the agreed rate for a corporate borrower only where home-state law denies that borrower a usury defense.

**Authority by component:**

- OCC §7.4001(a)–(d), complete definition, lender rule, state-law distinction and corporate condition, 1996-04-01; admitted noncase source effective April 1, 1996.
- Smiley — Parts I, II and IV, delegated interpretation, late-fee classification and specific interstate permission, 1996-06-03; Souter for all nine.
- Smiley — Part VI, statutory and compatible-law limits, 1996-06-03; Souter for all nine.

**Present operation effective:** Smiley, June 3, 1996, for the controlling late-fee construction; §7.4001(a)–(d) effective April 1, 1996.

**Controlling construction:** After independent examination of statutory limits and delegated authority, Chevron’s second step governs the Comptroller’s reasonable inclusion of contractual credit-card late fees. Fixed amount, contingency, payment incentives, continuing finance charges or nonproportionality to balance, time or administrative loss do not categorically exclude them. Home-state permission activates §85’s interstate allowance and defeats a conflicting host-state restriction, including its application through general liquidated-damages law. This classifies the fees without validating unauthorized contracts or every listed category.

**Exceptions and limits:** Compatible formation, disclosure, unconscionability and consumer-protection law survives. Section 85 retains the home-state rate or one percent above the specified Federal Reserve ninety-day commercial-paper discount rate, whichever is greater and no more, with its special state-bank-rate qualification; where no state rate is fixed, the greater of seven percent or that discount-rate alternative applies, with advance interest calculated by the instrument’s days to run. Foreign-branch local-law maxima, the bona fide bill-of-exchange current sight-draft exchange qualification and limited historical business/agricultural savings retain their separate scope. Section 86 retains knowing excess-rate liability, forfeiture and an action within two years for twice paid interest; classification alone awards none of that relief. Non-interest-charge rules supply no additional general reasonableness test here.

**Effective transition:** The regulation supplies no special retroactivity provision. “Medicare corrective adjustments and permissible interpretive change” states Smiley’s separately adopted qualified use of a current authoritative interpretation for antecedent transactions without clear prior agency guidance.

**Open question:** Smiley assumes without deciding that courts always independently determine statutory preemption. Substantive-term deference creates no freestanding agency preemption power and decides no independent procedural challenge under 12 U.S.C. §43: its applicability, compliance, exceptions, preservation and any violation remain unresolved. The decision does not treat interpretive form, published comments or litigation use as proof of compliance or an exemption.

**Holdings navigation:** Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A., 517 U.S. 735.

#### Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana

**Docket or dockets:** No. 96-1971.

**Decided:** 1998-02-24.

##### Federal claim preclusion remains a defense

**Holding:** Under 28 U.S.C. §§1331 and 1441, a prior federal judgment's claim-preclusive effect is a defense and does not transform a complaint presenting only state-law claims into an action arising under federal law. Complete statutory preemption differs: Congress must displace the state claim with the exclusive federal cause that supplies its legal character; the completeness of an asserted judgment defense does not perform that work.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Scalia), unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Louisiana mortgage claims may be defeated by bankruptcy judgments without acquiring a federal character. The Court decides neither the mortgage rights nor the bankruptcy orders’ validity or actual preclusive effect; an otherwise available enforcement proceeding needs its own authority.

##### Dependent merits judgments must be vacated

**Holding:** Because this action lacks the asserted original federal jurisdiction and no later jurisdictional cure is established, the dependent federal merits judgments must be vacated and the whole action returned to state court. A different merits ground for one respondent does not cure the same jurisdictional defect.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Scalia), unanimous.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The entire action must return to state court. Vacatur includes the Bank and Fountainbleau Storage Associates preclusion judgments and the Browns’ differently reasoned failure-to-state-a-claim judgment. The state court may apply governing federal preclusion law.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Moitie's ordinary claim-preclusion rule is preserved; its footnote is not extended into a general removal exception for prior federal judgments.
- Peacock supplies the independent-authority requirement for a later action while preserving ordinary enforcement within its proper sphere.
- City of Chicago is distinguished because substantial federal claims appeared on the complaints' face there; supplemental jurisdiction has no comparable original anchor here.
- Caterpillar is distinguished because actual jurisdiction arose before trial there; finality alone cannot save this action's merits judgments.
- Matsushita preserves judgment recognition without equating that effect with adjudicatory power.
- Richards preserves constitutional limits on binding parties; it supplies no removal authority and no finding about these petitioners' binding status.
- Baker preserves the distinction among recognition, execution and new duties in another forum; that distinction creates no universal removal grant.

#### Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A., 517 U.S. 735

**Docket or dockets:** 95-860
**Decided:** June 3, 1996

##### Who defines interest under section 85, and does the Comptroller have authority to interpret that obligation?

**Holding:** Federal law determines whether a charge is interest under section 85, and home-state law then supplies the permitted rate within the statute's terms. Congress did not unambiguously exclude the challenged late-payment charges, and the Comptroller's delegated responsibility for the substantive banking obligation supports authoritative interpretation within that ambiguity.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), Parts I, II, IV and VI, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; unanimous controlling rationale for all questions in this Business and Commercial Law entry. Parts III-A, III-B and V have only the eight Associate Justices' joins and appear under Administrative Law, Agency interpretation, policy change and antecedent transactions. Every part has a direct majority; no fractured-rationale analysis is required.

**Controlling explanation:** MCI requires identification of enacted boundaries before Chevron deference, and NationsBank applies that sequence within genuine national-banking responsibility. Sections 93, 1818 and 1813(q) assign enforcement responsibilities for national-bank obligations, including section 85. Section 93a authorizes rules carrying out those responsibilities except where authority is expressly and exclusively assigned to another agency, and its grant does not apply to section 36 or national banks' Glass-Steagall securities activities. Section 86's judicial remedy does not render all administration of the substantive section 85 obligation exclusively judicial. South Dakota may permit an agreed rate without a numerical cap, but cannot enlarge the federal category merely by calling a charge interest.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court enters no enforcement order and dispenses with none of the enforcement statutes' institutional scope, predicates, notice, hearing, findings or consent, review and remedy requirements. Agencies remain unable to redefine exclusively judicial remedies outside their actual delegation.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**
- Chevron supplies deference after identifying statutory ambiguity and delegated responsibility.
- MCI preserves enforcement of enacted boundaries before deference.
- NationsBank supports reasoned classification within actual banking responsibility, without power to override statutory conditions.
- Adams Fruit is distinguished because the Comptroller has relevant substantive administrative responsibility; its bar on agency redefinition of exclusively judicial remedies outside a delegation remains.
- Daggs supplies the home-state allowed-rate mechanism without classifying every contractual charge as federal interest.

##### Are these contractual late-payment charges within a permissible federal interpretation of interest?

**Holding:** The Comptroller reasonably includes these contractual charges for late repayment of credit within section 85 interest, and that interpretation controls under Chevron's second step. Fixed amount, contingency, an incentive to pay promptly, continuing finance charges, or lack of proportionality to balance, time or administrative loss does not categorically exclude a charge from that category.

**Controlling explanation:** A statutory reference to an interest rate does not demand a periodic percentage for every component. The Federal Reserve percentage alternative does not make that form exclusive, and section 86's twice-paid-interest remedy does not require a charge to be calculable when credit is initially extended. The contractual contingency makes the amount ascertainable when triggered. The Comptroller reasonably distinguishes payment for extending credit, making it available or defaulting on its conditions from reimbursement for unrelated services. Donnell rejects a penalty label as a categorical exclusion, although its percentage-overdraft and compounding rule did not itself decide flat credit-card charges. Meilink applied a statute expressly separating penalties and interest and does not impose that separation here.

**Material application:** Neither card's pleaded late-fee terms defeat the permissible classification. No factual cost study or new reasonableness formula is needed to decide the pleaded categorical objection.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court adopts no independently sufficient judicial best reading and approves no entire nonexclusive regulatory fee list. Attorneys' fees, litigation costs, prepayment charges, unrelated services and other breach charges remain undecided. Federal classification alone does not validate an undisclosed, unconscionable or contractually unauthorized charge. Compatible contract and consumer-protection rules remain.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**
- Donnell defeats a categorical penalty exclusion within its own percentage-overdraft and compounding limits; it does not itself decide flat late fees.
- Meilink is distinguished by its express statutory separation of penalties from interest.

##### May California prohibit these home-state-permitted charges through its liquidated-damages rule?

**Holding:** Federal classification as interest and South Dakota's undisputed permission bring these charges within section 85's interstate allowance, so California may not prohibit them by the conflicting application of section 1671. The holding neither invalidates section 1671 generally nor displaces compatible state regulation.

**Controlling explanation:** Marquette permits a national bank to collect home-state-allowed interest from out-of-state borrowers; lending to California customers does not itself relocate this South Dakota bank. California's asserted requirement would withdraw this particular federal permission, and describing it as general contract law does not remove the conflict. Barnett Bank protects the statutory banking option with its actual conditions; empirical proof of burden on the bank's entire business is unnecessary. Cipollone and Gade require attention to statutory scope and do not permit elimination of a federal option merely because the bank could abandon it. The Court assumes without deciding that statutory preemption must always receive independent judicial determination: deference concerns the substantive federal category, not a freestanding agency power to preempt.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Because federal law defines interest, the Court does not reach the abstract Article I or Article IV objection based on a supposed delegation of that definition to South Dakota. It reaches neither an independent section 24 incidental-powers ground nor compliance with California's 1995 credit-card legislation. Formation, disclosure, unconscionability and other generally applicable defenses remain available insofar as their application does not conflict with section 85.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**
- Marquette supplies interstate application after federal classification, with bank location and home-state permission remaining prerequisites.
- Barnett Bank protects the specific federal permission while preserving its statutory conditions and compatible state law.
- Cipollone and Gade retain their statute-specific scope; neither supplies general national-bank field immunity.

##### What statutory conditions and remedies remain outside this confined fee-classification decision?

**Holding:** Classification of these charges does not enlarge section 85's rate alternatives or section 86's separate remedial predicates, and does not establish blanket validity of every regulatory fee category. The decision adjudicates the challenged California categorical theory without determining an independent excessive-interest claim or remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 85 permits the home-state-allowed rate or one percent above the specified Federal Reserve ninety-day commercial-paper discount rate, whichever is greater and no more, subject to the special state-bank-rate qualification. If no state rate is fixed, the greater of seven percent or that discount-rate alternative applies; advance interest is reckoned by the instrument's days to run. The foreign-branch local-law maximum, the bona fide bill-of-exchange qualification permitting the current sight-draft exchange rate in addition to interest, and savings limited to historical business or agricultural transactions do not expand permission for these accounts. Section 86 retains the knowing excess-rate predicate and interest forfeiture, distinct from twice-paid-interest recovery in an action commenced within two years.

The regulation retains material home-state loan-class restrictions, including loan-size restrictions without requiring a state license. Solely state-law definitions and calculations remain different from federal classification; the corporate-borrower provision does not govern Smiley. Non-interest-charge rules remain separate. Ordinary exclusions for appraisal, repayment-insurance, finder, document-preparation, notarization and credit-report fees remain ordinary, not absolute. Neither every paragraph (c) application nor every listed category is approved.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Independent procedural challenges and section 43's distinct notice, comment, publication and exception provisions are preserved as stated under Administrative Law, Agency interpretation, policy change and antecedent transactions.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The California Supreme Court's judgment sustaining judgment on the pleadings for Citibank is affirmed, 9-0. The Court orders no evidence-taking, agency action, damages, restitution, injunction, class certification or special transition, and resolves no separate compatible-contract defense or related petition.

## Earlier completed public law: 2002-11-05 — Syngenta Crop Protection, Inc. v. Henson

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Syngenta-Crop-Protection-Inc-v-Henson_merits_2002-11-05.md`; Public Projection only.

## Event

Syngenta Crop Protection, Inc. v. Henson, No. 01-757. Merits decision, November 5, 2002. Argued October 15, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit. The question is whether the All Writs Act, alone or with ancillary enforcement authority, permits removal when §1441’s original-jurisdiction requirement is unsatisfied. Henson’s Louisiana chemical-exposure action was removed, transferred and dismissed to enforce a related Alabama federal settlement. The court of appeals vacated that dismissal because the state action supplied no independent original federal jurisdiction.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Eleventh Circuit’s jurisdictional judgment is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The federal merits dismissal remains vacated, and the removed Louisiana action must return to state court.

## Opinion Topology

Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer joined. STEVENS, J., filed a concurring opinion, joined by no other Justice. Stevens joins the Court’s entire opinion.

## Holdings

### Auxiliary enforcement power cannot create removal jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** The All Writs Act authorizes auxiliary measures within existing jurisdiction; it neither supplies original jurisdiction nor makes an exception to §1441’s removal conditions. Retained or ancillary power to enforce a federal settlement cannot eliminate the requirement of an independent original-jurisdiction basis for removing a distinct state action, and a federal preclusion defense does not supply that basis.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join the entire rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress specified the conditions for removing a state action. Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana holds that even a conclusive federal judgment defense does not turn a state-law complaint into a federal cause. Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. permits enforcement when an authorized dismissal order incorporates obligations or retains jurisdiction, while requiring an independent basis where it does neither. Peacock v. Thomas distinguishes genuine enforcement within existing authority from a new jurisdictional anchor. Combining those auxiliary powers with the All Writs Act cannot supply the missing statutory condition.

Pennsylvania Bureau of Correction v. United States Marshals Service likewise prevents residual writ authority from displacing a governing statute. United States v. New York Telephone Co. concerns assistance within existing jurisdiction; it does not authorize removal without original jurisdiction. The need to prevent evasion of a settlement can be serious, but practical enforcement value cannot create the power Congress withheld. Any separate enforcement motion requires lawful retained or independent jurisdiction, the actual settlement obligation and proper notice. Under §2283, an injunction against state proceedings additionally must be expressly authorized by an Act of Congress, necessary in aid of federal jurisdiction, or necessary to protect or effectuate a federal judgment. The availability of that remedy in a separate proceeding lies outside the removal question.

### The jurisdictional defect requires return to state court

**Holding and operative rule:** Because the removed action has no independent original-jurisdiction basis, the dependent federal merits dismissal must remain vacated and the action must return to state court.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Transfer and the asserted settlement breach cannot cure the absent jurisdictional prerequisite. The state forum can address a properly asserted federal preclusion defense, and a separately authorized federal enforcement proceeding retains its own scope. This judgment decides the court in which the removed action may proceed, not whether Henson breached the agreement or whether any particular injunction is available.

## Precedent Treatment

- Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana: applies the distinction between a federal preclusion defense and original jurisdiction, including the requirement to vacate dependent merits judgments where jurisdiction is absent.
- Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America: preserves enforcement when a lawful dismissal order incorporates settlement duties or expressly retains jurisdiction; that authority does not create jurisdiction over a separately removed action.
- Peacock v. Thomas: applies the independent-authority limit while preserving actual decree-effectuation powers within their proper source.
- Pennsylvania Bureau of Correction v. United States Marshals Service, 474 U.S. 34: applies the rule that residual writ authority does not displace a specifically governing statutory scheme.
- United States v. New York Telephone Co., 434 U.S. 159: distinguishes its auxiliary-assistance holding within existing jurisdiction; that holding remains intact. Stevens’s proposed overruling is not adopted.
- In re Prudential Insurance Co. of America Sales Practice Litigation: preserves the need for actual settlement scope, lawful retained authority and an independent Anti-Injunction Act basis for a separate restraint on state litigation.

## Law After Decision

All Writs and ancillary-enforcement theories cannot remove an action lacking independent original jurisdiction. The rule applies even when a federal settlement may furnish a conclusive defense or a strong reason to seek protection. Lawfully sourced enforcement remains available on its own predicates; New York Telephone’s auxiliary-assistance holding is preserved.

## Separate Writings

Justice Stevens concurs and joins the entire Court opinion. He would go further in treating United States v. New York Telephone Co. Rather than merely distinguish its expansive construction of the All Writs Act, he would overrule that construction. His own dissent in that case treated the Act as a procedural means of exercising jurisdiction already granted, using means consistent with established legal usages, rather than a general power to help the Government or litigants achieve their objectives. The effort to derive removal authority from that decision illustrates, in his view, why its overbroad construction should be repudiated. No other Justice joins this proposal, and it does not change his full agreement with the removal rule or the judgment.

## Procedure After Action

The removed Louisiana action returns to state court with the federal merits dismissal vacated. Any separate federal settlement-enforcement request must establish its own jurisdictional and remedial predicates; any restraint on state proceedings must also satisfy §2283 and the decree’s actual scope.

No. 01-757; 28 U.S.C. §§1441(a), 1651(a) and 2283. The underlying complaint supplied no independent original-jurisdiction basis. Settlement compliance and a separate injunction’s availability were outside the removal question.

## Earlier completed public law: 2003-05-19 — Breuer v. Jim's Concrete of Brevard, Inc.

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Breuer-v-Jims-Concrete-of-Brevard-Inc_merits_2003-05-19.md`; Public Projection only.

## Event

Breuer v. Jim's Concrete of Brevard, Inc., No. 02-337. Merits decision, May 19, 2003. Argued April 2, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit. The court of appeals affirmed the district court's refusal to remand Breuer's FLSA action to Florida state court, on a certified interlocutory appeal. The question is whether §216(b)'s authorization to maintain an action in a competent federal or state court expressly bars removal under §1441(a). Breuer seeks return to state court; his former employer seeks retention after removal. The complaint seeks unpaid wages and related statutory relief, and original federal jurisdiction over the pleaded federal claim is undisputed.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Eleventh Circuit's judgment sustaining refusal to remand is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

Breuer's request for remand on the asserted FLSA exception is denied, and the action remains in federal district court.

## Opinion Topology

Scalia delivers the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer join in full. The Court is unanimous.

## Holdings

### The FLSA forum clause permits this removal

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 216(b) of the Fair Labor Standards Act permits an employee to maintain a covered action in a competent state or federal court, but does not expressly prohibit removal under 28 U.S.C. §1441(a). An FLSA action within original federal jurisdiction is therefore removable by the defendant under the governing removal provisions unless another applicable express statutory exception bars removal; Breuer's pleaded federal wage claim meets that jurisdictional condition.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join the complete rule, its application and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Concurrent original jurisdiction and an exception to removal do different work. Section 1441(a) authorizes removal of an action within original federal jurisdiction unless Congress expressly provides otherwise. Section 216(b) allows an employee to maintain the wage action in either competent forum; removal continues that same action. The employee's power to sue in state court consequently remains meaningful without guaranteeing that the action will finish there.

Breuer's strongest argument gives maintain a continuation-through-judgment meaning. But the same subsection describes the private right as a right to bring an action when specifying its termination upon the Secretary's qualifying suit. Even continuation does not necessarily mean continuation in one tribunal. Section 1445, by contrast, expressly bars removal of railroad actions arising under the Federal Employers' Liability Act. That restriction does not cover this wage action. An express restriction need not use a prescribed phrase, but the whole FLSA provision supplies none.

Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets requires attention to Congress's removal limits; it does not erase the express-exception condition of the governing statute. Syngenta Crop Protection, Inc. v. Henson requires an independent original-jurisdiction basis, and Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana rejects a federal defense as a substitute. Breuer's own FLSA claim supplies that basis. Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis preserves the actual jurisdictional prerequisites, which are satisfied here. The asserted expense of federal litigation cannot create a statutory prohibition, particularly when the FLSA itself provides fees and costs upon a plaintiff's judgment. The conclusion follows from this FLSA text and the governing removal provisions, rather than a universal meaning assigned to maintain in every statute.

## Precedent Treatment

- Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets, 313 U.S. 100: preserves faithful construction of Congress's removal limits while giving effect to the governing §1441(a) express-exception condition.
- Syngenta Crop Protection, Inc. v. Henson, November 5, 2002: applies the requirement of an independent original-jurisdiction basis and distinguishes an action lacking that basis from Breuer's pleaded federal claim.
- Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana, February 24, 1998: preserves the distinction between a federal defense and a federal claim supplying original jurisdiction; the latter is present here.
- Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis, December 10, 1996: preserves actual statutory jurisdictional prerequisites; the established federal-question jurisdiction here makes its later-cure setting unnecessary.

## Law After Decision

The FLSA's concurrent-forum clause leaves the defendant's otherwise authorized federal-question removal right intact. Courts must examine original jurisdiction and any applicable express statutory exception separately. The decision resolves §216(b) in its complete context; other enactments retain their own text and structure.

## Earlier completed public law: 2003-04-22 — Jinks v. Richland County

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Jinks-v-Richland-County_merits_2003-04-22.md`; Public Projection only.

## Event

Jinks v. Richland County, No. 02-258. Argued March 5, 2003; decided April 22, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of South Carolina. The question is whether §1367(d) validly protects supplemental state-law claims, including claims against a county, during federal pendency and for its prescribed period after dismissal. The state court held the statute unconstitutional as applied to political subdivisions and reversed an $80,000 wrongful-death verdict as time-barred.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

## Public Action

The South Carolina Supreme Court's limitations judgment is reversed and the case remanded for remaining preserved issues affecting the verdict.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The federal tolling rule governs these claims. The competent state court shall apply the actual federal filing and dismissal record and the statutory protection, then determine the remaining preserved issues affecting the $80,000 verdict. The federal civil-rights summary judgment remains undisturbed.

## Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivers the opinion of the Court, in which Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer join. All nine Justices join the entire opinion; no separate opinion is filed.

## Holdings

### Valid federal tolling of qualifying supplemental claims

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1367(d) is a necessary and proper means of administering authorized federal adjudication and binds state courts as to qualifying supplemental claims, including these claims against Richland County. It tolls the limitations period for a claim asserted under subsection (a), and any other claim in the same action voluntarily dismissed at the same time as or after dismissal of a subsection (a) claim, while pending and for thirty days after dismissal unless state law provides a longer tolling period.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's opinion of the Court, fully joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer; all nine Justices directly adopt the constitutional and statutory-coverage grounds and the reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress may keep lawful federal adjudication from becoming a limitations trap for claims placed within supplemental jurisdiction. Article III and the power to establish inferior federal tribunals supply the end; McCulloch permits means adapted to that legitimate end without demanding absolute indispensability. The limited claim-linked period lets federal courts exercise lawful discretion under §1367(c) without forcing forfeiture or duplicative protective state actions. Subsection (c) permits declining jurisdiction where a claim raises a novel or complex state-law issue, substantially predominates over original-jurisdiction claims, all original-jurisdiction claims have been dismissed, or exceptional circumstances supply other compelling reasons. The court here dismissed the federal claim and lawfully declined the state claims; the subsequent refiling meets the federal period.

The County argues that state control over subdivision liability forbids this rule. But the rule governs time for covered claims, leaving substantive state tort standards operative. New York distinguishes commands to legislatures from competent state courts' duty to apply valid federal law. Raygor excludes a nonconsenting State's immune state-law claims from subsection (a) and therefore from the linked tolling provision; these covered County claims present a different defendant and statutory predicate. Monell supplies the relevant municipality–State distinction in its civil-rights setting. Alden and Florida Prepaid retain their actual holdings sustaining expressly authorized federal enforcement within valid enumerated power; the present county ground requires no enlargement of those holdings.

Syngenta rejected removal without an actual jurisdictional source. Here the federal civil-rights claim, the supplemental grant and the express tolling provision supply the legal sources. Calling the County's limitations defense immunity cannot defeat that valid federal rule. The state court must apply it and resolve the remaining preserved issues affecting the verdict.

**Precedent treatment:** McCulloch supplies fitted-means analysis; New York's legislative-command rule is distinguished; Raygor's excluded-State-claim rule remains operative on its own predicate; Syngenta's source requirement is satisfied; Alden, Florida Prepaid and Monell retain their actual scopes.

## Precedent Treatment

- McCulloch v. Maryland, 4 Wheat. 316 (1819): applied to a claim-linked means adapted to authorized federal adjudication.
- New York v. United States (June 19, 1992): distinguished between legislative commands and state-court application of valid federal law; its anticommandeering holding retains force.
- Raygor v. Regents of the University of Minnesota (February 27, 2002): preserved as a statutory exclusion for immune state-law claims outside §1367(a), distinguished from covered county claims.
- Alden v. Maine (June 23, 1999): preserved at its express FLSA state-court enforcement holding.
- Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank (June 23, 1999): preserved at its expressly authorized patent-enforcement grounds.
- Syngenta Crop Protection, Inc. v. Henson (November 5, 2002): distinguished because this proceeding and tolling rule have actual enacted jurisdictional sources.
- Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978): its municipality–State distinction supports rejecting automatic derivative state immunity while state law supplies the tort cause and liability standards.

## Law After Decision

Section 1367(d) validly protects its qualifying claims on the enacted terms, including covered claims against a county. State courts must honor that protection when adjudicating their own substantive causes of action. Raygor continues to exclude immune state-law claims outside the supplemental grant. The federal time rule applies in still-open cases within its scope; remaining trial objections and ordinary remedy questions remain for the competent state court.
