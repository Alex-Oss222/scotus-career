## Standards and Tests selection: Acquired source significance for product-design trade dress

### Acquired source significance for product-design trade dress

**Governing question:** The distinctiveness required for unregistered product-design trade dress under Lanham Act §43(a).

**Current rule:** Product design requires secondary meaning: its primary significance to the relevant public must identify a source rather than the product itself, even when consumers do not know the source’s name. Inherent distinctiveness is insufficient. Unusual design, producer intent, familiarity with the product and copying do not themselves establish acquired source association. Source significance, nonfunctionality and likelihood of confusion remain separate requirements; no universal survey method is imposed.

**Authority by component:**

- Acquired-source prerequisite and separate infringement requirements: Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc. — Product-design trade dress requires acquired source significance, March 22, 2000; Scalia’s opinion joined by all seven other Associate Justices.

**Present operation effective:** Wal-Mart, March 22, 2000.

**Exceptions and limits:** Qualifying restaurant presentation and packaging retain their separate inherent-distinctiveness routes. The opinion’s genuinely uncertain product-versus-packaging classification guidance is explanatory beyond the holding and supplies no additional operative component here.

**Related current rules:** Nonfunctionality burden for unregistered trade dress governs the distinct §1125(a)(3) burden. Wal-Mart supplies no decision about that 1999 provision’s application to antecedent conduct or the earlier trial.

## Standards and Tests selection: Inherently distinctive nonfunctional restaurant trade dress

### Inherently distinctive nonfunctional restaurant trade dress

**Governing question:** Whether acquired secondary meaning is additionally necessary under pre-1988 Lanham Act §43(a).

**Current rule:** Nonfunctional restaurant trade dress that is inherently distinctive may be protected without acquired secondary meaning if the remaining infringement requirements, including likelihood of confusion, are met. Protection does not expire after an unspecified period merely because acquired meaning has not developed. Functionality, distinctiveness and confusion remain independent requirements.

**Authority by component:**

- Inherent-distinctiveness alternative and rejection of an unwritten expiration period: Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., “Inherent distinctiveness suffices without acquired secondary meaning”, June 26, 1992; White, Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter.

**Present operation effective:** *Two Pesos*, June 26, 1992.

**Exceptions and limits:** No general ownership of cuisine, decorative style, useful designs or generic matter, later product-design rule, new proof burden or resolution of the excluded functionality challenge follows.

**Holdings navigation:** Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., 505 U.S. 763 (1992) — Trade dress.

## Standards and Tests selection: Source-identifying color and functionality

### Source-identifying color and functionality

**Governing question:** Color eligibility and the independent functionality limit on trademark and trade-dress protection.

**Current rule:** Color may receive trademark protection when it acquires source-identifying significance and is nonfunctional, subject to ordinary Lanham Act requirements including likelihood of confusion where required. Bare color requires secondary meaning; the law protects neither color in the abstract nor useful features. A feature essential to use or purpose or affecting cost or quality is functional, and protection cannot foreclose color shown competitively necessary. Source significance does not overcome functionality.

For product features, an expired utility patent supplies strong evidence of functionality at the level of the feature identified as useful, not a conclusive presumption about every appearance detail. Once essential use or purpose or an effect on cost or quality establishes utilitarian functionality, alternative workable designs do not require a further competitive-necessity inquiry and cannot save the feature. A genuinely separate ornamental, incidental or arbitrary appearance receives its own nonfunctionality analysis; its presence in a patented product alone does not defeat protection.

**Authority by component:**

- Color may identify source under the Lanham Act: Color may identify source under the Lanham Act, 1995-03-28. Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes.
- Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features: Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features, 1995-03-28. Justice Souter's opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer: nine direct rationale votes on this operative limitation.

- Feature-specific utility-patent evidence, independent utilitarian bar, alternative designs and separate ornament: Canonical Decision Record `TrafFix_Devices_v_Marketing_Displays_merits_2001-03-20.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / Useful features remain available to competitors, 2001-03-20; Souter’s unanimous opinion.

**Present operation effective:** TrafFix, March 20, 2001, for feature-specific patent evidence and the independent utilitarian bar; Qualitex’s March 28, 1995 color requirements remain operative.

**Exceptions and limits:** The decision does not resolve every aesthetic feature or color-market competition question or establish infringement merely from eligibility. The existing inherently distinctive trade-dress rule is not replaced by a universal secondary-meaning demand.

**Holdings navigation:** Color may identify source under the Lanham Act; Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features. [TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc., 532 U.S. 23 — Useful product features and trade-dress protection](HOLDINGS.md#traffix-devices-inc-v-marketing-displays-inc-532-us-23).

**Open question:** TrafFix adopts no automatic constitutional bar to all trade dress associated with expired patents or new aesthetic-functionality test. It does not resolve the 1999 nonfunctionality-burden amendment’s temporal reach.

#### Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc., 529 U.S. 205 (2000)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 99-150.

**Decided:** March 22, 2000.

##### Whether unregistered product design may rely on inherent distinctiveness without acquired source significance

**Holding:** A claimant asserting unregistered product-design trade dress under section 43(a) must prove secondary meaning: the design's primary significance to the relevant public identifies a source rather than the product itself, even if consumers do not know the source's name. Inherent distinctiveness alone is insufficient; acquired source significance does not dispense with nonfunctionality, likelihood of confusion or the remaining applicable requirements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by all seven other Associate Justices; eight direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela separately supports the acquired-source requirement and bounded judgment without joining the opinion.

**Material application:** Samara's asserted garment combination is product design, and secondary meaning was not asserted below. Recognition, producer intent and copying do not establish acquired source identification. The Court decides a legal prerequisite without retrying the jury's facts or imposing a universal survey requirement.

##### What relief may be disturbed by the product-design distinctiveness error

**Holding:** The Second Circuit's judgment is reversed insofar as it sustains this product-design claim through inherent distinctiveness alone, and the case is remanded to remove reliance on that federal trade-dress ground and address consequent relief only as necessary. The independent copyright and state-law merits remain undisturbed, with overlapping injunction, damages, fee and cost components separated according to their lawful bases.

**Proposition-level authority:** Scalia's opinion of the Court, joined by all seven other Associate Justices; eight direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela separately supports the acquired-source requirement and bounded judgment without joining the opinion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Remand supplies no presumed preservation of an unasserted theory, automatic reopening of proof or new trial. Earlier narrowing of the injunction and reductions of damages remain; independent copyright treatment is not converted into federal trade-dress relief.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The garments are product design; no particular bottle, container or hybrid requires classification here. The Court does not adjudicate the garments' functionality, reopen the separate confusion presumption or labeling instruction, determine the copyrights' originality, change the state-law preemption disposition or establish a general aesthetic-functionality rule. It prescribes no universal survey or new consumer-proof method. Design remains eligible for protection after acquiring source significance, and not every design necessarily qualifies for copyright or patent protection.

The 1999 unregistered-trade-dress nonfunctionality provision supplies no distinctiveness rule and no ruling about its temporal application to antecedent conduct or this earlier trial. Section 2(e)'s registration transition is not section 5's transition. The statutory design requirement and ordinary Rule 50 standard are separate; the Court neither retries the jury's historical facts nor supplies an unasserted secondary-meaning claim.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse and remand only to eliminate the inherent-distinctiveness ground and separate relief dependent on it from independently sustainable copyright and state-law relief. No new total, restored broad injunction or double recovery is ordered.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992:** Its inherent-distinctiveness route for qualifying nonfunctional restaurant presentation remains effective. Its express reservation of other product-design categories permits the distinct acquired-source requirement now adopted; the accepted restaurant findings are not retried.
- **Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co., March 28, 1995:** Its separation of eligible subject matter from acquired source significance is applied to product design. Its nonfunctional-color rule remains intact and supplies no blanket inherent-distinctiveness route for every aesthetic feature.
- **Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844 (1982):** Its definition of secondary meaning supplies the source-versus-product inquiry, while its essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality functionality boundary remains independent. Its distinct contributory-infringement holding is unchanged.
- **Weisgram v. Marley Co., February 22, 2000:** Its protection of an actual fair opportunity and genuinely supported new-trial grounds is preserved. It supplies neither an unasserted acquired-meaning theory nor an automatic right to reopen proof in this case.

#### Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1577.
**Decided:** March 28, 1995.

##### Color may identify source under the Lanham Act

**Holding:** A color is eligible for trademark protection when it has acquired source-identifying significance and is nonfunctional, subject to the ordinary Lanham Act requirements, including likelihood of confusion where required. Color alone is not categorically excluded, but neither bare color without secondary meaning nor ownership of color in the abstract is protected.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: applies the separation of qualifying source identification, nonfunctionality, and confusion; its inherent-distinctiveness rule for restaurant trade dress is not extended to bare color.
- Brown v. Gardner, December 12, 1994: applies fidelity to statutory terms without an invented category exclusion; it supplies no separate trademark proof rule.

##### Functionality preserves useful and competitively necessary color features

**Holding:** Acquired source significance does not make a functional color protectable: a feature essential to the product's use or purpose or affecting its cost or quality remains outside trademark protection, and protection cannot foreclose color features shown to be competitively necessary. The record's absence of a competitive need for this particular green-gold shade defeats a categorical functionality objection to its eligibility; no general rule about every aesthetic feature is decided.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc., 456 U.S. 844, 850 n.10: applies the essential-use-or-purpose and cost-or-quality functionality boundary to the asserted color feature; functionality remains independent of source significance.
- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: preserves the separate nonfunctionality requirement; neither its holding nor this decision confers rights in useful designs or generic subject matter.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The categorical color-registration bar and cancellation direction are reversed for ordinary further proceedings. The independently sustained §43(a) judgment, damages and injunction remain undisturbed; no new award or double recovery is authorized.

## Event

On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, which affirmed dismissal of the constitutional challenge. ## Holdings

### I. A finite extension of existing copyrights may rationally serve the copyright system

**Holding and operative rule:** The Copyright Clause permits a finite extension of subsisting copyrights when the extension bears a rational relationship to the promotion of progress through the copyright system; limited times requires bounded duration, not an irrevocable initial expiration date. The twenty-year extension challenged here satisfies that standard through reasonably supported systemwide coordination, dissemination, and preservation purposes, without requiring proof that the additional years newly induced creation of each already completed work.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg. These six adopt the complete standard and its application; the three dissenting grounds are not aggregated into a competing controlling rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that finite duration is necessary, while the promotion-of-progress requirement remains a real constitutional limit. A copyright system operates across works, time, and national markets. Congress may reasonably adjust the terms of existing rights as part of international coordination and future dissemination or preservation without claiming to induce completed creation retrospectively. Historical extensions support the conclusion that an initially stated endpoint is not immutable; repetition alone does not establish constitutional power. Feist’s originality boundary concerns what may receive protection and does not fix an unalterable duration. The petitioners correctly identify delayed public access and reject an incentive to create what already exists. Those points do not eliminate the distinct, forward-looking system purposes of a finite adjustment. The present extension rationally serves those purposes; constitutional review does not require the optimal policy or a new work-for-work exchange. Private reward alone would not establish progress, and the decision does not authorize indefinite evasion of limited times. The existing-term application is sustained. The unchallenged new-work term and restoration of public-domain material are outside this case.

### II-A. Copyright safeguards do not create categorical First Amendment immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Fair use and the distinction between ideas and expression do not categorically immunize copyright-duration legislation from the First Amendment. A challenger must identify the actual expressive burden of the attacked provision, and a court must assess the continuing safeguards in relation to that burden; the petitioners’ delayed reproduction and dissemination of protected expression identify a real burden requiring that inquiry.

**Authority:** Part II-A of the opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Stone-Zsela. These seven directly support this severable proposition; Stone-Zsela’s join does not reach Part II-B’s constitutional conclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** Harper & Row explains the legal work of copyright’s accommodation with expression. Campbell preserves actual fair use through its integrated statutory inquiry, not an owner’s veto over qualifying criticism. Neither makes the copyright label conclusive. Corley separately assessed a demonstrated access-circumvention function and a tailored posting-and-purposeful-linking injunction; it did not remove constitutional review or approve perpetual control of public-domain material. The present burden concerns twenty additional years of exclusivity over expression petitioners wish to reproduce, preserve, or disseminate. Availability of facts, ideas, and some fair uses does not equal the unrestricted uses allowed after expiration. The Court therefore examines that burden rather than treating safeguards as a reason to dispense with inquiry. The different conclusions about the present extension appear in Part II-B and the separate opinions.

### II-B. The actual duration change does not violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** The challenged finite extension does not violate the First Amendment because it leaves facts and ideas unprotected, preserves qualifying fair use of expression, and neither compels expression nor imposes a separately content-selected restriction. In this ordinary duration setting those operative safeguards suffice after examination of the actual burden, and the First Amendment does not require additional heightened review of the legislative term policy.

**Authority:** Part II-B of the opinion of the Court, joined by O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, and Breyer withhold assent; their Copyright Clause grounds do not supply affirmative votes for a separate speech-invalidity rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court recognizes that petitioners’ desired uses are expressive and that fair use will not authorize every reproduction they could make in the public domain. Section 102(b) nevertheless leaves facts and ideas available, and §107 retains a genuine defense for qualifying use of protected expression. Campbell’s four factors remain an integrated inquiry, with the defense burden unchanged and criticism injury distinguished from cognizable market substitution. The term adjustment removes neither safeguard and demands no endorsement or carriage of another’s message. Those features answer the presented burden without a further heightened review of the length Congress selected. Corley’s content-neutral scrutiny addressed the executable circumvention function and its separate injunction; that different restraint does not mechanically prescribe a universal duration test. Quality King concerns exhaustion of distribution rights in particular domestic copies, not reproduction, and Tasini creates no blanket archival privilege. These limits matter to what remains available, but neither reduces the present extension to a categorical immunity theory. The Court rejects the actual speech challenge on this examined statutory setting.

## Precedent Treatment

- Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co., 499 U.S. 340: its constitutional originality boundary is preserved and distinguished from duration; it does not fix the initial expiration date.
- Harper & Row, Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539: applied for the relationship between copyright, expression, and fair use; it supplies no categorical immunity from review of a duration burden.
- Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., March 7, 1994: its integrated four-factor analysis, defense burden, and distinction between criticism injury and cognizable substitution remain intact. It supplies an operative safeguard, not unrestricted public-domain use.
- Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Corley, April 23, 2002: distinguished as review of a demonstrated access-circumvention function and a tailored injunction. Its requirement to examine the particular speech burden is preserved; its function-specific scrutiny is not transposed into a universal duration test.
- Quality King Distributors, Inc. v. L’anza Research International, Inc., March 9, 1998: its domestic-copy distribution limit is preserved without creating a reproduction entitlement or deciding term-setting power.
- New York Times Co. v. Tasini, June 25, 2001: its right-specific collective-work rule remains; archival value alone creates neither a statutory reproduction privilege nor a duration rule.

## Law After Decision

Effective January 15, 2003, Congress may make a finite adjustment to existing copyright terms when the adjustment rationally serves copyright’s progress function at the system level; the initial expiration date is not constitutionally immutable. The challenged twenty years remain enforceable. The Court also rejects categorical speech immunity, while holding that this duration change’s actual burden is constitutionally accommodated by the retained safeguards without additional heightened term-policy review. Originality, finite duration, the availability of facts and ideas, and genuine fair use remain operative limits. There is no controlling retrospective-immutability rule, no general First Amendment scrutiny tier for all copyright provisions, and no adjudication of the unchallenged new-work term or restoration of public-domain material.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins Part II-A’s rejection of categorical First Amendment immunity but would invalidate the challenged existing-term increment under the Copyright Clause. An extension must genuinely and rationally serve progress; challengers must show that asserted purposes do not connect the additional exclusion to that function. He does not demand empirical proof of a new work-for-work bargain, optimal policy, or immutable original endpoints. Here completed creation cannot be induced; longer lives do not justify added postdeath protection for those works; and generalized harmonization and preservation claims do not explain the undifferentiated increment, which imposes no new dissemination or preservation obligation. Feist supplies a real constitutional boundary; Sony and Graham distinguish the exclusive right from the public end it serves. A supported systemic transition or conditioned preservation program could differ. Delayed verbatim dissemination is a real speech burden, but his Copyright Clause ground supplies complete relief without a universal independent speech tier. He preserves Campbell’s actual fair-use analysis.

He would reverse, declare the added twenty years invalid within the challenged application, and remand for severability and prospective relief against federal enforcement. Relief would presumptively cover the proved unconstitutional existing-term extension unless the defendant establishes that a narrower cure fully remedies it, within severability and the court’s authority over that defendant. The decree would use preextension expiration dates: lawful earlier terms survive, and works with unexpired earlier terms do not immediately enter the public domain. He would leave the new-work term intact and award no damages or personal judgments against absent owners. Under Harper the rule would govern the parties and cases still on direct review; Reynoldsville preserves independent immunity, procedural, and remedial limits, while reliance alone would not justify selective prospectivity. Final judgments would remain closed, and collateral relief would require its own authorization. Private infringement disputes would apply the constitutional rule through their own parties, defenses, and procedures.

**Stevens, dissenting.** He would reverse as to existing copyrights because completed creation cannot be induced by prolonging an existing federal bargain at the public’s expense. General parity, dissemination, and preservation rationales do not justify a blanket further restriction on the access originally promised. Initial establishment of the federal copyright system differs from enlarging an existing federal grant, and repeated legislation cannot itself establish constitutional power. Feist’s real constitutional boundary and Campbell’s and Quality King’s operative access limits support this objection. He would remand for relief confined to the existing-term application, without independently deciding First Amendment invalidity, a permissible new-work term, public-domain restoration, or remedies for older extensions. His existing-bargain ground is distinct from Stone-Zsela’s acceptance of genuinely justified later adjustments and Breyer’s assessment of this increment’s burdens and benefits.

**Breyer, dissenting.** He would reverse the challenged existing-term application under the Copyright Clause read in light of expressive interests. Meaningful legislative leeway remains, but a substantial additional restraint on use of already-created material must have an adequate copyright-related public justification. The reasons offered do not sufficiently connect this increment of exclusion to progress. Corley’s attention to functional benefit and actual expressive burden and Quality King’s preservation of operative limits support that assessment. His ground does not require a measured new-creation incentive for every work and does not impose a universal independent First Amendment tier. He would remand for relief confined to the existing-term challenge. This ground adopts no new economic calculation, aggregate royalty estimate, orphan-work count, or empirical preservation finding.

## Procedure After Action

The judgment dismissing the constitutional challenge is affirmed. The existing-term extension remains enforceable, and the requested declaratory and injunctive relief against that application is denied. The unchallenged new-work term and the distinct treatment of works already in the public domain receive no disposition.

## Event

Moseley v. V Secret Catalogue, Inc.; No. 01-1015. October Term 2002. Argued November 12, 2002; decided March 4, 2003. The Court reviews the Sixth Circuit's affirmance of a dilution injunction against Victor's Little Secret. The question is whether the operative 1995 Federal Trademark Dilution Act requires actual impairment, what proof can establish it, and whether this summary-judgment record suffices.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the dilution summary judgment, vacates its dependent injunction and remands.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reversed and remanded, 9–0. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. The restraint based on the deficient dilution judgment must cease upon implementation of the mandate. The rejected infringement claim remains undisturbed. Further proceedings must apply the actual-lessening requirement and ordinary proof rules.

## Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Kennedy concurs separately while joining the complete opinion.

## Holdings

### Actual dilution is required, with ordinary direct or circumstantial proof

**Holding and operative rule:** The operative Federal Trademark Dilution Act requires actual lessening of a famous mark's capacity to identify and distinguish goods or services, proved by a preponderance; a claimant seeking summary judgment must establish that injury without a genuine material dispute. Reliable direct or circumstantial evidence may suffice without lost sales, a mandatory survey or identical marks, but fame, mental association and offense directed at the junior seller do not themselves establish the required impairment.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's unanimous opinion; all nine Justices join the rule, record application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted cause and definition require actual statutory injury. The Act's causation language and definition of dilution describe lessening of identifying capacity, while its injunction provision supplies a remedy rather than a different likelihood-based threshold. Qualitex explains the source-identification function of trademark law but does not decide the separate injury Congress required for dilution. Ordinary infringement's confusion inquiry cannot replace that element.

Actual lessening is distinct from later financial consequences. A claimant need not wait for lost sales or prove destruction of the mark, and circumstantial evidence may demonstrate the impairment without a survey in every case. Nonidentical marks may dilute; identity alone does not create automatic liability. This preserves timely protection without rewriting actual causation as mere likelihood.

Here the officer associated the junior name with Victoria's Secret but continued to recognize the famous mark, and his offense concerned the junior store. The expert's general brand discussion supplied no demonstrated erosion of identifying capacity. Those proofs therefore lack the connection between the junior use and the statutory lessening required for summary judgment. The Court need not decide a complete taxonomy of tarnishment to resolve that evidentiary failure. On remand, evidence must establish actual impairment at the applicable procedural stage; any later injunction must identify and prevent the proved diluting use rather than rest on fame alone.

## Precedent Treatment

- Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co. (March 28, 1995) supplies source identification within statutory boundaries but does not establish dilution or replace its distinct injury element.
- Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc., 529 U.S. 205, and TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc., 532 U.S. 23, retain their distinct acquired-significance and functionality requirements. Their protection of source-related interests supplies no exemption from proof under this separate cause of action.

## Law After Decision

The 1995 Act requires actual dilution rather than likelihood alone, while allowing ordinary direct and circumstantial proof before distinct economic loss. Association is evidence of recognition, not automatically impairment. The current record cannot support the dilution judgment or its injunction.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy joins the opinion and emphasizes that identifying capacity has present and potential value. Reliable evidence of probable consequences may establish actual weakening and support prompt equitable protection before lost revenues or other distinct downstream harm. That practical account retains the actual-impairment requirement rather than making association or an unproved likelihood sufficient.

## Procedure After Action

The dilution restraint must be removed and the case proceeds under the actual-lessening standard. V Secret must produce sufficient proof at the applicable stage; any justified later restraint must fit the proved dilution. The infringement rejection remains intact.
