## Standards and Tests selection: Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments

### Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments

**Governing question:** Sufficiency and implementation of a welfare plan’s reservation of amendment power to a corporation.

**Current rule:** A written reservation of amendment to the Company can satisfy ERISA §402(b)(3)’s procedure and authorized-person requirements because §3(9) includes corporations and applicable corporate law identifies who can bind them. A list of named officers is not invariably required. Any additional procedure the plan actually adopts remains binding. Sufficiency of the reservation does not prove that an authorized actor adopted an amendment: actual express or implied authority, approval and any legally effective ratification, with its timing and consequences, require separate determination.

**Authority by component:**

- The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3): The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3), 1995-03-06. Souter’s unanimous opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; all nine adopt the statutory construction.
- A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action: A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action, 1995-03-06. Souter’s unanimous opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer; all nine adopt this required remand and its limits.

**Present operation effective:** 1995-03-06; the component authorities identified above.

**Exceptions and limits:** A summary plan description is not proof of authorized amendment. Sponsor amendment power remains distinct from fiduciary duties, written-instrument and disclosure requirements. No universal procedural-violation remedy, vesting rule or amendment immunity follows.

**Holdings navigation:** The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3); A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action.

## Standards and Tests selection: ERISA sponsor design, fiduciary implementation and genuine benefit payments

### ERISA sponsor design, fiduciary implementation and genuine benefit payments

**Governing question:** Whether plan amendment or release-conditioned retirement benefits establish a prohibited transaction.

**Current rule:** An employer designing and adopting pension-plan amendments acts as sponsor, not fiduciary. Actual implementation is a separate functional inquiry; party-in-interest status alone does not satisfy §406(a)(1)’s requirement that a fiduciary cause a transaction knowing or having reason to know that it is prohibited, subject to §408’s statutory exceptions. The asset-disposition branch does not acquire an extra discretion requirement. Payment of genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan is not prohibited by §406(a)(1)(D) merely because required employment-related releases benefit the employer. This assumes fiduciary implementation for that classification and creates neither a §408 exemption nor a general net-benefit defense.

Employee contributions do not turn amendment design into fiduciary conduct or make participants co-sponsors. Genuine benefit payments within a continuing single plan do not inure to the employer under §403(c)(1), or become prohibited §406(a)(1)(D) transfers, merely because they reduce employer contributions or payroll costs. Surplus remains plan property. Section 406(b)(2)’s adverse-party prohibition is not triggered merely by sponsor adoption of an advantageous benefit design; independent fiduciary implementation and prohibited transactions retain their actual elements.

**Authority by component:**

- Spink — Part I, sponsor capacity and separate implementation, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg for all nine.
- Spink — Part II, genuine release-conditioned benefit payments, 1996-06-10; Ginsburg, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas (seven); Souter and Breyer reserve the question.

- Contributory-plan sponsor capacity, anti-inurement and genuine-payment boundaries: Canonical Decision Record `hughes_aircraft_co_v_jacobson_merits_1999-01-25.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / I. Genuine payments within the continuing plan do not inure to the employer; II. Amendment design is distinct from fiduciary implementation; V. Incidental employer advantage does not make these benefit payments prohibited transactions, 1999-01-25; nine direct joins to sponsor capacity, eight to the independently stated anti-inurement and transaction grounds.

**Present operation effective:** Hughes Aircraft, 1999-01-25, for the contributory-plan, anti-inurement and §406(b)(2) components; Spink’s existing components remain effective from 1996-06-10.

**Exceptions and limits:** Amendment procedures, participation, funding, vesting, loyalty, anti-inurement and independently applicable §406(b) duties survive. No sham transfer, kickback, unlawful plan or unenforceable release is immunized. Particular release validity under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law remains open. Surplus does not become employer property.

**Related current rules:** “Corporate procedures for ERISA plan amendments” governs actual authorization and amendment procedure; “Discharge of pension funding obligations as an exchange” governs the distinct property-for-release transaction.

**Holdings navigation:** Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882. [Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson — I. Genuine payments within the continuing plan do not inure to the employer](HOLDINGS.md#i-genuine-payments-within-the-continuing-plan-do-not-inure-to-the-employer-1); [Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson — II. Amendment design is distinct from fiduciary implementation](HOLDINGS.md#ii-amendment-design-is-distinct-from-fiduciary-implementation); [Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson — V. Incidental employer advantage does not make these benefit payments prohibited transactions](HOLDINGS.md#v-incidental-employer-advantage-does-not-make-these-benefit-payments-prohibited-transactions).

#### Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1935.
**Decided:** March 6, 1995.

##### The company-amendment clause satisfies §402(b)(3)

**Holding:** In this employer-sponsored welfare plan, a written reservation of amendment to the Company provides an amendment procedure and identifies an authorized person under ERISA §402(b)(3), because §3(9) includes corporations and applicable corporate law identifies actors empowered to bind the Company. The statute does not invariably require a list of individual officers; actual additional procedures adopted by the plan remain binding.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, 503 U.S. 318 — ordinary legal meaning informs ERISA where the enacted text supplies no contrary rule; no employee-classification test is imported into amendment authority.
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank (December 13, 1993) — status and function remain separate from breach and relief; procedural sufficiency establishes no final amendment or immunity.

##### A sufficient procedure still requires authorized corporate action

**Holding:** A legally sufficient reservation of amendment power does not establish adoption of the challenged amendment. The lower courts must determine who had express or implied authority, whether an authorized actor approved the 1983 cutoff, and, if necessary, whether later ratification was legally effective, including its timing and consequences, before entering a conforming benefits judgment.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Souter), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 508 U.S. 248 — the reviewed §502(a)(3) compensation limit remains; it does not bar benefits due under §502(a)(1)(B).
- John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Harris Trust & Savings Bank — the distinction between legal status and ultimate liability is preserved; no new breach or remedy follows merely from identifying amendment power.

**Limits and questions not reached:** ERISA §402(b)(3), 29 U.S.C. §1102(b)(3), requires every plan to provide a procedure for amending it and for identifying the persons with amendment authority. Section 3(9), §1002(9), includes a corporation among persons, as well as an individual, partnership, joint venture, mutual company, joint-stock company, trust, estate, unincorporated organization, association, and employee organization. The plan Constitution reserves to the Company the right at any time and from time to time to modify or amend any or all provisions, in whole or in part, and separately reserves termination. Delaware §141(a) places corporate business and affairs under the board’s management or direction except as otherwise provided by that chapter or the certificate of incorporation. No charter, bylaw, delegation, or approval is established as a fact here. Section 402(a)(1)’s written-instrument and named-fiduciary requirements remain distinct from sponsor amendment authority. Sections 1022 and 1024 retain actual summary, disclosure, and instrument-access duties; an SPD is not proof of authorized amendment. Section 502(a)(1)(B) benefits enforcement is distinct from §502(a)(3)’s appropriate-equitable-relief category.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Third Circuit is reversed and the case remanded to identify authorized corporate actors, determine actual approval and legally effective ratification, including timing and consequences, and enter a conforming benefits judgment. Actual termination and amounts are not decided; rejected lifetime vesting is not automatically reopened.

#### Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882

**Docket or dockets:** 95-809
**Decided:** June 10, 1996

##### Does sponsor adoption of pension amendments create fiduciary prohibited-transaction liability?

**Holding:** Designing and adopting these pension-plan amendments is sponsor conduct, not ERISA fiduciary conduct. Subject to §408's statutory exceptions, §406(a)(1) requires a fiduciary to cause a transaction the fiduciary knows or should know is prohibited; employer party-in-interest status alone is insufficient, and actual implementation must be assessed separately under §3(21)(A)'s functional terms, including asset-disposition control without an additional discretion requirement.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), Part I, unanimous; Part IV-A's independent-claims directions likewise unanimous.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Amendment power establishes neither compliance with amendment procedures nor lawful administration. Surplus remains plan property. The sponsor-capacity rule forecloses §404 liability resting solely on design or adoption, while actually preserved fiduciary-implementation and §403(c)(1) anti-inurement grounds retain their own predicates and remedies.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The reviewed adverse judgment is reversed and remanded, 9–0 overall. The theory resting solely on amendment is dismissed; preserved independent Count III alternatives receive ordinary consideration without a new allegation, automatic liability or unconditional dismissal of Count III. Count V, refusal of offensive collateral estoppel and withdrawn Count IV remain outside the relief. Appellate fees are vacated for reconsideration because their stated grounds included the rejected substantive rulings; the fee rationale has eight Associate joins, with Stone-Zsela agreeing in that disposition only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Curtiss-Wright's sponsor-amendment role is applied to a pension plan while its procedural requirements remain distinct. Varity separates sponsorship from administration and preserves its qualified equitable-remedy rule. John Hancock keeps asset status, function, breach and remedy separate. Mertens and Peacock retain the distinct predicate and remedy requirements for any preserved claim.

##### Does employer benefit from a required release alone make enhanced retirement payments prohibited transfers?

**Holding:** Genuine enhanced retirement benefits under an otherwise lawful plan are not prohibited transactions under §406(a)(1)(D) merely because employment-related releases benefit the employer. The rule assumes fiduciary implementation for this inquiry and does not immunize sham transfers, kickbacks, an independently unlawful plan or an unenforceable release.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; seven. Souter and Breyer would reserve the question, not decide the payments unlawful.

**Material application:** The accepted complaint allegations describe participant benefits rather than diversion solely because releases have employer value. No sham or kickback is alleged in the decided issue.

**Limits and questions not reached:** No §408 exemption or general net-benefit defense is created. Independent participation, funding, vesting, loyalty and anti-inurement duties survive. Section 406(b)'s distinct prohibitions and particular waiver enforceability under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act or other law are not decided; no loss, deception or hidden diversion is found.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Keystone's transfer of property to discharge an enforceable funding obligation is distinguished; valuable consideration alone does not equate that transaction with these participant benefits.

##### Does OBRA require credit for Spink's earlier lawful exclusion from pension participation?

**Holding:** OBRA §§9201–9202 do not require credit for service during Spink's earlier lawful nonparticipation. Section 9204(a)(1) applies the new accrual protections to plan years beginning on or after January 1, 1988 and employees with an hour of service in a covered year; a smaller total benefit caused by earlier uncredited service is not itself a reduced accrual rate during a covered year.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's Part III, all eight Associate Justices; Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment only. Dismissal of Counts I–II is restored, 9–0.

**Material application:** The relevant plan year began December 25, 1988. The later calculation date does not turn prior lawful nonparticipation into credited service or establish a separate violation earlier in that calendar year.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Section 9204(b) separately applies §9203's participation amendments to covered plan years and service performed on or after January 1, 1988; that added service-date condition does not enter subsection (a). Under §9204(a)(2), benefits pursuant to and persons covered by employer–employee-representative collective agreements ratified before March 1, 1986 use the first plan year beginning on or after the earlier of January 1, 1990 or the later of January 1, 1988 and termination of the last qualifying agreement, disregarding extensions after February 28, 1986; no allegation invokes that transition. Section 9204(c) permits formal amendment to await the first plan year beginning on or after January 1, 1989 only if interim operations comply and amendment covers that interval retroactively. Operation under that provision alone does not make benefits or contributions indeterminable or the plan inconsistent with its terms, and does not delay substantive compliance. No general prohibition on using past facts or new default temporal rule is adopted.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Landgraf and Rivers require application of enacted temporal direction first; that inquiry resolves the claim without a new default presumption.

#### Rush Prudential HMO, Inc. v. Moran, 536 U.S. 355

**Docket or dockets:** No. 00-1021.
**Decided:** 2002-06-20.

For the June 20, 2002 holding in No. 00-1021 on enforcement of the saved medical determination through ERISA benefits relief, see Federal Courts: Immunities and Remedies.

##### Independent medical review regulates this insured coverage

**Holding:** Illinois HMO Act §4-10 is saved from ERISA preemption as applied to this risk-bearing HMO: when the primary care physician and HMO disagree about the necessity of a covered service the physician proposes, it requires timely independent physician review and provision of the service if found medically necessary. The rule supplies an enforceable insured-coverage condition; the three insurance guideposts are not indispensable conjunctive requirements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Ginsburg's signed opinion of the Court, adopted in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls with six votes.

**Limits and questions not reached:** This holding concerns an HMO that bears its members' medical-cost risk and the insured coverage before the Court. It leaves the deemer clause's protection of self-funded plans intact and does not decide whether independent review separately transfers risk.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts: applied to substantive insurance-contract regulation. UNUM Life Insurance Co. of America v. Ward: extended from notice-prejudice to this limited medical-necessity term, preserving its nonconjunctive guideposts and separate conflict inquiry.

## Event

Kentucky Association of Health Plans, Inc. v. Miller, No. 00-1471; 538 U.S. 329. Argued January 14, 2003; decided April 2, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit. The question is whether Kentucky's two any-willing-provider requirements are laws regulating insurance saved from ERISA preemption.

The Sixth Circuit affirmed the judgment rejecting the health plans' preemption challenge. Section 304.17A-270 concerns qualifying providers in the plan's geographic coverage area willing to accept the insurer's participation terms and conditions; §304.17A-171(2) concerns qualifying licensed chiropractors when the plan includes chiropractic benefits. The plans challenge the laws' effects on selective contracting and costs, their coverage of nonexempt self-insurers and multiple-employer welfare arrangements, and their coverage of HMOs supplying only administrative services to self-insured plans.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the Sixth Circuit's rejection of the preemption challenge and adopts the two-condition insurance-saving rule stated below.

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

All nine support rejection of the categorical preemption challenge to both statutory commands and both covered-entity objections. Kentucky may enforce the distinct requirements under their actual terms and applicable federal exemptions. Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment on the nonexempt self-insurer/MEWA definition and administrative-only-HMO components without adding a ground; his broader insurance rationale and limited joinder are stated below.

## Holdings

### I. The insurance saving clause requires two conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** A state law regulates insurance within ERISA §514(b)(2)(A) when it is specifically directed toward entities engaged in insurance in their insurance activity and substantially affects the risk-pooling arrangement between insurer and insured. Both conditions are necessary; a material change in promised coverage, conditions of access or the participating-provider network can satisfy the second without a separately demonstrated new actuarial transfer of risk.

**Authority:** Parts I–V of the opinion of the Court are directly joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment on his different stated insurance rationale and joins Part VI only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court adopts these two conditions to identify what distinguishes insurance regulation from general rules that merely affect an insurer's ordinary expenses. UNUM and Rush Prudential establish that substantive coverage conditions matter to the insurance bargain even without proof of an independent transfer of risk. The former common-sense inquiry and nonconjunctive McCarran-Ferguson guideposts do not give the inquiry the same definite operative structure. The new conditions preserve those coverage holdings while replacing that method. A law directed at insurers' network arrangements remains insurance-directed although providers also experience consequences and cannot make contracts insurers are forbidden to make. An exclusive network and one admitting qualifying willing providers offer different access under pooled coverage. The plans' cost-control argument identifies that regulated feature of the bargain rather than an exemption from regulation. Royal Drug and Pireno classified practices under a different statute; their factors no longer supply a separate ERISA checklist. Contrary to the concurrence's preference for the existing guideposts, the two conditions make the necessary industry connection and substantial contractual effect explicit while preserving the practical coverage principles on which those earlier results depend.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM and Rush retain their substantive coverage and compatible-enforcement holdings, while their guidepost method is replaced. Metropolitan Life's substantive insurance-contract principle is applied. Royal Drug and Pireno retain their force under their own statutory inquiry rather than supplying a further ERISA test.

### II. Section 304.17A-270's provider-participation command

**Holding and operative rule:** Kentucky's requirement that a health insurer refrain from discriminating against a provider within the health benefit plan's geographic coverage area who is willing to meet the insurer's participation terms and conditions is saved insurance regulation. Section 304.17A-270 includes the Kentucky state Medicaid program and Medicaid partnerships within its stated command and retains those actual participation and geographic conditions.

**Authority:** Part II of the same eight-Justice Court opinion applies both conditions in Holding I. Stone-Zsela supports affirmance for these insured arrangements under his separate existing-framework rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The provision directs the network-access obligation to health insurers and changes the provider access that they may promise as part of pooled coverage. It therefore regulates an insurance function and substantially affects the insured bargain. The insurer retains the offered participation terms and conditions, but may not exclude a qualifying provider within the stated area contrary to this command. The law's effects on providers and selective contracting follow from the insurance-network obligation; they do not turn it into general professional regulation. UNUM's recognition of coverage conditions and Metropolitan Life's treatment of substantive insurance terms support this characterization. The plan's economic preference for a closed network does not remove the State's saved regulatory authority.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM and Metropolitan Life are applied to the substantive access condition, using the two-condition rule announced in Holding I.

### III. Section 304.17A-171(2)'s chiropractic command

**Holding and operative rule:** When a health benefit plan includes chiropractic benefits, §304.17A-171(2)'s requirement that it permit a licensed chiropractor accepting the plan's terms, conditions, reimbursement rates and quality standards to serve as a participating primary chiropractic provider for covered persons is saved insurance regulation. The command is triggered by existing chiropractic coverage and operates on the plan's listed conditions.

**Authority:** Part III of the same eight-Justice Court opinion applies both conditions in Holding I. Stone-Zsela supports affirmance for these insured arrangements under his separate rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** This provision determines how an existing covered benefit is available through the insurance network. The condition directly addresses the coverage arrangement and materially changes the provider choices offered under it. Its licensed-provider, benefit-coverage, terms, reimbursement and quality conditions define its reach. The geographic condition of §304.17A-270 belongs to that different provision. The chiropractic command does not require a plan to add chiropractic benefits or accept a provider's demanded reimbursement rate. Rush Prudential recognizes that a condition governing access to covered treatment can regulate the insurance bargain; the present application follows that coverage principle under Holding I's two necessary conditions.

**Precedent treatment:** Rush Prudential's substantive coverage principle is applied, preserving the difference between a covered-benefit condition and a new benefit or remedy.

### IV. The specified self-insurer and welfare-arrangement coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 304.17A-005(23)'s inclusion, for §304.17A-270, of self-insurers and multiple-employer welfare arrangements not exempt from state regulation under ERISA does not defeat insurance direction. Such arrangements can conduct the relevant pooling activity; the statutory exclusion for federally exempt arrangements remains effective under the federal law governing the particular arrangement.

**Authority:** Part IV of the same eight-Justice Court opinion. Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment rejecting this categorical covered-entity objection and adds no ground on that component.

**Controlling explanation:** The saving inquiry concerns the regulated insurance activity, not whether the actor is a separately incorporated insurance company. An arrangement that pools the pertinent risk can engage in insurance for this purpose despite being called self-insured. The actual definition preserves the exclusion of arrangements exempt under ERISA, so its inclusion of other self-insurers and welfare arrangements does not establish forbidden regulation of every self-funded employee plan. Applicable federal exemptions and provisions governing the actual arrangement determine the permissible regulatory reach. The plans' proposed classification would erase the enacted difference between exempt and nonexempt arrangements rather than apply it.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM's insured-coverage analysis and retained deemer boundary remain consistent with the actual federal-exemption distinction; this holding resolves the presented definition objection under the new insurance-activity rule.

### V. Administrative-only HMOs and their separate plan customers

**Holding and operative rule:** The statutes retain their saved insurance character when applied to HMOs performing provider-network administration for self-insured plans, even if the HMO does not itself underwrite medical-cost risk. Regulating that separate actor's insurance activity does not itself deem its exempt plan customer an insurer; the plan's actual deemer protection continues to govern prohibited direct regulation.

**Authority:** Part V of the same eight-Justice Court opinion. Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment rejecting this categorical administrative-only-HMO objection and adds no ground on that component.

**Controlling explanation:** Administration of the pooled coverage arrangement and its provider network is the relevant insurance activity. An administrator's lack of its own underwriting obligation does not convert the statutes into general business regulation or remove their substantial effect on the coverage bargain. Nor does a separate administrator acquire every exemption available to a customer merely because it performs services for that customer. The statutes' network commands and the deemer clause address different legal subjects, and the latter must be enforced where its actual prerequisites are met. Rush Prudential's earlier holding concerned insured coverage furnished by a risk-bearing HMO. It supplies the substantive coverage principle but did not decide this administrative-only objection. The Court resolves that presented objection here through the two-condition rule and the distinction between the separate administrator and the protected plan.

**Precedent treatment:** Rush Prudential is applied for its coverage principle at its actual prior scope; this decision adds the administrative-only-HMO application rather than treating it as already resolved there.

### VI. Saving, the deemer clause and federal enforcement remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** Saving a substantive insurance requirement under §514(b)(2)(A) remains subject to §514(b)(2)(B)'s protection against deeming an exempt employee benefit plan an insurer or insurance business for state regulation. It also leaves ERISA's independent enforcement limits intact: benefits enforcement must use the applicable federal remedial channel, and a competing state enforcement scheme remains subject to its own preemption analysis.

**Authority:** Part VI of the opinion of the Court is directly joined by all nine Justices, including Stone-Zsela.

**Controlling explanation:** The general preemption clause, insurance saving clause and deemer clause perform separate tasks. Identifying a saved coverage condition does not resolve every plan's federal status or authorize a different remedial system. UNUM preserved the federal benefits action for its saved notice-prejudice condition. Rush Prudential likewise preserved enforcement through §502(a)(1)(B) for the covered medical-necessity determination, rather than an independent state damages action. Pilot Life addresses the separate conflict created by a competing state enforcement scheme. Those boundaries continue to govern Kentucky's enforcement of the provider-access commands under their actual conditions and applicable federal exemptions. For the covered insured arrangements, those boundaries do not sustain the plans' demand for an injunction on the ground that the challenged insurance requirements are categorically preempted.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM and Rush Prudential retain their compatible-federal-enforcement and deemer limits. Pilot Life retains its separate bar against an incompatible state enforcement scheme.

## Precedent Treatment

UNUM's saved notice-prejudice result and Rush Prudential's saved medical-necessity result remain governing substantive coverage decisions, with their federal-enforcement and deemer boundaries. Effective April 2, 2003, their nonconjunctive guidepost method is replaced by the two necessary conditions in Holding I. Rush's prior holding remains confined to the risk-bearing HMO and insured coverage then before the Court; the administrative-only-HMO objection is resolved in this decision. Metropolitan Life's recognition of substantive insurance-contract regulation remains. Royal Drug and Pireno no longer provide a separate checklist for ERISA saving, and Pilot Life retains its independent enforcement-preemption rule.

## Law After Decision

Courts applying ERISA's insurance saving clause must ask whether the law targets entities engaged in insurance in their insurance activity and substantially affects the insurer-insured risk-pooling arrangement. Material coverage and network-access conditions can satisfy that rule without a separate actuarial transfer. Kentucky's two commands satisfy it under their distinct provider and coverage conditions, including the presented nonexempt-arrangement and administrative-only-HMO coverage. Actual federal exemptions, the deemer clause and ERISA's separate remedial requirements continue to control enforcement.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment. He would apply UNUM's practical, nonmechanical guideposts to the insurer-directed changes in the actual coverage bargain. Network access affects promised care and the arrangement for furnishing and financing it; the plans' cost-control objection identifies the economic effect of insurance regulation rather than a reason to disregard the saving clause. Rush Prudential preserves saved coverage conditions and the federal benefits-remedy channel. He therefore supports saving the requirements within their lawful insurance scope but would retain the existing framework rather than replace it with two universal necessary conditions. He joins Part VI's deemer and independent enforcement boundaries. Particular disputed plan status must be established under applicable federal law. His agreement in judgment on the nonexempt self-insurer/MEWA definition objection and the administrative-only-HMO objection adds no separate rationale for those components.

## Procedure After Action

The Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed. Kentucky may enforce §304.17A-270 and §304.17A-171(2) under their respective conditions and applicable federal exemptions. A particular enforcement dispute must identify the regulated actor, the arrangement's federal status and the applicable remedial channel under the preserved rules.
