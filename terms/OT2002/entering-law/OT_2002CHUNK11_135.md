## Standards and Tests selection: Initiative circulator qualifications and individual identification

### Initiative circulator qualifications and individual identification

**Governing question:** Speech burdens imposed on initiative circulators by registered-voter, personal-name and individual-payee requirements.

**Current rule:** Excluding otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators solely because they are unregistered voters substantially burdens protected petition conversation; ease of registration does not remove the burden. Traceability justifications must address retained affidavits, signature review, process and targeted fraud enforcement rather than treating voter registration as a necessary speech qualification. A requirement to display the circulator’s personal name during face-to-face advocacy must be justified at that immediate expressive encounter; materially less burdensome post-circulation identification and enforcement matter to fit.

Compelled publication of each paid circulator’s identity, addresses and individual compensation in final or monthly reports requires a substantial relationship to important informational or enforcement interests. The messenger is not automatically a financing source or candidate contributor. Sponsor identity and aggregate spending disclosures retain a distinct financing function. Timing may matter independently, but disclosure during circulation does not cure an insufficient relationship between each messenger’s identity and the asserted interest.

**Authority by component:**

- Voter-registration and badge rules: Canonical Decision Record `buckley_v_american_constitutional_law_foundation_merits_1999-01-12.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / I. Registered-voter condition; II. Personal name displayed during solicitation, 1999-01-12; six direct joins to I, eight to II.
- Individual-payee reporting inquiry and timing: Canonical Decision Record `buckley_v_american_constitutional_law_foundation_merits_1999-01-12.md`, Public Projection / Holdings / III-A. Final individual-circulator reports; III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports, 1999-01-12; six direct joins.

**Present operation effective:** 1999-01-12.

**Exceptions and limits:** These holdings concern Colorado’s reviewed restrictions and their supported burdens and alternatives. Residence, age and circulation-period requirements, standalone paid-status or employer attribution, and independent monthly-report fields are not adjudicated. No categorical initiative-finance disclosure exemption or anonymity from lawful investigation follows; materially narrower reporting requires its own justification.

## Standards and Tests selection: Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

### Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

**Governing question:** Whether a ban on write-in voting violates electoral-choice or independent expressive rights.

**Current rule:** Under the existing Anderson inquiry, the court assesses the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. When the complete access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a write-in prohibition is presumptively valid and may be sustained by proportionate interests, including primary finality and prevention of evasive candidacies or party raiding. The ballot need not provide an official count and publication of votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent expressive entitlement.

**Authority by component:**
- Governing inherited framework: Burdick v. Takushi, “1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden”, June 8, 1992; White, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas (five), applying Anderson and the earlier simulated Norman rule.
- Limited-burden write-in presumption: Burdick, “2. Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid in its reasonably accessible system,” June 8, 1992; same five Justices.
- Arbitrary-message claim: Burdick, “3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages,” June 8, 1992; same controlling five-Justice opinion, with all nine agreeing in rejection of that component.

**Present operation effective:** Burdick v. Takushi, June 8, 1992, for the write-in presumption and expressive boundary; the underlying Anderson framework is applied unchanged.

**Exceptions and limits:** No absolute validation of all write-in bans follows. Materially more restrictive access systems may impose severe burdens. The rule does not replace strict scrutiny of content-based campaign restrictions on public sidewalks.

**Holdings navigation:** Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.

#### Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-930; 120 F.3d 1092.
**Decided:** 1999-01-12.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stevens's Court opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg throughout. Those six directly support Parts I, III-A and III-B. O'Connor and Breyer additionally join Part II, giving that component eight full rationale votes. Thomas concurs in every invalidity judgment on separate grounds; his judgment votes are not full joins in the Court's methodology. The rule's limits accompany each component.

##### I. Registered-voter condition

**Holding:** Colorado may not require otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators to be registered voters on this record. Excluding otherwise qualified speakers because they are unregistered substantially burdens protected petition circulation, while the retained identification and enforcement mechanisms serve traceability more closely.

##### II. Personal name displayed during solicitation

**Holding:** Colorado may not compel every circulator to display a personal name on a badge during protected face-to-face petition advocacy when the retained post-circulation identification and enforcement measures serve the asserted interests with materially less speech burden. The personal-name component is invalid; standalone paid-or-volunteer and employer-information requirements are not adjudicated.

##### III-A. Final individual-circulator reports

**Holding:** The challenged final-report requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, address and individual compensation fail the required substantial relationship to Colorado's asserted informational and enforcement interests on this record. Disclosure of initiative proponents and aggregate spending remains distinct; the decision creates no categorical exemption from initiative-finance disclosure.

##### III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports

**Holding:** Colorado's challenged monthly requirements publishing each paid circulator's identity, residential and business addresses and individual amounts paid or owed are likewise invalid on this record. The Court decides that individual-information package, not the independent validity of every other monthly-report field.

**Limits and operative remedy:** The reviewed registration, personal-name and individual-disclosure invalidations stand. Age, circulation timing, verification affidavits, fraud and signature-review mechanisms are outside the reviewed invalidations and remain governed by their own terms. This decision does not invalidate standalone paid-status or sponsor attribution, create a replacement initiative code, grant damages, destroy public records, authorize fraud or decide every future disclosure rule. Unreviewed requirements receive no automatic new constitutional endorsement.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414:** Applied to the protected political interaction and reduction of available speakers; ordinary properly fitted administration remains possible.
- **McIntyre, April 19, 1995:** Extended to the personal-name demand during live petition advocacy at the stated narrow-fit scope; candidate-finance, organizational and independent post-circulation settings retain their distinctions.
- **Riley v. National Federation of the Blind, 487 U.S. 781:** Applied to protection of paid solicitation and the burden of compelled speech during the encounter; its discussion supplies no adjudication of the reserved badge fields.
- **Anderson v. Celebrezze and Burdick, June 8, 1992:** Their burden-sensitive administrative inquiry remains; the Court applies the actual petition-speech burdens rather than treating every election regulation as equally severe.
- **Timmons, April 28, 1997:** Distinguished as a candidate-nomination setting; it supplies no automatic lesser review for these speaker restrictions.
- **Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1:** Its disclosure scrutiny and legitimate financial-information interests are applied to each report; no categorical initiative-disclosure immunity is adopted.
- **Kentucky Right to Life, September 14, 1998:** Its payer and source-identification distinctions remain; identification of the financer does not automatically justify identifying each messenger.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Registration cannot be used to exclude otherwise qualified resident-adult circulators; personal names cannot be compelled on the challenged solicitation badge; and the reviewed final and monthly individual-disclosure packages cannot be enforced. Governing law preserves distinct authority for lawful administration, verification, fraud controls, sponsor and aggregate disclosures. The independent validity and remedial treatment of unreviewed badge and monthly fields remain outside this judgment. No fractured-opinion or narrowest-ground synthesis is needed: each Court holding has at least six direct joins, and the badge holding has eight.

## Event

Cartwright v. Barnes; judgment below, 304 F.3d 1138. October Term 2002. Decided February 25, 2003, after full merits submission. The Court reviews dismissal of the challenge to Georgia's congressional petition requirement of signatures equal to five percent of the registered voters eligible to vote in the preceding election in the relevant electorate. The questions separately concern an added Article I qualification and the combined practical burden on First and Fourteenth Amendment ballot access.

## Holdings

### A genuine support petition is not an added personal qualification

**Holding and operative rule:** A genuine requirement to demonstrate electoral support regulates ballot access rather than adding a personal qualification for congressional membership, while remaining subject to independent First and Fourteenth Amendment review. Georgia's pleaded petition requirement measures support rather than age, status, prior service or another personal characteristic, so the Article I theory fails.

**Authority:** Thomas's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the rule's operation, not its ballot label, determines its classification. Thornton/Bryant prohibits States from adding personal congressional qualifications indirectly through ballot restrictions while distinguishing ordinary administration. Cook rejects ballot handicaps designed to penalize a candidate's refusal of a state-selected legislative position. This petition requires a showing of support and imposes neither kind of personal or policy penalty. Its procedural classification leaves the asserted associational burden to separate review.

### The accepted allegations do not materially distinguish the complete system sustained in Jenness

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Anderson–Burdick, courts weigh the character and magnitude of the actual burden against the State's precise interests and the need for its restrictions; severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests, while reasonable nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. Accepting the complaint's facts, this six-month, broad-signer, sheet-affidavit petition system and its alleged district-identification, surplus-signature and qualification-history burdens do not materially distinguish Jenness sufficiently to state the asserted constitutional access claim.

**Authority:** Thomas's Part II, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Breyer; five direct votes control this application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the complete system remains the relevant unit of analysis. Jenness sustained a five-percent demonstration of support within Georgia's open petition arrangements; Burdick and Timmons preserve contextual review rather than strict scrutiny of every election rule. Candidates have about six months to circulate, and voters may sign multiple petitions and participate in primaries. Verification requires an affidavit for the circulator's sheet, not notarization of every signature. Those features remain material when the pleaded district-identification difficulties and surplus needs are considered together.

The Court accepts the allegation that no Libertarian congressional candidate qualified by petition since 1943. That history is relevant, but the fact of nonqualification does not itself identify a changed legal or operational barrier in the support route. The added allegations concern identification and verification costs within the same basic open system; combined with the accepted history, they do not make this complaint materially different from the system Jenness sustained. Georgia's interests in a modicum of support and reliable verification justify this application. The dissent would require development before that conclusion; the Court finds the pleaded distinctions legally insufficient. The holding neither creates a universal five-percent safe harbor nor independently resolves the statewide one-percent rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton / Bryant v. Hill (May 22, 1995) is applied to distinguish genuine support procedures from personal congressional qualifications.
- Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510, retains its prohibition on official policy-compliance penalties; this support petition imposes a different kind of condition.
- Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431, is applied to the materially comparable complete Georgia petition system rather than treated as approval of a percentage in isolation.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428, and Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party (April 28, 1997) retain the calibrated burden, precise-interest and fit framework; this decision applies it without replacing it with rational-basis review.

## Law After Decision

Congressional support petitions remain distinct from personal qualifications and remain subject to meaningful access review. The particular allegations here do not state a materially different constitutional barrier from the open system sustained in Jenness. Materially different or severe burdens continue to receive the scrutiny Anderson–Burdick requires.

## Separate Writings

Souter, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, agrees on qualifications but would remand the access claim. Jenness's system-specific holding does not answer the cumulative allegations of district identification, affidavits, surplus needs and prolonged exclusion. The formal access opportunities favor Georgia, but factual examination should determine actual severity and justification.

Stone-Zsela also joins the qualifications holding and would remand access. He requires examination of the complete system before its burden is settled. If an unjustified common barrier is proved, he would require a workable lawful nomination route with relief tied to that barrier and the real election timetable, presuming correction for governed candidates and voters unless a narrower complete cure is demonstrated. His proposed remedy would begin with a state proposal and proceed to a fitted judicial order if necessary; it would not select a replacement percentage at the pleading stage.

## Procedure After Action

The mandate leaves the dismissal of both claims in force. The challenged congressional petition requirements remain operative.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas agrees that every reviewed restriction is invalid but applies traditional strict scrutiny to the direct speech and severe associational burdens. Registration reduces the Meyer-protected speaker pool without a narrowly fitted traceability justification. The personal-name badge directly intrudes on core political speech and fails narrow tailoring even assuming a compelling anti-fraud interest. The reports' effect on circulation is more attenuated; compelled disclosure independently burdens association and belief. Singling out paid circulators poorly fits general identification, and the need to expose each individual's identity and compensation has not been demonstrated. Monthly exposure is considered separately from final reporting. As an alternative, the challenged packages also fail Buckley's more forgiving substantial-relationship test. He does not displace Buckley's surviving disclosure holdings, adopt the Court's entire method, invalidate retained reports or decide reserved fields.

**O'Connor, joined by Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Part II's complete personal-name-badge explanation: direct intrusion into political conversation requires narrow tailoring, and the retained affidavit and enforcement tools defeat this demand on this record. They would sustain registration and the challenged final and monthly reports. Under Anderson–Burdick, readily available enrollment provides an administrable connection to the formal petition process without the severe burden found by the Court; Meyer does not make every qualification equivalent to a ban on paid circulators. Reports are indirect financial-disclosure burdens rather than badges worn during persuasion. Identifying payees and individual disbursements can permit checking campaign activity beyond aggregate sums, and contemporaneous monthly information can expose abuse before the vote. They find the relationship sufficient even under more demanding disclosure review. They acknowledge the excluded unregistered speakers and possible exposure costs, but reach different applications. They propose no automatic admission rule, universal minimum scrutiny or independent disposition of reserved fields.

#### Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-535.
**Decided:** June 8, 1992.

##### What level of constitutional scrutiny applies to a restriction on voting or candidate access

**Holding:** A court must weigh the character and magnitude of the injury to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the State's precise asserted interests and the extent to which they require the burden. Severe restrictions must be narrowly drawn to advance a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily are justified by important regulatory interests, so strict scrutiny does not govern every election rule.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices control the complete rule. Kennedy's dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone's dissent also accept the calibrated framework without making their contrary application controlling.

**Material application:** A write-in prohibition must be examined with the available routes to ballot access and its role in the selection system. Neither the prohibition alone nor the general need for election administration decides the burden.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary administration remains subject to meaningful scrutiny. The holding neither imposes strict scrutiny on all ballot regulations nor extends *Burson*'s public-sidewalk campaign-content holding to the official ballot.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, supplies the burden, precise-interest and necessity inquiry. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, preserves actual access review and permissible electorate-specific support requirements, including its protection against cross-component exclusion. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, recognizes substantial regulation of candidate selection while preserving review of severe barriers. *Burson v. Freeman*, May 26, 1992, concerns ordinary public sidewalks; neither its strict-scrutiny proposition nor its fractured judgment changes this ballot analysis.

##### Whether Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid within the ballot-access system reviewed

**Holding:** Where the complete ballot-access system imposes only reasonable burdens, a write-in prohibition is presumptively valid and may be sustained by proportionate state interests. Hawaii's party and nonpartisan routes provide adequate access, and its prohibition validly supports primary finality, prevention of sore-loser candidacies and party raiding, and its treatment of uncontested primaries.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion of the Court, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices support both the rationale and affirmance. Stone, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy disagree with this application.

**Current force:** Remains controlling for the complete access system reviewed and its reasonable burdens.

**Material application:** New parties need one percent of registered-voter signatures 150 days before the primary; qualifying established parties retain access. Candidates need 15 signatures for legislative or county offices, or 25 for statewide or federal offices, 60 days before the primary. Nonpartisan candidates advance with ten percent of the primary vote or the votes sufficient to nominate a partisan candidate, whichever is lower; voters choose one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices. These combined routes and restrictions support the limited-burden finding. Lawful selection deadlines need not accommodate every later preference.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Both primary and general-election applications within this system are reached. The Court does not independently validate every surrounding access rule or decide a prohibition within materially more restrictive access rules. Candidate qualifications and reasonable write-in procedures elsewhere remain open; no absolute write-in right or guarantee of competitive races is created.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Affirm the Ninth Circuit; its reversal of the district court's injunction remains effective. No prospective injunction or replacement election-administration scheme issues.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Jenness v. Fortson*, 403 U.S. 431, permits reasonable demonstrations of support. *Munro v. Socialist Workers Party*, 479 U.S. 189, permits primary winnowing and preventive regulation without proof of a particular past disruption. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supports primary finality and restrictions against circumvention. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, governs this limited-burden application; the decision creates no exception to its inquiry. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, retains protection against unjustified party-name and cross-component disqualification.

##### Whether the Constitution requires the State to count and publish fictional candidates or arbitrary protest messages

**Holding:** The First and Fourteenth Amendments do not require a State to count and publicize write-in votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent form of expression. Elections select public officials, and a desire to use the ballot for such messages does not create an entitlement to a write-in option.

**Proposition-level authority:** White's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas, controls. Kennedy's dissent, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and Stone's dissent expressly agree on rejection of this broader claim; all nine support the component without issuing a joint opinion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A genuine interest in supporting an actual alternative candidate requires the contextual access analysis separately stated above. Political expression outside the ballot and every restriction on eligible candidates in a different system are not decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reject the arbitrary-message tally claim; it supplies no independent ground for relief from the prohibition.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supplies the office-selecting function of elections; the ballot is not a general expressive forum. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, protects electoral participation without requiring an arbitrary-message tally.
