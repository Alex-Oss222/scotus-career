### Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC, No. 98-963

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Justice Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Justices Scalia and Thomas.**

The dissent accepts Russell's close-fit standard and the sufficiently important anticorruption interest, but would affirm invalidation of this presented statewide ceiling. The large-gift episodes support some prophylaxis without adequately justifying this amount. The severe statewide contraction in funding, contested primaries and candidates, together with campaign scale, requires a stronger operational answer than general findings of campaign capacity, indexing or examples of successful financing. The contributor-count statistic does not establish the amount of funding lost, and a ceiling's being higher than Arkansas's invalid limits does not establish its fit.

The dissent does not treat the comparative trends as conclusive proof of causation, demand an exact optimal amount or completed bribe, dismiss the corruption evidence, or adopt expenditure-level strict scrutiny for all direct contributions. Kennedy's Russell separate opinion, joined by Scalia and Thomas, preserves tailored conduit and earmark regulation and meaningful association while rejecting inadequately fitted burdens. That ground supports invalidating this limit on this record without overruling Buckley, ordering a replacement ceiling or recognizing unlimited candidate giving.

### Mariani v. United States, Third Circuit No. 99-3875

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They would sustain both corporate challenges within the presented direct business-corporation treasury-to-federal-candidate class while joining the independent threshold and false-source answers. Their common ground accepts the contribution standard and legitimate direct-candidate corruption risk but finds the government's justification insufficient for complete exclusion of this source. A fund made from other voluntary contributions is a different pool, and lawful corporate administration does not establish that it answers the chosen association's lost treasury support. Candidate-connected party financing sharpens the fit problem without proving that every transaction is identical or that all underinclusive laws fail.

National Right to Work Committee's solicitation holding, protection for economic payers, and corporate-channel rationale are substantial contrary authority. The dissent preserves those holdings but would not treat their deference as conclusive under the later actual close-fit rule and this complete-source challenge. Austin's independent-expenditure holding likewise remains undisturbed; the dissent's position is not expenditure scrutiny transplanted into contributions. No completed-bribe requirement, unlimited giving rule, new dollar ceiling, or finding of an actual failed fund is adopted. The three would reverse only the corporate constitutional answers and remand for conforming declaratory relief and ordinary determination of effects on actual charges. They would affirm the independent false-source answer and order neither acquittal nor a remedy against unchallenged party practices.

### Denver Area Educational Telecommunications Consortium, Inc. v. FCC; Alliance for Community Media v. FCC, Nos. 95-124 and 95-227

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, joined by Stone-Zsela and Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment on (c) and dissenting on (a):** Congress itself selects protected content for loss of access protection. Legislative action is reviewable even when every resulting private refusal is not governmental. Turner supplies ordinary cable protection and the content inquiry; ACT and Sable require adequate means as well as a compelling purpose. A permission can deny willing adults access altogether and does not become tailored merely because a private operator chooses whether to use it. The policy safeguards in (a) do not cure that underlying selective veto; (c) does not even contain those same limits. Neither popular offensiveness nor moral disapproval alone supplies a sufficient governmental justification, and the protection extends to unpopular expression without ideological preference.

These Justices recognize Hurley's genuine private-presentation right, but reject assuming that every independently programmed access channel carries the operator's own message. A locally dedicated channel open to private speakers may support a forum analysis under Perry and Cornelius; their opinion does not find that dedication in every franchise, declare all PEG governmental or authorize local managers to censor protected expression at will. They join the Court's (b) explanation because the combined compulsory burdens fail the required means review. They reserve §506(a) and (b), lawful obscenity prohibitions, distinct unlawful-conduct questions and concrete editorial claims. They seek no unconditional carriage order. Their strict sufficient framework for (a)/(c) has three votes.

**O'Connor, dissenting on (c):** She accepts constitutional scrutiny of Congress's permissive law, but finds its accommodation of genuine independent editorial choice and protection of children sufficient against this facial challenge. Turner and Hurley give that editorial interest substantial weight. The permission does not itself order an unwilling operator to exclude speech, and the different origins of leased and locally arranged access do not, for her, justify the different constitutional result. She recognizes that (c) lacks (a)'s written/published-policy and reasonable-belief conditions; she does not silently supply them. Their absence does not persuade her that all applications of this uncoerced permission must fail. Actual governmental institutions, an established local obligation or coercive administration may present different claims.

Her position differs from Scalia and Thomas's independent private-editor ground: private choice does not end review of Congress's content selection. It also differs from her vote on (b), where willing communication encounters the government's integral compulsory structure. An ordinary age screen or subscription request alone is not condemned, but the operator-retained lockbox accommodation leaves that structure intact. She joins the Court's (b) invalidity and the four-Justice contextual (a) rationale, without joining a universal cable forum or a categorical absence of governmental action.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment on (a) and dissenting on (b)/(c):** Their permission analysis starts with the difference between a protected speaker's ability to seek a willing outlet and a constitutional right to force an unwilling private editor to carry the message. Scalia's Sable concurrence, their Turner positions and Hurley support that distinction. Congress's partial relaxation of imposed access duties is not itself a direct governmental ban on the programmer's speech. Statutory designation alone does not constitutionalize a forum or every former access entitlement. They reserve an actually governmental institution and independently established obligations without finding that any particular franchise lacks them; they decide neither the entire access regime's constitutionality nor a taking. This is a merits ground, not a denial of Article III standing. They do not join the plurality's contextual balancing as the sufficient reason to sustain (a) or (c).

They separately apply strict scrutiny to (b), which burdens transmission by a willing operator. Mixed and unpredictable schedules make ordinary subscriber-initiated blocking a less effective default protection; segregation leaves the other basic channels available while preserving a real adult election. A written request can reduce requests made by children without authorization, and thirty days is a compliance maximum, not a compulsory waiting period. Household controls remain usable after opt-in. They find that design sufficiently fitted despite its adult-access burdens, rather than requiring government to accept a materially weaker parental-assistance mechanism. They neither require a flawless alternative nor presume an official list, universal affordability, actual disclosure or implementation of the 1996 blocking laws. Thomas distinguishes ACT's mandatory broadcast-hours exclusion from the present adult election; Scalia's practical ACT concerns do not lower the governing standard. Their strict-(b) analysis has two votes.

### Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part.** Butler, Sable and Bolger protect adult access; Ginsberg permits genuine restrictions confined to minors. The Internet presently lacks generally usable identity gates, so the display offense and mixed adult/minor conversations cannot be saved by treating the network as divided into adult and child zones. She agrees with the injunction to that extent and with preservation of independent obscenity enforcement.

She would retain the transmission provisions only where the initiating adult knows all recipients are minors, with corresponding facility liability confined to those underlying communications. She construes §223(d)(1)(A) to require knowledge of minority, relying on its wording and DeBartolo's constitutional-avoidance principle. Such a communication has no other adult audience whose access is lost. Brockett's application-severability principle and the statutory severability provision support preserving that application. Although the CDA's content categories are broader than Ginsberg's obscenity-for-minors rule, she finds no showing under Broadrick that the additional protected minor speech is substantial relative to the provisions' legitimate sweep. She does not deny minors all First Amendment protection or foreclose individual vagueness challenges. Her view relies on current technology, not predicted improvements. No other Justice joins her separate writing.

### National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley, No. 97-371

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Supplementary spending discussion by Breyer, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy.** These four Justices add that program-specific appropriations may define what government chooses to support without financing every activity. Rust and Maher illustrate distinctions between funding a defined program and prohibiting independent activity. The observation does not replace the narrower statutory construction or authorize every discriminatory arts award. Ginsburg withholds assent from this further discussion; it has no independent controlling force.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Government may choose the art it subsidizes, including a preference for decency and respect, without thereby preventing a rejected artist from creating the same work independently. This broader account treats the statutory command as real and does not depend on reducing it to a harmless consideration. Rosenberger's broadly available printing benefit and Forbes's opened candidate opportunity differ from discretionary arts patronage. The concurrence likewise rejects importing speech-related vagueness doctrine from coercive regulation into ordinary grant selection as such. It supplies no approval of penalties on independently funded speech, disguised regulation, every possible unconstitutional condition or departures from the rules of a forum actually opened.

**Souter, joined by Stone-Zsela in the First Amendment part, dissenting.** Requiring officials to consider decency and respect for public beliefs directs attention to whether private art conforms to prevailing views. That criterion disfavors offensive, irreverent and challenging expression even if it is not dispositive in every case. Treating mandatory consideration as legally inconsequential would delete Congress's command, while panel diversity does not remove it. Rosenberger's protection of independent private viewpoints and Forbes's continuing viewpoint constraints cannot be evaded merely because artistic support is selective. The program supports private expression, not a governmental message. Genuine neutral judgments concerning merit, subject, audience, educational suitability, finances and administration remain available; invalidating the proviso would not require funding every applicant. These two Justices would retain facial First Amendment relief without deciding settled individual claims, unrelated remedial questions or public financing of religious exercise.

Souter alone adds that artistic comparative judgment necessarily permits imprecision and joins the Court's narrow rejection of facial vagueness. His objection is to the viewpoint criterion, not unavoidable uncertainty in assessing art. Stone-Zsela does not join this statement and leaves vagueness unresolved.

### Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez / United States v. Velazquez, Nos. 99-603 and 99-960

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas on the common service-definition ground, dissenting.** Congress may subsidize routine enforcement of existing welfare law without assisting the recipient's validity-challenging representation. The dissent accepts that the lawyer advocates for the client, the restriction reaches non-LSC funds, and the lawyer must leave the whole case even if independent counsel could handle the prohibited issue. It nevertheless regards those terms as defining the selected recipient's welfare service, not penalizing independent speech outside it. The broad ban's symmetry, substantial permitted enforcement arguments, advice and referral, and legally separate affiliates support that classification without proving any particular substitute actually available. Rust's professional relationship shows why private professional judgment alone does not settle a program boundary. Their view creates no constitutional obligation to finance continuation when the case crosses that boundary.

Scalia and Thomas additionally rely on their Finley subsidy account and Urofsky grounds distinguishing assistance from an entitlement to public means, while preserving the limit against penalties on independent expression. O'Connor joins the particular service defense rather than a general immunity for conditions on nonfederal money. The common dissent neither denies the burden nor treats an affiliate as an established cure.

**The same dissent's conditional severability ground, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** Even if the condition is unconstitutional, the three would reject preserving the individual-benefits exception without it. Rule 24.1(a) permits review of evident plain error within jurisdiction despite the question limitation, and they regard the expanded unconditional service as sufficiently evident error to exercise that discretion. Alaska Airlines requires more than a remainder capable of operating: Congress must have been willing to enact it independently. They infer from the expressly conditioned exception, legal-assistance purpose and political-pressure concern an integrated bargain, not willingness to provide the permission without its limit. They would remove the entire individual-benefits exception with the invalid condition, leaving the broad welfare-reform restriction according to its terms. That alternative neither invalidates the whole LSC program nor declares every ordinary benefits case reform litigation. It applies only if invalidity is adopted; under their preferred merits view the condition remains enforceable and nothing is severed.

### Farmer v. Brennan, No. 92-7247

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Blackmun joins the Court because it applies the unchallenged deliberate-indifference framework while preserving workable circumstantial proof and prevention of future harm. He would overrule Wilson's general subjective requirement: objectively inhumane conditions may violate the Eighth Amendment without identifying an official with the required state of mind. That broader position does not change the rule the Court adopts here.

Justice Stevens joins the Court's application but maintains that confinement conditions imposed on a prisoner can constitute punishment without improper subjective motivation. His disagreement with Wilson does not impose a purposeful-malice requirement or prevent his join in the present actual-awareness rule.

Justice Thomas concurs only in the judgment. In his view, not every condition or unauthorized omission is punishment within the Eighth Amendment. Because the parties do not seek overruling Estelle or Wilson, he accepts a cautionary remand within their framework to determine whether Farmer's silence was treated as dispositive. The remand does not declare the officials liable or prevent renewed summary judgment after the proper inquiry. His judgment vote does not join the Court's broader constitutional or prospective-relief explanation.
