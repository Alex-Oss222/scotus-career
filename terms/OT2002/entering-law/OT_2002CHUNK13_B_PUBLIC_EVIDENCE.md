### Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, No. 90-970

Merits decision, January 27, 1992

*Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB*, 502 U.S. 527 (1992), No. 90-970. Argued November 12, 1991; decided January 27, 1992. The Court granted review of the First Circuit's judgment, 914 F.2d 313, which enforced the National Labor Relations Board's order requiring access for nonemployee union organizers. Lechmere challenges the Board's application of Jean Country, 291 N.L.R.B. 11, to its nondiscriminatory exclusion policy. The questions are the governing statutory access rule and its application to this record.

All nine Justices participated at argument and at decision.

The First Circuit judgment is reversed and enforcement of the Board's nonemployee-access order is denied, 6-3. Stone, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support reversal and denial of enforcement; White, Blackmun and Stevens would affirm enforcement.

Stone delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. White dissents, joined by Blackmun. Stevens dissents separately, adopting White's first two accommodation-and-precedent reasons but not his criticism of Babcock as wrongly decided.

For a nondiscriminatory exclusion policy, nonemployee organizers must show that employees are practically beyond reasonable offsite communication before the Board may balance a need for entry against the employer's property rights; greater convenience or organizing success does not satisfy that threshold. This workforce's community residence, public approaches and substantial direct contacts defeat the asserted exception, while discriminatory exclusion, employees' own solicitation and genuinely inaccessible workforces remain governed by their distinct rules.

Stone's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls both the statutory rule and denial of enforcement through six Justices.

The Court holds that the Board cannot replace Babcock's threshold with general balancing. Section 7 protects employee self-organization; access for outside organizers serves that right derivatively and is limited. Babcock distinguishes employees' solicitation from nonemployee entry, and Central Hardware requires the need for entry to be shown before accommodation. Hudgens's flexible language does not erase that distinction, while Sears confirms the demanding burden to establish that reasonable alternatives are unavailable. The Board's expertise remains relevant to actual access obstacles, but an established statutory construction fixes the legal inquiry. These employees live in the surrounding community. Organizers used public approaches and obtained substantial direct contacts; the small number of signed cards does not establish inability to communicate. Jean Country improperly treats alternatives as merely one factor in balancing rather than an antecedent requirement here. The Court demands neither organizational success nor exhaustion of every imaginable technique. It preserves the separate rules for discriminatory exclusion, employee solicitation and workers isolated by their living or working location.

*NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co.*, 351 U.S. 105, is applied and reaffirmed: employee and nonemployee activity differ, and demonstrated inaccessibility precedes balancing under a nondiscriminatory rule.

*Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, preserves employees' distinct organizing rights, which are not narrowed here.

*Central Hardware Co. v. NLRB*, 407 U.S. 539, supplies the requirement of a prior showing that nonemployee access is needed.

*Hudgens v. NLRB*, 424 U.S. 507, retains its accommodation language within Babcock's nonemployee threshold; it does not authorize balancing in every case.

*Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Carpenters*, 436 U.S. 180, supplies the heavy burden concerning alternative access and preserves the separate route based on discrimination.

*Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, and *Maislin Industries, U.S., Inc. v. Primary Steel, Inc.*, 497 U.S. 116, preserve general administrative deference while requiring a later agency approach to respect a governing judicial statutory construction.

*Jean Country*, 291 N.L.R.B. 11, is rejected insofar as its universal balancing replaces the prior inaccessibility inquiry for nondiscriminatory exclusion of nonemployee organizers; other contexts are not adjudicated.

Babcock's nonemployee threshold controls: demonstrated practical inaccessibility precedes balancing, and a more productive organizing location does not establish necessity. The Board may assess actual obstacles within that rule; it may not replace the rule through Jean Country. Discriminatory exclusion, employee activity, public-property advocacy and genuinely isolated workforces retain distinct treatment. No new general deference or constitutional-entry rule is adopted.

White, joined by Blackmun, would uphold the Board's accommodation and its assessment of effective alternatives. He reads Babcock, Central Hardware and Hudgens as leaving broader room to balance and argues that modern administrative deference also supports Jean Country; he criticizes Babcock's original approach. Stevens agrees only with the accommodation and subsequent-precedent reasons. He defends Babcock's employee/nonemployee distinction and declines to join the claim that it was wrongly decided. All three would affirm enforcement.

The Board's order is set aside insofar as it requires the nonemployee organizers' access on these facts and cannot be enforced on this record. The judgment permits no interference with employees' distinct organizing rights or lawful activity on public property. Supreme Court merits proceedings end. No remand for a new balancing exercise or retained supervision is ordered.

**End of entry: Lechmere, Inc. v. NLRB, merits decision, January 27, 1992.**

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, agrees with the statutory-direction discussion and the judgment but uses a different general method. A court should identify the activity the statute regulates and require a clear textual statement or unavoidable necessary implication before applying the new law to earlier activity. Section 102 regulates the discriminatory conduct by attaching compensation and punishment; the later date of judgment does not transform that liability into regulation of future judicial procedure. His Kaiser concurrence supplies this approach, while his Bowen concurrence distinguishes new consequences for past events from regulation of future activity affected by old facts. A testimony rule ordinarily regulates the taking of testimony, a jurisdictional rule the exercise of judicial power, and prospective relief future conduct. Those examples require identifying the relevant event, not assigning dispositive labels such as substantive, procedural or vested. He does not adopt an open-ended fairness exception or treat legislative history as a substitute for the necessary textual command. The conditional jury provision fails with the unavailable damages claim. The concurrence does not require overruling Bradley’s actual fee holding or deciding every fee statute’s relevant event.

Justice Blackmun would make compensatory damages available in this pending action and permit the consequent statutory jury election. He reads §402(a)’s immediate effectiveness together with the separate exceptions in §§109(c) and 402(b): Congress expressly confined the extraterritorial amendments and excluded the defined older disparate-impact cases, supporting application of compensation in other pending cases. Alternatively, Bradley’s current-law principle and his Kaiser dissent join support remedial compensation for conduct already prohibited. Bennett’s protection of substantive obligations is the strongest contrary precedent, but he distinguishes changing the prohibition from supplying relief for its violation. An employer has no vested entitlement to discriminate. He does not treat the legislative history as reliable evidence defeating his reading. Punitive damages present a harder question not squarely addressed here, on which he expresses no opinion. His proposed remand would determine the remaining compensation predicates and proper trial scope; it would not award damages, find malice, order every issue retried or erase every earlier finding.

## Bennis v. Michigan
Source: `terms/OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK4.md`.

#### Opinion Topology

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–III, joined throughout by Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Breyer. Those five directly adopt the qualified due-process rule and claimant burden, the lawful-forfeiture/takings distinction and its application to the unresolved defense, and the limited vacatur and conditional remedy.

O'Connor dissents, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg. Thomas and Ginsburg each also file a separate dissent, joined by no other Justice. All four dissenters accept the premise that an independently lawful forfeiture does not require additional compensation, but reject the defense and remand here. That agreement does not join them to Part II's application, the Court's opinion or its judgment.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg, dissenting.** Goldsmith-Grant, Van Oster and Calero-Toledo sustain forfeiture despite ignorance when an owner voluntarily makes property available and it is actually used unlawfully. This car transported the participants and provided the place of the act; its association was not remote or fortuitous. Assumed ignorance does not prove reasonable prevention, and Calero-Toledo's reservation does not require adoption of a new ground for remand.

Austin's punitive-forfeiture treatment, Buena Vista's enacted defense and Good's process protection do not already create the asserted constitutional immunity. The dissent preserves those limits, statutory authority and required process. It finds no omitted available precaution, decides no proved all-reasonable-prevention case and authorizes no forfeiture of every property loosely associated with wrongdoing. Sustaining this forfeiture defeats the separate compensation claim; it does not exempt invalid forfeitures or every governmental acquisition from constitutional scrutiny.

**Thomas, separately dissenting and joining O'Connor.** Entrusted-property precedent supports affirmance, but the absence of personal-fault requirements makes historical instrumentality limits especially important. Calling property a nuisance cannot erase them. The car's admitted transport and use permit application of Van Oster without an instrumentality concession. Thomas creates no comprehensive nexus test, adopts no earlier exclusive excessiveness formula of Scalia's and does not characterize every historical forfeiture as personal punishment. Possible remedial costs establish neither an exclusively remedial forfeiture nor the absence of constitutional cost to Tina.

**Ginsburg, separately dissenting and joining O'Connor.** Voluntary sharing and actual equitable consideration distinguish this case from unrestricted punishment of innocent third parties. The trial court considered alternatives, but qualitative cost concerns and another family automobile establish no expense calculation or substitute transportation. Ginsburg would apply the inherited entrustment rule to assumed ignorance while reserving the owner who proves all reasonably expected prevention and materially different exorbitant applications.

A hearing does not cure every substantive defect, and low value does not erase ownership rights. Equitable discretion supplies context, not a mandatory federal offset or an answer to established constitutional protection. Her conclusion rests on the existing rule, without a precise ownership fraction, vehicle concession or newly found valuation.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela agrees that the independent felony arrest permitted immediate removal from sensitive police duties and joins the Court's requirement of prompt, informed process during the continuing deprivation. He would nevertheless affirm the Third Circuit's reversal of summary judgment and remand.

Mathews must give separate weight to removing an officer from duty and stopping the officer's salary. Removal protects the public and the University while the accusation is assessed. Arrest substantially reduces the risk of error concerning the need for that removal, but it does not automatically establish a need to stop wages before a prompt opportunity to respond. A demonstrated fiscal, integrity, evidence-preservation, flight, or comparable urgency could justify immediate nonpayment together with prompt corrective process. On this record the officials have not established such a separate urgency as a matter of law.

This position would not require paid suspension in every case or a full evidentiary trial before provisional action. It would require the lower courts to examine the asserted nonpayment justification rather than treat the arrest as conclusive of both interests. After dismissal of charges, the employer must proceed on its own employment grounds and provide an effective opportunity to answer them. Stone-Zsela leaves final application, liability, damages, qualified immunity, and the validity of the later demotion unresolved.

Justice Souter joins the Court in full and emphasizes that the decision does not foreclose the Secretary's authority to issue a valid future regulation restricting compelled use. The present statute-and-letter dispute does not decide that different exercise of power.

This bounded alternative does not require a final finding that the letter conclusively has authoritative status or a universal rule about informal agency forms. The own-regulation argument remains distinct and subject to Auer's responsibility and textual-fit predicates. These grounds support agreement-specific remand without presuming the letter's full contents or any particular agreement terms.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Justice Scalia joins Parts I and III: immediate review, the supported hearing threshold and its application, the defendant's confined preponderance burden, and the conditional release consequence. He does not join Part II's Mathews rationale. His sufficient ground is the distinction between preliminary seizure and the meaningful later adversary opportunity in this continuing civil-seizure setting. His Good position accepted the sufficiency of preliminary procedures on that different record; it did not authorize a warrant to eliminate the later contest altogether. Monsanto left this hearing question open. The prolonged civil custody, particular claim of legitimate property, immediate trial-counsel need and government's conditional acceptance of a hearing support the focused opportunity here. He rejects an open-ended balancing license to redesign all criminal restraint procedures; he agrees that the hearing neither decides the indictment nor authorizes spending proved proceeds.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The Chief Justice joins Parts I and II and the order requiring a prompt hearing. Farmer's specific sworn showing meets the threshold; the time-sensitive defense interest, concrete risk of error and value of bounded testing satisfy Mathews. He agrees that Farmer must establish actual need for the identified funds by a preponderance. He dissents from Part III because, once taint is materially contested, he would require the government to justify continued restraint through the applicable probable-cause connection to forfeiture or another independently valid basis. Probable cause of an offense does not establish the taint of every asset.
