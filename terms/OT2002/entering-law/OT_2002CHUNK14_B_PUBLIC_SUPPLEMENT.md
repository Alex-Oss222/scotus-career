# Ewing-v-California_merits_2003-03-05

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Ewing-v-California_merits_2003-03-05.md`

## Event

Ewing v. California, No. 01-6978, 538 U.S. 11. Argued November 5, 2002; decided March 5, 2003. Direct review of the California Court of Appeal's April 25, 2001 judgment in No. B143745 sustaining the sentence; the California Supreme Court denied review. The question is whether twenty-five years to life for theft of three golf clubs worth approximately $1,197, with Ewing's prior robbery and three residential burglaries, is grossly disproportionate.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission or argument and at decision. No recusal or other nonparticipation is identified.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the sentence and remands for lawful resentencing.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Sentence reversed and remanded for lawful resentencing, 5–4. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The theft conviction remains intact. California must replace the twenty-five-years-to-life sentence with a lawful proportionate sentence that may substantially account for the established priors. The state remand process must provide a reasonable, enforceable resentencing schedule. A new lawful sentencing judgment establishes compliance; the invalid punishment may not be repeated under another label. The surviving conviction and any valid custody authority govern custody during implementation.

## Opinion Topology

Stevens delivers the signed Opinion of the Court.

## Holdings

### The actual sentence is grossly disproportionate despite the serious prior record

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth Amendment's narrow gross-disproportionality principle prohibits this twenty-five-years-to-life sentence for Ewing's nonviolent theft of approximately $1,197, even after substantial weight is given to his prior robbery and three residential burglaries. In applying that principle to this recidivist sentence, the present offense, the established prior record and the actual minimum before parole eligibility must be assessed together: recidivism aggravates the gravity of the offense but cannot replace it; the challenger must establish gross disproportionality rather than a preferable sentence.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court has this complete coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All five directly adopt this as-applied holding, its actual-offense and actual-severity grounds, and sentence-only remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the quarter-century minimum is constitutionally excessive on this record. Rummel permits severe recidivist punishment and gives meaningful parole availability significance. Solem preserves a proportionality limit despite prior convictions. Harmelin's narrow proportionality reasoning, adopted by only part of that Court, accords substantial legislative latitude; it does not erase the surviving limit. Henderson applies that limit to actual offense, offender and release conditions. Its first conviction, tiny completed sale and absence of ordinary parole materially differ from Ewing's case, so its outcome alone does not decide this one.

California may punish repeated serious offending more severely and use incapacitation to protect the public. Ewing's robbery and burglaries therefore carry substantial weight; he is not being sentenced as a first-time shoplifter. But the new crime remains nonviolent theft of three golf clubs, and twenty-five years is the earliest ordinary parole eligibility, not assured release. Giving the priors their full force still leaves an exceptional mismatch between the actual current offense and that minimum with a possible life term. The prior crimes do not transform this theft into a new robbery or burglary.

The dissent's incapacitation rationale explains a substantial enhancement, but does not establish a constitutionally sufficient relation for every duration. Nor does the sentencing judge's discretion to reduce the felony classification or dismiss strikes validate the sentence actually imposed. The constitutional limit requires judgment about degree; that feature does not justify abandoning the protection retained in Solem and the narrow Harmelin approach. California must impose a lawful replacement sentence that may substantially reflect the serious priors. The holding concerns this offense, offender and punishment combination.

## Precedent Treatment

- Rummel v. Estelle: applied for the legitimacy of substantial recidivist enhancement and the relevance of actual parole availability; its result does not make all enhanced terms interchangeable.
- Solem v. Helm: applied for an enforceable gross-disproportionality limit despite recidivism and attention to the actual offense and punishment.
- Harmelin v. Michigan: its narrow proportionality rationale is accurately attributed to the Justices who adopted it; its noncapital individualized-sentencing holding remains intact, and its serious drug-offense result does not settle this recidivist theft application.
- Henderson v. Norris: applied for actual-offense, actual-offender and release analysis, while distinguishing its first-offender, tiny-sale and no-ordinary-parole combination.

## Law After Decision

The narrow proportionality principle invalidates this offense, serious-prior-record and minimum-sentence combination. Recidivism receives substantial weight while the present offense and actual release minimum retain constitutional significance. California retains authority to impose a substantial lawful enhancement; the replacement term is determined through resentencing under that limit.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, dissenting, joined by O'Connor.** The narrow proportionality principle requires exceptional restraint in reviewing legislatively authorized prison terms. Ewing's current felony theft follows robbery and repeated residential burglary, permitting California to give substantial force to incapacitation after lesser sanctions failed. The actual serious recidivism and ordinary parole eligibility distinguish Henderson's first tiny-sale conviction and permanent imprisonment. Kennedy and O'Connor acknowledge the long minimum and nonviolent present theft, but find no initial inference of gross disproportionality requiring additional comparisons. They would affirm this sentence without validating every recidivist punishment or abandoning the constitutional limit.

**Scalia, dissenting.** The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause does not provide the asserted constitutional right to proportionality in prison duration. His textual and historical position in Harmelin remains his ground. Choosing among incapacitation, deterrence and retribution, and then balancing those purposes against a term of years, supplies no administrable constitutional measure on his account. The punishment may be challenged as sentencing policy, but the proposed proportionality inquiry does not establish the asserted right. His acceptance of monetary excessiveness review in Bajakajian does not supply a prison-term proportionality rule. He would affirm on this distinct ground and does not join the other dissent's application.

**Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas likewise rejects a constitutional prison-duration proportionality requirement. His objection is not limited to the difficulty of administering a test: even an administrable formulation would not require him to retain Solem under stare decisis. His conditional application in Henderson assumed the framework for that case and did not adopt its foundation or commit him to applying it in every later case. He would affirm because the asserted right does not exist on his reading, and joins neither the Court's proportionality holding nor Kennedy's application of that principle.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for resentencing under the stated remand schedule and constitutional rule. Unjustified failure to resentence is subject to enforcement through available judicial process; the theft conviction remains in force.

Helm, 463 U.S. 277; Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957. The sentence permits consideration for parole only after the actual minimum; it does not assure release then.

# Lockyer-v-Andrade_merits_2003-03-05

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Lockyer-v-Andrade_merits_2003-03-05.md`

## Event

Lockyer v. Andrade, No. 01-1127, 538 U.S. 63. Argued November 5, 2002; decided March 5, 2003. Review of the Ninth Circuit's judgment, 270 F.3d 743, granting federal habeas sentence relief after the District Court denied it. The underlying state merits affirmance occurred in 1997. The question is whether sustaining consecutive twenty-five-years-to-life terms for two low-value thefts unreasonably applied the then-established Eighth Amendment proportionality principle under §2254(d)(1).

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission or argument and at decision. No recusal or other nonparticipation is identified.

## Public Action

The Court affirms entitlement to conditional sentence relief and remands for its implementation.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Entitlement to conditional sentence relief affirmed; remanded for implementation, 5–4. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Ninth Circuit's judgment granting sentence relief is affirmed on the objective-unreasonableness and independent constitutional grounds stated here. Both theft convictions remain intact. The District Court must implement the conditional sentence-only writ prescribed in Holding III.

## Opinion Topology

Souter delivers the signed Opinion of the Court.

| Writing | Author | Complete coalition for the identified part, including author | Relationship to judgment |

## Holdings

### I. Objective statutory review is separate from the constitutional merits

**Holding and operative rule:** For a claim decided on the merits in state court, §2254(d)(1) requires a decision contrary to this Court's clearly established governing holdings or an objectively unreasonable application of them; an independently incorrect result alone is insufficient. The relevant substantive holdings are those established at the state merits adjudication, a general principle may clearly establish law without a factual twin, and entitlement to a writ additionally requires the underlying constitutional violation and appropriate relief.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Opinion of the Court has this complete nine-Justice coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The statutory framework has nine direct supporters; the later application and relief do not.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the state adjudication must be assessed as a whole. Terry Williams separates the contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses and requires objective legal judgment rather than a count of judges. Bell preserves the additional need to establish the actual constitutional violation. Early rejects a citation-based defect and a mandatory factual-twin requirement; Visciotti requires the whole supported record and actual analysis rather than isolated shorthand or nominally correct terminology.

Those current review-method decisions do not supply substantive proportionality law newly available to the state court in 1997. Rummel, Solem and Harmelin furnish the earlier substantive law used here. The state court's recidivism reasoning and the two separate offenses must receive their full weight. The Ninth Circuit's clear-error formulation is not the statutory test, but correcting that formulation does not itself decide whether the state result falls within the range the earlier law permits.

### II. Sustaining the actual fifty-year minimum unreasonably applied the established principle

**Holding and operative rule:** The state court's decision sustaining two consecutive twenty-five-years-to-life terms for these two nonviolent thefts totaling approximately $150, with the established serious prior burglaries fully considered, objectively unreasonably applied the preexisting gross-disproportionality principle. The actual fifty-year minimum must be evaluated as imposed, including the separate criminal occasions and prior record, rather than divided into terms whose combined severity escapes review.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's Opinion of the Court has this complete coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five directly support the unreasonable-application ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this is an extreme application beyond the latitude preserved by the earlier cases. Rummel sustained severe recidivist punishment with meaningful earlier parole availability. Solem enforced a proportionality limit for a recidivist property offender and distinguished ordinary parole from executive grace. Harmelin sustained punishment for a materially different serious drug offense while preserving the narrow principle through the Justices who accepted it. These distinctions leave considerable legislative choice, but they also give the general limit practical content.

The State's strongest ground is the serious burglary history, two distinct new thefts and incapacitation of a persistent offender. The Court accepts those predicates and assesses the full state rationale, including the possibility of eventual parole. They justify substantial punishment. They do not reasonably explain a fifty-year minimum for the actual two low-value thefts. Separate occasions support separate punishment, but repeating the same incapacitation premise does not make the additional twenty-five-year block automatically reasonable: the second minor theft does not, without further justification, double the already asserted long-term danger.

The dissent regards this as an area left contestable by the earlier cases. The Court instead finds their existing limit exceeded by the actual aggregate, not merely a sentence it would prefer to reduce. The conclusion depends on neither a missing citation nor an identical earlier fact pattern. It follows from the established distinctions concerning offense gravity, recidivism and real release conditions applied to this extraordinary combination.

### III. The constitutional violation requires a conditional sentence-only writ

**Holding and operative rule:** The actual fifty-year minimum for these two low-value nonviolent thefts is grossly disproportionate even with the serious priors and separate occasions fully considered. Andrade is entitled to a conditional writ requiring California, within a reasonable period fixed by the District Court, to impose a lawful proportionate sentence or end custody attributable to the invalid punishment; both theft convictions remain intact.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's Opinion of the Court has the same five-Justice coalition as Part II. These Justices independently find the underlying Eighth Amendment violation and adopt the conditional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that satisfying §2254(d)(1) is not itself a substitute for deciding constitutional entitlement. Solem's actual-offense proportionality, Rummel's recidivism and parole distinctions, and Harmelin's narrow constitutional limit govern the merits as well. On the supported record, the exceptional disparity between these two thefts and the actual half-century minimum persists after substantial weight is assigned to the serious priors. That same supported disparity establishes the violation as a separate legal conclusion.

California retains responsibility for selecting a lawful replacement sentence under its valid recidivist law and the constitutional limit. The District Court must hear the parties, fix a reasonable compliance period, and enter a conditional order specifying resentencing and proof of compliance. The State must file a lawful new judgment or establish that custody attributable to the invalid punishment has ended. If it fails to comply in time, the writ must operate against that custody; any independent valid custody basis must be identified separately. This corrects Andrade's aggregate punishment while preserving the convictions.

## Precedent Treatment

- Rummel v. Estelle: applied for legitimate recidivist punishment and the constitutional significance of actual parole availability; its earlier meaningful release conditions distinguish its result.
- Solem v. Helm: applied as preexisting enforceable proportionality law for a recidivist property sentence, with actual conduct and release conditions central to the comparison.
- Harmelin v. Michigan: its serious drug-offense result is distinguished and its narrow proportionality rationale is attributed to the Justices adopting it; neither the result nor the fracture makes the surviving principle unenforceable.
- Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams): applied for the separate statutory clauses, objective unreasonableness, general governing holdings and distinct actual constitutional entitlement.
- Bell v. Cone: applied to preserve objective statutory review and the independent merits requirement.
- Early v. Packer: applied to the actual contextual adjudication; lack of citations or a factual twin does not decide statutory eligibility.
- Woodford v. Visciotti: applied to require whole-opinion and whole-record evaluation; its reasonable prejudice assessment is distinguished from the extreme proportionality application here.
- Henderson v. Norris: its later, pre-AEDPA sentence holding does not supply substantive law established at Andrade's earlier state adjudication; its separate temporal and review premises remain effective.

## Law After Decision

Section 2254(d)(1) remains an objective restriction distinct from the underlying constitutional claim. This actual fifty-year minimum for the two low-value thefts, with the serious priors and separate occasions considered, crosses both boundaries. Courts must assess the sentence as imposed and identify the earlier substantive holding before independently establishing the statutory defect, actual violation and available remedy. Present review-method cases do not move the date of the substantive law governing the state adjudication.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Parts II–III, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The dissent accepts the objective §2254(d)(1) inquiry, the 1997 substantive-law baseline and the need to consider the actual fifty-year aggregate. It would reverse federal relief because Rummel, Solem and Harmelin leave this recidivist application within the range of reasonable state adjudication. The serious burglaries, two separate theft occasions and retained parole possibility allow substantial incapacitative punishment even where the present offenses are low-value and nonviolent. Solem's permanent-imprisonment result and Harmelin's different offense do not make the exact state application objectively indefensible.

The dissent does not demand a factual twin or treat the general principle as categorically incapable of supporting relief. It concludes that this particular application remains contestable within the law's permitted range, which is different from approving the punishment on independent direct review. Rejecting the Ninth Circuit's clear-error formulation is therefore only part of its analysis; the state result also survives the correct statutory standard on its assessment. Scalia and Thomas join this statutory ground without adopting the prison-duration proportionality foundation they reject. They apply the existing holdings in measuring the statutory constraint rather than removing those holdings from the inquiry.

The case returns through the Court of Appeals to the District Court for the conditional order and implementation specified in Holding III.

# Pierce-County-v-Guillen_merits_2003-01-14

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Pierce-County-v-Guillen_merits_2003-01-14.md`

## Event

Pierce County v. Guillen, No. 01-1229. Merits decision, January 14, 2003. Argued November 4, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Washington. After a fatal intersection accident, Guillen sought highway and accident records through a public-disclosure action and a separate negligence action. The state court’s consolidated decision invalidated the collection protection in 23 U.S.C. §409. The questions are the scope of that protection and Congress’s authority to enact it; review first requires separate finality determinations for the two judgments.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer participated at argument and decision.

## Public Action

Tort-discovery writ dismissed for want of a final judgment; public-disclosure judgment reversed and remanded, 9–0 on each component.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interlocutory tort-discovery judgment | Writ dismissed for want of a final judgment, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Review of this component is dismissed; its merits and discovery order receive no appellate mandate. |
| Public-disclosure judgment | Reversed and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | The federal-law basis for blanket production and its dependent fee determination are set aside. The state court must classify the holdings by purpose and custody, apply the remaining state disclosure law, and recalculate any fee entitlement. |

## Opinion Topology

Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer joined. All nine Justices join every holding and the component-specific disposition. No separate opinion is filed.

## Holdings

### I. Finality must be established for each judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1257 requires a final state judgment in each action brought for review; consolidation does not transfer finality between actions. The public-disclosure judgment is reviewable because it conclusively determines entitlement to the records with only collateral fee calculation or implementation remaining, while the separate interlocutory tort-discovery judgment is not reviewable.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices identified in Opinion Topology directly join this rule and both component dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn’s practical-finality principles to the proceedings that actually remain. The disclosure determination conclusively resolves the federal question and effectively determines the requested production; further collateral calculation does not reopen that determination. The negligence action still requires substantive proceedings, and its discovery ruling can be reviewed through the ordinary final-judgment route. Jefferson v. City of Tarrant and Florida v. Thomas preserve that distinction between a conclusive federal ruling within an unfinished case and a judgment qualifying for review. Neither a consolidated caption nor the importance of the common statutory question supplies missing finality. Review of the tort component is therefore dismissed without passing on its discovery merits.

### II. Section 409 protects purpose-qualified program holdings, not every copy of the information

**Holding and operative rule:** Notwithstanding contrary law, §409 protects reports, surveys, schedules, lists, and data compiled or collected to identify, evaluate, or plan safety enhancement of potential accident sites, hazardous roadway conditions, or railway-highway crossings under §§130, 144, or 152, or to develop a highway-safety construction improvement project that may use federal-aid highway funds: those holdings are not subject to discovery or admission into evidence in a federal or state court proceeding, or consideration for other purposes in a damages action arising from an occurrence at a location they mention or address. The proponent must establish the qualifying purpose and custodial holding; obtaining preexisting material for that purpose is collection, but independently compiled or collected nonprogram records held by an independent nonprogram custodian do not become protected merely because a program agency obtains a copy.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join the complete purpose, custody, covered-use, and proof requirements.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court gives the addition of collected independent effect. A program can obtain existing accident information from another agency and assemble it for the safety task; protection is not confined to documents it newly creates. But the words defining the purpose continue to limit the privilege. Baldrige v. Shapiro supplies the rule of narrow construction for an evidentiary privilege, and the enacted purpose prevents collection of a copy from suppressing every independent source. The opposing newly-created-material construction erases the amendment; the information-wide construction erases its limiting purpose. The County must identify the statutory safety purpose and the holdings collected or compiled for it. Before production, the state court must resolve disputed purpose and custody from the records and an adequate agency explanation without disclosure that would defeat an established protection. It must withhold protected holdings from the barred uses and order disclosure of nonprotected records when state law requires. Classification of the actual documents is application of the settled rule on remand, not a finding that all requested records qualify.

### III. This confined information protection implements Congress’s highway-safety power

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress may protect purpose-qualified highway-safety information under the Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses when the protection reasonably implements safety administration of interstate transportation channels and instrumentalities. Section 409, including its collection amendment as construed here, satisfies that requirement because it addresses the supported disincentive to candid hazard collection while leaving independent nonprogram records outside the privilege.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join this confined constitutional ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Lopez requires an identified interstate connection before aggregate consequences can establish power; Harris applies the direct transportation-instrumentality route. Here the relevant connection is the highway-safety program itself. Compulsory centralized disclosure can discourage candid hazard collection used to identify and correct dangerous locations. Protecting that collection reasonably serves the operation of interstate transportation channels and instrumentalities. Home Builders preserves the need for an identifiable connection and a fitted implementation of a valid interstate regime. Purpose and custody confine this measure to that function. The Court therefore rejects the objection that the protection is merely a general federal regulation of state evidence resting on remote litigation costs. The state court’s contrary constitutional ground cannot sustain blanket disclosure. The Commerce ground suffices without deciding an alternative Spending Clause ground. The separate Tenth Amendment dual-sovereignty contention was not addressed below and remains outside the question decided; the Court also does not reach the United States’ alternative objection to the private respondents’ standing to raise that federalism contention. Affirmative enumerated power does not decide either distinct question.

## Precedent Treatment

- Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 U.S. 469: applied to the completed federal disclosure determination and collateral proceedings; consolidation supplies no additional exception.
- Jefferson v. City of Tarrant and Florida v. Thomas: applied to preserve §1257 finality and dismiss review of the unfinished tort action.
- Baldrige v. Shapiro, 455 U.S. 345: applied to confine the statutory privilege to its enacted purpose while giving collection meaningful effect.
- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: applied through the direct transportation connection, preserving the prohibition on manufacturing power from remote downstream costs.
- United States v. Harris, April 27, 1995: its protection of transportation instrumentalities supports the confined highway-safety ground; the forcible-taking application does not itself determine this privilege’s scope.
- National Association of Home Builders v. Babbitt, June 8, 1998: applied for an identified interstate connection and fitted implementation; its independent externality route supplies no additional holding here.

## Law After Decision

Effective January 14, 2003, §409 follows the qualifying act of compilation or collection and the holding in the program capacity. It protects obtained material as well as newly prepared material, without converting independently maintained source copies into privileged information. The limited federal protection is a permissible means of administering highway safety; state disclosure law governs the remaining nonprotected material. The ordinary §1257 finality rule and its established practical-finality routes remain unchanged. Document classification and the resulting fee calculation are remand work under these rules. The distinct Tenth Amendment contention and alternative private-plaintiff standing issue remain undecided on the grounds stated in Holding III.

## Procedure After Action

The writ is dismissed as to the separate tort-discovery judgment. The public-disclosure case returns to the Washington courts for purpose-and-custody classification, application of §409’s covered-use protection and remaining state disclosure requirements, and recalculation of fees. The state court must protect qualifying holdings while resolving the statutory facts; program possession of a copy or a conclusory privilege label does not resolve an independently held original.

# State-v-Oakley_merits_2003-06-09

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/State-v-Oakley_merits_2003-06-09.md`

## Event

State v. Oakley; Wisconsin No. 99-3328-CR; 2001 WI 103, reconsideration 2001 WI 123. Merits decision, June 9, 2003. On review of the Wisconsin Supreme Court's July 10, 2001 judgment, as explained on reconsideration on November 23, 2001. The questions are whether probation for intentional nonsupport may condition additional parenthood on an advance judicial showing concerning support, and whether Oakley's later express plea undertaking forecloses his challenge to withdrawal from the first bargain.

Oakley had nine children and pleaded no contest to three intentional-nonsupport counts. The sentence included imprisonment and five years' probation. The condition requires an advance showing to the court concerning both support of his existing children and the additional child's needs. Wisconsin construed those requirements to call for bona fide lawful support efforts within ability, rather than sufficient wealth to meet every need, while retaining the advance judicial showing.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Holdings

### I. The separate reproductive permission requirement is not justified by this sentence's support-enforcement purpose

**Holding and operative rule:** A conviction for intentional nonsupport permits direct supervision and enforcement of lawful support duties, but does not itself justify making additional parenthood depend on advance judicial clearance. Oakley's condition, even as authoritatively narrowed to bona fide support efforts within ability, imposes an additional restraint on retained procreative liberty that lacks an adequate relationship to the asserted rehabilitative and child-support purposes on this record; the condition must be removed.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those seven Justices adopt the complete rule, explanation and removal remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Skinner identifies procreation as a fundamental liberty even in the criminal-punishment setting. Turner establishes that conviction does not extinguish every protected family choice, and Gerber rejects categorical extinction of procreative protection through confinement. Griffin recognizes probation's conditional liberty, but that principle requires attention to the particular restraint. Zablocki illustrates the constitutional problem with making a family choice depend on support-related judicial permission, although its marriage restriction did not concern probation.

Wisconsin's construction matters. Under Bearden's distinction between willful refusal and inability despite genuine effort, Oakley need not attain prosperity to comply. But the construction changes what satisfies the showing, not the requirement to obtain clearance before another child. That separate restraint reaches procreation before any new willful nonsupport occurs. The State may require work and payments, supervise compliance and punish deliberate violations through lawful procedures.

The intentional convictions, arrears, children's interests and finite probation term make the State's preventive argument substantial. They justify demanding supervision; they do not establish the fit of the additional reproductive prerequisite. The available alternatives matter to this particular restraint's justification, without requiring exhaustion of every collection method or assuming each will succeed. Available imprisonment supplies no independent answer to a restriction on a retained right.

The condition as construed fails this constitutional inquiry. The sentencing court must remove it and determine lawful sentence modification.

### II. The later express undertaking forecloses the earlier-bargain challenge

**Holding and operative rule:** A knowing and voluntary express waiver of a challenge to withdrawal from an earlier plea bargain forecloses that challenge within the undertaking's lawful terms. Oakley's later agreement establishes that specific waiver, while leaving his distinct constitutional sentencing claim reviewable; the no-contest label alone does not supply the waiver.

**Authority:** Part II of O'Connor's opinion, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Tollett and Mabry distinguish the validity of the operative plea from events in earlier bargaining. The later agreement expressly settled the withdrawal objection in return for a sentencing-recommendation limit. Its consensual adoption and particular terms establish intentional relinquishment of that dispute. The State relies on the actual undertaking, not simply the plea's form or the fact that Oakley obtained concessions. Enforcing that term gives effect to the bargain without extending it to the separate reproductive condition. The earlier-bargain challenge therefore fails, and the sentencing challenge receives its own constitutional determination.

## Precedent Treatment

- Skinner v. Oklahoma supplies the fundamental procreative interest; that protection applies to examination of this direct probation restraint.
- Turner v. Safley supplies the retained-family-right principle despite conviction; available incarceration does not alone validate the chosen condition.
- Gerber v. Hickman, March 25, 2003, forecloses categorical extinction of procreative protection through confinement; its prison-specific Turner inquiry remains within that setting.
- Bearden v. Georgia supplies the distinction between willful refusal and inability despite bona fide effort; Wisconsin's narrowing implements that distinction but leaves the reproductive showing to be justified.
- Griffin v. Wisconsin supplies probation's conditional-liberty principle, which does not itself settle the validity of this different restraint.
- Zablocki v. Redhail supplies the related family-permission and direct-support-enforcement analogy, with the difference between ordinary civil status and probation preserved.
- Tollett v. Henderson and Mabry v. Johnson supply the operative-plea distinction applied to the specific express undertaking; their rules are not enlarged into universal waiver by a no-contest label.

## Law After Decision

Support enforcement and reproductive permission are distinct constitutional questions. A court may supervise lawful support efforts and punish willful violations, but the conviction alone cannot justify an additional judicial-clearance requirement for parenthood. The ability-sensitive construction does not cure the inadequate fit established here; the reproductive condition must be removed. Actual express plea waivers remain enforceable within their knowing, voluntary and lawful scope.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's opinion and removal remedy. He would also apply a stronger rule to conditions that prohibit additional children or make parenthood depend on advance judicial approval: even when good-faith support efforts suffice for approval, the direct restraint burdens a fundamental choice and must be necessary to a compelling interest. In his view, the State may punish intentional nonsupport and enforce support duties directly, but it may not make this probationer's decision to have a child depend on prior approval of those efforts.

Skinner supplies procreation's fundamental character in a criminal-punishment setting; Turner preserves family rights despite conviction; Bearden directs attention to willful conduct rather than inability. Wisconsin's narrowing addresses inability but leaves a restraint operating on conception. Work and payment requirements, supervision, and sanctions for willful nonpayment after the required hearing fully serve the support interest, in his judgment, without making conception or lack of permission the violation. Available imprisonment does not independently justify that condition. Owens and Newman support examining real burdens and concrete alternatives within their reproductive-care settings; they do not supply the probation test. His rule concerns direct reproductive prohibitions and permission requirements, while preserving lawful nonsupport punishment and independently justified supervision.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** They join the express-waiver holding but would affirm the reproductive condition as authoritatively construed. Griffin's conditional-liberty principle permits finite, offense-related supervision of a person convicted of deliberately refusing the same support duty. Genuine lawful efforts within ability satisfy the condition; it cannot demand wealth sufficient to meet every need or clearance of arrears regardless of ability, nor may a court withhold permission after bona fide compliance. Judicial verification of that compliance, in their view, reasonably protects existing and future children and promotes rehabilitation.

They distinguish Skinner's permanent physical deprivation and Turner's unsupported marriage prohibition from this temporary verification requirement. They accept that incarceration does not extinguish every family right and rest affirmance on the condition's own offense-related fit. Their disagreement with the Court concerns whether the remaining advance showing is a justified means of supervision, not whether poverty alone disqualifies a person from parenthood.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Wisconsin courts. The sentencing court must remove the reproductive condition from the enforceable judgment and supervision instructions and determine lawful sentence modification consistent with the surviving convictions, payment obligations and independently justified supervision. The condition may not remain enforceable while that work occurs. The rejection of the earlier-bargain challenge stands.

## Holdings

### 1. The final privacy rule exercises actual delegated interpretive authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 504 of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act authorizes implementing regulations for financial institutions within the agency's jurisdiction, and the FTC exercised that authority in its final privacy rule. Courts enforce the statute's clear text and delegation boundaries before deferring to a reasonable agency choice within genuine statutory ambiguity; the First Amendment inquiry remains independent.

**Authority:** The signed Opinion of the Court, delivered by Breyer and joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, controls all eight holdings and their operative qualifications. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt every rule, explanation and component disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the FTC's rule rests on Congress's actual implementing grant. Mead requires authority to make interpretations carrying the force of law and an interpretation issued through that authority; Haggar preserves judicial enforcement of statutory limits while recognizing authorized implementing regulations. Section 504 supplies that grant for the institutions within the jurisdiction identified by section 505, and the challenged final rule exercises it. The question is therefore unlike Christensen's nonbinding advisory letter. NationsBank and Smiley support authoritative interpretation within a real delegation. The Act expressly defines publicly available information through regulations and leaves the financial-information term for implementation within the enacted financial domain. Neither the rule's official status nor agency expertise authorizes amendment of clear statutory permissions, and none supplies a constitutional answer.

### 2. The incorporated financial-activity definition covers Trans Union's credit-bureau business

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 509(3) and 16 C.F.R. § 313.3(k) cover an institution significantly engaged in the financial activities incorporated from Bank Holding Company Act § 4(k), including the credit-bureau services Trans Union performs. Section 4(k)(4)(F) incorporates activities recognized by a Federal Reserve order or regulation in effect on November 12, 1999, subject to that instrument's terms and conditions unless modified by the Board.

**Operative qualifications:** The exclusions preserve persons or entities with respect to activities subject to Commodity Futures Trading Commission jurisdiction under the Commodity Exchange Act; the Federal Agricultural Mortgage Corporation and entities chartered and operating under the Farm Credit Act; and the identified congressionally chartered secondary-market institutions. For the last category, section 509(3)(D) requires that the institution not sell or transfer nonpublic personal information to a nonaffiliated third party; rule 313.3(k)(3)(iii) preserves the exclusion when such transfers occur only as permitted by rules 313.14 and 313.15. The category consists of institutions chartered specifically for securitizations, secondary-market sales, including sales of servicing rights, or similar transactions related to a consumer transaction. The rule also excludes entities not significantly engaged in financial activities.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts the coverage construction with these exclusions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that financial-institution coverage follows the incorporated activity, rather than whether the business itself lends money. Regulation Y's credit-bureau category covers maintaining consumer credit-history information and providing it to a credit grantor considering an application or one that has extended credit. Trans Union performs that business. Congress chose a financial-activity definition broader than banks, while significant engagement and the express exclusions bound its reach. The claim that an information business necessarily falls outside the Act therefore fails. This is application of the statutory incorporation, not a general agency power over information companies.

### 3. Financial context and source-derived selection can protect identifying information

**Holding and operative rule:** The Act permits the rule to treat as financial information what a consumer provides to obtain a financial product or service, what results from a financial transaction with that consumer, and what is otherwise obtained in connection with providing such a product or service. Section 509(4) and rules 313.3(n)–(p) also protect a consumer grouping derived using nonpublic personally identifiable financial information, even when individual identifiers are publicly obtainable, while preserving independently public information on the terms below.

**Operative qualifications:** Publicly available information is information reasonably believed lawfully available to the general public from government records, widely distributed media, or legally required public disclosures. The institution must take steps to determine that the information is of a publicly available type and, where an individual may direct that it remain unpublished, that the consumer has not exercised that choice. A grouping derived without nonpublic personally identifiable financial information and containing only public information is outside the protected category when its disclosure does not identify the listed individuals as financial-institution consumers. Lists of names and addresses of customers of an entity that is not a financial institution, and information that does not identify a consumer, including aggregate or blind data without names, account numbers or other personal identifiers, are excluded from personally identifiable financial information.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts the source, relationship and public-information distinctions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a financial relationship can be conveyed by the context of an identifier or by the criteria used to select a list. The Act does not confine financial information to a balance or a creditworthiness assessment. It expressly includes groupings derived through nonpublic financial information and separately excludes genuinely independent public groupings. Treating every identifier as unprotected because it might appear elsewhere would erase that distinction. The opposite conclusion—that every name sold for money becomes financial information—would also disregard the source and relationship requirements. A bare identifier, a derived grouping and an independently acquired public list must therefore be assessed on their actual statutory terms. The financial-relationship fact communicated by disclosure, or the protected financial trait used in selection, explains why the challenged definition is reasonable within the delegated domain.

### Operative disclosure terms governing the receipt, reuse and speech holdings

These are the statutory and regulatory conditions on the disclosure routes addressed in Holdings 4, 5 and 7. They are permissions and duties; whether a particular institution, consumer or recipient complied is an application of those conditions.

**Ordinary notice and opt-out:** Under section 502(a)–(b) and rule 313.10, disclosure of nonpublic personal information to a nonaffiliated third party generally requires the applicable initial privacy notice, an opt-out notice explaining the choice, a reasonable opportunity to exercise it before disclosure, and no consumer opt-out. The notices must satisfy the applicable clear-and-conspicuous requirements. The rule's mail example allows thirty days after mailing the notices, with response by mailed form, toll-free call or another reasonable means. Its electronic example allows thirty days after acknowledgment of electronic notices in connection with opening an online account where the customer agreed to electronic receipt. Its isolated-transaction example allows contemporaneous notice and a choice required before completing that transaction. Those are examples of a reasonable opportunity, not one universal thirty-day deadline. The requirements apply even without an established customer relationship and to collected information whether obtained before or after the consumer's direction. An institution may offer a partial opt-out by information or recipient; the permission does not require such an offering.

**Service providers and joint marketing:** Section 502(b)(2) and rule 313.13 allow disclosure for services to, or functions on behalf of, the originating institution, including marketing its own products or services and qualifying joint financial marketing. The initial notice remains required. A contract must prohibit use and disclosure beyond the disclosed purposes, including any permitted use under rules 313.14 or 313.15 in the ordinary course of carrying out those purposes. Joint marketing requires a written contract under which the participating financial institutions jointly offer, endorse or sponsor a financial product or service. This is a distinct route from receipt under rules 313.14 and 313.15.

**Processing and servicing:** Section 502(e)(1) and rule 313.14 permit disclosure as necessary to effect, administer or enforce a consumer-requested or authorized transaction; in connection with servicing or processing a requested or authorized financial product or service; maintaining or servicing the account with the institution, or with another entity through a private-label credit-card arrangement or another extension of credit on its behalf; or a proposed or actual securitization, secondary-market sale, including servicing-right sales, or similar transaction related to the consumer's transaction. Necessary disclosure includes a required, lawful or appropriate method of enforcing the rights of those carrying out the transaction or providing the product or service. It also includes required, usual, appropriate or acceptable methods of ordinary transaction and account recordkeeping and servicing; benefits or claims administration; confirmations, transaction records and status or value information for the consumer or the consumer's agent or broker; and accrual or recognition of associated incentives or bonuses. The insurance functions include underwriting at the consumer's request, reinsurance, account administration, reporting, fraud or material-misrepresentation investigation or prevention, premium and claim processing, benefits administration including utilization review, research projects, and purposes required or specifically permitted by federal or state law. Payment functions include authorization, settlement, billing, processing, clearing, transfer, reconciliation or collection of payments; receivable or account transfers; and payment-information audits. Necessity here is not confined to physical indispensability.

**Other exceptions:** Section 502(e) and rule 313.15 preserve disclosure with the consumer's unrevoked consent or direction; to protect relevant record confidentiality or security; to prevent actual or potential fraud, unauthorized transactions, claims or other liability; for required institutional risk control or resolution of consumer disputes or inquiries; to persons with a legal or beneficial interest relating to the consumer, or acting as the consumer's fiduciary or representative; and to insurance-rate advisory organizations, guaranty funds or agencies, institutions' rating agencies, industry-compliance assessors, and the institution's attorneys, accountants and auditors. They also preserve specifically authorized or required law-enforcement, self-regulatory and public-safety-investigation disclosures consistent with the Right to Financial Privacy Act; disclosure to a consumer reporting agency in accordance with the FCRA or from a consumer report reported by such an agency; a proposed or actual sale, merger, transfer or exchange of a business or operating unit when the disclosure concerns solely that business's consumers; compliance with applicable law; properly authorized investigations, subpoenas or summonses; and judicial process or regulators acting within their jurisdiction as authorized by law.

**Which requirements are excepted:** Rules 313.14 and 313.15 excuse the initial-notice requirement in rule 313.4(a)(2), the opt-out requirements in rules 313.7 and 313.10, and the service-provider/joint-marketing requirements in rule 313.13 on the stated terms. They do not erase the distinct customer-relationship notice duties. Section 503 requires the applicable privacy-policy disclosure when a customer relationship is established and at least annually while it continues. The separate section 502(d) account-number marketing restriction and rule 313.12 qualifications stated in Holding 6 retain their own operation.

### 4. Exception-based receipt carries its purpose limits into reuse and redisclosure

**Holding and operative rule:** Under rule 313.11(a) and (c), information received from a nonaffiliated financial institution under rule 313.14 or 313.15 may be disclosed to the originator's affiliates, or to the recipient's affiliates subject to the same downstream use and disclosure limits. Other use or disclosure under those exceptions must occur in the ordinary course of carrying out the activity covered by the exception under which the information was received; a new recipient opt-out does not authorize unrelated marketing of information received for a bounded servicing or other excepted purpose.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts this exception-receipt construction and the operative terms set out above.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that lawful access for a limited activity does not confer an unrelated marketing entitlement. Section 502(c)'s direct-disclosure comparator operates within section 501's confidentiality framework and section 504's implementing authority. Although section 502(c) expressly addresses redisclosure, the subtitle authorizes defined, purpose-limited access; that access would lose its limitation if receipt automatically created unrestricted internal marketing rights. The rule reasonably preserves the activity for which the exception permitted disclosure. An account processor may use or disclose a list under an applicable exception in providing the processing service, including a properly authorized subpoena, but may not convert that access into its own marketing list or furnish it to another marketer. The named affiliate routes carry the stated limits forward. Christensen requires fidelity to the rule's actual permissions, so this construction remains distinct from the outside-exception own-use permission in Holding 5.

### 5. Receipt outside the processing and other exceptions preserves the recipient's own-use permission

**Holding and operative rule:** Under rule 313.11(b) and (d), a recipient may use for its own purposes a list lawfully obtained outside rules 313.14 and 313.15. Redisclosure is limited to the originator's affiliates, the recipient's affiliates with the recipient's downstream disclosure limits, or another person to whom the originating institution could lawfully disclose directly, including disclosure under the originator's privacy policy as limited by each consumer's actual opt-out, or disclosure under an applicable rule 313.14 or 313.15 exception.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts this separate route and its operative conditions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the rule preserves the difference between a permitted use and a permitted further disclosure. Section 502(c) requires the lawfulness of direct disclosure by the originator when the further recipient is unaffiliated with both institutions. The regulation implements that comparator rather than treating receipt as a way to erase the originator's lawful terms or the consumer's election. A recipient's substitute notice and opt-out would not necessarily preserve either. At the same time, rule 313.11(b)(2) expressly permits the recipient's own use under this route. The Court gives that permission effect; section 313.13's separate contractual purpose restriction also remains effective when that service-provider or joint-marketing route supplies the lawful receipt. The two recipient-use routes, the named affiliate permissions and the exception conditions must each be applied according to their terms.

### 6. The reporting-agency exception does not authorize unrestricted onward marketing of account numbers

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 502(d), 15 U.S.C. § 6802(d), and rule 313.12 prohibit disclosure, directly or through an affiliate, of a consumer credit-card, deposit or transaction account number or similar access number or code to a nonaffiliated third party for telemarketing, direct-mail marketing or electronic-mail marketing, other than disclosure to a consumer reporting agency. That recipient exception preserves properly authorized FCRA uses; it does not exempt a reporting agency's own onward disclosure from the governing limits or create an unrestricted account-number marketing right.

**Operative qualifications:** Rule 313.12 separately permits disclosure to an agent or service provider solely to market the institution's own products or services if that recipient is not authorized to directly initiate charges to the account, and to a participant in a private-label credit-card, affinity or similar program whose participants were identified to the customer upon entry into the program. An encrypted number or code is outside the account-number example only when no means of decoding it is supplied. A transaction account, distinct from a deposit or credit-card account, excludes an account to which third parties cannot initiate charges. Ordinary notice and opt-out do not displace this separate account-number restriction. Section 506(c), apart from its enacted FCRA amendments, and rule 313.16 preserve the FCRA's operation and permit no inference from these provisions about whether information is transaction-or-experience information under FCRA § 603.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts the challenged account-number construction with these distinct qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an exception identifying a permissible recipient is not a status-based immunity when that recipient later becomes the discloser. Trans Union's proposed construction would let routing through a reporting agency defeat the financial institution's disclosure limits. The FCRA savings clause preserves that statute's actual information, purpose, furnishing and use permissions and conditions. The June 2002 Trans Union decision concerns those bounded FCRA channels; it supplies no general marketing entitlement. Accordingly, the reporting-agency carveout accommodates properly authorized FCRA uses while the onward-transfer rule, the separate account-number prohibition and its own exceptions retain their work. The holding rejects the claimed unfettered marketing permission without treating every receipt by a reporting agency as an authorized transfer.

### 7. The ripe source- and use-bound marketing restrictions satisfy independent commercial-speech review

**Holding and operative rule:** Restrictions on the described credit-derived commercial targeting and financial-information transfers require independent review: government must establish a substantial confidentiality interest, direct material advancement and a reasonable proportional fit. The challenged ripe restrictions satisfy that standard because they protect against unrelated commercial exploitation of covered financial relationships through their actual source, purpose, consumer-election and onward-transfer limits, including the permissions and qualifications stated above; neither a universal empirical-study rule nor strict least-restrictive-means review applies.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts this complete Central Hudson/Fox application. Thomas's broader theoretical reservation does not qualify his adoption of the sufficient analysis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the commercial targeting function, rather than corporate identity, payment or a bare identifier, governs this application. Central Hudson and Fox require substantial interest, material advancement and reasonable fit. Edenfield demands a demonstrated connection to the actual harm; 44 Liquormart rejects unsupported suppression of truthful product information to influence consumer choice. Here the protected interest is financial confidentiality against unrelated repurposing, and the restricted reuse or disclosure can itself produce that harm. The June 2002 Trans Union decision supplies the closest source-derived marketing analysis, while U.S. West requires speech scrutiny independent of statutory authority and preserves its different approval mechanism.

The Court has considered the real burden on useful marketing information and the actual disclosures the scheme permits. Ordinary notice and opt-out, contract-limited services and joint marketing, consumer direction, servicing, reporting, professional functions and legally required disclosure have distinct purposes and conditions. Their permissions do not generally reproduce unrestricted unrelated commercial reuse. Discovery Network and Greater New Orleans require that comparison, rather than either automatic invalidation for incomplete regulation or automatic acceptance of a privacy label. A new recipient election would not necessarily preserve the originator's terms, the consumer's actual election or a purpose-limited grant of access. The remaining lawful transfers, independent-public-information route and consumer-directed permissions support proportional fit. Alternative advertising opportunities alone would not suffice. This application concerns the presented commercial marketing activity; noncommercial reporting and independently acquired public facts lie outside that question.

### 8. Both challenges to producing aggregate or blind data remain unripe

**Holding and operative rule:** When the agency has adopted no concrete position or enforcement application addressing whether transforming protected input into aggregate or blind data is restricted use, and the challenger shows no present hardship from awaiting such an application, the statutory and First Amendment objections to that transformation are unripe. The judgment withholding both merits questions is affirmed.

**Authority:** The unanimous Opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts this nonmerits disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the definition of an output and the permissibility of using protected inputs to create it are different questions. Rule 313.3(o) excludes genuinely nonidentifying aggregate or blind output from personally identifiable financial information. That exclusion alone does not resolve whether a particular transformation is a restricted use of information lawfully received through a bounded channel. Without an operative agency position, a concrete application or demonstrated hardship, the Court would be deciding an assumed restriction. Both statutory authority for such an application and its speech validity therefore remain open for a ripe controversy. Affirmance sustains the operative ripe rules at their stated scope and supplies neither an aggregation prohibition nor an aggregation immunity.

## Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Mead Corp. (June 18, 2001) and United States v. Haggar Apparel Co. (April 21, 1999):** Apply the actual-delegation threshold and judicial enforcement of statutory boundaries to the FTC's final implementing rule.
- **Christensen v. Harris County (May 1, 2000):** Its advisory-letter setting differs from this delegated final rule; its prohibition on rewriting an unambiguous regulatory permission preserves the recipient's own-use route.
- **Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837; NationsBank of North Carolina, N.A. v. Variable Annuity Life Insurance Co., 513 U.S. 251; and Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A., 517 U.S. 735:** Retain reasonable statutory interpretation within actual delegated authority after enforcement of clear text.
- **Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557, and Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S. 469:** Supply the independent substantial-interest, advancement and proportional-fit framework.
- **Edenfield v. Fane (April 26, 1993) and 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island (May 13, 1996):** Preserve the government's burden to connect the restriction to a real harm and to justify the chosen scope; the present application regulates confidential-source exploitation rather than indirect suppression of lawful product knowledge.
- **Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, Inc. (March 24, 1993) and Greater New Orleans Broadcasting Association, Inc. v. United States, 527 U.S. 173:** Require comparison of actual permitted disclosures with the asserted harm, without an all-sources-at-once requirement.
- **U.S. West, Inc. v. Federal Communications Commission (February 7, 2000):** Its independent speech scrutiny and confined confidentiality analysis apply, while its affirmative-approval mechanism remains specific to its statute and service categories.
- **Trans Union Corp. v. Federal Trade Commission (June 24, 2002):** Its independent analysis of unrelated credit-derived marketing extends to these separately authorized GLBA channels; the FCRA's distinct permissions and conditions retain their statutory scope.
- **Los Angeles Police Department v. United Reporting Publishing Corp. (December 7, 1999):** Its maintained-own-injury holding permits consideration of this concrete marketing challenge without substituting commercial overbreadth for the claimant's injury.
- **National Electrical Manufacturers Association v. Sorrell (March 17, 2003):** Its bounded factual-product-disclosure inquiry remains distinct from the present restrictions on disclosure and reuse.

## Law After Decision

Credit-bureau activity within the incorporated financial category is subject to the GLBA privacy rules at their actual significant-engagement and statutory limits. Financial-relationship information and lists derived using protected information retain their protection, while genuinely independent public information follows the stated exclusions. The original receipt route controls reuse: exception-based access remains purpose-bound, and outside-exception receipt preserves its own-use permission subject to applicable contractual limits, while redisclosure follows the originator's lawful permissions and the actual consumer election. The account-number marketing rule has its separate recipient exception and regulatory qualifications. These ripe restrictions are enforceable under both the statute and the independent commercial-speech analysis. The statutory and constitutional questions concerning production of aggregate or blind data remain open until a concrete, ripe application presents them. Lower courts apply the defined rules and exceptions to particular sources, uses and transactions; the statutory permissions do not themselves establish actual compliance.

## Separate Writings

Justice Thomas concurs, joining the Court's opinion in full. He retains his broader reservation about treating commercial expression as having less constitutional value, but accepts the Court's complete Central Hudson/Fox analysis of this application. The operative distinction concerns exploiting information obtained through a confidential financial relationship, rather than withholding truthful product information to influence consumers for their own good. The actual source, purpose, consumer-election and transfer limits sustain the result; corporate identity or payment alone would not classify expression or supply the justification.

## Procedure After Action

The judgment for the agencies stands. The rules continue to govern within the receipt, source, use, redisclosure and account-number terms upheld by the Court. Both aggregate-use theories remain outside merits adjudication; review of an actual agency application proceeds when the dispute is ripe.

# Illinois-ex-rel-Madigan-v-Telemarketing-Associates-Inc_merits_2003-05-05

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Illinois-ex-rel-Madigan-v-Telemarketing-Associates-Inc_merits_2003-05-05.md`

## Event

Merits decision, May 5, 2003. Argued March 3, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Illinois. The question is whether the First Amendment bars an action alleging deliberately false factual representations about charitable donations when professional fundraisers retain eighty-five percent of receipts. The Illinois Supreme Court affirmed dismissal as an impermissible percentage restriction. Illinois seeks reinstatement of its fraud claims.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate.

## Public Action

Reversed and remanded, 9–0, on the affirmative-fraud claims; stipulated dismissals remain effective.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The constitutional dismissal of sufficiently pleaded affirmative-fraud claims is reversed. Liability based solely on the retained percentage or silence about it remains unavailable. The stipulated dismissal of the other claims remains effective. Further proceedings apply each surviving Illinois cause and its applicable proof and remedy requirements.

## Opinion Topology

Kennedy delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. All nine join Parts I–II.

## Holdings

### I. Deliberate factual deception may be proved without regulating fundraising percentages

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Amendment permits an action against a charitable fundraiser for material factual representations knowingly used to deceive donors about the use of their contributions. High fundraising costs or failure to volunteer the fee percentage alone cannot establish fraud; the State must prove the independently identified representation, its materially false contextual meaning, intentional deception and the remaining requirements of its actual cause and remedy.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Schaumburg, Munson and Riley protect charitable solicitation from percentage restrictions and compelled fee disclosures while preserving genuine antifraud enforcement. A charity may spend money communicating its cause, and a statement that a contribution helps the charity does not become false merely because expenses consume most receipts. Information and advocacy undertaken during solicitation may themselves perform charitable work. The Court therefore rejects both a percentage presumption and a general duty to announce unfavorable financial information.

The alleged promises that at least ninety percent would go to veterans and that contributions would pay no labor expenses because all workers were volunteers present a different question. Their meaning and truth can be tested independently of whether an eighty-five-percent fee was reasonable. The contractual allocation may be evidence contradicting a specific promise; it cannot supply a promise that was never made. Requiring the State to establish actual, deliberate factual deception answers the claim that fraud litigation necessarily imposes the same restraint as a forbidden fee cap. Edenfield recognizes the distinct treatment of deception, but does not reduce charitable advocacy to ordinary commercial advertising. These particular allegations permit the fraud litigation to proceed.

**Precedent treatment:** Schaumburg, Munson and Riley are applied to preserve protected solicitation and the fee/disclosure boundary while permitting genuine fraud enforcement. Edenfield supplies the deception distinction at its own scope.

### II. Each restored cause retains its own proof and remedial requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** For the intentional common-law fraud restored here, Illinois must establish by clear and convincing evidence a materially false factual representation, knowledge of falsity, intent to mislead, successful deception through justifiable reliance, and resulting damage. A distinct public statutory theory must satisfy its own elements and evidentiary burden and the First Amendment boundary in Part I; it cannot evade that boundary by relabeling a fee cap.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join.

**Controlling explanation:** Hofmann supplies Illinois's rule that fraud is not presumed and requires clear and convincing proof. Witt identifies the factual falsity, knowledge, intended inducement, justifiable reliance and damage required for common-law fraud, and distinguishes actual deceptive intent from conduct violating a different duty. Those demanding requirements keep the burden on Illinois and protect truthful fundraising while allowing proof of the deliberate deception pleaded here.

The complaint invokes common-law and statutory causes. Neither Witt's disciplinary burden nor the elements of a private common-law action establish every requirement of a public consumer-protection cause. The lower court must identify the invoked theory and apply its actual law, including its culpability, causation, injury and remedy requirements. The clear-and-convincing rule stated here governs the intentional fraud at issue; it is not announced as a universal constitutional burden for all statutory enforcement. Reversal removes the constitutional dismissal of sufficiently pleaded affirmative deception. The State must then prove its claims under these rules, and any injunction must address established deception rather than lawful advocacy or the fee level itself.

**Precedent treatment:** Hofmann v. Hofmann, 94 Ill. 2d 205, 222–225 (1983), supplies the common-law proof burden. In re Witt, 145 Ill. 2d 380, 390–391, 397 (1991), supplies the fraud elements and deliberate-intent distinction without supplying a universal statutory burden.

## Precedent Treatment

Schaumburg, Munson and Riley retain their protection against percentage restrictions and compulsory fee disclosure; the deliberate-fraud route remains available. Edenfield preserves the distinction between truthful expression and deception without diminishing charitable advocacy's protection. Hofmann and Witt govern the described Illinois common-law fraud requirements, with distinct statutory causes retaining their own law.

## Law After Decision

Charitable fundraisers remain protected against liability inferred from fundraising costs or nondisclosure alone. A State may prove deliberate material factual deception about contributions under the applicable cause of action. The operative inquiry concerns the actual representation and proof of deception, with financial allocation usable as evidence of an independently established false representation.

# Smith-v-Doe_merits_2003-03-05

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Smith-v-Doe_merits_2003-03-05.md`

## Event

Smith v. Doe, No. 01-729. Argued November 13, 2002; decided March 5, 2003. On review of the Ninth Circuit's judgment holding Alaska's retrospective registration and notification scheme punitive under the Ex Post Facto Clause. The district court had entered judgment for the State. The question is whether applying the challenged scheme to persons whose offenses predated its 1994 enactment imposes forbidden retrospective punishment. The two offender respondents fall within lifetime registration and quarterly verification; a spouse also challenges the scheme.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

## Public Action

Reverse the Ex Post Facto judgment and remand, 5–4.

## Judgment & Remedy

The Ninth Circuit's judgment on the Ex Post Facto claim is reversed and the case remanded, 5–4. **Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. **Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The challenged retrospective operation receives no relief on this Ex Post Facto claim. The lower court must implement that disposition and give any independently pending privacy or accuracy claim its proper treatment under its own governing law. Those independent claims lie outside the question decided here.

## Opinion Topology

O'Connor announces the judgment of the Court and delivers the opinion of the Court as to Part I and an opinion as to Parts II and III. The full joiners are Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

## Holdings

### The governing inquiry considers both intended character and actual combined operation

**Holding and operative rule:** A court first determines whether the legislature intended a civil or criminal measure. An established civil design may be overcome only by the clearest proof that the challenged measure's purpose or effects are punitive, assessed cumulatively through its supported design and actual operation; no civil label or single punitive indicator is conclusive.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor's opinion, joined by all nine Justices: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. This common method controls; the different applications retain the support identified below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Hudson's sequential intent-and-effects inquiry and Seling's requirement to examine the operation actually challenged. Mendoza-Martinez supplies relevant considerations within that inquiry: restraint or disability, historical punishment analogues, the role of scienter, retribution and deterrence, connection to conduct already criminal, an alternative protective purpose and excess in relation to that purpose. They are considerations to assess together, not separate burdens or individually decisive conditions.

Hendricks shows that a civil designation can be overcome by cumulative punitive operation; its custody facts are not facts about Alaska's registry. W.P. sustained notification on the supported combination of risk grading, individual exceptions and likely-encounter recipients. That decision neither supplies missing Alaska safeguards nor disposes of a materially different scheme by analogy alone. Alaska's continuing duties, duration, publication and protective design must be assessed together with contrary evidence, including the absence of direct residence, employment or movement prohibitions. Reporting is not quarterly physical appearance, and public Internet search access is not compelled viewing.

The parties' competing analogies therefore require an examination of the State's own measure, rather than a conclusion from its title, the availability of old conviction records or an assumed private reaction. Paul P. governs a distinct limited residential-information privacy interest. A possible defect under that doctrine does not itself establish punishment under the Ex Post Facto Clause.

### The judgment rejects this claim without a majority application rationale

**Disposition and authority:** Five Justices reverse the reviewed Ex Post Facto judgment: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas through the plurality's established-civil-intent and clearest-proof application; Souter through his independent ambiguous-intent and ordinary-presumption route. The judgment binds the parties. No five Justices adopt either complete application rationale.

**Fractured authority:** The plurality finds a demonstrated civil design and asks whether the demanding override is met. Souter withholds that premise and resolves a close direct-effects balance through the ordinary presumption of constitutionality. His different actual-intent finding and residual rule are not a logical subset of the plurality's rationale merely because its burden is more demanding. Marks supplies no controlling synthesis of those routes. Their precise grounds appear in Separate Writings.

## Precedent Treatment

- Hudson v. United States: apply its sequential intent-and-effects inquiry, cumulative assessment and demanding rebuttal after an established civil choice.
- Seling v. Young: apply actual design-and-operation review; a statutory label does not foreclose proof through implementation.
- Kansas v. Hendricks: preserve its cumulative punitive-operation holding and distinguish its custodial facts from the registry's operation.
- Kennedy v. Mendoza-Martinez: apply its punitive indicators within the common inquiry, without a conclusive single factor.
- W.P. v. Verniero: preserve its tailored-notification holding; its safeguards cannot be assumed in Alaska's different scheme.
- Paul P. v. Verniero: preserve its independent protection for the specified nonpublic residential information; privacy and punishment remain distinct questions.
- Marks v. United States: the differing intent findings and rules of decision yield no narrower controlling application rationale here.

## Law After Decision

The common sequential framework and actual-operation method remain controlling. The judgment rejects respondents' Ex Post Facto claim, but the established-civil-intent application has four votes and Souter's close-case application has one. Those routes create no unified controlling rule validating the same class of registries. Independent residential-information privacy remains governed by Paul P. and the applicable claim's own requirements.

## Separate Writings

**The plurality's application, O'Connor, with the other full joiners named above.** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas find civil intent established. Registration's placement in the criminal-procedure title does not alone classify it as punishment, especially when that title includes nonpunitive subjects; the registry's health-and-safety placement supports the protective character. Plea notice and the conviction judgment's statement of registration requirements inform a person of an independently imposed legal consequence rather than establish that the sentencing judge selected the duty.

They then examine the entire operation under the clearest-proof standard. Lifetime duties and unrestricted current-information access are serious burdens, and the absence of a rehabilitation exit matters. But the scheme confines no one, directly prohibits no residence, work or movement, and requires no in-person quarterly appearance. A viewer must navigate to and search the registry, which provides no comment forum beneath a person's entry. Those features weaken the proposed equivalence to compelled public humiliation or criminal supervision. Knowing failure to comply creates a separate offense; conviction's role in a protective classification is not alone punishment. The lack of W.P.'s tailoring warrants careful review but does not itself supply the demanded punitive proof. The dissent's cumulative account remains insufficient, for these four, to overcome the established civil design. They reject Souter's treatment of ordinary notice and mixed codification as enough to withhold that design's demanding rebuttal protection.

**Souter, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Souter joins the abstract framework but finds actual intent ambiguous. Criminal-procedure placement and integration with plea and conviction-judgment administration establish a meaningful penal connection; the protective purpose and health-and-safety registry provisions point toward civil regulation. Under Ursery and Hudson, special clearest-proof deference follows an established civil choice, not every asserted beneficial aim. Souter therefore examines purposes and effects directly without requiring the heightened override.

This is a close balance. Lifetime current-information maintenance, an unrestricted audience and the absence of a response to rehabilitation carry substantial punitive force. The noncustodial reporting, absence of in-person supervision and direct conduct restraints, continuing informational purpose and searchable rather than compulsory public encounter carry civil force. The absence of a comment feature further limits the shaming analogy. Souter resolves the remaining doubt for the State under the ordinary presumption of constitutionality. He does not affirmatively validate every such registry or supply a fifth join to the plurality's premise and application.

**Ginsburg, dissenting, joined in Part I by Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Breyer, and in Part II by Breyer.** Part I finds the clearest proof of punishment even assuming civil intent. Offense-triggered lifetime duties continually replenish an unrestricted official dossier of present life, without tailoring audience or duration to present risk. W.P.'s risk grading, individual exceptions and likely-encounter recipients made its civil fit materially stronger. Hendricks and Seling require actual operation to receive weight and do not make custody indispensable. Search-on-request and the absence of comments qualify the shaming analogy, but official availability and compulsory replenishment remain affirmative state acts. The post-enactment plea and judgment provisions add weight without transforming respondents' original sentences into findings of registration punishment. Doe I's custody-court rehabilitation determination and very-low-risk evaluations make the absence of a responsive mechanism concrete, without establishing zero future risk or rehabilitation of all registrants.

The four would protect respondents from the punitive retrospective combination, with any necessary state-law severability determination identifying a genuinely lawful remainder. Part II, joined only by Ginsburg and Breyer, additionally finds intent ambiguous and the effects punitive without special deference to a clearly selected civil design. That is an independent sufficient route for those two, not a join in the plurality's actual-intent conclusion.

**Stone-Zsela, further dissenting.** Stone-Zsela joins the common cumulative-effects dissent and would presume relief against the proved unconstitutional retrospective policy for the persons it governs unless Alaska establishes a narrower complete cure, within the court's remedial jurisdiction. The decree would identify the reporting, updating and state-maintained dissemination obligations that must cease and direct responsible officials to conform registry treatment and enforcement instructions. Alaska would have to substantiate any claimed severable civil remainder. This proposed remedial presumption is distinct from the common dissent's protection for respondents; it preserves ordinary lawful conviction records and does not create a replacement dangerousness-hearing system.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the lower court to implement the reversal and address independently pending claims as stated in the mandate.
