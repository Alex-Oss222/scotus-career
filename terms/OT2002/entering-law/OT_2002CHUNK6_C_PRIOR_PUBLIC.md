### Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union, No. 96-511

Merits decision, June 26, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 19, 1997; decided June 26, 1997. On direct appeal from a three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under §561 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The question is whether the CDA's indecency and patently-offensive restrictions are facially invalid under the First Amendment because they are content based, vague, substantially overbroad and insufficiently tailored to protecting minors.

The Act was enacted February 8, 1996. The district court's preliminary-injunction order was dated June 11 and entered June 12, 1996; its June 28 clarification preserved June 12 as the appeal-clock entry date. The plaintiffs demonstrated present speech restrictions and practical compliance burdens. The record describes diverse lawful Internet expression and age-verification costs and barriers, especially for noncommercial and small speakers.

Section 223(a)(1)(B) reaches knowing creation or solicitation and initiation of transmission by a telecommunications device of an obscene or indecent communication, knowing its recipient is under eighteen, regardless of who placed the call or initiated the communication. The enacted definition of telecommunications device excludes an interactive computer service. Section (d)(1)(A) separately reaches knowing use of an interactive computer service to send to specified persons under eighteen; (d)(1)(B) reaches display available to a person under eighteen. Their content category covers contextual depictions or descriptions of sexual or excretory activities or organs in terms patently offensive under contemporary community standards. The corresponding facility offenses require knowing permission and intent that the facility be used for the prohibited activity. Violations carry fines, imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

Section (e)(1) protects mere access or connection to a facility, system or network outside the provider's control, including incidental functions that do not create content. Section (e)(2) denies that defense to a conspirator with an entity actively creating or knowingly distributing prohibited communications, or one knowingly advertising them; the conspirator need not personally create content. Section (e)(3) separately denies it for access to an offending network the provider owns or controls. Employer liability under (e)(4) requires within-scope employee or agent conduct plus knowing authorization or ratification, or reckless disregard. Section (e)(5) provides alternative defenses: good-faith reasonable, effective and appropriate measures under the circumstances to restrict minors' access, or restricted access requiring a verified credit card, debit account, adult access code or adult personal identification number. Under (e)(6), the FCC may describe suitable measures; it may not approve, sanction, compel or enforce their use or endorse particular products. Their use is evidence of good faith. Interactive services do not thereby become common or telecommunications carriers.

Section (f)(1) protects lawful good-faith implementation of defenses or access restrictions against suit. Section (f)(2) bars inconsistent state or local liability for the identified commercial, library and higher-education activities, while allowing complementary regulation confined to intrastate services without inconsistent interstate duties and preserving regulation of conduct outside this section. Section (g) preserves other federal laws. Section (h)'s device definition adds no broadcast-licensee or cable-operator obligations under other indecency provisions; its access-software definition covers content-neutral filtering, selection and transmission tools that do not create or provide the communication's content. These distinct savings provisions supply no absent narrow content definition.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Authority for both holdings:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts the following propositions, explanations, limits and remedy. O'Connor's agreement with part of the judgment does not join this opinion.

##### I. The challenged content restrictions violate the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** A content-based criminal restriction on expression through a general-purpose communications network is facially invalid when uncertain and substantially overbroad categories suppress substantial protected adult speech and the Government fails to establish narrow tailoring in light of practical, materially less restrictive means of limiting minors' access. On this Internet record, §223(a)(1)(B)'s indecency prohibition and §223(d)(1)'s patently-offensive transmission and display prohibitions, together with their corresponding facility-permitting offenses, fail that requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** Sable recognizes the compelling interest in protecting children but forbids reducing adults to children's speech when less restrictive means are available. Ginsberg sustained a confined commercial restriction on material obscene for minors, including a value limitation absent here. Pacifica's scarcity, intrusiveness and broadcast setting do not describe this medium; ACT/Pacifica requires independent protection of adult access even there. Turner concerns content-neutral carriage, not this direct selection of prohibited content. Denver Area's compulsory-blocking holding supplies the practical adult-access analogy, without extending its fractured permission judgments into a universal rule.

The criminal categories reach serious discussion of health, sexuality, art, politics and personal experience. Uncertainty about content and community standards chills speech; scienter cannot tell a speaker which protected discussion is criminal. Knowledge of a minor or reference to specified recipients does not prevent the prohibition from reaching conversations also intended for adults. Other communication channels do not cure exclusion from this one.

The defenses do not remove those burdens. Many small and noncommercial speakers lack feasible age verification; costs, loss of anonymity and exclusion of lawful adults persist. Proposed tagging does not establish the effective protection the defense requires on the present record. User-controlled filtering and precise regulation of unprotected material offer materially less restrictive approaches. The Government has not justified this broad criminal suppression in light of those alternatives. The Court does not find every age screen invalid or all future controls ineffective. Vagueness contributes to the First Amendment holding; no independent Fifth Amendment holding is needed.

##### II. Textual severance preserves obscenity law; judicial rewriting cannot save the remaining restrictions

**Holding and operative rule:** The discrete words imposing indecency liability in §223(a)(1)(B) are severable from its obscenity prohibition. The Court affirms the clarified preliminary injunction against that indecency branch and its corresponding §223(a)(2) liability, and against §223(d)(1)–(2); the general severability provision does not authorize creating a new minor-only offense or inserting a value exception absent from the enacted text.

**Controlling explanation:** The word choice permits removal of the indecency branch without disabling the distinct obscenity rule. The proposed further construction would instead change the offenses' substantive coverage. Knowledge and audience limits do not provide the missing narrow content definition, and a serious-value or harmful-to-minors exception cannot be supplied by judicial legislation. The statutory defenses preserve their own terms but do not establish workable protection for the record's diverse speakers. The challenge's varied activities and substantial protected reach make a selective judicial reconstruction inappropriate. O'Connor would retain knowing transmissions solely to minors; the Court does not adopt her conclusion that minors' protected-speech overbreadth is insubstantial or her proposed application severance.

The ruling does not hold that Congress can never regulate protected Internet indecency, decide the proposed blanket ban on such messages to seventeen-year-olds or the role of parental approval, or resolve the separate question of extraterritorial efficacy and scope. It does not require one constitutional classification for every Internet service. Independently lawful obscenity, child sexual-abuse material, true-threat, fraud, unlawful-solicitation and direct-harmful-contact provisions remain governed by their own predicates; they do not save these overbroad restrictions. A future precisely drawn rule using effective, nonexclusionary age assurance remains open.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Sable, Butler and Bolger preserve adult access despite the compelling interest in protecting minors; child protection does not alone justify suppressing adult expression.
- Ginsberg's commercial restriction on material obscene for minors is distinguished because the CDA reaches broader protected categories without its value limitation.
- Pacifica retains its contextual broadcast force and supplies no reduced Internet protection.
- ACT/Pacifica's independent review of content-selected restrictions and adult access remains intact; the Internet record is separately examined.
- Turner retains the distinction between content-neutral carriage and content discrimination, with independent scrutiny of the evidentiary fit.
- Denver Area's compulsory-segregation holding is applied by analogy to practical adult-access burdens; its separate permission judgments create no universal Internet rule.
- Broadrick supplies the substantial-overbreadth framework; the enacted breadth cannot be repaired by an unavailable narrowing construction. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged indecency and patently-offensive restrictions cannot be enforced at the clarified scope. Content-based criminal regulation of this medium must protect lawful adult expression and satisfy narrow tailoring on the actual record. Obscenity and other independently unprotected conduct retain their own legal treatment. Future, precisely drawn regulation and genuinely effective nonexclusionary age controls remain open; no general Internet-service classification or freestanding Fifth Amendment rule is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part.** Butler, Sable and Bolger protect adult access; Ginsberg permits genuine restrictions confined to minors. The Internet presently lacks generally usable identity gates, so the display offense and mixed adult/minor conversations cannot be saved by treating the network as divided into adult and child zones. She agrees with the injunction to that extent and with preservation of independent obscenity enforcement.

She would retain the transmission provisions only where the initiating adult knows all recipients are minors, with corresponding facility liability confined to those underlying communications. She construes §223(d)(1)(A) to require knowledge of minority, relying on its wording and DeBartolo's constitutional-avoidance principle. Such a communication has no other adult audience whose access is lost. Brockett's application-severability principle and the statutory severability provision support preserving that application. Although the CDA's content categories are broader than Ginsberg's obscenity-for-minors rule, she finds no showing under Broadrick that the additional protected minor speech is substantial relative to the provisions' legitimate sweep. She does not deny minors all First Amendment protection or foreclose individual vagueness challenges. Her view relies on current technology, not predicted improvements. No other Justice joins her separate writing.

The clarification did not restart the June 12 appeal clock. The Court adds no damages, new deadline, mandated technology or permanent decree.

### Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition, No. 00-795

Merits decision, April 16, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

535 U.S. 234. Argued October 30, 2001. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 198 F.3d 1083, in a pre-enforcement facial challenge. The questions are whether §§2256(8)(B) and (D), covering apparent minority and presentation as depicting minors, substantially burden protected expression and whether faithful constructions or the enacted defenses save them. The Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment for the Government, invalidated the two definitions, and treated the remaining categories as severable. No conviction involving an identified abused child is under review.

All nine Justices participate at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Section 2256(8)(B), youthful-looking actual adults | Affirm invalidation, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia | Relief against enforcement of the challenged application stands. |
| Section 2256(8)(B), wholly virtual realistic images | Affirm invalidation, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia | Relief against enforcement of the challenged application stands. |
| Section 2256(8)(D), presentation-based definition | Affirm invalidation, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia | Relief against enforcement of the challenged application stands. |

The Ninth Circuit judgment invalidating the challenged definitions is affirmed. Relief against enforcement through §§2256(8)(B) and (D) stands; the separate actual-child and obscenity prohibitions and the unchallenged identifiable-minor provision retain their independent operation. Substantial overbreadth supplies the ground without a separate Fifth Amendment vagueness judgment.

O'CONNOR, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part, in Part II of which SCALIA, J., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**I. Apparent minority does not replace exploitation or obscenity.** A criminal definition substantially reaching nonobscene depictions produced without exploiting an actual child cannot be sustained merely because the depicted person appears to be a minor. Section 2256(8)(B)'s restriction fails the narrow tailoring required for this content-based burden on protected expression, including its wholly virtual realistic and actual-adult applications.

**Authority:** The six Justices joining Parts I and II of Justice Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, named above, directly support both rules and the statutory qualifications below.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Ferber's exclusion rests on injuries to actual children in production and circulation; visual resemblance creates neither injury by itself. Miller's independent obscenity elements remain necessary to that category. Reno and Playboy require the Government to justify this protected-speech burden, while Broadrick requires substantial protected reach relative to legitimate applications. The asserted grooming, demand and proof difficulties are serious interests, but they do not establish the fit required for this prohibition. A lawful image's possible misuse cannot itself supply production abuse, and difficulty proving an actual child does not permit suppression of substantial protected substitutes. A realistic-image construction does not cure that defect or supply the absent serious-value protection. Actual exploitation, obscenity and independently unlawful solicitation retain their own predicates.

**II. Presentation does not transform a protected work into contraband.** Section 2256(8)(D) is independently substantially overbroad because promotional presentation can make a nonobscene, nonexploitative work prohibited material in connected distribution and possession offenses, including for persons unconnected with the presentation. A court may not save this definition by rewriting it as a separately defined intentional unlawful-offer offense.

**Authority:** The same six directly join Part II and this independent invalidity ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Ginzburg makes pandering evidence relevant within an obscenity inquiry; it does not automatically remove protection from the underlying nonobscene work. The enacted definition is broader than the Government's proposed knowing-promoter construction, and ordinary scienter does not confine all connected liability to the original promoter. Reno permits genuine severance but not invention of absent offense limits. A differently enacted unlawful-offer offense lies outside the question, as does the validity of the identifiable-actual-minor definition in (C).

**Operative statutory qualifications.** A minor is under eighteen. Sexually explicit conduct covers actual or simulated genital-genital, oral-genital, anal-genital or oral-anal intercourse, whether between persons of the same or opposite sex, bestiality, masturbation, sadistic or masochistic abuse and lascivious exhibition of the genitals or pubic area; mere nudity does not itself establish the last category. Section 2252A(c) applies only to (a)(1)–(4), requires actual adult performers at production, and requires that the defendant did not advertise, promote, present, describe or distribute the work as depicting minors. It excludes wholly generated images and ordinary possession under (a)(5). Section 2252A(d), separately, concerns possession of fewer than three images: prompt, good-faith reasonable destruction steps or reporting the images to law enforcement and affording access to each image, without retaining any image or copy or permitting anyone other than law enforcement access to one. It requires reasonable destruction steps, not successful destruction, and provides no right to retain protected expression. The unchallenged (C) definition concerns a recognizable actual person who was a minor at the relevant creation, adaptation or modification or whose image as a minor was used; proof of the person's actual identity is unnecessary. These provisions do not cure the challenged protected reach.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **New York v. Ferber, 458 U.S. 747, and Osborne v. Ohio, 495 U.S. 103:** Their actual-child injury predicates are preserved; they are not extended to an invented image or an adult performance solely because it resembles a child.
- **Miller v. California, 413 U.S. 15:** Obscenity remains an independent category with its own elements, not a substitute apparent-age rule.
- **Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union:** Its protected-adult-expression and substantial-overbreadth reasoning applies; its distinction between genuine severance and judicial rewriting defeats the proposed new offense limits.
- **United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc.:** Its content-based scrutiny and governmental justification burden govern the fit of the challenged criminal restrictions.
- **United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.:** Knowledge of depiction characteristics remains distinct from constitutional authority to suppress those characteristics; this case changes no surviving factual-knowledge requirement.
- **Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601:** Substantial protected reach, rather than a conceivable unusual application, supports facial relief here.
- **Ginzburg v. United States, 383 U.S. 463:** Pandering may inform an obscenity inquiry; it does not independently make a protected nonobscene work contraband for all possessors.

#### Law After Decision

The two challenged definitions cannot support enforcement against the substantial protected expression they cover. Apparent minority without exploitation or obscenity does not suffice, and promotional characterization does not automatically transform a protected work into prohibited material. Independent actual-child, obscenity and unlawful-conduct rules retain their predicates. The identifiable-actual-minor category and a differently enacted unlawful-offer offense are outside this decision.

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Thomas agrees with both invalidations but does not join the Court's principal reasoning. Enforcement of actual-child prohibitions is the Government's strongest concern. The present predictive showing does not establish a technological proof obstacle sufficient for this breadth, but practical impossibility of distinguishing fabricated from actual-child images could present a different constitutional question. An appropriate affirmative defense or another narrowly drawn restriction might matter on that demonstrated premise. He neither requires a particular future defense nor approves shifting the actual-child element through a current presumption. His agreement in judgment does not endorse a categorical foreclosure of every enforcement-based restriction affecting protected expression.

**O'Connor, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part; Scalia joins Part II of her writing only.** In Part I she agrees with invalidation of the youthful-adult application: adult performers' protected expression remains exposed because the limited adult defense does not reach ordinary possession. She separately agrees that (D)'s presentation category lacks an adequately justified compelling fit, particularly when describing adult performers as minors removes the defense. In Part II she would preserve (B) for wholly virtual images virtually indistinguishable from actual-child images. The narrow construction excludes cartoons, sculptures and plainly unreal depictions. Congress may respond to supported enforcement predictions before actual proof failure becomes universal; in her view the child-protection interest and limited application satisfy the constitutional inquiry. Broadrick and application-severability principles permit preserving that application without extending the adult defense to possession. Her vagueness conclusion is confined to the realistic-image construction. Scalia joins only this virtual-image sustaining ground, not her adult or presentation invalidations.

**Scalia, dissenting.** Scalia would sustain both definitions under narrowing constructions. The statutory conduct requirement reaches actual or realistically simulated hardcore sexual acts, not merely a film's subject matter, and apparent minority means realistic images virtually indistinguishable from depictions of actual children. He adopts O'Connor's narrow virtual-image enforcement rationale but does not accept her adult-application invalidation. For (D), he reads offense scienter together with the definition to confine liability to knowing pandering by the responsible actor, excluding innocent downstream possession. Ginzburg supports the relevance of deliberate presentation to this confined transaction in his view. The Court regards that reading as alteration of a definition used in connected offenses and finds the remaining protected reach substantial. Scalia does not treat all nonobscene adult expression as unprotected or enlarge §2252A(c)'s defense.

[535 U.S. 234](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-535/pdf/USREPORTS-535-234.pdf), for the statutory text, facial posture and chronology. The identifiable-minor provision was not challenged; this judgment does not decide its constitutionality.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment protecting the challengers against enforcement of §§2256(8)(B) and (D) remains in force. This merits review is concluded subject to the ordinary mandate; the surviving independent statutory categories remain enforceable on their own terms.

### Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez / United States v. Velazquez, Nos. 99-603 and 99-960

Merits decision, February 28, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 2000. On writs of certiorari to the Second Circuit, 164 F.3d 757, which invalidated the challenged welfare-law condition and approved limited injunctive relief after the district court denied preliminary relief. The question is whether Congress may authorize representation of eligible individuals seeking specific welfare-agency relief while excluding efforts to amend or challenge the validity of existing welfare law. Section 504(a)(16) contains a broad welfare-reform litigation, lobbying and rulemaking restriction, an individual-benefits representation exception, and the challenged condition on that exception. The condition refers to law in effect when representation begins. LSC construed the broad reform ban to exclude both challenges to and defenses of reform law; that interpretation does not itself classify the distinct condition inside the individual exception. Recipient restrictions extend to non-LSC as well as LSC funds. The Government and LSC defend a limited service; challengers contend the condition disables independent advocacy within the authorized representation. The grant did not include severability, which was neither briefed nor argued.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed in Nos. 99-603 and 99-960, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Affirmance in both dockets, 6–3 | Entire independent-advocacy holding and scope-limited preservation of existing excision |
| Dissent, common service-definition ground | Scalia | O'Connor, Thomas | Would reverse in both dockets | Program-specific defense accepting recipient-wide reach, withdrawal and joint-counsel limits |
| Same dissent, additional subsidy explanation | Scalia | Thomas | Additional explanation for reversal | Broader grant-selection account with independent-penalty limits; O'Connor does not join |
| Same dissent, conditional severability ground | Scalia | O'Connor, Thomas | Independently opposes affirmance if condition is invalid | Would remove the whole individual-benefits exception with its condition, preserving the broad reform restriction |

#### Holdings

##### I. The condition impermissibly disables independent client advocacy

**Holding and operative rule:** When Congress supports independent representation of eligible individuals seeking specific welfare relief, it may not condition that authorized representation on withholding otherwise relevant arguments challenging the validity of the law governing the claim. Section 504(a)(16)'s condition against efforts to amend or challenge existing law in effect when representation begins is unconstitutional within that individual-benefits service; Congress retains authority to define lawful subject, client and activity limits and need not fund every form of litigation.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts both holdings, their limits and the affirmance in both dockets.

**Controlling explanation:** Rosenberger and Southworth examine whose expression the actual program supports. Here the lawyer represents the client, including against government, while opposing counsel presents the government's position. The program's function is independent advocacy, not delivery of a government-authored message. A challenge to governing law may be the necessary legal basis for the very individual relief Congress permits. Finley's narrow approval of nonconclusive selection considerations does not authorize this categorical disability. Urofsky likewise rejects automatic governmental attribution from public resources and professional duties, without eliminating legitimate institutional control.

The condition permits facts, misreading, misapplication and agency-policy objections under existing law, but prohibits statutory and constitutional validity arguments, including federal preemption and facial or as-applied constitutional claims. The lawyer must then withdraw consistently with professional duties, even when independent counsel would present the validity argument and the recipient's lawyer would continue the other issues. Counsel must explain the withdrawal, may discuss views of the law and may refer the client; those permissions do not supply the missing representation. Separate affiliates are legally permitted, but their permission establishes neither available replacement counsel nor its impossibility.

The Government's program-boundary argument correctly recognizes authority to fund selected services. It fails because the condition disables a class of legal grounds inside the authorized client-directed service, including representation financed with non-LSC money. Symmetry of the broad reform ban concerns a different statutory component and does not neutralize this restriction inside the individual exception. Rust and Regan retain their lawful program applications; professional independence alone is not decisive, but the actual function and argument-based operation distinguish this program. Distortion of the court's receipt of legal arguments reinforces the speech violation without creating a separate structural cause of action, appropriation power or universal civil-counsel right.

##### II. The existing limited remedy stands without adjudicating an unpresented severability question

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court affirms the constitutional invalidation in both dockets and leaves the lower court's condition-only excision undisturbed because severability lies outside the limited grant and was not briefed or argued. Rule 24.1(a) permits discretionary consideration of an otherwise jurisdictionally proper plain error evident from the record, but the Court finds no sufficiently evident error warranting that enlargement here.

**Authority:** The same six Justices directly join this scope ruling and remedy; they do not adopt a new substantive severability conclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 14.1(a) ordinarily confines review to the questions presented and fairly included subsidiary questions. Rule 24 preserves a distinct discretionary exception; lack of briefing is neither an absolute bar nor automatic permission to invoke it. The dissent argues that the exception and its condition form a legislative bargain that cannot survive excision of the condition. That is a substantive severability question, not a necessary consequence of the speech holding.

Alaska Airlines requires lawful independent operation and inquiry into whether Congress would have enacted the remainder without the invalid provision. The lower court's report of unclear intent does not by itself prove that Congress would reject the remaining individual service. Conversely, the legal-assistance purpose does not itself prove Congress would enact that service without the condition. Removing the whole exception would itself withdraw an express permission. On this unbriefed question the Court does not find an evident error that justifies deciding between those substantive alternatives.

The limited excision therefore remains operative without a holding that the parties conceded expanded funding or established a legislative preference. The reviewed stage remains injunction litigation. The Court orders no new appropriation, whole-program invalidation, benefit award or relief against unrelated restrictions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia: applies the distinction between independent expression and the government's message within the actual funded activity; reasonable program boundaries remain permissible.
- Board of Regents v. Southworth: applies attention to independent expression and viewpoint-neutral administration without converting every grant into a public forum.
- National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley: distinguishes its nonconclusive selective-grant criteria from this categorical restriction; its narrow controlling rule supplies no universal subsidy exemption.
- Urofsky v. Gilmore: applies the actual-function distinction against automatic governmental attribution from public means and professional duties; its employment framework is not mechanically transferred to all legal-service grants.
- Rust v. Sullivan: distinguishes its defined governmental program from the actual independent client advocacy here, without treating professional relationships alone as dispositive or displacing its lawful application.
- Regan v. Taxation With Representation: preserves legitimate funding selection at its actual scope; it does not answer the condition operating inside this independent representation.
- Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock: preserves the independent-operation and congressional-intent inquiry; no new severability merits determination is made here.

#### Law After Decision

Effective February 28, 2001, the challenged condition may not disable otherwise relevant welfare-law validity arguments within Congress's authorized independent individual representation. Ordinary factual, statutory-application and validity arguments remain distinct, and the decision creates no right to support for every subject, client or form of litigation. The lower court's limited excision stands through the Court's scope ruling; unrelated welfare-reform, lobbying, class-action and other LSC restrictions are not invalidated. Neither the dissent's conditional whole-exception remedy nor a new legislative-intent rule becomes law.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas on the common service-definition ground, dissenting.** Congress may subsidize routine enforcement of existing welfare law without assisting the recipient's validity-challenging representation. The dissent accepts that the lawyer advocates for the client, the restriction reaches non-LSC funds, and the lawyer must leave the whole case even if independent counsel could handle the prohibited issue. It nevertheless regards those terms as defining the selected recipient's welfare service, not penalizing independent speech outside it. The broad ban's symmetry, substantial permitted enforcement arguments, advice and referral, and legally separate affiliates support that classification without proving any particular substitute actually available. Rust's professional relationship shows why private professional judgment alone does not settle a program boundary. Their view creates no constitutional obligation to finance continuation when the case crosses that boundary.

##### An ongoing federal property-right claim may proceed against responsible officers within real remedial limits

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by the other four members of the five-Justice Court coalition identified in Opinion Topology. The rule has five direct joins and requires no fractured-opinion synthesis.

##### 1. Constitutional power before this disputed statutory merits question

Whether that action reaches reports filed late but before suit is a substantial dispute about statutory liability, not authority to render a binding merits judgment while constitutional power is assumed. CBE's reliance on the express jurisdictional wording does not erase this distinction.

Norton involved an appellate-route question when a contemporaneous decision already controlled the merits; Avrech concerned review of a court-martial after an intervening merits decision. Neither supplies a merits answer to this contested EPCRA question. The Court preserves their actual settings and does not claim an exhaustive order among every possible nonmerits threshold. It therefore determines redressability and leaves EPCRA's temporal reach undecided.

##### 2. None of the requested remedies redresses this completed reporting deprivation

**Rule and authority.** Assuming concrete past informational injury and traceability, a plaintiff lacks Article III standing when the requested relief neither repairs that deprivation nor prevents a supported continuing or imminent personal injury. Part II controls by the agreement of Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer; citizen-suit authorization and litigation expenses alone cannot supply the missing remedial connection.

Brown Group's authorization of representative enforcement preserves that requirement; Bennett likewise requires a supported link between pleaded injury and relief.

The reports were current before this suit. A declaration of their past lateness neither restores timely information nor resolves an alleged ongoing legal consequence. Treasury penalties do not compensate CBE or its members. Deterrence can prevent a threatened injury, but no continuing or imminent reporting violation is alleged. Annual duties alone do not supply that allegation. This is absence of standing at commencement, not cessation of a live controversy during suit.

Periodic facility or records access and future report copies likewise cannot repair the lost timeliness on these allegations. Section 11022(f)'s fire-department inspection right creates no automatic inspection entitlement for CBE. The scope of monitoring in a properly live action remains open.

A lawful claim compensating independent replacement-information expenses could present a different question; none is supplied. Which prefiling litigation expenses might be recoverable in a proper action is also left open.

### County of Sacramento v. Lewis, No. 96-1337

Plenary merits decision, May 26, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 98 F.3d 434. The case concerns the constitutional culpability required when a rapid police pursuit ends in an unintended fatal collision. After final summary judgment, the court of appeals reversed Deputy Smith's individual qualified-immunity judgment using a deliberate-indifference or reckless-disregard standard. Its affirmance of the municipal judgments and the unappealed state-law dispositions are outside this Court's review. No Fourth or Eighth Amendment claim is presented.

#### Judgment

**Reversed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The case returns for proceedings consistent with termination of the reviewed federal individual claim, leaving the district court's favorable individual judgment in effect. Six Justices adopt the Court's contextual constitutional ground; Scalia and Thomas rely on a distinct constitutional ground, and Stevens relies only on qualified immunity. No damages, trial, new evidentiary hearing, municipal retrial or state-law judgment is directed.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal | Complete rapid-pursuit culpability rule, application, qualifications and remedy |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | No other Justice | Supports reversal | Individual immunity because May 22, 1990 law did not make this conduct's federal unlawfulness sufficiently apparent; reserves constitutional merits |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Supports reversal | Distinct constitutional ground; no join in the Court's conscience-based purpose-to-harm standard |
| Concurrence | Breyer | No other Justice | Fully joins the Court and supports reversal | Preserves flexibility to decide an appropriate case on immunity before difficult constitutional merits |

#### Holdings

**Holding and operative rule:** In the rapidly evolving high-speed pursuit presented here, where officers must make instant choices amid competing enforcement and safety obligations, reckless or deliberately indifferent risk-taking alone does not establish a substantive-due-process violation. A purpose to cause harm unrelated to a legitimate law-enforcement objective is required; because this plaintiff-favorable record does not establish that purpose, the reviewed federal individual claim fails.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, supplies six direct votes for the complete rule and application. Neither the two distinct constitutional votes nor Stevens's immunity vote supplies additional support for that rule. No combination of separate rationales is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** Rochin identifies the constitutional boundary against arbitrary executive abuse; its conscience-shocking standard must be applied through precedent, constitutional tradition and the actual setting, not a judge's personal reaction. Daniels excludes negligence but left intermediate culpability unresolved. Whitley explains why competing obligations and an emergency matter to fault, while reserving the civilian due-process question. Farmer's actual-awareness standard concerns prison safety with a materially different opportunity to respond; Doe likewise requires knowledge, authority and a reasonable opportunity for protection against deliberate abuse. Those holdings do not mechanically determine culpability in a rapid pursuit.

The officer chose to pursue, and the favorable account includes dangerous speed, close following distance, danger to others, inadequate communication and alleged policy violations. The lack of a serious predicate offense and the ability to abandon pursuit are substantial criticisms. They do not, in this rapidly developing encounter, establish the improper harmful purpose required for constitutional liability. The collision was unintended, and the supported record supplies no purpose to injure unrelated to enforcement. The Court finds neither wise police judgment nor an adjudicated benevolent motive.

Actual opportunity for deliberation remains important. This rule is not a universal intent requirement, an automatic deliberate-indifference rule whenever an official could desist, or permission to manufacture an emergency. Purposeful collisions, deliberate terrorizing conduct, contrived encounters and materially different opportunities for considered action require their own facts and analysis. The decision creates no seconds-based cutoff, broad noncustodial immunity or mandatory order between constitutional merits and qualified immunity. It resolves neither the excluded municipal and state claims nor a procedural-due-process challenge to state remedies.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165:** Applied as the executive-abuse boundary, with the required culpability identified through the actual emergency setting.
- **Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327:** Its negligence exclusion remains; the Court resolves the previously open intermediate-culpability question only for this rapid pursuit.
- **Whitley v. Albers, 475 U.S. 312:** Its competing-obligation analysis informs the rule, but its prison-security holding is not treated as already deciding civilian pursuit liability.
- **Farmer v. Brennan, June 6, 1994:** Distinguished by the custodial duty and actual opportunity for response. Its awareness, reasonable-response and permissible-inference rules remain intact.
- **Doe v. Taylor Independent School District, October 16, 1995:** Distinguished because knowing supervisory acquiescence in deliberate abuse requires authority, opportunity and causal participation not supplied merely by continuing this pursuit.
- **Collins v. City of Harker Heights, February 26, 1992:** Its bounded workplace-omission holdings do not automatically resolve affirmative police conduct or merge the underlying right with municipal attribution.
- **Pinder v. Johnson, May 21, 1996:** Its distinction between nonprotection and affirmative deprivation remains; it did not decide this culpability question.
- **Uhlrig v. Harder, February 18, 1997:** Its conduct-time immunity disposition is preserved without importing the lower court's unadopted merits test or a mandatory merits-first sequence.
- **United States v. Lanier, March 31, 1997:** Its rejection of categorical immunity for noncustodial official wrongdoing remains. A governing specific constitutional provision must be used where it covers a claim; that principle does not itself dispose of every due-process claim.
- **Albright v. Oliver, January 24, 1994:** Its fractured judgment supplies no controlling general displacement rationale for this case.
- **Washington v. Glucksberg, June 26, 1997:** Its assistance-in-suicide holding remains limited; no exclusive most-specific-history methodology governing all liberties is established or displaced here.

#### Law After Decision

The decision establishes a purpose-to-harm requirement for the supported rapid-pursuit setting and rejects the circuit's use of reckless disregard or deliberate indifference alone there. Existing deliberate-indifference rules retain their distinct duties, opportunities for response and proof requirements. Constitutional wrongdoing remains separate from qualified immunity, municipal attribution and state-law liability. A purposeful or materially more deliberative encounter is not resolved merely by calling it a pursuit.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs only in the judgment. He would resolve the individual claim on qualified immunity without deciding the difficult constitutional standard. All relevant law existing on May 22, 1990 must be considered under the particularized-notice approach. Garner and Brower concern intentional force or intentionally applied stopping means; Checki concerns intentional misuse of police power to terrorize; Wood II concerns affirmative interference with protective circumstances. The reported pursuit decisions did not establish this claim's constitutional boundary merely by recognizing a possible egregious case, and Daniels reserved intermediate culpability. Neither the absence of an identical case nor a tally of judicial disagreement decides immunity. On the complete favorable account, the existing law did not make this pursuit's federal unlawfulness sufficiently apparent. Later decisions cannot supply retrospective notice. He makes no finding that the conduct was constitutional.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs in the judgment on a different constitutional ground. The carefully described asserted right against this reckless pursuit and unintended collision lacks sufficient textual, historical or precedential support. A general protection of life or bodily security does not by itself define the constitutional tort. They reject the Court's reliance on an open-ended conscience standard and do not join its affirmative purpose-to-harm definition, although they agree that these facts establish no constitutional violation. Glucksberg does not impose a universally exclusive history method; their objection rests on the absence of adequate support for this particular asserted right and on the limits of judicially defined constitutional tort liability. Their position does not categorically exclude direct intentional assaults outside custody.

Breyer fully joins the Court. He separately preserves flexibility to decide a suitable case on qualified immunity before resolving a difficult or poorly presented constitutional question. That approach is consistent with Uhlrig and the distinction between actual wrongdoing and conduct-time notice. Today's choice to resolve the pursuit standard is not a compulsory merits-first sequence for every later case.

The lower account does not establish that the riders were dangerous felons or that Smith had a purpose to injure. The immunity notice date is May 22, 1990.

The Ninth Circuit must give effect to reversal of its ruling against Smith and termination of the reviewed federal individual claim. The district court's favorable individual judgment remains in effect. The municipal judgments and unappealed state-law dispositions are not reopened, and no additional evidentiary proceeding or relief is ordered.

### United States v. Playboy Entertainment Group, Inc., No. 98-1682

Merits decision, May 22, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 30, 1999. Direct appeal from the three-judge District of Delaware's December 28, 1998 judgment, 30 F. Supp. 2d 702. The question is whether §505 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996's complete-scrambling or time-channeling requirement satisfies the First Amendment in regulating the protected adult programming at issue. The lower court declared §505 unconstitutional and enjoined enforcement; it did not reach equal protection or vagueness.

**Separate statutory commands.** Section 505, codified at 47 U.S.C. §561, applies when a multichannel video programming distributor provides sexually explicit adult or other indecent programming on a channel primarily dedicated to sexually oriented programming. Subsection (a) requires complete scrambling or blocking of the channel's audio and video so that a nonsubscriber to the channel or programming does not receive it. Until compliance, subsection (b) requires withholding during FCC-determined hours when significant numbers of children are likely to watch. The FCC permits transmission from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. Subsection (c) defines scrambling as signal rearrangement preventing understandable viewing or hearing; it creates no additional FCC compliance determination. The amendment took effect March 9, 1996. The Act's separate direct-review provision numbered §561 is not this codified section.

Section 504, codified at §560, requires a cable operator, upon a subscriber's request and without charge to that subscriber, to fully scramble or block each channel's audio and video so that a nonsubscriber does not receive it. It has no independent indecency or sexually oriented channel trigger. Subsection (b) supplies the same scrambling definition. This command took effect February 8, 1996. Subscriber-selected blocking and §505's default/time restriction are distinct; compliance with one does not automatically satisfy the other. Free service to the subscriber does not mean zero operator equipment or installation cost. Section 504 itself contains no notice program.

**Signal bleed and the proposed alternative.** The challenged nonobscene programming is protected for adults. Analog scrambling may leave understandable audio or pictures when a subscriber who has not purchased the premium service tunes to its scrambled channel; a child may tune without parental knowledge. Reception is not found to occur irrespective of channel selection. Traps used with the identified scrambling systems, channel-mapping converters and digital transmission can prevent bleed, but no universal device reliability or completed digital conversion is found.

The alternative is household-request blocking with adequate notice identifying the affected channels, understandable audio/video bleed, possible child exposure without parental knowledge, free blocking and the telephone request method. It includes repeated notice at reasonable intervals and special mailed notice to prior blocking recipients after the adult channel changes. Billing inserts, preview channels and advertisements elsewhere are examples, not cumulative compulsory media. No precise interval, installation deadline, repair protocol or automatic replacement rule is established.

The lower court found sufficient risk to susceptible minors notwithstanding weaknesses in exposure measurements and clinical inferences. Potential-exposure estimates do not measure actual exposed children; anecdotes establish incidents without a population rate. The Government's deposition-supported contentions concerning weeks-long fulfillment, device failure and channel changes remain material, but establish no universal failure or delay rate. Neither system is shown to protect every determined minor.

#### Holdings

##### I. Congress's content-selected cable restriction requires strict scrutiny

**Holding and operative rule:** A governmental restriction selecting protected adult cable programming by its sexual content must satisfy strict scrutiny: it must be narrowly tailored to a compelling interest, with the Government bearing the ultimate burden of justifying its means. When a concrete, plausibly effective and less speech-restrictive arrangement is presented, the Government must establish why its comparative inadequacy warrants the broader adult burden.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The law selects speech by content, not merely the technical operation of cable service. Protecting susceptible children, assisting parents and safeguarding privacy at home are substantial and compelling concerns in this setting; they do not themselves establish necessity. ACT/Pacifica requires independent review of means while preserving broadcasting's actual characteristics. Denver likewise subjects Congress's compulsory content restrictions to review despite distinct permissions for private editorial choice. Reno protects adult access and requires attention to the medium rather than automatic transfer of broadcast assumptions.

Turner's intermediate standard for content-neutral carriage does not govern this rule. Legislative predictions receive serious consideration, but the constitutional burden remains with the Government. Scientific certainty, quantified harm to every child and a perfect alternative are not required. Conversely, an alternative's mere existence on paper does not demonstrate practical effectiveness. The comparison must address the concrete household arrangement, the actual speech burden and supported implementation concerns. The Court does not convert the lower opinion's separate wording about Playboy demonstrating efficacy into an ultimate burden to prove that the broader governmental restraint is unnecessary.

##### II. The Government has not justified this adult burden against the noticed household alternative

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 505 cannot impose its complete-blocking or sixteen-hour time-channeling burden on the protected programming at issue because the Government has not established that household-request blocking with adequate notice is comparatively inadequate enough to justify that burden. This is a developed-record failure of necessity, not a finding that adequate notice already occurred, that household blocking is flawless, or that every narrower child-protection measure is invalid.

**Authority:** Part II of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven direct joins. Scalia and Breyer dissent from this application.

**Controlling explanation:** Time channeling substantially burdens willing adult audiences, including viewers who ordinarily watch during the restricted hours. An operator's formal ability to choose complete blocking does not restore that access where the practical choice is the timing restriction. The narrower channel trigger and retained nighttime period limit the burden but do not eliminate it.

The actual alternative combines complete household blocking with adequate recurring notice and channel-change notice. It is technically concrete and can protect concerned parents while leaving other adults' access intact. The record does not establish the comparative inadequacy needed to suppress protected programming for most of the day. Low device distribution during a period of unproved notice is not evidence that informed parents rejected protection or that a properly publicized arrangement failed.

The Government's strongest objection concerns children whose parents miss, misunderstand or disregard notice and households encountering delay, malfunction or changed channels. Those risks matter. The Court accepts the susceptible-minor risk finding and does not demand protection of every determined minor. But the supported activation difference and implementation concerns do not establish that this broader restraint is necessary. The cost calculations show real feasibility issues, not measured post-notice demand or inevitable failure; the Court assumes neither free operator installation nor guaranteed profitability.

ACT/Pacifica rejects an inadequately justified broad schedule without making all media identical. Denver identifies the practical burden of compulsory machinery rather than validating every household device. Here a completed trial permits final assessment; the Government receives no new opportunity to justify the same restraint. The conclusion rests on the burden of comparative justification, not on judicial creation of a notice regime, speculative digital conversion or denial that children can encounter harmful material. Obscenity and evidence-supported narrower measures remain outside the holding.

##### III. Affirmance preserves the injunction without creating a judicial notice program

**Holding and operative rule:** The declaration and injunction against §505 are affirmed at the adjudicated scope, while §504's distinct requested-blocking command remains effective. The affirmance creates no Supreme Court notice order, statutory publicity command or new authority over nonparty operators, and leaves unreviewed collateral lower relief only at its actual scope and procedural status.

**Authority:** Part III of Ginsburg's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven direct joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The constitutional failure concerns §505's broader restraint. It does not require abolition of household blocking, judicial selection of replacement hours or a new technical design. Targeted blocking, notice and improved scrambling remain available within lawful authority, without a ruling on every future arrangement.

The lower opinion states that it will require Playboy to secure notice through contractual arrangements and expressly lacks jurisdiction to command the nonparty operators directly. The record establishes no later formal implementation order, completed contract changes or delivered notices. The Court does not convert that announcement into an enacted §504 duty or adopt a new controlling notice-power rule. No independent challenge to that collateral direction is decided. Its actual lower-court scope and procedural status remain unaltered, and the constitutional holding does not depend on inventing notice authority.

The lower court's observation that inadequate notice would undermine the alternative does not create automatic restoration of §505 or a new enforcement procedure. The Court directs no new study or proof-repair remand and resolves neither equal protection nor vagueness. Ordinary obscenity enforcement and supported narrower regulation retain their lawful boundaries.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Action for Children's Television v. FCC / Pacifica Foundation v. FCC:** Applies independent review of necessity and the practical burden of a broad adult exclusion. Its broadcast-specific holding does not establish that every medium or every schedule is identical.
- **FCC v. Pacifica Foundation:** Preserves its confined contextual broadcast permission. It does not automatically sustain this cable restriction or withdraw protection from nonobscene adult programming.
- **Denver Area Educational Telecommunications Consortium, Inc. v. FCC / Alliance for Community Media v. FCC:** Applies the distinction between private editorial permission and Congress's integral compulsory restriction. Its invalidation of that scheme neither condemns every household-request tool nor validates the present timing burden.
- **Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union:** Applies protection of adult access and medium-specific scrutiny. It does not decide cable technology or create a finding that every parent receives effective notice.
- **Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC:** Preserves independent evidentiary review and the distinct intermediate standard for content-neutral carriage. Neither conclusive deference nor that intermediate test governs §505.
- **City of Erie v. Pap's A.M.:** Applies the distinction between communicative selection and genuinely general conduct regulation. The present content-directed command cannot receive incidental-burden treatment merely because it concerns distribution technology.

#### Law After Decision

Section 505's adjudicated restraint remains unenforceable under the affirmed injunction. Section 504 remains a distinct request-triggered blocking duty without a newly added statutory notice command. Government restrictions on protected adult programming must carry their actual constitutional burden against concrete less restrictive means; neither a compelling child-protection interest nor an untested assertion of parental inadequacy automatically justifies broad adult exclusion. No perfect-notice premise, universal device rule, new hours, future-conversion assumption or authority over nonparties is established. Obscenity and evidence-supported narrower measures remain outside this decision. No precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Writings

**Breyer, dissenting, joined by Scalia.** The dissent joins Part I's protected-speech premise and strict-scrutiny framework but would reverse the First Amendment invalidation and enforcement injunction. It finds the defined default-protection scheme sufficiently tailored to the actual channel-bleed problem. Complete audio/video blocking remains an alternative to time channeling, the trigger concerns channels primarily devoted to the covered programming, and the remaining nighttime period preserves an important opportunity for adult access.

The dissent accepts the significant loss of daytime viewing and the concrete alternative with adequate recurring notice. Its disagreement concerns comparative protection: a request system depends on parental receipt, understanding and action, and it leaves implementation problems involving delay, malfunction and channel changes. In the dissent's assessment the supported difference between that activation-dependent system and default protection is sufficiently important to carry the Government's burden. The Court gives too little weight to children whose parents do not activate the alternative.

ACT/Pacifica's dissenting approach to justified channeling and Denver's distinct default-protection reasoning support that conclusion without importing Turner's intermediate scrutiny. Actual operator costs and technological limits also matter, but the break-even scenarios are not request forecasts and low distribution does not establish parental indifference. Neither universally failing devices, scientific certainty, perfect protection, cost-free installation nor inevitable digital conversion is assumed.

The dissent would remand for lawful disposition of properly preserved grounds not reached below, deciding equal protection and vagueness neither way and prescribing no interim relief. Section 504 would remain separate; reversal would create no statutory notice duty or technical-compliance finding. The dissent treats the challenged nonobscene programming as protected expression throughout its analysis.
