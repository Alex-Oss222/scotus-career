## Standards and Tests selection: Initiative circulator qualifications and individual identification

### Initiative circulator qualifications and individual identification

**Governing question:** Speech burdens imposed on initiative circulators by registered-voter, personal-name and individual-payee requirements.

**Current rule:** Excluding otherwise qualified resident-adult initiative circulators solely because they are unregistered voters substantially burdens protected petition conversation; ease of registration does not remove the burden. Traceability justifications must address retained affidavits, signature review, process and targeted fraud enforcement rather than treating voter registration as a necessary speech qualification. A requirement to display the circulator’s personal name during face-to-face advocacy must be justified at that immediate expressive encounter; materially less burdensome post-circulation identification and enforcement matter to fit.

Compelled publication of each paid circulator’s identity, addresses and individual compensation in final or monthly reports requires a substantial relationship to important informational or enforcement interests. The messenger is not automatically a financing source or candidate contributor. Sponsor identity and aggregate spending disclosures retain a distinct financing function. Timing may matter independently, but disclosure during circulation does not cure an insufficient relationship between each messenger’s identity and the asserted interest.

Final individual-circulator reports; III-B. Monthly individual-circulator reports, 1999-01-12; six direct joins.

**Present operation effective:** 1999-01-12.

**Exceptions and limits:** These holdings concern Colorado’s reviewed restrictions and their supported burdens and alternatives. Residence, age and circulation-period requirements, standalone paid-status or employer attribution, and independent monthly-report fields are not adjudicated. No categorical initiative-finance disclosure exemption or anonymity from lawful investigation follows; materially narrower reporting requires its own justification.

## Standards and Tests selection: Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

### Write-in voting within the complete ballot-access system

**Governing question:** Whether a ban on write-in voting violates electoral-choice or independent expressive rights.

**Current rule:** Under the existing Anderson inquiry, the court assesses the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise interests and the extent to which those interests require the burden. Severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. A write-in prohibition is assessed within the complete access system by what the prohibition itself contributes to the asserted interests; the independent validity of surrounding filing, support and primary rules does not make it presumptively valid. When ordinary nomination routes close before voters can respond, the prohibition must leave an effective route for a genuine, legally eligible alternative candidate. The ballot need not provide an official count and publication of votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent expressive entitlement.

**Authority by component:**
- Governing inherited framework: Burdick v. Takushi, “1. Review of election regulations follows the actual burden”, June 8, 1992; Stone, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter (five), applying Anderson and the earlier simulated Norman rule.
- Complete-system assessment and effective-route requirement: Burdick, “2. Hawaii’s complete write-in prohibition leaves no effective route for a genuine alternative candidate,” June 8, 1992; same five Justices.
- Arbitrary-message claim: Burdick, “3. The ballot need not serve as an official tally of arbitrary political messages,” June 8, 1992; same controlling five-Justice opinion, with all nine agreeing in rejection of that component.

**Present operation effective:** Burdick v. Takushi, June 8, 1992, for the complete-system assessment, effective-route requirement and expressive boundary; the underlying Anderson framework is applied unchanged.

**Exceptions and limits:** No absolute write-in right follows, and no particular route, counting mechanism or ballot form is required. Valid qualifications, narrow anti-raiding and sore-loser rules and materially different systems remain open. The rule does not replace strict scrutiny of content-based campaign restrictions on public sidewalks.

**Holdings navigation:** Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992) — Ballot access and political parties.

## Holdings

### A genuine support petition is not an added personal qualification

**Holding and operative rule:** A genuine requirement to demonstrate electoral support regulates ballot access rather than adding a personal qualification for congressional membership, while remaining subject to independent First and Fourteenth Amendment review. Georgia's pleaded petition requirement measures support rather than age, status, prior service or another personal characteristic, so the Article I theory fails.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by all eight other Justices; nine direct votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the rule's operation, not its ballot label, determines its classification. Thornton/Bryant prohibits States from adding personal congressional qualifications indirectly through ballot restrictions while distinguishing ordinary administration. Cook rejects ballot handicaps designed to penalize a candidate's refusal of a state-selected legislative position. This petition requires a showing of support and imposes neither kind of personal or policy penalty. Its procedural classification leaves the asserted associational burden to separate review.

### Anderson review has three levels, and these cumulative allegations require factual development

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Anderson–Burdick, courts weigh the character and magnitude of the actual burden against the State's precise interests and the need for its restrictions, and must explain where the burden falls on that calibrated scale. Severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests; substantial burdens require an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship between that objective and the restriction's actual reach, without proof that success is impossible; reasonable nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests. Accepting the complaint's facts, the alleged combination of a registered-voter denominator fixed by the preceding election, district identification after reapportionment, surplus-signature and sheet-affidavit verification burdens and the absence of any Libertarian congressional petition qualification since 1943 plausibly states a substantial or severe burden that Jenness does not answer as a matter of law, so the access claim requires factual development.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct votes control this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the complete system remains the relevant unit of analysis and that the calibrated scale Anderson describes has three levels. Burdick, in an opinion and a concurrence that each called Hawaii's complete write-in ban a substantial burden on electoral choice, required the State's interests to justify the ban's full reach; Timmons held Minnesota's fusion ban a substantial but not severe burden that conjecture could not sustain; Wood rejected the suggestion that fit ceases to matter once a burden is called reasonable. A severe burden requires narrow tailoring to a compelling interest. A substantial burden requires an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship between that objective and the restriction's actual operation; it need not make success impossible. An ordinary burden imposed by reasonable, nondiscriminatory administration may rest on important regulatory interests without proof of past disorder. A court must explain its placement, and a valid interest does not by itself establish a sufficient relationship. These levels describe Anderson's single scale, not a new tier.

Jenness sustained a five-percent showing within Georgia's open system, not a percentage exempt from later scrutiny. The complaint alleges that the requirement is measured against registered voters eligible in the preceding election, that reapportionment made district-specific signers difficult to identify, that verification losses force surplus collection under sheet affidavits, and that no Libertarian congressional candidate has qualified by petition since 1943. Accepted as true, those allegations plausibly describe at least a substantial burden, and listing formal opportunities does not answer them. The six-month period, multiple signing and primary participation favor Georgia and remain part of the assessment; party-specific failure alone does not prove severity. The dissent would treat these as familiar costs within the system Jenness sustained, but that conclusion requires facts the pleading does not supply: whether candidates with genuine support can reasonably qualify, and whether Georgia's interests in a modicum of support and reliable verification require the requirement's actual reach. On remand the district court places the burden and applies the corresponding justification. The holding does not independently resolve the statewide one-percent rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton / Bryant v. Hill (May 22, 1995) is applied to distinguish genuine support procedures from personal congressional qualifications.
- Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510, retains its prohibition on official policy-compliance penalties; this support petition imposes a different kind of condition.
- Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431, is reaffirmed at the scope of the complete Georgia system it sustained; it does not answer as a matter of law the materially different combined burdens pleaded here.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428, and Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party (April 28, 1997) retain the calibrated burden, precise-interest and fit framework; with Wood v. Meadows (June 11, 2001), they supply the three levels this decision states as that framework's structure, without creating a new tier or substituting rational-basis review.

## Law After Decision

Congressional support petitions remain distinct from personal qualifications and remain subject to meaningful access review. Courts applying Anderson–Burdick place a burden at one of three levels of its calibrated scale and explain that placement: severe burdens require narrow tailoring to a compelling interest; substantial burdens require an important objective and a demonstrated substantial relationship, without proof that success is impossible; reasonable, nondiscriminatory administration may rest on important regulatory interests. A petition percentage sustained within one open system does not answer as a matter of law a pleaded claim that a later system's combined operation imposes a substantial or severe burden, and Georgia's congressional access claim proceeds to factual development.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela joins the Court's opinion in full and concurs. He would treat valid support equal to one percent of the votes cast at the last comparable election, or 10,000 valid signatures for a statewide office where that number is smaller, as a rebuttable presumption of meaningful support, severable from the Court's holding; Georgia's registered-voter denominator is a different measure, and Norman's and Rednour's requirements, sustained on their records, are not reopened. If an unjustified common barrier is proved, he would require a workable lawful nomination route tied to that barrier and the real election timetable, presuming relief for governed candidates and voters unless a narrower complete cure is demonstrated, beginning with a state proposal and proceeding to a fitted judicial order if necessary.

Thomas, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Kennedy, agrees on qualifications but would affirm dismissal of the access claim. Jenness's system-specific holding answers allegations of familiar identification, verification and surplus costs within the same open six-month, broad-signer system, and the accepted absence of Libertarian qualification does not itself identify a changed barrier. Because the allegations fail under any formulation of Anderson's calibrated review, the dissent does not reach Part II's statement of levels.

## Procedure After Action

The mandate leaves dismissal of the qualification claim in force and returns the access claim to the district court for factual development under Part II. The challenged congressional petition requirements remain operative while that claim proceeds.

## Additional opening-law selection

#### Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428 (1992)

**Docket or dockets:** No. 91-535.
**Decided:** June 8, 1992.

##### What level of constitutional scrutiny applies to a restriction on voting or candidate access

**Holding:** A court must weigh the character and magnitude of the injury to First and Fourteenth Amendment rights against the State's precise asserted interests and the extent to which they require the burden. Severe restrictions must be narrowly drawn to advance a compelling interest; reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions ordinarily are justified by important regulatory interests, so strict scrutiny does not govern every election rule.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone's opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; five Justices control the complete rule. White's dissent, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, also accepts the calibrated framework without making its contrary application controlling.

**Material application:** A write-in prohibition must be examined with the available routes to ballot access and its role in the selection system. Neither the prohibition alone nor the general need for election administration decides the burden.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Ordinary administration remains subject to meaningful scrutiny. The holding neither imposes strict scrutiny on all ballot regulations nor extends *Burson*'s public-sidewalk campaign-content holding to the official ballot.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, supplies the burden, precise-interest and necessity inquiry. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, preserves actual access review and permissible electorate-specific support requirements, including its protection against cross-component exclusion. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, recognizes substantial regulation of candidate selection while preserving review of severe barriers. *Burson v. Freeman*, May 26, 1992, concerns ordinary public sidewalks; neither its strict-scrutiny proposition nor its fractured judgment changes this ballot analysis.

##### Whether Hawaii's complete write-in prohibition is valid within the ballot-access system reviewed

**Holding:** When a State closes its ordinary nomination routes before voters can respond, it must leave an effective route by which voters may support a genuine, legally eligible alternative candidate; a reasonable registered write-in procedure is one such route, and the State may require eligibility, the candidate's consent and declaration, and a reasonable registration date. Hawaii's complete prohibition leaves no such route within its reviewed system, and its interests in primary finality, sore-loser prevention, party raiding and uncontested primaries do not justify that full reach. The prohibition is invalid as applied to genuine eligible candidates.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone's opinion of the Court, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter; five Justices support both the rationale and reversal. White, O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas disagree with this rule and its application and would affirm.

**Current force:** Controls write-in prohibitions that operate after ordinary nomination routes close, as applied to genuine, legally eligible alternative candidates.

**Material application:** New parties need one percent of registered-voter signatures 150 days before the primary; qualifying established parties retain access. Candidates need 15 signatures for legislative or county offices, or 25 for statewide or federal offices, 60 days before the primary. Nonpartisan candidates advance with ten percent of the primary vote or the votes sufficient to nominate a partisan candidate, whichever is lower; voters choose one party or nonpartisan primary ballot for all offices. The small filing requirements favor Hawaii, but every ordinary route closes before the primary, and the all-office ballot choice and advancement conditions govern how an alternative reaches the deciding election. Burdick faced an actual single-candidate contest. Sore-loser prevention cannot explain a primary ban, anti-raiding interests do not justify excluding genuine support for a candidate on the selected primary ticket, and an uncontested-winner rule leaves no general-election ballot to protect.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Both primary and general-election applications within this system are reached. The Court does not independently invalidate any surrounding access rule or prescribe statewide filing, certification or counting machinery or a ballot form. Valid candidate qualifications, narrow anti-raiding and sore-loser restrictions, the design of a qualifying route and other States' materially different systems remain open; no absolute write-in right, guarantee of competitive races or entitlement to every late candidacy is created, and no new scrutiny tier is adopted.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reverse the Ninth Circuit and remand for prospective relief confined to votes for legally eligible, genuine alternative candidates. Hawaii chooses the qualifying route, and a reasonable registered write-in procedure suffices. This Court does not itself reinstate the district court's injunction; no special election, reopening of completed elections, or prescribed counting mechanism or ballot form issues.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, requires examining the prohibition's own contribution to the asserted interests; the independent validity of surrounding rules supplies no presumption. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supports primary finality and sore-loser prevention but not this ban's full reach. *Jenness v. Fortson*, 403 U.S. 431, permits reasonable demonstrations of support. *Munro v. Socialist Workers Party*, 479 U.S. 189, excuses proof of past disruption, not a showing of fit. *Rosario v. Rockefeller*, 410 U.S. 752, permits timely measures against raiding. *Lubin v. Panish*, 415 U.S. 709, is consistent: a write-in option is neither an automatic cure nor the only qualifying route. *Norman v. Reed*, January 14, 1992, retains protection against unjustified party-name and cross-component disqualification.

##### Whether the Constitution requires the State to count and publish fictional candidates or arbitrary protest messages

**Holding:** The First and Fourteenth Amendments do not require a State to count and publicize write-in votes for fictional figures or arbitrary protest messages as an independent form of expression. Elections select public officials, and a desire to use the ballot for such messages does not create an entitlement to a write-in option.

**Proposition-level authority:** Stone's opinion, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy and Souter, controls. White's dissent, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, expressly agrees on rejection of this broader claim; all nine support the component without issuing a joint opinion.

**Limits and questions not reached:** A genuine interest in supporting an actual alternative candidate is protected by the effective-route holding separately stated above, and relief is confined to that interest. Political expression outside the ballot is not decided.

**Operative remedy or transition:** Reject the arbitrary-message tally claim; it supplies no independent ground for relief, and relief on the electoral-choice claim does not extend to votes for fictional figures or protest messages.

**Treatment of earlier authority:** *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, supplies the office-selecting function of elections; the ballot is not a general expressive forum. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, protects electoral participation without requiring an arbitrary-message tally.

## Holdings

### District residence cannot exclude these otherwise qualified petition witnesses

**Holding and operative rule:** New York violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments by excluding otherwise qualified in-state petition witnesses solely because they reside outside the candidate's electoral district. Under Anderson–Burdick, plaintiffs must establish the character and magnitude of the burden; this direct exclusion severely burdens protected petition circulation, and the State must show narrow tailoring to a compelling interest, which the asserted accountability, fraud-prevention, local-support and administrative interests do not establish for this condition.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These eight directly support the method, application and condition-specific facial relief. Thomas supports the same judgment on his separate traditional strict-scrutiny ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Meyer protects petition circulation as interactive political expression. Buckley applies that protection to the exclusion of otherwise qualified speakers and tests traceability against retained enforcement tools. Anderson and Burdick provide the governing inquiry: examine the actual burden in the complete system and the precise interests said to require it. That inquiry preserves fitted election administration while demanding strict scrutiny here, where a district boundary directly prevents a willing speaker from performing the witnessing activity.

Lerman's fifty-eight signatures were rejected solely for her address, although the candidate needed thirty-eight. The availability of other speech and professional witnesses does not restore her excluded petition activity. Qualified district signers establish local electoral support; the witness need not live among them to attest their signatures. Statewide subpoenas and supplied addresses already make this in-state witness accountable. The notary and commissioner exception further undercuts the claimed necessity of district residence, although an exception alone would not decide constitutionality. The candidate context does not turn this expressive activity into mere ballot mechanics. The support requirement reviewed in Cartwright concerns a different function.

These defects operate across the challenged condition, supporting relief against that separable exclusion rather than only these fifty-eight signatures. The Court extends Buckley's circulation reasoning to this geographic exclusion.

## Precedent Treatment

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, is applied for the actual-burden, precise-interest and necessity inquiry.
- Burdick v. Takushi (June 8, 1992) is applied for graduated scrutiny; severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests.
- Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, is applied to petition circulation as protected interactive political speech.
- Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation (January 12, 1999) is extended from its reviewed speaker exclusions to this unsupported district-residence condition.
- Cartwright v. Barnes (February 25, 2003) is distinguished: local-support mechanics concern signers' demonstrated electoral support, not exclusion of an otherwise qualified witnessing speaker.

## Law After Decision

Otherwise qualified in-state witnesses may participate across electoral-district boundaries. The invalid condition may not be enforced directly or replaced by a nominally different rule producing the same unsupported district exclusion.

## Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** A direct restriction on political petition speech receives traditional strict scrutiny. His Buckley concurrence supplies that method; candidate petitioning remains protected communication even though the State may regulate genuine support mechanics. Witness residence does not measure local electoral support, which qualified signers provide, and statewide process and witness addresses secure accountability. The professional-witness alternative does not restore the excluded speakers' participation. He therefore agrees with facial invalidation and the same remedy, while withholding assent to a general method that calibrates scrutiny through an administrative burden balance.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's constitutional holding and facial remedy. He would additionally presume relief against an established common eligibility barrier for the affected group unless the election defendants prove that a narrower order fully cures the violation. The presumption depends on a live claim and the proved barrier and reaches only the actual defendants and injuries within the court's jurisdiction; it does not assume class certification. The decree should identify the forbidden condition and require conforming instructions and review criteria before it is used again. Rejections resting only on district residence must be withdrawn under the decree, while genuine signature defects receive separate adjudication.
