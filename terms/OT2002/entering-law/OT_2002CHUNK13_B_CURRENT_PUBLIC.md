## Event

Gibson v. County of Washoe, 290 F.3d 1175. Merits decision, January 14, 2003. On review of the Ninth Circuit's May 22, 2002 judgment reversing summary judgment for the County on supported mental-health policy claims and affirming judgment for the named deputies and supervising sergeant. The question is whether a County policy may cause an actionable denial of a detainee's serious medical needs despite the named officers' lack of the subjective awareness required for personal liability.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

## Public Action

Affirmed, 9–0; the supported County screening/evaluation and medication-policy claims return for ordinary further proceedings, with the remaining dispositions preserved.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The supported County claims concerning screening/evaluation and medication handling return for ordinary further proceedings, including trial if the remaining procedural requirements are satisfied. Summary judgment for the named deputies and sergeant, rejection of the unsupported communications-policy theory, and the unappealed consortium and state-law dispositions remain intact.

## Opinion Topology

Stevens delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer joined. The opinion and judgment are unanimous.

## Holdings

### The municipality's own policy may cause a constitutional deprivation

**Holding and operative rule:** A municipality may be liable under §1983 for its own deliberately indifferent custodial policy that actually causes a denial of constitutionally required medical care, even when the named employees lack the personal awareness required for their liability. The claimant must prove an actual constitutional deprivation, an attributable municipal policy or legally effective custom, a deliberate municipal choice concerning a known or obvious constitutional need, and an identified defect closely related to and actually causing the injury; negligence, employment alone and pooled employee knowledge do not suffice.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court, authored by Stevens and joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer, controls the rule, the summary-judgment application and the limited return for further proceedings.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a named employee's exoneration does not collapse the separate inquiries into the deprivation and its municipal source. Monell requires the municipality's own causal policy or custom. Collins separates the underlying constitutional wrong from attribution; the absence of a custody-based duty in its voluntary-employment setting does not govern a pretrial detainee's custody. Canton requires deliberate municipal choice and a close causal connection. Carter permits notice through a pattern or a sufficiently obvious need to perform a clear constitutional duty in known recurring circumstances; identical prior injuries are not invariably necessary.

The County correctly insists on an actual deprivation and more than negligent administration. But the named deputies' lack of relevant awareness establishes neither that all required care was furnished nor that a County policy could not disable that care. Farmer's individual-awareness rule does not permit combining employees' innocent information to manufacture personal culpability. The municipal claim instead must identify and prove the County's own deliberately indifferent policy and its injury-producing operation. This preserves the underlying constitutional-injury requirement rather than substituting institutional responsibility for it.

### The supported screening and medication evidence permits further proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** At summary judgment, a claimant must identify evidence permitting a reasonable finding on every required municipal-liability element. The evidence concerning delayed mental-health evaluation and handling of psychotropic medication meets that standard here; the surviving claims return under the ordinary preponderance-of-the-evidence burden, while the unsupported communications theory and the preserved personal and unappealed dispositions remain excluded.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the evidence, viewed under Rule 56, supports a distinct policy inquiry. The jail regularly held mentally ill detainees, policymakers knew of that recurring population, and the evidence concerns practices postponing evaluation until an acutely disturbed person cooperated and treating relevant medication as property rather than a signal for assessment. General knowledge of mental illness alone would not suffice. These particular practices, combined with the recurring constitutional responsibility and medication evidence, permit a finding beyond hindsight that more training might have helped.

The County remains entitled to contest policy authority or effective custom, deliberate choice, deprivation and actual cause. Death during restraint does not itself establish a medical-care violation or that a different policy would have prevented the injury. The reasonable-force and personal-awareness rulings therefore survive, but they do not decide the independent medical-policy claim. The communications theory lacks the necessary policymaker-notice support and cannot survive merely by association with the supported policies. On remand, the family must prove the surviving elements and any compensable injury; ordinary factual and remedial adjudication under those rules does not make the municipal standard unresolved.

## Precedent Treatment

- **Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658:** Applies the municipality's own policy-or-custom and causation requirements; employer-based liability remains forbidden.
- **Collins v. City of Harker Heights, 503 U.S. 115:** Applies the separate inquiries into constitutional deprivation and municipal attribution; distinguishes voluntary employment from actual pretrial custody.
- **City of Canton v. Harris, 489 U.S. 378:** Applies demanding municipal fault, notice, identified deficiency and causal connection; no negligence or hindsight standard replaces them.
- **Carter v. City of Philadelphia, March 13, 2000:** Applies its pattern-or-obvious-recurring-need notice rule and actual-cause requirement without adding an invariably identical-prior-incident condition.
- **Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825:** Preserves individualized actual awareness and the protection for a reasonable response; neither collective awareness nor a failed reasonable response supplies personal culpability. The distinct municipal inquiry does not alter that rule.

## Law After Decision

Existing municipal and custodial law governs. A municipality cannot obtain judgment automatically from the named employees' lack of personal awareness when supported evidence independently connects an actual medical-care deprivation to its own deliberately indifferent policy. The same demanding deprivation, attribution, fault and causation requirements control analogous custodial policy claims. The decision supplies neither institutional strict liability nor a new aggregate-awareness test. Section 3626's requirements for prospective prison-conditions relief remain unchanged.

## Event

In re Bridgestone/Firestone, Inc., Tires Products Liability Litigation. Merits decision, November 5, 2002, on review of the Seventh Circuit's May 2, 2002 judgment, 288 F.3d 1012, with rehearing material dated June 5, 2002. That court exercised Rule 23(f) review and reversed certification of two nationwide economic-loss classes concerning Ford Explorers and Firestone tires. The question is whether those classes satisfy Rule 23(b)(3) despite material differences in governing state law, products and proof. Purchasers seek restoration of the certifications; Ford and Firestone seek to retain decertification.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Seventh Circuit judgment decertifying the two nationwide classes is affirmed on the demonstrated failure to satisfy Rule 23.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The two nationwide certifications remain reversed. The underlying claims return for individual proceedings or consideration of a properly supported narrower certification proposal under all applicable requirements.

## Opinion Topology

Ginsburg delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer joined. The complete-opinion coalition is Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Every Justice joins the entire opinion. No separate writing is filed.

## Holdings

### The proposed nationwide classes lack a common legal and evidentiary basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Before certification under Rule 23(b)(3), proponents must demonstrate from the actual governing law, material claims and defenses, and anticipated proof that common questions predominate and class treatment is superior, while satisfying Rule 23(a). These two nationwide litigation classes fail because material state-law and product differences remain unaccounted for by a workable common adjudication; multistate variation itself is not a categorical prohibition.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court; the complete-opinion coalition adopts the certification rule, application, qualifications and affirmance.

**Controlling explanation:** Castano requires the legal and evidentiary showing before certification; a common description of manufacturer conduct cannot replace it. Rhone-Poulenc requires actual state law and genuine issue separability, protecting substantive defenses and each jury's assigned work. Here warranty and consumer-protection law differ on actionable loss, manifestation, reliance, notice and relief. Designs, production years, uses and transactions also differ in ways affecting proof. Allegations of unsafe design and common marketing do not show which product breached which duty or which purchaser suffered the loss that the applicable law recognizes.

The appropriate choice-of-law rules govern the constituent actions. Consolidation does not create national warranty law or permit selection of a convenient State's law for materially disconnected claims. The proposed efficiency depends on suppressing rather than resolving those differences, defeating both the demonstrated predominance and superiority of this arrangement. Amchem preserves the independent certification requirements; its permission to omit trial-management analysis for settlement-only classes does not apply here.

Purchasers correctly observe that economic injury can precede physical failure under some substantive rules and that manageable groups may be possible. Those observations do not demonstrate the required law and proof for these classes. Compensable injury from an unfailed product depends on its governing law and evidence, not a universal injury or no-injury assumption. Materially equivalent rules, workable state-law or product groupings, or genuinely separable issues may support a properly presented narrower proposal under the applicable requirements. Hypothetical subclasses supply no present cure. Class size, novelty and ordinary settlement pressure add no categorical ground for rejection.

**Scope:** Decertification resolves the representative device. Individual claims and a supported new proposal remain for ordinary adjudication. The full relationship between action-wide predominance and Rule 23(c)(4) issue certification remains outside this ruling because no developed alternative issue proceeding is presented.

## Precedent Treatment

- Castano v. American Tobacco Co.: applies its actual-law-and-proof requirement and preservation of materially supported narrower proposals; no tort-maturation, size or novelty bar is added.
- In re Rhone-Poulenc Rorer: applies its governing-law, substantive-defense and jury-separability safeguards; distinguishes the extraordinary-writ posture because Rule 23(f) supplies review here.
- Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor: applies its independent certification requirements; distinguishes the settlement-only permission to omit trial-management analysis.
- Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts: applies the restriction on imposing one State's substantive law on materially disconnected claims, without turning it into a universal single-law certification rule.
- Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Manufacturing Co.: preserves the applicable choice-of-law rules for the constituent actions; consolidation supplies no substitute national rule.

## Law After Decision

These nationwide economic-loss certifications cannot proceed on a common defect narrative that bypasses materially different claims, defenses, products and proof. Courts must examine an actual workable arrangement before certifying comparable classes. The decision applies existing certification law without categorically barring multistate classes or deciding the independent scope of issue certification. Individual economic injury remains governed by the applicable substantive law.

The trial court retains ordinary responsibility for the individual claims and any properly presented narrower proposal, which must establish its own compliance with Rule 23, applicable substantive law and lawful trial arrangements.

# Source Los-Angeles-v-David_merits_2003-05-19.md — effective 2003-05-19

Canonical source: `terms/OT2002/records/Los-Angeles-v-David_merits_2003-05-19.md`.

## Event

City of Los Angeles v. David, No. 02-1212. Summary merits decision, May 19, 2003. On review of the Ninth Circuit's October 9, 2002 judgment, 307 F.3d 1143, reversing summary judgment for the City on the timing component of David's §1983 action. The question is whether the twenty-seven-day interval before a hearing on repayment of towing and storage fees violated due process after David recovered his car. He paid $134.50 and requested review on August 13, 1998; the hearing occurred September 9 and repayment was denied. The court of appeals rejected his other procedural claims. The City seeks judgment on the timing claim; David seeks to preserve the claim and earlier-review requirement.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate.

## Public Action

The timing judgment is reversed and the case remanded, 8–1.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded on the timing claim, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela.

The twenty-seven-day-delay claim is resolved in the City's favor. The rejection of David's other procedural claims remains effective.

## Opinion Topology

Per curiam, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela files a dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

### I. The twenty-seven-day interval satisfies due process on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** After David paid $134.50 and recovered his automobile, the twenty-seven-day interval before review of repayment satisfied due process on this record. The timing inquiry weighs the actual continuing private loss, the risk of error and value of earlier review, and the government's practical reasons for the interval; a timetable directed to continued vehicle detention does not automatically govern a completed-payment claim.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Those eight directly adopt the complete rule and explanation; Stone-Zsela dissents from its application and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews v. Eldridge requires attention to the deprivation that actually continues. FDIC v. Mallen makes the duration and reasons for delay part of that inquiry. Gilbert v. Homar requires a separate justification for continued deprivation even when the original action was lawful. David had regained his car; the remaining loss was use of the disputed money. That interest remains protected, but it differs in practical consequence from inability to use an automobile. The record identifies no added evidentiary impairment attributable to these twenty-seven days.

Arranging hearing space, personnel and the possible attendance of the towing officer requires coordination. The City's reported annual volume and expedited hearings for owners unable to obtain release give context to that work. The officer ultimately could not attend; David declined both opportunities to continue the hearing, and the examiner considered the officer's report and David's explanation. The decision does not depend on treating the officer as present or the City's operational assertions as universal findings.

The contrary view demands a more particular scheduling account before summary judgment. Here the known continuing loss, completed hearing and absence of supported additional error risk permit the balance to be resolved without a further search for an unshown material timing dispute. Potential interest compensation illustrates the financial character of the loss; it does not automatically cure untimely process. Newman v. Sathyavaglswaran, involving an irreversible deprivation on undeveloped pleadings, preserves the contextual inquiry but does not require another remand here. Barry v. Barchi's demand for timely effective review is satisfied on these facts. The holding establishes no fixed constitutional safe harbor for other delays and leaves California's separate forty-eight-hour command distinct from the federal standard.

**Precedent treatment:** Mathews supplies the balance; Mallen supplies delay-specific scrutiny; Gilbert preserves separate review of continuing deprivation; Barry supplies the meaningful-time requirement. Newman is distinguished by the irreversible interest and pleading posture. City of West Covina v. Perkins addresses notice and access, not a timing exemption. Stypmann v. City & County of San Francisco and Goichman v. Rheuban Motors do not establish a compulsory forty-eight-hour or five-day federal deadline for this returned-car claim.

## Precedent Treatment

- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319: applies the actual-interest, error-risk and governmental-burden balance.
- FDIC v. Mallen, 486 U.S. 230: applies scrutiny of delay and its reasons, without transferring its timetable to impound hearings.
- Gilbert v. Homar: applies independent review of continuing deprivation after justified initial action.
- Barry v. Barchi, 443 U.S. 55: preserves the requirement of prompt, effective postdeprivation review.
- Newman v. Sathyavaglswaran, January 15, 2003: preserves contextual scrutiny and distinguishes its irreversible loss and pleading posture.
- City of West Covina v. Perkins: preserves its notice-and-access holding; it supplies no exemption from timing review.
- Stypmann and Goichman: their vehicle-related process analysis does not impose the lower court's categorical short timetable on this monetary deprivation.

## Law After Decision

A returned-vehicle fee-review claim receives a contextual timing inquiry directed to the money retained and the process actually provided. This twenty-seven-day interval is constitutional on its record. Continuing vehicle detention, demonstrated additional injury or impaired review, and materially different intervals require application of that same contextual rule to their own facts. California's separate hearing command retains its own legal operation.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** The Chief Justice would affirm reversal of summary judgment for the City while rejecting a universal forty-eight-hour or five-day constitutional requirement. Mathews, Mallen, Gilbert and Barry require a practical review-related explanation for material delay. Recovery of the automobile changes the interest but leaves the compulsory payment in the City's hands. He finds annual workload and general coordination needs insufficient to establish that this entire interval was justified at summary judgment; he would require the City to connect supported scheduling, priority and staffing needs to the elapsed period and feasible earlier process.

He would remand to determine those reasons, the actual deprivation, error risk and value of earlier review. If a City-attributable timing violation and causal injury were proved by a preponderance, the court would determine compensation, with nominal relief where warranted if compensable loss was not proved. A late hearing would not itself require refunding a lawfully imposed fee. Prospective relief would require a live threat and a proved scheduling defect, with a defined correction and means of demonstrating compliance. The later hearing and adverse repayment result would not decide those questions. His rule would govern still-open direct-review claims under Harper, with Reynoldsville foreclosing reliance alone as a reason to withhold it; ordinary immunity, damages and finality rules would remain. He would sustain a delay conclusively shown substantially necessary for meaningful review when earlier process offered no material protection, but does not find that showing established here.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit to conform its judgment on the timing claim to the Court's determination. The separate adverse dispositions of David's other procedural challenges remain in force.
