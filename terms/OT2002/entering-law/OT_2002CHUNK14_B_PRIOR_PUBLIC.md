### 44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island, No. 94-1140


Source: `terms/OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK6.md`


#### Opinion Topology

Justice Kennedy announces the judgment. His opinion has the following Court and noncontrolling portions; all listed joiners are in addition to him.

Justice O'Connor concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by Justice Breyer. Justices Scalia and Thomas each concur in part and in the judgment separately. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in the constitutional judgment and specified Court portions, but dissents from the remedy insofar as it omits his injunction directive. No majority adopts Parts IV-A–C or Thomas's categorical rule. The direct Court coalitions require no Marks synthesis.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy's noncontrolling Parts IV-A–C.** In IV-A, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, he emphasizes the consumer's interest in accurate commercial information and the special care appropriate when the State suppresses the entire relevant category to influence lawful purchases. That view is distinct from a rule invalidating every such restriction categorically. In IV-B, joined by Stevens and Ginsburg, he would apply more rigorous review to complete truthful-speech suppression unrelated to protecting consumers from deception or comparable injury. It is not announced as universal strict scrutiny for commercial speech. These Justices also adopt the narrower independently sufficient applications in I and II; Stevens's informational objection is not reduced to insufficient evidence alone.

In IV-C, joined by Stevens, Thomas and Ginsburg, Kennedy rejects Posadas's highly deferential permission to choose informational suppression for paternalistic ends, the inference that power to prohibit a product necessarily includes power to suppress truthful promotion, and a freestanding lawful-vice exception. This does not repudiate every Posadas holding. Thomas's join in this treatment does not add a balancing vote, and Souter does not join it. None of these portions commands five votes.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Breyer.** They join II, III-A and V-A–B. Ordinary Central Hudson/Fox review suffices, with temperance substantial and advancement assumed. Economic prediction and expert concessions deserve genuine weight; they do not decide that the State necessarily fails the advancement prong. The permanent burden on all covered truthful price references nevertheless lacks proportional fit in light of concrete direct controls. Their prior support for supported prophylaxis and finite privacy restrictions does not validate this different burden. They require neither definitive empirical proof nor selection of the least restrictive policy. They distinguish LaRue's licensed-premises conduct and apply subsequent searching doctrine rather than join III-B or IV-C. No new tier, categorical anti-paternalism ground or interstate rule is necessary. Souter joins Part II's independent fit ground, but does not join this separate writing or withdraw his affirmative advancement holding.

**Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins III-A–B and V-A–B. Existing commercial-speech jurisprudence supports invalidation, but he does not expressly join either the advancement or fit analysis and declines to develop or reinforce the doctrine through a new test here. His doubts about Central Hudson and distaste for governmental paternalism do not themselves supply a replacement constitutional rule. The available historical-practice account is inadequate for that change in this case. He nevertheless joins the broader Twenty-first Amendment treatment and preservation of LaRue's conduct holding, but not the broader Posadas passage. His agreement with the result is not another vote for either sufficient balancing ground.

**Thomas, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins III-A–B, IV-C and V-A–B, and independently regards the asserted interest in keeping lawful purchasers ignorant in order to manipulate their choices as per se illegitimate. Successful informational suppression would not cure that defect. He does not join Central Hudson balancing, an assumption legitimizing that informational objective, a failure-of-proof rationale or the Fox holding. His rule does not decide whether balancing remains appropriate for materially different interests. It reaches Peoples' presented lawful-sale information as well as 44 Liquormart's speech, while leaving unresolved its relationship to Edge when the forum State has prohibited or regulated the underlying domestic transaction. His broader Posadas and Twenty-first Amendment positions do not overrule Edge by implication. His agreement with the implementing remand rests on his categorical ground and does not adopt the Court's balancing analyses.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the constitutional judgment and specified Court portions, and dissenting in part as to remedy.** He joins I, II, III-A and V-A. Ordinary Central Hudson review resolves the case: temperance is substantial, the actual proof does not demonstrate material alleviation, and direct controls reveal inadequate fit. He endorses neither a heightened tier nor a categorical anti-paternalism rule. He does not join the broader LaRue or Posadas treatment. He would expressly enjoin enforcement of the challenged ban against truthful, nonmisleading retail-price advertising, rather than stop at the declaration-preserving remand, and therefore does not join V-B. His injunction would preserve otherwise lawful direct regulation of alcohol sales, prices, taxes, hours and unlawful promotions. False, misleading and transaction-integral advertising remain subject to applicable regulation; his position creates no general immunity for alcohol marketing.


### Romer v. Evans, No. 94-1039


Source: `terms/OT1995/output/OT_1995CHUNK6.md`


#### Opinion Topology

Justice Souter delivers the Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II, joined throughout by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. All seven directly adopt the same rational-basis ground, its complete explanation and qualifications, and the full injunction. Justice Scalia dissents, joined throughout by Justice Thomas.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** The two Justices would reverse the reviewed equal-protection judgment and remove that foundation for the injunction, with remand bounded by any independently preserved unresolved claims. They reject an extension of Hunter and Seattle into a fundamental entitlement of every group to pursue legislation at every governmental level, and they would sustain the amendment under ordinary rationality.

They accept its full restriction on state as well as local institutions, including public and nonintimate settings. In their view, regulatory restraint, religious and associational choice, finite enforcement resources and statewide uniformity rationally support withholding the disputed antidiscrimination protection. Reasonable predictions and imperfect coverage suffice; narrower exemptions are not constitutionally mandatory. The breadth implements the substantive policy rather than defeating its connection to those purposes. Constitutional reservation does not immunize the policy from federal review, but neither makes an otherwise rational policy unconstitutional.

Bowers is a sufficient additional premise in their reasoning: constitutional power to criminalize the specified conduct supports the lesser choice of withholding the disputed protection. Conduct-based moral judgment is distinct from hatred of persons, and orientation may serve as a proxy in this noncriminal policy. They do not claim Bowers literally adjudicated Amendment 2 or that every moral dislike is legitimate. Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid bare harm; the dissent finds legitimate policy grounds here. Heller's controlling proof holding is distinguished as addressing an inadequately justified confinement risk, not displaced by its opposing opinion.

Their conditional facial argument also respects the state construction. If orientation, conduct, practices and relationships identify the same class, the Bowers inference, on their view, reaches the common operation. If a distinct orientation-only application is instead posited, constitutionally permissible conduct applications would defeat wholesale invalidation under their Salerno argument. That contention depends on their rejection of the Court's defect throughout the actual class-wide operation; it does not independently answer that defect. They order no severance, find no conduct by every class member and rely on no unverified admission by a respondent.

Scalia's commercial-speech position supplies no general prohibition of conduct-based moral legislation. Thomas's rejection of suppressing independently protected truthful speech, and his separate rule against keeping lawful purchasers ignorant to manipulate their choices, remain confined to that speech setting; he does not extend them into a universal bar on the different Bowers inference. Neither Justice treats actual religious conflicts as automatic exemptions for every ordinance. Their proposed remand would not adjudicate surviving First Amendment claims, invent a new hearing or authorize enforcement despite any independent lawful restraint. Their grounds and disposition are noncontrolling.


### Washington v. Glucksberg, No. 96-110


Source: `terms/OT1996/output/OT_1996CHUNK13.md`


#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion; five direct joins including the author |
| Concurrence | O'Connor | Breyer, except insofar as the concurrence joins the principal opinions; Ginsburg adopts substantially its separate reasons in her own concurrence | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Separate imminent-death, suffering and palliation analysis; the qualified adopters do not join the Court's opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Author alone | Reversal and remand | General prohibition sustained; particular individual interests reserved |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Souter | Author alone | Reversal and remand | Independent substantive-liberty and commensurate-justification ground |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Ginsburg | Author alone | Reversal and remand | Substantially O'Connor's separate reasons; no principal-opinion join |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Breyer | Author alone | Reversal and remand | Combined manner-of-death, medical-assistance and severe-suffering interest; also the qualified O'Connor join stated above |

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**O'Connor, concurring.** She joins the Court's opinion but does not decide whether a competent person facing imminent death and great suffering has a cognizable interest in controlling those circumstances. Washington permits qualified physicians to alleviate suffering with medication even to unconsciousness and despite foreseeable hastening of death. Given that legal availability, the risks of erroneous judgments about terminal condition, competence and voluntariness sufficiently support the prohibition even if the narrower interest is assumed. Her Cruzan concurrence supports States' initial development of safeguards and democratic consideration. This neither guarantees effective delivery of pain care, eliminates suffering associated with sedation, requires legislative exhaustion nor creates a freestanding constitutional pain-care standard. Ginsburg adopts substantially these separate reasons. Breyer joins the separate analysis except insofar as it joins the principal opinions.

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** No person has complete autonomy to end life regardless of others' interests, and the State may maintain a general prohibition. That conclusion does not decide a competent individual's well-supported claim concerning the manner of an unavoidable imminent death. His Cruzan dissent and Casey's defined protected choice support attention to dignity, intolerable suffering and genuine justification. Preservation of life does not automatically outweigh every such interest. Mistake, coercion and professional-integrity concerns justify the general rule but may carry different weight in an exceptional case. Palliation does not logically eliminate every form of suffering or the distinction between conscious relief and terminal sedation. He also questions whether intent and causation invariably distinguish treatment withdrawal, sedation and lethal prescriptions; those overlaps do not establish this class-wide entitlement. He finds no individual entitlement proved here and orders no additional hearing.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment.** Harlan's Poe analysis asks whether the restraint is arbitrary or purposeless in light of the relative importance and fit of the individual and state interests. Traditions retained and abandoned inform that inquiry; neither longstanding prohibition alone nor an abstract autonomy formula resolves it. Responsible terminally ill patients seeking medical counsel and assistance present an important interest connected to bodily integrity, treatment refusal and defined reproductive choice. Casey's complete framework is not a universal medical test. He accepts the competent and voluntary premises and provisionally accepts the professional premises for this claim without inventing incapacity or misconduct.

Protection against mistaken or involuntary suicide and expansion into euthanasia supplies the sufficient justification. Family pressure alone would not settle the matter, because proposed physician screening is intended to answer it. The harder problem is reliably assessing a knowing, responsible decision and ensuring that physicians preserve the asserted boundaries. The disputed evidence concerning safeguards and the Dutch experience establishes a serious empirical uncertainty, not an incontrovertible foreign-law conclusion. Courts cannot presently displace the State's protective judgment with adequate assurance; legislatures can investigate and develop experience.

His conclusion rejects this entitlement now without permanently foreclosing recognition, demanding legislative experiments, imposing a judicial moratorium or assuming inevitable logical expansion of every protected right. Legislative foot-dragging and a materially different individual record remain for another case. Incidental difficulty does not automatically require compelling justification, and not every liberty claim demands elaborate balancing: the importance of the interest and the burden determine the inquiry. His view does not become the Court's general standard.

**Ginsburg, concurring in the judgment.** She adopts substantially O'Connor's separate reasons about the unresolved imminent-death and suffering interest and the legal availability of palliation. She does not join the principal opinion or a declaration sustaining every future application. Her agreement creates no additional independent theory.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** The potential liberty combines personal control over the manner of death, willing medical assistance and avoidance of unnecessary severe physical suffering. Describing it merely as any right to suicide misses the strongest claim without establishing that claim as fundamental. Poe, Cruzan and the defined personal-liberty authorities support serious consideration. Because the law does not forbid the pain treatment central to that interest, the present case need not decide its status; the stated protective interests suffice here. Delivery failures are not the same as a legal prohibition. He recognizes sedation and does not find every person's suffering treatable. A prohibition of necessary palliation would present a materially different case. These reasons supplement his qualified O'Connor join; they do not adopt the principal opinion or one universal liberty method for all judgment supporters.


### Jefferson v. City of Tarrant, No. 96-957

#### Opinion Topology

Justice Breyer delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Justice Stevens dissents from jurisdictional dismissal and would exercise jurisdiction; he supplies no decision on the federal merits.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stevens's jurisdictional dissent.** Stevens would exercise jurisdiction. Although he dissented in Ritchie, its practical-finality holding remains binding. Its recognition that repeating a settled federal question through state appeals may serve no useful finality purpose cannot be answered simply by identifying a possible later appeal. Alabama has conclusively resolved the federal remedy issue and bound the trial court; he would apply Ritchie to permit review. This position decides neither the federal damages merits nor a final merits mandate.


### Carmell v. Texas, No. 98-7540


Source: `terms/OT1999/output/OT_1999CHUNK6.md`


#### Opinion Topology

Stevens delivered the Opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer, including the distinction between proof sufficiency and admissibility and the existing-record remedy. O'Connor dissented, joined by Kennedy and Ginsburg. The dissent would affirm rejection of the federal challenge on its witness-specific credibility classification, without a categorical exemption for procedural rules.

#### Separate Writings

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy and Ginsburg, dissents. She would affirm because the age and reporting conditions regulate the credibility of a particular class of testimony, leaving the charged acts, lawful punishment and ultimate burden of persuasion unchanged. Hopt permits a formerly disqualified witness to supply evidence for an earlier offense; Thompson permits formerly excluded comparison evidence. For the dissent, removal of this special credibility disability has the same constitutional function. The State's sufficiency terminology and acquittal remedy show the rule's importance but do not alone determine its federal character: permissible evidence restrictions can also decide whether a prosecution succeeds.

The dissent does not exempt every rule called procedural or abolish Calder's evidence category. A true reduction in the required proof or burden remains distinct. It disagrees with the Court's classification of this age-and-report rule, makes no finding that corroboration actually existed, and does not treat the unchallenged sentences as eliminating the controversy. Its position would defeat the presented federal challenge without deciding unpresented state-law claims.


### Apprendi v. New Jersey, No. 99-478


Source: `terms/OT1999/output/OT_1999CHUNK8.md`


#### Opinion Topology

**Opinion of the Court.** Stone-Zsela writes for the Court, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. Their agreement covers the present-offense maximum rule, jury and reasonable-doubt guarantees, application, limits and remedy in full and supports reversal, enhanced-sentence vacatur and bounded remand.

**Concurrence.** Thomas joins the Court in full. His separate writing addresses a broader element principle, including punishment-range changes and prior convictions, with separate capital and Guidelines reservations. No other Justice joins it.

**Dissent.** O'Connor, joined by Kennedy and Breyer, would affirm against the presented federal allocation/proof challenge. The dissent addresses the bounded legislative sentencing classification and objections to the maximum-based rule.

**Dissent.** Breyer joins O'Connor's dissent and would affirm. His separate writing supplies additional sentencing-administration and proportionality grounds. No other Justice joins it.

The six Justices joining the Opinion of the Court adopt its governing propositions at the same level of generality. Thomas's concurrence adds no controlling proposition. No partial join, judgment-only coalition or Marks synthesis supplies the Court's rule.

#### Separate Positions

**Thomas, concurring.** Thomas joins the Court's rule and remedy in full but adopts a broader principle: a fact that the law makes necessary to increase the kind, degree or range of punishment is an element of the aggravated crime. His common-law account draws on the treatment of value and grading in Smith, Hope, Larned, McDonald and Lacy and on Archbold's and Bishop's description of legally aggravated offenses. Those authorities concerning accusation help identify the offense to which jury and proof safeguards attach; he does not infer federal grand-jury incorporation against a State.

The principle includes legally aggravating prior convictions and mandatory-minimum facts. A prior adjudication's validity and the lawful manner of proving or admitting its existence remain separate; adequate admissions, stipulations or lawful bifurcation may avoid prejudicial detail. He acknowledges that his earlier Almendarez-Torres dissent relied on the special prior-adjudication setting, and now concludes that this distinction does not categorically remove a fact's element status when the law uses it to enlarge punishment. His earlier statutory joins did not already decide that constitutional point. His view of minima conflicts with McMillan's contrary reasoning, but this case does not overrule McMillan.

Thomas distinguishes lawful discretion inside an established range and facts mitigating an already authorized punishment. He reserves the capital question in light of its distinct constitutional structure, general Guidelines implications, limits on within-range discretion and legislative authority to prescribe broad ranges. He accepts the narrower present-offense holding as sufficient here. No other Justice joins this concurrence, and its broader conclusions do not control.

**O'Connor, joined by Kennedy and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent would sustain the challenged allocation under the legislature's bounded authority to distinguish an offense from sentencing considerations. Patterson preserves a substantive legislative role; Gaudin governs an element after it has been identified. The guilty plea establishes a complete serious firearm offense, motive traditionally informs sentencing, and the general enhancement provision combines a defined criterion with notice and adversarial inquiry. These features, in the dissent's view, permit the particular racial-purpose classification without granting unlimited power to relabel ordinary offense elements.

The dissent accepts that this statute raises the maximum and therefore cannot be resolved merely by citing McMillan, Witte or Watts. Almendarez-Torres and Jones supply no constitutional holding sustaining it. Its conclusion instead rests on the merits of the legislative allocation. Notice and a hearing would not replace the jury if the fact were an element; the disagreement is its constitutional classification. The dissent objects that a legislature could enact a broad maximum and permit extensive judicial discretion, yet the Court forbids a more specifically guided increase. Walton presents a substantial tension because its factual aggravator bears on death eligibility, but no capital ruling is sought here. The dissent relies on neither hypothetical aggregate punishment, a fictional plea admission nor harmlessness, and would affirm only against the federal allocation/proof claim presented.

**Breyer, dissenting.** Breyer also joins O'Connor's dissent and separately emphasizes proportionality and workable sentencing. Legislatures and judges need to account for varied offense and offender circumstances. He does not find a constitutionally decisive difference between broad judicial discretion under a high ceiling and narrower, legislatively guided discretion that adjusts a ceiling. A rigid maximum boundary may encourage broad ranges or mandatory minima without improving defendants' practical protection. Those concerns do not answer every jury objection or validate every label; he would sustain this defined scheme attached to a completed serious offense. He recognizes the real additional exposure and disputed purpose, but regards the bounded legislative classification as permissible. He adopts no universal conclusion about every Guidelines system and seeks no remedy beyond affirmance of this challenged judgment.


### Rogers v. Tennessee, No. 99-6218


Source: `terms/OT2000/output/OT_2000CHUNK5.md`


#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas | Reversal and remand, 5–4 | Shared case-specific insufficient-warning ground, actual state-law premise and bounded remedy |
| Concurrence | Scalia | Stevens, Thomas | Joins the complete Court opinion and reversal | Additional objection to avowed retrospective removal of a substantive offense restriction |
| Concurrence | Breyer | — | Joins the Court's reversal | Expressly adopts the common-law due-process framework stated in the dissent, while rejecting its application here |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Would affirm | Unexpected-and-indefensible framework and conclusion that this abolition was foreseeable |

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Stevens and Thomas, joins the Court's bounded fair-warning holding and adds that an acknowledged substantive offense restriction cannot be erased retrospectively merely because reform was foreseeable. Bouie protects the criminal law governing the act, not only an estimate of what judges may later wish to change. Carmell reinforces respect for existing legal conditions while remaining a legislative case. Ordinary application of existing law and prospective reform remain permissible. These three also agree that, even under the competing foreseeability framework, medical obsolescence, infrequent litigation and change elsewhere supply inadequate warning here. Their additional position does not make disputed historical theories of common-law judging necessary to the judgment.

Breyer's concurrence expressly adopts the unexpected-and-indefensible due-process framework stated by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg and rejects automatic incorporation of every legislative category. He nonetheless finds the warning inadequate. A sound reason for future reform does not show that the existing Tennessee limitation had ceased to govern Rogers's earlier conduct. His concurrence rejects the State's application while preserving the legitimacy of ordinary common-law development; it does not join the stronger structural concurrence.

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg, would affirm. Their framework asks whether the common-law development was unexpected and indefensible by reference to preconduct law. They accept the limitation's survival but regard its limited local use, obsolete causal-proof rationale and supported preconduct legal landscape as making abandonment foreseeable. Prior existence and constitutional unexpectedness are distinct questions. Lanier's notice inquiry and the difference between legislative and judicial change permit this application in their view. They do not rely on the rejected statutory-repeal theory, the assault's immorality alone, subjective reliance, or later authorities to establish prior notice.
