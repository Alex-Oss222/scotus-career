# 2003-06-26 ? Lawrence v. Texas

Source: `terms/OT2002/records/Lawrence-v-Texas_merits_2003-06-26.md`, Public Projection only.

## Event

Lawrence v. Texas, No. 02-102, 539 U.S. 558. Merits decision, June 26, 2003. Argued March 26, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Texas Court of Appeals, sitting en banc, which upheld Lawrence's and Garner's convictions under Texas Penal Code §21.06. The questions are whether Texas may punish private consensual adult same-sex intimacy, whether its selective prohibition violates equal protection, and whether Bowers v. Hardwick should be overruled.

The prosecution and review proceed on private, consensual conduct between adults. Lawrence and Garner pleaded no contest while preserving their constitutional objections and received fines. They seek reversal and relief from the prohibition as applied to that conduct; Texas seeks affirmance.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or submission and at decision.

## Public Action

The judgment is reversed. The convictions and dependent penalties must be set aside, and the prosecutions for the protected conduct must end.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The state court must vacate both convictions and their dependent penalties and terminate these prosecutions. The liberty holding prevents Texas from curing the violation by imposing the same criminal prohibition on different-sex participants. The opinion joins supporting each independently sufficient ground are stated separately below.

## Opinion Topology

O'Connor filed an opinion concurring in part and in the judgment. Scalia filed a dissenting opinion, joined by Thomas. Thomas filed a separate dissenting opinion.

## Holdings

### I. Private consensual adult intimacy is protected against this criminal prohibition

**Holding and operative rule:** The liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment includes private consensual sexual intimacy between adults. A State may not criminally punish that conduct solely to enforce moral disapproval of the participants' intimacy, and Bowers v. Hardwick is overruled insofar as it permits that punishment; the convictions and penalties resting on the prohibition must be set aside.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg's opinion of the Court, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author. All six adopt the complete rule, explanation, overruling and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** Griswold and Eisenstadt protect individual intimate choice rather than merely a particular physical act. Casey retains the constitutional protection of the person's reproductive choice and supplies the related requirement that a departure from precedent have a justification beyond renewed disagreement. Those decisions do not already decide this prohibition; they expose the defect in Bowers's account of the personal interest at stake. Its isolated description of the act permits inherited disapproval to replace constitutional examination of a criminal intrusion into private adult intimacy.

Glucksberg sustained a different prohibition because protection of life, error, pressure on vulnerable people and medical responsibility rationally supported it. Its holding adopted no exclusive method requiring every liberty to have been historically described in the precise terms of the forbidden act. The accepted private, consensual adult setting here presents no comparable protective feature. Texas identifies no separate injury sufficient to justify punishment. Romer's demand for a connection to the actual disadvantage reinforces attention to real justification, while its preservation of Bowers makes express overruling necessary.

The dissent's reliance on longstanding prohibition and the continuing criminal precedent is substantial. But the continuing intrusion, the inadequacy of Bowers's description of liberty and its conflict with the individual-choice principles retained in the later cases warrant overruling. Continued criminal punishment supplies no comparable settled private-transaction reliance. An equality-only remedy would leave the same protected choice exposed to an evenhanded prohibition; the preserved liberty question therefore receives its own answer. The rule concerns private consensual adult intimacy. Its category does not include coercion, minor-involved conduct, public conduct or commercial sex, and it determines no civil relationship status.

### II. The selective criminal disadvantage independently violates equal protection

**Holding and operative rule:** Under ordinary rational-basis review, Texas may not impose this criminal penalty on same-sex participants while leaving otherwise comparable private consensual adult conduct unpunished when the distinction has no rational connection to a legitimate reason for the additional disadvantage. The challenger must negate a reasonably conceivable legitimate explanation; that burden is met here because disapproval of the same-sex class does not explain a separate injury from the matched conduct.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, directly supported by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. This seven-Justice ground independently requires reversal of both convictions.

**Controlling explanation:** Romer requires a rational relationship to the actual disadvantage, while Heller preserves legislative generalizations, conceivable grounds and imperfect fit without formal findings, empirical certainty or a least-restrictive-means requirement. Moreno and Cleburne establish the limits of bare harm and irrational prejudice within that ordinary review. The relevant comparison is the same private, voluntary adult intimacy with different-sex participants. Texas identifies no difference in injury explaining why only these participants receive criminal punishment.

The authority to regulate an act generally does not answer the distinct question why the State adds a penalty for one class. For that reason, the conduct-based morality argument accepted by the dissent does not rationally explain this selective disability. The rule rests on the particular disadvantage and creates no new sexual-orientation tier or universal prohibition on moral interests in legislation.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bowers v. Hardwick, 478 U.S. 186: overruled insofar as it authorizes punishment of covered private consensual adult intimacy; it no longer sustains either these convictions or a comparable criminal prohibition within the protected category.
- Griswold v. Connecticut, 381 U.S. 479: its protection of intimate choice informs the personal liberty extended here.
- Eisenstadt v. Baird, 405 U.S. 438: its individual-choice principle informs the adult intimacy protected here.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, 505 U.S. 833: its protected-choice foundation and stare-decisis requirements are applied; its six-step abortion-regulation framework retains that distinct setting.
- Washington v. Glucksberg: distinguished by its concrete protective grounds for the assisted-death prohibition; its holding establishes no exclusive historical-description method.
- Romer v. Evans, 517 U.S. 620: applied to the actual unequal disadvantage under ordinary review; its former preservation of Bowers gives way to Part I's express overruling.
- Heller v. Doe, 509 U.S. 312: applied to the challenger burden, conceivable grounds and required connection to the actual disadvantage.
- United States Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S. 528, and City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432: applied for the ordinary-review limits on bare disadvantage and irrational prejudice.

## Law After Decision

Private consensual adult sexual intimacy is protected from criminal punishment resting solely on moral disapproval. The protection covers the defined category of conduct, including a formally evenhanded prohibition, and applies in other covered nonfinal prosecutions and direct appeals. Texas's additional same-sex penalty also fails ordinary equal protection. Bowers supplies no authority for continued enforcement within that category. Courts must give the rule effect through the lawful procedures governing the case before them; this mandate requires vacatur and termination for Lawrence and Garner.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins both controlling grounds and the complete conviction remedy. He would expressly call the protected liberty fundamental and require government to establish a compelling harm-preventing objective and a closely fitted necessity for invading it. The claimant must first establish private, consensual adult conduct; the State then must supply actual reasons and evidence rather than presumed harm. Texas fails that requirement and independently fails the ordinary equality comparison. Casey, Griswold and Eisenstadt support this development without making Casey's abortion framework universal. Glucksberg's life-protection, coercion, error and professional concerns distinguish its assistance-in-death claim. Bowers must be overruled because inherited disapproval cannot itself justify this intrusion.

He understands the rule to apply under Griffith to covered nonfinal cases and under Teague's substantive exception in otherwise proper collateral proceedings because it removes the covered conduct from permissible criminal punishment. Jurisdiction, procedural default, a lawful reopening vehicle and independent statutory gates remain separately applicable. The criminal-review mandate does not itself reopen absent persons' judgments. Civil relationship status and conduct outside the adult, private, consensual category require their own governing law and facts.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and in the judgment.** She joins the ordinary-equality holding and reversal, but would retain Bowers and decide no additional liberty ground. The authority to prohibit conduct generally does not justify singling out same-sex participants for the additional criminal disadvantage. Romer and Heller permit ordinary legislative latitude while requiring a rational connection to that actual distinction. This conclusion neither adopts an orientation-based tier nor declares all moral interests illegitimate. Equality supplies complete relief from these convictions.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** Bowers directly permits the specified prohibition, and the intimate-choice precedents do not justify overruling it in their view. Romer concerned a broad withdrawal of ordinary civil protection and expressly preserved the conduct question. They regard this statute as regulating conduct that the State may condemn on moral grounds, rather than imposing a distinct disability without a rational connection. The selective category therefore survives ordinary review. They reject the Court's extension of substantive liberty and would affirm both convictions. Their position does not depend on treating Glucksberg as an exclusive historical methodology or on equating every moral distinction with constitutionally permissible hostility.

**Thomas, dissenting.** He joins Scalia's dissent and separately states that disagreement with the wisdom of the criminal prohibition does not establish a constitutional right to invalidate it. Bowers and the limited reach of the intimate-choice cases govern his constitutional vote despite that policy objection.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the Texas courts to enter the appropriate conviction-vacatur and dismissal orders and remove the legal penalties dependent on these convictions. Prosecution of the covered conduct must cease. Other nonfinal covered cases receive the substantive rule through their ordinary proceedings.

# 2003-06-26 ? Limon v. Kansas

## Event

Limon v. Kansas, No. 02-583; Kansas Court of Appeals No. 85,898; District Court No. 00CR36. Merits decision on full merits briefing, June 26, 2003. On review of the Kansas Court of Appeals' February 1, 2002 judgment affirming the conviction and sentence. The questions concern the opposite-sex condition on close-in-age criminal leniency, the proportionality of the 206-month prison term, and use of nonjury juvenile adjudications to authorize a higher adult sentencing maximum.

The February 16, 2000 encounter occurred when Limon was eighteen and the other participant was fourteen years and eleven months old, less than four years younger. The stipulated conduct was voluntary and ended on request. The participants resided at a school for students with developmental disabilities. After a June 27, 2000 jury waiver and trial on the current-act stipulation, the court imposed 206 months' imprisonment and sixty months' postrelease supervision. Two prior juvenile aggravated-sodomy adjudications produced category B; the reported range without that history was fifty-five to sixty-one months. The same act and history qualified for thirteen to fifteen months under the opposite-sex reduced offense. Limon seeks relief from the unequal treatment and the independent sentencing defects; Kansas seeks affirmance.

## Holdings

### I. Close-in-age leniency must be administered without this unequal sex condition

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may not withhold otherwise applicable close-in-age criminal leniency solely because the participants are of the same sex when that distinction has no rational connection to the asserted protection of minors. The challenger must establish the unequal classification and the remaining eligibility conditions and negate a reasonably conceivable legitimate explanation for the disadvantage; relief must eliminate the discriminatory offense-and-sentence treatment through extension of the reduced category, unless an actual state-law severability obstacle requires another lawful cure consistent with equality and fair notice.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author. These seven adopt the equality rule and nondiscriminatory remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Romer requires a rational relationship to the actual disadvantage; Heller preserves conceivable grounds, reasonable generalizations and imperfect fit without formal findings, empirical certainty or a least-restrictive-means demand. Kansas's own comparison supplies the defect. Section 21-3522 reduces punishment for voluntary intercourse, sodomy or lewd fondling or touching when the child is at least fourteen and under sixteen, the offender is under nineteen and less than four years older, and only those two participate. It assigns severity levels eight, nine and ten respectively, but excludes same-sex participants. Limon's stipulated act satisfies the other reduced-sodomy conditions. His same history produces thirteen to fifteen months there, compared with 206 months imposed under the severity-level-three classification.

Protecting minors, developmental vulnerability and sexual health are legitimate concerns. They do not rationally explain radically greater punishment for this same act, ages, consent, age gap and history solely because both participants are male. Describing the favored category as approved adolescent development restates the distinction rather than explaining its protective connection. Bowers's authority over specified conduct does not answer this separate unequal-mitigation question.

The statutory age, consent, conduct and two-participant limits remain. Equal treatment concerns eligibility for an enacted reduced offense, not an entitlement to adult-minor sexual conduct. The court on remand must eliminate the exclusion in applying the reduced category unless a genuine state severability obstacle prevents that cure. Any alternative must end Limon's unequal aggravated treatment without retroactively increasing criminal liability. The existing sentencing judgment is vacated now; the State's remedial inquiry cannot preserve it indefinitely.

### II. Nonjury juvenile predicates do not categorically dispense with adult element protections

**Holding and operative rule:** When a fact concerning a nonjury juvenile adjudication is legally necessary to increase the otherwise authorized adult maximum, including a binding grid ceiling, that fact must be validly admitted as an aggravating element or established beyond a reasonable doubt to the proper guilt factfinder. A lawful jury waiver permits judicial determination under the same proof standard; the adjudication's label, the larger offense-wide maximum, or a waiver and stipulation confined to the present act do not themselves authorize the higher maximum.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. These six adopt the defined rule and the actual-record remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Apprendi makes legal punishment authority, rather than the sentencing label, decisive. Angle establishes that a binding grid ceiling is punishment authority even within a larger offense-wide statutory maximum. Harris and Ring apply element protection to their present-offense aggravators while reserving prior-conviction facts. Almendarez-Torres supplies a statutory aggravated-element construction, not a constitutional recidivism exception. The Court now resolves the identified nonjury-predicate question. The treatment of a prior adult conviction obtained with a jury remains outside this holding.

McKeiver permits adjudication without a jury in the juvenile setting. That original validity does not determine what establishes an aggravating element in a later adult prosecution. Monge addresses double-jeopardy treatment of noncapital recidivist proceedings and expressly leaves the independent jury and proof issue open. Those decisions therefore do not sustain the categorical exception applied below. When the adult legal predicate is the adjudication's existence, identity and qualifying character, the required element finding concerns that predicate; the rule does not demand retrial of underlying conduct that the adult statute does not make an element.

The state court must begin with the corrected offense and its authorized maximum, including any binding grid ceiling, without the disputed history, then identify what fact increases that maximum. It must examine any actual admission and the lawful scope of waiver and adjudication. The current-act stipulation, notice of the grid and an opportunity to object later are insufficient by themselves. Actual equivalent findings or a valid admission may matter; a forecast that a jury would have agreed cannot replace them. The existing sentence is already vacated on equality grounds. This inquiry governs any continuing reliance on a higher maximum; ordinary selection within an independently authorized range remains lawful.

## Law After Decision

Otherwise eligible close-in-age participants receive the equality protection against denial of mitigation solely because they are of the same sex. Kansas must give Limon a lawful nondiscriminatory offense classification and replacement sentence while retaining the reduced provision's remaining eligibility conditions. The rule governs other covered nonfinal cases through their ordinary procedures.

A nonjury juvenile adjudication supplies no categorical exemption from the element protections for a fact that increases the otherwise authorized adult maximum, including a binding grid ceiling. Courts must identify that maximum, the historical predicate necessary to raise it and any valid admission or proper adjudication. These are applications of the announced rule.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's two grounds and the immediate vacatur of the discriminatory aggravated disposition. Extension of the reduced category is the effective cure unless the State identifies an actual severability obstacle; another remedy must eliminate the unequal punishment and respect fair notice. He would treat the equality rule, to the extent it removes aggravated punishment from state authority for the defined category, as substantive in otherwise proper collateral proceedings. Jurisdiction, default, lawful reopening procedures and independent statutory gates still apply. The juvenile-predicate rule instead concerns the procedure authorizing punishment: it applies to nonfinal direct-review cases under Griffith, while final collateral claims retain Teague's procedural-rule limits and the governing habeas statutes. His account does not itself reopen absent persons' judgments.

He makes no independent proportionality determination. He would first require the state court to resolve the disputed appellate-preservation question under the actual appellate rules and their application to this case. The established trial objection cannot alone settle that distinct question. He withholds assent to the additional reviewability conclusion and proportionality ground while joining the complete relief afforded by the Court's other holdings.

**Stevens, concurring, joined by Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.** They would also hold the 206-month term grossly disproportionate. Ewing requires assessment of the present offense, serious history and actual term together. Both prior juvenile aggravated-sodomy adjudications receive substantial weight, but cannot replace the current voluntary close-in-age act. The thirteen-to-fifteen-month treatment of the same act and history with opposite-sex participants is strong comparative evidence, not an automatic sentence-ratio rule. Solem preserves an enforceable limit despite recidivism, and Henderson requires attention to the actual offense, offender and release conditions. These Justices find the extraordinary term excessive even with the serious history included; they propose sentence-only relief and a lawful replacement, without selecting a numerical term.

They also conclude that the trial ruling permits review of this claim. The written motion and sentencing argument raised the Eighth Amendment. Under K.S.A. §21-4721(c)(1), as construed in Clemons, a presumptive-sentence challenge could not receive state appellate review. Largent, Costarelli and Nash therefore identify the trial court as the highest court in which this particular decision could be had under §1257. The appellate statement that the issue was not raised does not erase that independently available route.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Kennedy and, as to the juvenile discussion, Breyer.** They join the equality rule and its sentence vacatur but reject the separate juvenile-factfinder ground. McKeiver validates the juvenile adjudicative process within its setting, and Monge recognizes the distinct recidivist context. Apprendi reserved that context. They would treat the valid adjudication as an established offender-status fact usable in a structured adult sentencing system, rather than require its renewed allocation as an element. They rely on that affirmative ground, not a supposed existing constitutional exception.

O'Connor and Kennedy also reject the proposed proportionality application. Their Ewing position gives exceptional deference to legislative judgments about incapacitation after repeated serious offending. The present minor-conduct setting and two serious juvenile priors distinguish the nonviolent property offense in Ewing. They would cure the sex-based disparity under equality while rejecting the additional inference of gross disproportionality. Breyer does not join that portion.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** They join the Court's nonjury-predicate rule and actual-admission inquiry. They would nevertheless uphold the sex condition on reduced punishment. Under their Romer and Bowers reasoning, the legislature may encourage opposite-sex adolescent development while discouraging the specified same-sex conduct through a conduct-based moral judgment, especially in laws protecting minors. They regard the distinction as rational despite its imperfect fit. They also reject the asserted constitutional right to proportionality in prison duration on their Harmelin and Ewing grounds, rather than adopt the other dissent's proportionality balancing.

**Thomas, concurring.** He joins the narrower nonjury-predicate rule and retains the broader position expressed in his Apprendi concurrence: a fact legally necessary to aggravate punishment is an element even when it concerns a prior adjudication. Monge's double-jeopardy classification does not settle that jury-and-proof question. The required fact is the aggravating predicate the adult law uses, not every underlying fact of a valid juvenile judgment.

# 2003-06-26 ? Stogner v. California

## Event

Stogner v. California, No. 01-1757, 539 U.S. 607. Merits decision, June 26, 2003. Argued March 31, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the California Court of Appeal, which rejected Stogner's renewed challenge to the pending prosecution. The questions are whether the Ex Post Facto Clause permits revival after the governing criminal limitations period expired and whether due process permits withdrawal of the protection acquired at expiration.

The April 1998 charging instrument was a complaint. The pending indictment was returned March 13 and filed March 14, 2001, after the complaint was reinstated and then dismissed on the prosecutor's motion. Count one alleges conduct from January 1, 1955 through September 30, 1964; count two alleges conduct from January 1, 1964 through September 27, 1973. Each acknowledges expiration of the otherwise applicable period and relies on California Penal Code §803(g). The offense-time three-year periods had expired by 1976, long before the 1993 enactment effective January 1, 1994. Stogner seeks termination of the revived charges; California seeks authority to prosecute them.

Section 803(g)'s separate transition and refiling provisions apply to causes arising before, on or after January 1, 1994. They cover complaints or indictments filed on or before January 1, 1997, or after that date, within the subdivision's allowed period. For a report made after January 1, 1994, when a charge was not filed within that period, or a timely charge was dismissed, a complaint or indictment may be filed no later than 180 days after the earlier of a published California Supreme Court opinion on the subdivision's retroactive constitutionality becoming final or this Court filing an opinion deciding that question. For a report made after January 1, 1994, a separate clause permits refiling after dismissal of a timely complaint, indictment or subsequently filed information notwithstanding other law, including §§871.5(c) and 1238(b). A dismissal entered or effective before that same 180-day cutoff does not terminate the action for §1387 purposes, and a prior trial or intermediate appellate ruling on retroactivity or constitutionality does not bind the refiling. These provisions concern the statutory charging routes; both pending counts still depend on restoration of an already expired prosecution period.

## Holdings

### A legislature may not restore criminal exposure after the governing period has expired

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ex Post Facto Clause forbids a State to apply a later enactment to revive criminal prosecution after the previously applicable limitations period has completely expired. Once the defendant establishes that completed bar under governing limitations law, prosecution requires an independently timely basis or genuinely applicable tolling rule; a count dependent on the later revival must be dismissed.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court by Breyer, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer, including the author. All six adopt the rule, its categorical basis and dismissal of both counts.

**Controlling explanation:** Calder's second category protects against retrospective aggravation of penal liability, including restoration of punishment when the accused was no longer legally exposed to it. Completed expiration supplies the decisive boundary. The later statute does more than regulate how an existing prosecution proceeds: it restores authority that the governing law had withdrawn. Brown v. Walker explains the legal protection resulting from expiration. Carmell requires examination of a change's legal effect while Collins confines the Clause to its received categories; this decision does not freeze ordinary procedure.

California emphasizes that the acts were already criminal, the substantive sentence is unchanged and delayed reporting can make child-abuse prosecutions difficult. Those points explain prospective reform but do not answer restoration of expired exposure. Section 803(g)'s reporting and corroboration safeguards address reliability, a different question from authority to revive punishment. The ordinary complaint provision allows filing within one year after a report to California law enforcement by a person of any age alleging victimization before eighteen through an offense designated in §§261, 286, 288, 288a, 288.5, 289 or 289.5. It requires prior expiration, substantial sexual conduct excluding nonmutual masturbation, and independent clear-and-convincing corroboration. Otherwise inadmissible evidence cannot satisfy that requirement, and mental-health professionals' opinions are not independent corroboration. Even satisfaction of those conditions cannot cure the constitutional revival defect.

The indictment acknowledges expiration for both counts. The offense-time three-year periods expired by 1976; even the alternative six-year calculation would have expired by 1979. Section 803(g) became effective in 1994. Both counts therefore require forbidden revival and must end. This holding is confined to already expired criminal limitations periods, preserving the distinction from a still-running period, actual tolling and civil claims. The requested dismissal follows fully from the Ex Post Facto Clause; the alternative categorical due-process ground supplies no additional relief requiring decision here.

## Precedent Treatment

- Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386: its second category is applied to restoration of criminal exposure after completed expiration; the present holding resolves that distinct limitations application.
- Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591: its explanation of completed limitations protection supports the legal-effect analysis, without supplying an earlier holding on this revival statute.
- Carmell v. Texas, 529 U.S. 513: its examination of operative legal effect is applied; its proof-sufficiency category and completed-trial remedy remain distinct from this pretrial limitations dismissal.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37: its received-category constraint and rejection of a general substantial-procedural-protection theory remain controlling.
- Rogers v. Tennessee, 532 U.S. 451: its protection of an operative offense boundary informs attention to actual legal effect; its judicial fair-warning setting remains distinct from legislative ex post facto categories.
- Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania: distinguished because its defined mandatory-final-life judgment is a different form of completed legal protection; its holding supplies no universal finality rule for limitations.

## Law After Decision

A later criminal revival statute cannot restore exposure after the governing limitations period has fully expired. Courts apply state limitations law to the actual dates and any genuine tolling, then dismiss a count that depends on revival. Reliability requirements or the accusation's gravity do not alter the legal consequence of established expiration. The rule governs this prosecution and covered nonfinal cases on direct review. Ordinary extensions of an unexpired period and civil claims remain outside the completed-criminal-bar rule.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the completed-bar rule, its Calder basis and dismissal of both counts. He would also treat the rule as substantive for otherwise proper collateral review because it withdraws authority to restore criminal exposure, rather than merely changing trial procedure. Griffith governs covered nonfinal direct-review cases; Teague's substantive distinction, in his view, permits invocation in an otherwise proper collateral proceeding. That position does not itself reopen final convictions or dispense with jurisdiction, default, limitations or independent statutory habeas requirements. Carmell's evidentiary rule and Rogers's judicial fair-warning inquiry support attention to legal operation without already deciding this revival question. Sattazahn's defined capital-finality holding remains distinct.

**Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** They would reject both granted categorical challenges. On their reading, Calder's categories compare substantive criminality, the offense's degree, prescribed punishment and necessary guilt proof with the law governing the act. Section 803(g) changes the period for enforcing the same criminal prohibition, without changing those features. An expired limit supplies important statutory immunity, but does not become an acquittal or a substantive element merely because prosecution formerly was barred. They therefore regard revival as outside the Clause's received categories. Carmell's alteration of legally necessary proof and Rogers's removal of an acknowledged offense boundary do not require the same result here.

They also reject the claim that expiration alone creates a constitutionally irrevocable due-process immunity. Brown describes the effect of the then-operative statute, not an absolute constitutional right to repose. The original criminality supplies warning, while the reporting and independent-corroboration safeguards bear on fairness. Their proposed affirmance would permit proceedings only under the statute's actual requirements and would preserve the lower court's fact-specific inquiry into preaccusation delay. It would not treat allegations of qualifying conduct or corroboration as established, or decide a claim of actual defense prejudice on facts not determined in this record.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns to the California courts with directions to dismiss both pending indictment counts because each requires revival of an expired criminal limitations period. The court must terminate any custody or conditions supported only by those counts. The constitutional dismissal ends this revival-dependent prosecution.

# 2003-06-26 ? Wiggins v. Smith

## Event

Wiggins v. Smith, No. 02-311, 539 U.S. 510. Argued March 24, 2003; decided June 26, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, 288 F.3d 629. Review concerns the second question presented: whether counsel's limited mitigation investigation and presentation, in light of the strategy disputing principal responsibility for the killing, denied effective assistance at capital sentencing and permits federal habeas relief. The Fourth Circuit reversed the District Court's grant of sentencing relief. Wiggins seeks relief from his death sentence; the separate guilt-sufficiency question is outside the grant.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the rejection of the sentencing ineffective-assistance claim and remands for a conditional sentence writ.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded on the sentencing claim, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The lower federal court must enter a conditional writ requiring Maryland, within a reasonable period that court fixes, to conduct a constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding or impose a lawful noncapital sentence. The conviction and independently lawful custody remain undisturbed.

## Opinion Topology

O'Connor delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Scalia dissents, joined by Thomas. The dissent would affirm because §2254(d)(1) is unsatisfied; it does not independently decide actual deficient performance or prejudice.

## Holdings

The following holdings are adopted by the Court's seven-Justice opinion: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

### 1. An informed strategy requires a reasonable investigation

**Holding and operative rule:** Counsel must reasonably investigate available mitigation or make a reasonable, informed decision that further inquiry is unnecessary. A strategic presentation choice does not excuse ending the investigation when information already obtained supplies substantial unexplored leads that reasonable counsel would pursue; the petitioner must establish deficient performance through an objective assessment of the work and circumstances at the time, with appropriate deference to informed professional judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Strickland protects strategic choices supported by reasonable investigation and requires reasonable grounds for limiting it. The question here is the reasonableness of the investigation supporting counsel's decision, not whether counsel had to present every item of mitigation. The presentence and social-services materials indicated serious deprivation and substantial further leads, and resources for developing the history were available. Counsel stopped without developing that available account. Their lawful choice to dispute whether Wiggins was the actual killer did not reveal what a mitigation case would contain or explain why the leads should remain unexplored. Knowing that a childhood was difficult is different from investigating the nature and extent of the available mitigation. The unsupported stopping point was professionally unreasonable. Terry Williams explains this existing Strickland distinction; it does not supply a later-created constitutional obligation. Lockett and Eddings establish the legal relevance of individual background, leaving informed presentation choices to counsel rather than requiring indiscriminate use of every fact.

### 2. The state performance adjudication unreasonably applied established law

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §2254(d)(1), a state merits decision permits federal relief only when it satisfies the statute's independent legal threshold; an incorrect application alone is insufficient. Maryland's approval of this uninformed investigative limit was objectively unreasonable under Strickland as established when the state court decided the claim, independently of the Court's separate finding of actual deficient performance.

**Controlling explanation:** Terry Williams makes unreasonable application an objective legal inquiry and measures clearly established substantive law at the state merits decision. Early requires consideration of the state court's actual rationale and the complete constitutional context, rather than treating citation omissions or the absence of a factually identical precedent as decisive. Maryland relied on the existing records and the principalship strategy. The statutory margin protects a reasonable informed limit, but those considerations do not answer the substantial unexplored leads that made this stopping point unreasonable. The Court therefore crosses the statutory threshold because of the actual investigation and rationale, not merely because further investigation would have been preferable. Lockyer and Price preserve the separation between statutory eligibility and the underlying violation. Actual state factual determinations retain §2254(e)(1)'s presumption and clear-and-convincing rebuttal requirement where applicable; an unmade finding that a complete investigation occurred receives no invented presumption, and that factual burden does not replace the objective legal inquiry. The sufficient ground here is §2254(d)(1).

### 3. The complete supported sentencing record establishes prejudice

**Holding and operative rule:** A sentencing ineffective-assistance claim requires a reasonable probability of a different lawful penalty decision, sufficient to undermine confidence in the result, after the reliable omitted mitigation is assessed with all aggravation and the original presentation. Wiggins establishes that probability here; because neither state court adjudicated prejudice, the Court determines that distinct constitutional element without displacing a state finding on it.

**Controlling explanation:** Strickland requires actual prejudice, not an assumption that deficient investigation or an unhappy childhood changes the sentence. Terry Williams applies the reasonable-probability standard to the complete supported record, without demanding that a different result be more likely than not. The jury heard the stipulated absence of prior violent convictions and counsel's principalship argument but no developed history of Wiggins's sustained deprivation and physical and sexual abuse. The supported omitted account materially changes the sentencing picture. The murder of a seventy-seven-year-old victim by drowning and the robbery retain their full aggravating weight. The Court relies on the reliable core of the history, with no identified prior violent-conduct history to offset it, rather than automatically crediting every later allegation. Under the applicable Maryland penalty structure, the record creates a reasonable probability that at least one conscientious juror would decline death; a bare possible holdout or idiosyncratic mercy would not suffice. Visciotti sustained an actual state no-prejudice assessment within the lawful range. Here there was no such state assessment, and its whole-record method supports rather than replaces the independent prejudice inquiry.

### 4. The established sentencing violation requires a conditional sentence writ

**Holding and operative rule:** After the independent §2254(d)(1) showing and proof of both Strickland elements, Wiggins is entitled to a conditional writ requiring a constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence within a reasonable period fixed by the District Court. The writ leaves the conviction and independently lawful custody intact.

**Controlling explanation:** The investigation and prejudice holdings establish entitlement to sentence relief on the supported record. Terry Williams provides the conditional remedy that gives the State a reasonable opportunity to conduct lawful sentencing. Maryland may choose an adequate new penalty proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence; repeating the original penalty decision without adequate defense preparation would not comply. Sattazahn protects a defined final mandatory life judgment after a completed capital opportunity. This case instead concerns an invalid death judgment, so that final-life predicate does not remove Maryland's lawful resentencing option. The District Court must implement the writ through a definite deadline, notice of the State's selected course and verification of the resulting sentencing order. If Maryland fails to comply, the court must enforce the writ against confinement under the invalid death sentence and any execution, while preserving custody supported by the undisturbed conviction subject to a lawful sentence.

## Precedent Treatment

- Strickland v. Washington is applied to reasonable investigation, informed strategic choice and separate reasonable-probability prejudice.
- Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams) explains the objective §2254(d)(1) inquiry and whole-record prejudice and supplies the conditional sentence remedy; its later explanation does not create the earlier substantive duty.
- Lockett v. Ohio and Eddings v. Oklahoma supply the legal relevance of individual mitigation without making every item compulsory or dispositive.
- Early v. Packer requires whole-rationale review and preserves the application of general constitutional rules without a factual twin.
- Woodford v. Visciotti retains its whole-record method and is distinguished because its state court actually adjudicated prejudice, while Maryland's courts did not.
- Lockyer v. Andrade and Price v. Vincent retain the independent statutory restriction; the present record crosses it on its own facts.
- Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania is distinguished at its final-life predicate; lawful new sentencing remains available after relief from this death judgment.

## Law After Decision

Strickland's established investigation duty applies to the actual decision to stop gathering available mitigation, separately from counsel's choice about presentation. Federal courts must still establish the statutory gateway and both constitutional elements. Where the state court adjudicated performance only, clearing that gateway does not supply prejudice; the supported full record must establish it independently. These requirements warrant a conditional sentence writ for Wiggins while preserving the distinction between lawful resentencing and custody under the undisturbed conviction.

## Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, dissenting.** They would affirm denial of relief because Maryland's assessment remained within the range permitted by §2254(d)(1). Strickland allows reasonable limits on investigation, and the state court could reasonably view counsel's existing records and their principalship strategy as an adequately informed choice in the actual context, even if additional investigation would have been better. The statutory inquiry is not a fresh selection of the preferred professional judgment. Their disagreement concerns whether Wiggins has established objective unreasonableness; they do not endorse an automatic defense whenever counsel invokes strategy, demand a factually identical precedent, treat Williams as the source of a new earlier duty, or presume that a complete investigation occurred. Because this statutory ground is sufficient for denial, they do not independently decide actual deficiency or prejudice. Their votes therefore supply neither a no-deficiency nor a no-prejudice holding.

## Procedure After Action

The District Court must set a reasonable, definite compliance deadline, require notice of Maryland's selected sentencing course and verify completion through the resulting sentencing order. It must enforce the writ if the State fails to complete a constitutionally adequate penalty proceeding or impose a lawful noncapital sentence within that period. The conviction and independently lawful custody remain intact.
