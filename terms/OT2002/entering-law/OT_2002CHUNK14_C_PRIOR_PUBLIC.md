### Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, No. 94-329

Merits decision, June 29, 1995

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 1, 1995. On writ of certiorari to the Fourth Circuit, which sustained denial of the publication’s printing benefit on Establishment Clause grounds after identifying viewpoint discrimination. The federal speech and establishment questions concern this program.

All nine Justices participate: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Fourth Circuit and requires removal of the religious-viewpoint exclusion from the generally available printing benefit.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Viewpoint exclusion and asserted Establishment justification | Reversed, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Viewpoint-neutral eligibility on otherwise lawful program terms; remand |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Both program-specific holdings and remedy |
| Concurrence | O’Connor | Unjoined | Full bounded Court join; safeguards and compelled-fee challenge reserved |
| Concurrence | Thomas | Unjoined | Full Court join; separate civic-benefit/history explanation |
| Dissent | Souter | Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer | Would affirm because of direct devotional-production support |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Religious viewpoint in the private publication program

**Controlling proposition:** The University may not exclude an otherwise eligible private student publication from its generally available printing benefit because its editorial viewpoint is religious. The rule applies to this privately edited, independently attributed publication program on its otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms; it does not require funding every religious activity or every subject.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas; five Justices adopt the program-specific viewpoint rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the University’s decision to support independent student expression does not make each publication its own speech. Perry permits definition of a limited forum and its subjects, but not discrimination against a religious perspective on otherwise included subjects. Lamb’s Chapel and Widmar supply religious-viewpoint equality in genuinely opened private-expression channels. Here the broad publication program leaves editing with the students, requires independence disclaimers and pays approved outside printing expenses. The denial treats primarily religious belief or manifestation as a forbidden activity while allowing comparable student perspectives. That operation excludes viewpoint rather than neutrally defining a government message. The Court does not erase the distinction between recognition, facilities, distribution and money, or equate every expenditure with a speech forum. Nor does it find the organization ineligible under a separate organizational rule. The religious-viewpoint exclusion must be removed; other lawful program terms remain. Actual payment amounts and properly available independent defenses remain subject to the federal viewpoint holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association: preserves limited-forum definition while forbidding viewpoint discrimination.
- Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches; Widmar v. Vincent: apply private religious-viewpoint equality without assuming that facility access decides every subsidy.
- Regan v. Taxation With Representation: distinguishes definition of a government financing program from discriminatory exclusion within this opened private-publication arrangement.

##### Permissible printing support on the actual program terms

**Controlling proposition:** Providing this neutral printing benefit to the otherwise eligible private religious publication does not itself violate the Establishment Clause. Its secular student-expression purpose, broad neutral availability, independent editorial attribution and controlled vendor payments matter together; neutrality alone is not a universal authorization of devotional financing or governmental sponsorship of worship.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia and Thomas join this concrete program conclusion. Lemon remains governing law; no new universal framework is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the publication’s religious message remains private under the actual safeguards of this program. The fund serves broad student expression rather than government worship, and officials approve printing expenses without adopting the students’ editorial choices. Required disclaimers and independent organizations separate the private message from the University. Vendor payments provide the defined printing service rather than unrestricted grants to churches. Witters and Mueller supply the relevance of neutral benefits and private direction, without making beneficiary neutrality conclusive in every setting. Under the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry, this arrangement does not become governmental religious speech merely because one publication includes devotion and proselytizing. Kiryas Joel’s denominational allocation of governmental power is different; its neutrality limits and retention of Lemon remain. The contrary direct-financing objection gives too little weight to the concrete private-expression structure. Zobrest’s actual procedural remand supplies no aid-merits approval. Compelled-fee refund claims, unrestricted religious support and any particular monetary award remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lemon v. Kurtzman: retains the existing purpose, effect and entanglement inquiry; no universal replacement is adopted.
- Witters v. Washington Department of Services for the Blind; Mueller v. Allen: apply neutral-benefit and private-direction distinctions within the actual program, not neutrality alone.
- Board of Education of Kiryas Joel v. Grumet: distinguishes denominational governmental authority and retains religious-neutrality limits.
- Zobrest v. Catalina Foothills: preserves the actual antecedent-law remand; supplies no religious-aid merits holding here.
- Lee v. Weisman: preserves limits on governmental religious participation and coercion; this private printing benefit compels no student worship.
- Church of Scientology v. Clearwater: distinguishes RFRA burdens and exemptions from constitutional financing authority.

The denial concerned religious activity, defined by the Guidelines as primarily promoting or manifesting belief in or about deity or ultimate reality, rather than an established finding of religious-organization ineligibility. The organization rule separately concerns devotion as organizational purpose. Political restrictions separately exclude principal-purpose lobbying and candidate electioneering while permitting incidental or insubstantial advocacy, nonpartisan study/research and ideological views; those permissions are not exceptions to the religious-activity rule. Permitted ability-to-perform membership limits likewise remain distinct. Abandoned free-exercise/state individual-rights claims and unchallenged equal-protection intent findings are not reopened.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Lemon remains governing; Zobrest is procedural rather than an aid-merits holding. No denominational political power, compulsory worship or unrestricted religious subsidy is authorized.

Otherwise eligible private student publications receive viewpoint-neutral access to this defined printing benefit. Independent expression and the program’s safeguards do constitutional work; this is no rule that every public expenditure creates a forum or every formally neutral religious subsidy is lawful.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O’Connor concurs and joins the Court in full at its program-specific level. Organizational independence and disclaimers, approved vendor payments, a vigorous competing forum and the availability of a properly presented objection by students who oppose compelled fees provide safeguards; the last is a possible challenge, not an existing opt-out or refund award. Neutrality alone is insufficient. Thomas also concurs, joining the Court while explaining his own view that equal participation in broadly available civic benefits should not turn solely on a financial-versus-in-kind classification; that broader historical theory is not adopted by the Court. Souter, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer, dissents. Mandatory fees pay for devotional production, and routing money to the printer does not turn that support into mere facility access. Ball and Nyquist distinguish direct support of religious activity; the beneficiary’s own editorial choice is not an independent third party’s selection under Witters and Mueller. The dissent does not exclude all religious viewpoints or invalidate every neutral private religious benefit.

#### Source Notes

Fourth Circuit, 18 F.3d 269; Student Activities Fund Guidelines, petition pp. 61a–67a; respondent’s opposition, p. 20 n. 11, and reproduced appendix passages, Records and Briefs collection, No. 94-329. The asserted Virginia funding defense is distinct from abandoned affirmative state-rights claims. Its applicability and available remedies remain unresolved; no payment amount is guaranteed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Fourth Circuit is reversed and the case remanded. The University shall remove the religious-viewpoint exclusion and apply otherwise lawful eligibility, payment and administrative terms. Any properly available independent Virginia funding defense must be examined consistently with the federal viewpoint holding; no applicability, validity or revival of forfeited defenses is declared. No full requested amount, damages, immunity, fee or compelled-fee refund determination is made.

**End of entry: Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, merits decision, June 29, 1995.**

### Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, No. 94-12

Merits decision, March 27, 1996

#### Chronology and Posture

Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, No. 94-12; 517 U.S. 44. October Term 1995. Argued October 11, 1995; decided March 27, 1996. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1254(1) to the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing its January 18, 1994 immunity judgment, 11 F.3d 1016.

The Tribe sued Florida and Governor Lawton Chiles in his official capacity under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. The district court denied immunity dismissal on June 18, 1992; the court of appeals reversed and directed dismissal. The questions concern congressional authorization of a prospective direct-State action under the Indian Commerce Clause and a prospective officer action under Ex parte Young. Alabama is not a party to this docket.

A separate September 22, 1993 merits summary judgment for Florida and the Governor and its stayed Eleventh Circuit appeal, No. 93-5256, remain outside this immunity review. The complaint alleges a January 29, 1991 negotiation request and September 19 filing; the United States describes a December filing. Either interval exceeds 180 days, but the discrepancy establishes no amendment history. The actual prayer for a compact within sixty days, later mediation and a game-scope declaration is distinct from the government's narrower proposed good-faith negotiation or declaratory relief.

#### Judgment

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Operative effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Governor Chiles | Reversed and remanded, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Remove the categorical bar to the connected officer action; retain IGRA's conditions and lawful prospective limits. |
| Florida | Immunity-dismissal direction set aside and remanded, 5–4 as to this common relief | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas | Remove that appellate command without a majority holding establishing State amenability. |

The Florida component contains three distinct primary positions: Stone-Zsela and Stevens would reverse and sustain the direct State action; Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer would vacate without deciding amenability; O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would affirm. The first five share removal of the dismissal direction and remand, not a constitutional or avoidance rationale. Stone-Zsela expressly disagrees with stopping short of sustaining the State action.

The State claim is not terminated by the immunity ruling. This mandate supplies no majority determination establishing jurisdiction over Florida and no finding that jurisdiction is absent. Any State merits adjudication requires a lawful jurisdictional basis. The three Justices' additional avoidance sequencing is not a Court instruction. The separate September 1993 merits judgment and stayed appeal are not vacated, decided or treated as an undecided district-court merits action. No bad faith, compact, gaming entitlement, license, damages or taxing power is awarded.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing or component | Author | Joined by | Scope and relationship to judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Souter | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Seven Justices reject the categorical Article III objection. Stevens retains serious doubt; Stone-Zsela does not join this explanation. |
| Opinion of the Court, Parts II–III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer | Five Justices adopt the IGRA-specific officer route and its statutory and remedial limits. |
| Florida judgment announcement and shared remand | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer as to the common operative relief | Five support setting aside the immunity-dismissal direction. Stone-Zsela and Stevens agree with that component of the judgment only; they additionally would sustain direct-State amenability. |
| Opinion respecting the State claim, Part IV | Souter | Ginsburg and Breyer throughout | Three prefer avoidance and explain an alternative common-law/Article I analysis. The alternative's activation condition is absent; it supplies no actual abrogation joins. |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins Parts II–III and common State removal; would additionally sustain the prospective direct-State action and rejects treating officer availability as sufficient reason to withhold that relief. |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stevens | No other Justice | Joins Parts II–III and common State removal; would sustain the State action on his broader nontextual-immunity ground; retains serious Article III doubt without an independent dismissal vote. |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas throughout | All four join Part I; they would affirm both immunity dismissals, overrule Union Gas's constitutional conclusion and exclude the officer route under this statute. |

Six Justices—Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas—actually adopt unmistakable direct-State statutory intent in their separate writings. The other three accept that proposition only in their unused alternative. This agreement concerns statutory authorization, not constitutional power. No aggregation of conditional votes or broader separate reasons supplies a controlling State-amenability rule.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The statutory judicial decision is not categorically advisory

**Controlling proposition:** Adjudicating IGRA's federal good-faith negotiation dispute is a judicial function where the judgment conclusively determines the parties' legal relations and activates constrained statutory consequences. The prospect of later mediation and executive action, and the absence of a guaranteed compact, do not make the court's decision merely advisory.

**Authority:** Part I has seven direct supporters: O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens's serious reservation is not an eighth join; Stone-Zsela supplies no independent join.

**Controlling explanation:** A court decides an existing dispute over a federal duty and, upon the requisite finding, changes the parties' legal obligations and access to the next statutory stage. That supplies effective partial relief even though neither the desired compact nor every desired game is assured. The decision's legal operation matters; the answer does not rest solely on whether the Secretary can edit individual findings.

Aetna and Nashville reject a universal requirement that judicial relief take the form of an immediately executable coercive decree. Brimson supports judicial adjudication in aid of administration, while its actual enforceable-order setting prevents treating it as an answer to every intermediate proceeding. Ferreira and the relevant Gordon and Hayburn materials require attention to whether the purported judgment has legal effect or remains subject to executive revision. IGRA's binding determination and prescribed consequences satisfy that concern here.

Wisconsin's partial-redress rule concerns the judicial capacity to afford meaningful relief. Morgan Stanley and Plaut address Congress's ability to establish a new rule requiring renewed adjudication of specified completed proceedings without dictating the judicial resolution of underlying claims. Neither line determines whether IGRA's conclusive adjudication of a good-faith negotiation dispute ceases to be judicial merely because subsequent mediation or executive action remains. The Court neither determines every later preclusion question nor manufactures coercive remedies or a new cause of action to make the statutory decision judicial.

##### II. IGRA permits this connected officer action within its own limits

**Controlling proposition:** Ex parte Young permits a prospective official-capacity action against Governor Chiles to enforce an ongoing violation of IGRA's good-faith negotiating duty, subject to the statute's full sequence and remedial limitations. IGRA's State-directed wording and detailed procedure do not exclude that route in this case.

**Authority:** Part II has five direct supporters: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. The common rule is specific to IGRA; it does not adopt the three Justices' additional general clear-indication theory.

**Controlling explanation:** Young provides the established route against a responsible officer for an ongoing federal violation. The Governor's representatives participated in negotiations, supplying the required connection to the alleged failure. Rejecting categorical dismissal does not require proof that he alone can bind Florida to every compact term. Mandatory good faith differs from discretion over those terms.

Congress imposed a federal negotiating duty and prescribed how its enforcement progresses. References to the State do not, in this arrangement, exclude the official through whom its obligations are performed. The scheme limits the officer action rather than authorizing a free-standing injunction with greater remedies. This construction answers the objection that an officer could evade Congress's chosen restraints: both sixty-day periods, mediation, selection, consent and Secretarial conditions remain effective. It does not treat remedial detail as irrelevant or establish that every statutory right carries officer relief.

The State's involvement in negotiations establishes neither waiver nor unlimited compacting authority. Incorporation of state gaming law into the federal obligation does not itself convert the claim into a Pennhurst demand to enforce state law. Hafer's individual-capacity damages rule supplies no basis for this action, and Edelman's retrospective-relief boundary remains.

The actual request for a compact and declaration does not establish entitlement to either. A court cannot prescribe its preferred compact, enlarge the good-faith duty, bypass mediation or recast accrued compensation as an injunction. The statutory progression, rather than an added coercive order compelling agreement, governs failure to reach agreement. The categorical officer dismissal must therefore be reversed while the remaining merits and lawful relief receive their proper treatment.

##### III. Removing the officer bar does not establish the predicates for compacting relief

**Controlling proposition:** The remand permits only relief authorized by IGRA upon the actual statutory predicates. It neither resolves good faith or gaming scope nor disturbs the distinct merits judgment and appeal. The following conditions govern any relief through the recognized officer route.

**Authority:** Part III has the same five direct supporters. The four opposing the route preserve the same enacted conditions in their dissent; recognizing those conditions does not imply their agreement to reversal.

**Controlling explanation:** A valid duty, an available enforcement defendant and entitlement to a particular remedy are different questions. IGRA makes adverse findings, successive waiting periods and specified mediation and administrative steps do separate work. Removing immunity cannot substitute for any of them. Nor does the actual prayer enlarge the relief Congress supplied. The September 1993 merits judgment and its stayed appeal must receive their own lawful disposition; this immunity mandate decides neither. The Court accordingly preserves the statutory progression and independent gaming conditions below without ordering that progression to begin as though bad faith were already established.

**Complete statutory conditions and limits:**

- Under 25 U.S.C. §2710(d)(3)(A), a qualifying request by the Tribe with jurisdiction over the relevant Indian lands triggers the State's duty to negotiate in good faith, not a duty to accept preferred terms. A failure-to-negotiate suit under (d)(7)(A)(i) may begin only after 180 days. The effective-compact enforcement action under (A)(ii) and Secretary's enforcement action under (A)(iii) remain distinct.
- Under (B)(ii), tribal evidence that no compact exists and that the State failed to respond or respond in good faith precedes the State's burden to prove good faith. Nonagreement alone does not establish bad faith. An actual adverse finding triggers (B)(iii)'s order to the State and Tribe to conclude a compact within the first sixty-day period. The court **may** consider public interest, public safety, criminality, financial integrity and adverse economic effects on existing gaming; it **shall** consider a demand for direct taxation of the Tribe or Indian lands as evidence of bad faith.
- If no agreement follows during that period, (B)(iv) requires each side's last-best proposal to a court-appointed mediator. The mediator selects the proposal best comporting with IGRA, other applicable federal law, and the court's findings and order. Submission to both parties under (B)(v) begins a **second sixty-day period** for State consent. Consent makes the proposal an agreed compact under (B)(vi).
- Nonconsent during that second period triggers notice to the Secretary under (B)(vii). The Secretary **shall** prescribe procedures, consulting the Tribe and acting consistently with the selected proposal, IGRA and relevant state law. The express findings/order selection criterion appears in (iv), not verbatim in (vii). The Secretary lacks unrestricted discretion to withhold all action after the enacted predicates occur.
- State nonparticipation does not automatically select the Tribe's proposal. The government's qualified prediction supplies no textual default. The adopted construction adds neither contempt nor an order compelling agreement merely because agreement or participation during the ordered period fails; statutory progression is the consequence. No rule concerning every separate obstruction or disobedience sanction is decided.
- Section (d)(4) grants no new state taxing power while preserving regulatory-cost assessments agreed under (d)(3)(C)(iii). That exception is distinct from the mandatory treatment of direct-tax demands as bad-faith evidence. Absence of other taxing authority does not justify refusing negotiation.
- Class III gaming still requires approved tribal legislation, a State permitting such gaming for some person, organization or entity, and an effective compact subject to applicable remedial provisions; licensing and regulation remain separate. Under (d)(8), disapproval may rest on IGRA violations, other federal law **not relating to jurisdiction over gaming on Indian lands**, or federal trust obligations. Forty-five-day inaction produces approval only insofar as consistent with IGRA; Federal Register publication remains necessary.
- Section 2721's severability provision preserves qualifying remaining law but does not satisfy a finding, waiting period, mediation, selection or nonconsent condition and creates no alternative program. The distinct asserted Secretary substitute remedy remains unadjudicated. The Court neither endorses an automatic bypass nor reverses the lower court's separate substitute-remedy determination. Immunity dismissal alone satisfies none of the enacted predicates.

##### IV. Direct-State intent is agreed upon, but State amenability is unresolved

**Majority-supported statutory proposition:** IGRA unmistakably authorizes a direct action naming the State. Its specified State-party action, State evidentiary burden, order to State and Tribe and successive State-directed steps satisfy the clear-statement requirement. General federal-question jurisdiction, §1362 or scattered references to States alone would not suffice.

**Authority and force:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens and the four dissenters actually adopt this statutory construction across their writings. Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer accept it only if their alternative State route must be reached. The six-position agreement establishes only the exact statutory-authorization proposition stated above; it creates no common State-amenability opinion. Statutory intent and constitutional authority remain separate: no controlling holding sustains or rejects Florida's amenability, and no majority opinion decides the constitutional question. It also supplies no sequencing rule requiring abrogation to be decided before officer relief.

**Common judgment, without common State rationale:** Five support setting aside the immunity-dismissal direction and remand. Two support affirmative direct-State relief; three support nonmerits vacatur; four oppose either change. Avoidance is not a logical subset of the two Justices' affirmative constitutional grounds. No narrower controlling constitutional rationale can be extracted by treating their different reasons as interchangeable.

**Other limits:** No express waiver, automatic reciprocal tribal/state consent, constructive waiver by negotiating, or delegation of the United States' immunity exemption establishes this suit; the United States is an amicus. The Court does not reach the distinct Tenth Amendment defense first raised on appeal and not decided below. The question-scope limitation is prudential: X-Citement Video permits an appropriate respondent defense without requiring resolution of this distinct constitutional issue. No universal forfeiture rule, jurisdictional incapacity or independent mediator-appointments invalidation is announced.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Ex parte Young:** Applied to this connected officer and ongoing federal statutory duty. The recognized route carries IGRA's limits and establishes no universal rule that detailed statutes preserve officer equity.
- **Edelman:** Its prospective/retrospective boundary remains. An injunction cannot be used to obtain accrued treasury compensation; prospective compliance costs alone do not decide that classification.
- **Pennhurst:** Its restriction on federal orders enforcing state law remains. Incorporation of state gaming law as part of the federal IGRA obligation does not itself turn this action into a state-law enforcement claim.
- **Union Gas:** Not overruled. Its actual damages judgment, statutory holding and five-vote constitutional conclusion remain precedent within their decided scope. The fractured rationale neither erases that conclusion nor makes every plurality sentence binding. Four Justices would overrule the constitutional conclusion; their dissent changes no law. Three unused constitutional alternatives cannot be aggregated with two affirmative State votes to create a present holding.
- **Hans:** Not overruled. The separate common-law accounts of its nontextual immunity do not become a Court holding, and the State set-aside does not abolish its rule.
- **Fitzpatrick:** Its distinct Fourteenth Amendment authority is unaffected; the decision establishes no new Reconstruction ground.
- **Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227:** Applied for the judicial character of specific, conclusive declaratory relief in an actual dispute over present rights. The declaratory statute is procedural; it creates neither a constitutional controversy nor an otherwise missing substantive action. The absence of a damages or injunction award is not alone disqualifying.
- **Nashville, Chattanooga & St. Louis Railway v. Wallace, 288 U.S. 249:** Applied for the proposition that an adverse, conclusive declaratory adjudication can be judicial without execution or coercive process. Its actual state-tax controversy does not establish that a declaratory label alone supplies legal effect or an IGRA remedy.
- **ICC v. Brimson, 154 U.S. 447:** Applied as support for judicial adjudication assisting administration, while preserving its ordinary adversarial proceeding, conclusive judgment and judicially enforceable order. Contempt there followed disobedience of the court's order, not merely the agency's request. It neither establishes an IGRA contempt remedy nor alone answers whether a differently structured intermediate judgment is judicial.
- **United States v. Ferreira, 54 U.S. 40:** Distinguished. The judge acted as a claims commissioner in a process lacking ordinary suit, parties, process and court record; no debt arose without Treasury approval, and the Secretary could revise the award. IGRA's conclusive adjudication and prescribed legal consequences differ. Ferreira's reserved appointments question supplies no mediator ruling; the appended Yale Todd account is a later inferential report, not a recorded reasoned holding.
- **Gordon v. United States, 69 U.S. 561, and the later appendix at 117 U.S. 697:** The actual disposition dismissed for want of appellate jurisdiction without a contemporaneous reasoned opinion. Taney's prepared paper, considered after his death and later printed, was not a delivered majority opinion; the contemplated Court opinion was never prepared. Its execution language informs the historical concern about legal effect contingent on another branch, but is not an unqualified holding requiring execution in every judicial case.
- **Hayburn's Case, 2 U.S. 409:** The reported Supreme Court proceeding produced no merits decision. The circuit judges' communications printed in the note explain objections to executive suspension and legislative revision of purported judicial decisions. They remain relevant historical materials, not a reasoned Supreme Court holding deciding IGRA's constrained later process.
- **Wisconsin v. City of New York:** Its concrete partial-redress holding supplies a limited analogy. It did not decide whether IGRA assigns a judicial function, eliminate Stevens's doubt or guarantee the claimant's preferred ultimate result.
- **Morgan Stanley/Plaut:** Article III permits Congress to require reinstatement of qualifying completed private securities limitations dismissals under §27A(b)'s objectively defined procedure while courts independently determine eligibility and adjudicate the claims. The holdings do not establish an unrestricted power to reopen completed merits judgments and do not determine whether IGRA's conclusive adjudication ceases to be judicial because mediation or executive action follows.
- **Puerto Rico Aqueduct:** Applied to the immediate appeal of the conclusive immunity denial. Appealability resolves neither immunity nor waiver, abrogation or the officer route's merits.
- **Hess:** Its entity-specific arm-of-State analysis does not resolve Florida's own immunity or congressional abrogation.
- **Hafer:** Its personal-capacity damages holding remains separate from the official-capacity prospective action recognized here.
- **New York v. United States:** Its compulsory regulation-or-ownership rule and distinct permitted inducements remain effective. It does not resolve the separate Tenth Amendment defense left unadjudicated here.
- **Voting Rights Coalition:** Its election-power grounds and United States enforcement posture supply no general Indian Commerce abrogation or state-command rule.
- **X-Citement Video:** Its allowance for an appropriate prevailing-respondent defense is preserved without compelling adjudication of this distinct Tenth Amendment issue. The Court's question-scope limit remains prudential.

#### Law After Decision

From March 27, 1996, the controlling rules reject the categorical advisory-function objection to this legally effective IGRA adjudication and permit the connected prospective officer action within the complete statutory sequence. No general rule that every detailed scheme preserves or excludes Young is adopted. Six actual writers agree that IGRA clearly authorizes a State action, but the judgment creates no controlling rule on Congress's power to subject Florida to that action.

The State-removal judgment binds the parties without a controlling State-amenability rationale. Union Gas remains precedent; the decision neither adopts a general Article I abrogation power nor establishes the dissent's general Article I prohibition. Statutory duty, cause, defendant, constitutional power, merits and remedy remain separate. The judgment supplies no direct-State jurisdiction by default, Secretary bypass, new damages action or gaming authorization.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins the officer holding and statutory limits and supports removal of Florida's dismissal direction, but would affirmatively sustain Congress's clearly enacted prospective direct-State action. Indian Commerce authority, the ongoing federal duty and unmistakable authorization support that route. Prospective compliance costs do not become accrued treasury compensation merely because payment is needed. Congress's express enforcement channel and the Tribe's continuing-duty claim warrant retaining a forum. Young independently reaches the connected officer, with the same statutory restraints. Neither route permits a preferred compact, bypassed mediation, enlarged duty or disguised retrospective damages; lawful relief should avoid double or inconsistent commands.

He expressly refuses to treat officer availability as sufficient reason to stop short of sustaining the State action. He retains Union Gas's actual damages judgment without adopting every broader abrogation rationale. He reserves materially new compensatory, punitive or restitutionary liability, compulsory state-court actions and different statutory schemes, while recognizing that already-decided Union Gas questions remain settled within its scope. He supplies no new waiver or Reconstruction ground.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins Parts II–III and would sustain both routes. Outside the Eleventh Amendment's actual textual restriction, Hans immunity is judicial doctrine subject to displacement by valid congressional legislation. IGRA supplies the affirmative direction absent from Hans; Congress cannot amend the Constitution or exceed Article III. His rationale is not confined to prospective Indian Commerce litigation and does not require overruling Hans's actual result. He retains Union Gas's constitutional conclusion and leaves the textual issue concerning out-of-state or foreign-citizen plaintiffs open; the Tribe is not treated as such a plaintiff. His own remedial-intent analysis supports the constrained officer action, and he accepts lawful officer-only resolution as a narrower sufficient alternative without abandoning his affirmative State position.

He nevertheless retains serious doubt whether this intermediate judicial step, particularly if separate executive relief could make it dispensable, is fully judicial under Article III. That is a reservation, not a vote to dismiss independently. Wisconsin's partial-redress holding does not settle this different judicial-function issue. He does not formally join Souter's entire separate theory.

**Souter, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, respecting the State claim in Part IV.** They prefer the established officer route and vacatur of Florida's immunity-dismissal direction without deciding abrogation. Intricate remedies and State wording alone do not clearly exclude established prospective equity; Congress may limit statutory enforcement, but they find no sufficient indication of exclusion here. This is distinct from inventing a personal damages action. They would address whether State adjudication is necessary in light of lawful officer relief while preserving lawful jurisdiction before State merits adjudication or a proper nonmerits resolution. The Tribe might forgo its State claim or adequate officer relief might support denying duplicative relief, but neither fact is found. They prescribe no automatic abandonment, mootness, dismissal or indefinite stay. Their additional sequence is not the Court's mandate.

If Young were rejected and Florida's still-live immunity claim adjudicated, they would recognize the direct-State clear statement and uphold congressional displacement of Hans immunity under valid Article I power. They regard that nontextual immunity as federal common law, retained by stare decisis absent deliberate legislative displacement, rather than constitutional immunity Congress can never alter. Constitutional text, limited prior holdings and legislative control of common law independently support that account; it is not a wholesale adoption of Brennan's Union Gas reasoning. Their view is not confined to prospective relief or commerce, but decides no damages or unrelated statute. Because Young succeeds here, these constitutional views remain conditional alternatives, not actual joins in a State-amenability holding.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join the judicial-character ruling and find unmistakable direct-State intent, but would affirm both dismissals. Their constitutional rule is broad: original Article I powers cannot overcome the constitutional immunity limiting federal judicial power over a nonconsenting State. Prospective relief does not cure the defect; neither Indian/interstate commerce nor proprietary/governmental distinctions justifies retaining Union Gas. They would expressly overrule its constitutional conclusion while leaving its statutory construction and Fitzpatrick's later Fourteenth Amendment authority intact.

They acknowledge Union Gas's five-vote constitutional conclusion rather than declaring it never precedent. In their view its conflict with the constitutional immunity structure, fractured rationale and deficient integration with prior immunity decisions supply the special justification for correction. They reject the opposing distinction between a common-law default and congressional displacement, without assuming zero reliance or proof that clear-statement rules are empirically unworkable. Their premise does not adjudicate every other statute or remedy, grant immunity to every public entity, foreclose United States enforcement or eliminate ordinary Young relief.

#### Chronology and Posture

#### Holdings

##### An ongoing federal property-right claim may proceed against responsible officers within real remedial limits

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eleventh Amendment does not bar a claim for prospective declaratory or injunctive relief against officials actually connected to an alleged ongoing violation of federal tribal property rights merely because adjudicating that violation requires resolving a disputed federal title question or affects important sovereign interests. The decree must address proved unlawful future official conduct and may not bind the immune State's title, award damages or retrospective compensation, execute against state property or require the State itself to convey title.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela's Opinion of the Court, joined by the other four members of the five-Justice Court coalition identified in Opinion Topology. The rule has five direct joins and requires no fractured-opinion synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** Ex parte Young permits federal courts to restrain officers' continuing violation of federal law. Tindal and the officer-property reasoning of Treasure Salvors distinguish adjudication affecting an officer's lawful conduct from a judgment binding the sovereign's own title. Oneida supplies the federal character of the pleaded tribal property rights. The complaint identifies land-regulating officials, including Land Board officials and the water-resources director, whose challenged authority allegedly causes the interference; a general duty to obey law would not suffice.

The State's strongest objection concerns the actual breadth of the prayer: exclusive use and exclusion of state regulation could transfer practical control of the waters. Calling relief prospective does not answer that objection. But the Ninth Circuit expressly withheld a decree binding Idaho's title, and the case remains at the pleading stage. Whether a federal right exists and what lawful officer remedy follows must be adjudicated, not assumed. Every Young injunction constrains asserted state authority; the importance of that authority cannot itself decide the federal claim or create a property-specific exemption from review.

Edelman and Green preserve the limits against retrospective relief; Pennhurst bars federal commands founded on state law, not these pleaded federal rights. Seminole's actual officer holding is consistent with bounded enforcement but does not decide every statute or the separate State-amenability question. No IGRA procedure is transferred to this property dispute. The separate United States case covers only part of the area and supplies neither complete substitute relief nor waiver. Affirmance permits ordinary adjudication and tailored relief against connected officers; it does not approve every requested remedy or determine title.

**Precedent treatment:** Young is applied without a sovereign-lands exception or generalized state-interest balance. Tindal and Treasure Salvors supply the officer/sovereign property distinction; Oneida supplies federal-question character. Edelman, Green and Pennhurst retain their respective limits. Seminole remains at its actual officer-route scope and is not recast as a universal immunity or abrogation ruling.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Ex parte Young:** Applied to alleged ongoing federal violations by responsible officers; no categorical sovereign-lands exception or general state-interest balancing rule is added.
- **Tindal v. Wesley:** Applied to distinguish relief involving officers' possession from a judgment binding an absent sovereign's title.
- **Florida Department of State v. Treasure Salvors:** Its officer-property reasoning supports that distinction; the decision does not authorize a decree binding Idaho itself.
- **Oneida Indian Nation:** Applied for the federal-law character of the asserted tribal property rights, without deciding those rights' merits.
- **Edelman v. Jordan:** Preserves the bar on retrospective monetary recovery against the State.
- **Green v. Mansour:** Preserves limits on retrospective declaratory relief; a continuing violation must support the prospective officer action.
- **Pennhurst:** Preserves the distinct state-law restriction; the pleaded claims here arise under federal law.
- **Seminole Tribe:** Its actual prospective IGRA officer holding remains valid within that statute's sequence. Its separate State-amenability disposition supplies no controlling general rationale; Union Gas is not overruled by this decision.

#### Law After Decision

Prospective federal enforcement against responsible officers remains available when a disputed federal tribal title question must be decided, subject to genuine limits protecting the immune State and excluding retrospective or State-directed relief. The Court rejects a sovereign-lands exception to that route. The Tribe must still establish its federal rights, an ongoing violation and an appropriate remedy. The judgment supplies no title result or general Article I abrogation rule.

#### Separate Writings

**O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** They would reverse the officer portion because this actual demand seeks comprehensive practical ownership and exclusion of Idaho's sovereign regulation over navigable waters. In their view that combination is the functional equivalent of the barred sovereign-title decree, despite nominally prospective language and the limitation that Idaho is not formally bound. Tindal and Treasure Salvors do not, for them, permit this complete displacement of sovereign control. Their proposed exception turns on the unusual scope and operation of the requested relief, not every suit involving land, costly compliance or an important state interest. They preserve ordinary Young actions and reject free-ranging balancing, state-forum exhaustion and the assumption that the narrower United States case provides complete relief. They would dismiss the present comprehensive officer demand without determining title or foreclosing a genuinely different prospective claim.

### Inter-Modal Rail Employees Association v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co., No. 96-491

Merits decision, May 12, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued March 17, 1997. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, March 27, 1996, 80 F.3d 348. The question is whether ERISA §510 excludes interference with welfare-benefit attainment because those benefits do not vest. The claim arises from a transfer of cargo-handling work and alleged discharges intended to avoid benefit obligations; the Court reviews a categorical pleading-stage dismissal, not proved intent or liability.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 8–1 as to the formal disposition.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, who would reverse and remand with a direction to reinstate the welfare claims for the remaining statutory inquiries. He does not favor affirmance of the categorical exclusion.

The Court sets aside the reviewed categorical welfare ground only. It finds no §510 violation, orders no employment reinstatement or damages, and does not independently reopen the other lower-court components.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela in Part I | Part I supplies the unanimous coverage rule; Part II supports the eight-Justice vacatur | Part I: statutory coverage and limits. Part II: remand, reservations and unaffected claims. |
| Concurrence in part and dissent from the formal disposition | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins Part I; would reverse rather than vacate | Reinstatement of the welfare claims for consideration of remaining predicates, without finding a violation. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Section 510 includes prospective welfare-plan rights

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §510 protects against a covered discharge or other prohibited action undertaken for the purpose of interfering with attainment of a right a participant may become entitled to under an employee welfare benefit plan, even though the benefit is not subject to ERISA's statutory vesting requirements. Lawful authority to amend or terminate a welfare plan does not itself authorize interference through conduct forbidden by §510.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Ginsburg's opinion is joined by all eight other Justices. All nine directly adopt this statutory coverage rule and its limits, notwithstanding their different formal dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that welfare benefits are within the attainment clause's protection. ERISA defines employee benefit plans to include welfare and pension plans. Section 510 addresses future attainment, while Congress elsewhere used narrower pension and nonforfeitable-benefit terms. Section 1051(1) exempts welfare plans from the participation and vesting part; it does not create an additional exemption from §510. The separate exercise-of-right and prospective-attainment clauses must retain their distinct work.

Respondents' strongest objection is that protection of amendable benefits would vest them indirectly and obstruct ordinary cost-saving restructuring. But a lawful revision of plan terms differs from a covered employment action undertaken for the prohibited purpose. Curtiss-Wright preserves amendment authority while requiring its lawful exercise. Lockheed separates sponsor design from implementation and independent statutory commands. Those decisions do not authorize evasion of plan procedures through a prohibited discharge. Enforcing §510's own conduct and purpose conditions answers the objection without erasing an entire defined category of plans.

The employees allege that the transfer of cargo-handling work and their discharge were designed to avoid contributions and minimize benefit payments. Those allegations must not be treated as findings. The absence of statutory vesting cannot alone sustain dismissal of their welfare theory. This holding does not promise continued employment, freeze plan terms, establish intent from cost reduction or settle every meaning of attainment. In particular, the separate argument that employees already eligible for benefits have already attained the relevant right remains open. Actual eligibility, entitlement, action, purpose, causation and authorized relief require their own consideration.

##### II. The categorical dismissal is vacated for the remaining statutory inquiries

**Holding and operative rule:** The Ninth Circuit's welfare-interference dismissal is vacated because its categorical nonvesting ground is erroneous, and the case is remanded for consideration of the properly remaining grounds. Plaintiffs must establish the applicable participant or beneficiary status, relevant plan right, prohibited action, interference purpose, causation and remedy authorized through §510's reference to §502; this decision establishes no violation or automatic recovery.

**Authority:** Part II of Justice Ginsburg's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; eight direct joins support this vacatur and its scope. Stone-Zsela does not join Part II and would reverse the welfare dismissal with the reinstatement direction stated in his separate opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The court of appeals dismissed the welfare theory because the benefits did not vest. Rejection of that ground does not decide every alternative argument the lower courts have not addressed. Whether a participant already eligible for the particular welfare benefit can invoke interference with attainment requires first consideration below. So do properly preserved questions concerning specific intent, eligibility, causation, mixed motives or business justification, proper defendants and relief. The Court adopts no new business-necessity test or presumption that a benefits-reducing transfer is unlawful.

Varity's function-specific fiduciary holdings and qualified individual-equity rule do not resolve this independent §510 theory. Mertens preserves the limits of the remedy actually authorized by §502; statutory coverage does not itself authorize compensatory damages. No employer or administrator status automatically establishes liability, and the circuit's nonemployer-conspiracy footnote is not adopted as a new holding of this Court.

The pension claim's reinstatement, Railroad Retirement Act exclusion, association-standing ruling, conditional request for individual class representatives, FELA and Rule 23 rulings, and fee-sanction remand are not independently reopened. The limitations contention declined below because it was first raised at appellate argument is not revived. These boundaries follow the granted welfare question; they do not affirm or reverse unrelated lower rulings by implication.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen:** Preserves lawful amendment authority and its actual procedural exercise; that power supplies no immunity for independently prohibited discharge.
- **Lockheed Corp. v. Spink:** Applies the distinction between sponsor design and independent statutory obligations without classifying every business decision as fiduciary administration.
- **Varity Corp. v. Howe:** Its fiduciary and qualified individual-equity holdings remain separate; neither those holdings nor the disagreement about them eliminates §510's express route.
- **Mertens v. Hewitt Associates:** Preserves the equitable-category limit where §502(a)(3) relief is sought; coverage creates no compensatory-damages authorization.
- **Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon:** Its pension-interference setting remains intact; it did not already decide the disputed welfare-plan coverage question.

#### Law After Decision

Section 510 now expressly reaches prospective welfare-plan attainment without a vesting prerequisite. The distinction between lawful changes to plan terms and prohibited conduct directed at participants governs; nonvesting benefits remain amendable. Purpose, actual protected rights, proper defendants, causation and authorized remedies retain independent force. The meaning of attainment for already eligible participants remains unresolved, and no general unfair-dismissal or permanent-benefits rule is created.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Chief Justice Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and dissenting from the formal disposition.** He agrees that the enacted definition and future-attainment language include welfare plans, and that lawful amendment power does not authorize evasion through a prohibited discharge. Congress knew how to refer to pensions and nonforfeitable benefits and did not impose a vesting element in §510. He would reverse the welfare-claim dismissal and direct reinstatement of those claims for consideration of specific intent, eligibility, causation, any preserved mixed-motive or business-justification issue, proper defendants and remedy. He makes no finding of a violation. Whether these employees had already attained the particular benefits remains open, and neither nonvesting benefits nor a defendant's nonemployer or nonadministrator role is converted into automatic liability. His disposition does not restore every dismissed claim, guarantee recovery or create permanent benefits.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The consideration requirement is not facially invalid under the First Amendment

**Holding and operative rule:** In this competitively selective arts-grant program, §954(d)(1)'s requirement to consider general decency and respect for diverse public beliefs and values, alongside artistic excellence and merit, is facially permissible because it prescribes neither a categorical viewpoint exclusion nor fixed disqualifying weight. The consideration is mandatory; an invidious discriminatory application, a penalty on independently financed expression or coercive suppression remains separately reviewable.

**Authority:** The narrow facial-validity part is directly joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas support the judgment on a broader ground; their votes are not added to this rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The statute requires consideration, so the Court cannot treat the words as optional or erase them through panel diversity. But attention to a consideration is not an instruction to deny every work that causes offense. Artistic grants require comparative judgments about excellence, merit, audience and suitability within a program that cannot support every applicant. These considerations can operate contextually without a mandatory viewpoint veto. The facial challenge therefore does not establish that the enacted criterion must operate unconstitutionally in the manner claimed.

Rosenberger protects viewpoints within a generally available private-publication benefit; it does not classify every expenditure as that benefit. Forbes permits genuine selection in its particular candidate debate while preserving review for viewpoint discrimination; it supplies no automatic immunity for arts officials. Denver Area keeps private editorial permission distinct from governmental burdens and yields no universal subsidy rule. Those distinctions permit this narrow conclusion without treating private art as the government's own speech.

The contrary argument correctly insists that consideration must do real work. It does not establish that every meaningful contextual judgment must become a prohibited disqualification. Scarcity alone would not excuse an actual invidious denial. The Court decides neither a particular award nor the legality of a condition suppressing speech financed outside this program. The separate obscenity prohibition in §954(d)(2) is not rewritten.

##### 2. Comparative artistic standards do not establish facial vagueness here

**Holding and operative rule:** In this selective grant scheme, the decency and respect considerations, read with artistic excellence and merit, are not facially void for vagueness merely because comparative artistic judgment cannot be reduced to precise objective criteria. This conclusion concerns discretionary arts patronage, not a criminal prohibition, a license required to speak or an objective entitlement to payment.

**Authority:** Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer directly join the narrow vagueness part. Scalia and Thomas independently reject the claim through their broader subsidy rationale. Stone-Zsela does not decide it.

**Controlling explanation:** The scheme necessarily asks decisionmakers to compare works according to artistic judgments. Precision appropriate to a coercive speech prohibition cannot simply be demanded of every such comparison without also disabling ordinary judgments of excellence and merit. The challenged considerations operate within that selective function and impose no criminal liability or permission requirement for creating art. The possibility that uncertainty can discourage applications does not make all aesthetic imprecision unconstitutional. Actual viewpoint discrimination remains distinct and reviewable; permissible imprecision is not permission to conceal an unlawful criterion. Souter's First Amendment objection addresses that separate alleged criterion rather than uncertainty inherent in awarding scarce artistic support.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Rosenberger:** Its viewpoint rule remains tied to the private-publication opportunity actually opened; not every subsidy must be structured as that program.
- **Forbes:** Genuine selective judgment and review of viewpoint discrimination remain compatible. Public ownership and a selective purpose create no blanket constitutional immunity.
- **Denver Area:** Its distinct permission and compulsory-burden holdings remain distinct; none supplies a universal governmental-speech or subsidy exception.
- **Rust and Maher:** The four-Justice spending discussion uses their program-definition and funding-priority distinctions. That discussion establishes no broader holding authorizing viewpoint conformity throughout private arts patronage.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged consideration requirement survives this facial challenge, and permissible imprecision in this comparative arts setting supplies no separate vagueness basis for invalidation. No categorical viewpoint veto, automatic-disqualification rule, required weight, panel-composition safe harbor or universal grant exemption is created. Invidious applications and conditions burdening independent speech retain their own constitutional questions. Section 954(d)(2)'s obscenity prohibition and otherwise lawful artistic and administrative criteria remain separate.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Supplementary spending discussion by Breyer, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy.** These four Justices add that program-specific appropriations may define what government chooses to support without financing every activity. Rust and Maher illustrate distinctions between funding a defined program and prohibiting independent activity. The observation does not replace the narrower statutory construction or authorize every discriminatory arts award. Ginsburg withholds assent from this further discussion; it has no independent controlling force.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** Government may choose the art it subsidizes, including a preference for decency and respect, without thereby preventing a rejected artist from creating the same work independently. This broader account treats the statutory command as real and does not depend on reducing it to a harmless consideration. Rosenberger's broadly available printing benefit and Forbes's opened candidate opportunity differ from discretionary arts patronage. The concurrence likewise rejects importing speech-related vagueness doctrine from coercive regulation into ordinary grant selection as such. It supplies no approval of penalties on independently funded speech, disguised regulation, every possible unconstitutional condition or departures from the rules of a forum actually opened.

**Souter, joined by Stone-Zsela in the First Amendment part, dissenting.** Requiring officials to consider decency and respect for public beliefs directs attention to whether private art conforms to prevailing views. That criterion disfavors offensive, irreverent and challenging expression even if it is not dispositive in every case. Treating mandatory consideration as legally inconsequential would delete Congress's command, while panel diversity does not remove it. Rosenberger's protection of independent private viewpoints and Forbes's continuing viewpoint constraints cannot be evaded merely because artistic support is selective. The program supports private expression, not a governmental message. Genuine neutral judgments concerning merit, subject, audience, educational suitability, finances and administration remain available; invalidating the proviso would not require funding every applicant. These two Justices would retain facial First Amendment relief without deciding settled individual claims, unrelated remedial questions or public financing of religious exercise.

Souter alone adds that artistic comparative judgment necessarily permits imprecision and joins the Court's narrow rejection of facial vagueness. His objection is to the viewpoint criterion, not unavoidable uncertainty in assessing art. Stone-Zsela does not join this statement and leaves vagueness unresolved.

### Almendarez-Torres v. United States, No. 96-6839

Merits decision, March 24, 1998

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 14, 1997; decided March 24, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, reviewing its affirmance of an 85-month sentence. The question is whether the prior aggravated-felony conviction in 8 U.S.C. §1326(b)(2) is an element of aggravated unlawful reentry or a sentencing factor that need not appear in the indictment.

The September 1995 indictment charged unlawful reentry under §1326 without alleging the prior aggravated-felony convictions. At his December 1995 plea, Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions and deportation; he preserved his objection that the indictment authorized no more than §1326(a)'s two-year maximum. The District Court imposed the enhanced sentence in March 1996.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter and Ginsburg.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer.

The enhanced sentence is vacated. On the existing indictment and plea, the sentence may not exceed §1326(a)'s two-year maximum. The guilty plea is not withdrawn and no acquittal is entered. Whether another source of law permits a further proceeding on a properly charged aggravated offense is not decided.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg | Supports reversal and resentencing | Statutory element classification, charging consequence and resentencing remedy |
| Opinion announcing the judgment, Part II | Scalia | Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg | Supports reversal and resentencing | Constitutional-avoidance explanation; no definitive constitutional classification rule |
| Concurrence in Part I and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Concurs in Part I and in the judgment | Definitive constitutional ground for the element classification; does not join Part II's reservation of that question |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer | Would affirm | Penalty construction and bounded constitutional permission for this recidivist enhancement; would affirm |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Section 1326(b)(2) requires an aggravated-offense allegation before its higher maximum may apply

**Holding and operative rule:** The prior aggravated-felony conviction specified in §1326(b)(2) is an element of the aggravated unlawful-reentry offense. It must be alleged in the federal indictment and, unless validly admitted as an element of the charged offense, proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt; an admission to the fact of a prior conviction does not cure its omission from this indictment, so the existing conviction permits no sentence above §1326(a)'s two-year maximum.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. These five expressly adopt the statutory rule, its ordinary element consequences and the remedy. Four explain the construction through constitutional avoidance; Stone-Zsela gives a definitive constitutional ground separately. No majority adopts a general constitutional rule governing all facts that increase a statutory maximum.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that subsection (a)'s complete unlawful-reentry offense and two-year maximum must be distinguished from subsection (b)(2)'s aggravated offense carrying a twenty-year maximum. The prior conviction authorizes a different punishment ceiling; it does more than inform selection of punishment within the ordinary range. The provisions' cross-references permit that element construction. Hamling requires the indictment to state the offense's elements, and Gaudin requires proof of an actual element to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt when it has not validly been admitted. Almendarez-Torres admitted the prior convictions, but the indictment did not charge that aggravating element. His factual admission therefore does not answer the preserved objection to the punishment legally authorized by this charge.

The traditional use of recidivism at sentencing does not decide this statute's classification. McMillan addressed a minimum within an unchanged maximum, and Watts permits ordinary sentencing findings within conviction-based authority while reserving element evasion and other constitutional limits. Custis addresses collateral attacks on existing convictions, not the indictment's content. Old Chief confirms that prior-conviction status can be proved without unnecessary prejudicial detail; that evidentiary concern does not require treating status as a sentencing factor. The decision does not determine a subsidiary standard-of-proof claim, invalidate the admitted prior convictions, select a replacement sentence, or decide facts that do not increase the statutory maximum.

**Precedent treatment:** Hamling and Gaudin govern the consequences of the statutory classification; McMillan, Watts, Custis and Old Chief retain the distinct boundaries stated below.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Hamling v. United States:** Applied to require an indictment alleging each element of the aggravated offense.
- **United States v. Gaudin:** Applied after statutory classification; its jury-and-proof rule for actual elements remains controlling without deciding all sentencing classifications.
- **McMillan v. Pennsylvania:** Distinguished because its mandatory minimum left the statutory maximum unchanged; its limited holding remains intact.
- **United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra:** Preserved for ordinary sentencing within punishment authorized by the conviction; it does not resolve this higher-maximum classification.
- **Custis v. United States:** Distinguished as a collateral-review decision; the existing limits on attacking prior convictions remain intact.
- **Old Chief v. United States:** Applied to the distinction between element status and the form of adequate prior-conviction proof; no broader compulsory-stipulation rule is created.

#### Law After Decision

Section 1326(b)(2)'s higher range requires the aggravated-offense element and the corresponding charging and proof protections. This statutory rule binds lower courts. The Court establishes no general constitutional prior-conviction exception and no general constitutional rule that every maximum-increasing fact is an element. Ordinary consideration of prior convictions within an independently authorized range remains available, subject to governing law.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, Part II.** These Justices regard the tenfold increase in maximum punishment as presenting serious doubt about Congress's ability to assign the aggravating fact solely to a sentencing judge. The element reading is fairly available, so they apply constitutional avoidance and decline to decide that ultimate question. McMillan's unchanged ceiling and Watts's ordinary sentencing setting do not eliminate the doubt. The risk of prejudice from prior-conviction evidence can be addressed through adequate admissions or other proper proof rather than removal of an element from the charge.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in Part I and in the judgment.** The constitutional allocation turns on the fact's legal effect. A fact necessary to increase the authorized maximum from two years to twenty years performs the work of an element and must be charged and either validly admitted as an element of that offense or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Legislative placement cannot transfer that work entirely to the sentencing judge. Gaudin protects element determination; Watts permits ordinary judicial sentencing findings only within conviction-based authority. Custis concerns collateral attack, and Old Chief demonstrates how adequate status proof can avoid unnecessary prejudice. The admitted facts here do not cure an indictment that never charged the aggravated offense. This position permits consideration of prior convictions within a separately authorized range, preserves Custis's collateral-review limits, requires no prejudicial details when adequate admission or stipulation establishes the element, and decides no sentencing fact that leaves the statutory maximum unchanged.

**O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, Thomas and Breyer, dissenting.** Subsection (b)(2)'s recidivist subject, reciprocal cross-references and punishment structure make it a penalty provision for the alien already described in subsection (a). Existing conviction status differs from facts about commission of the present crime, and Congress may treat that prior adjudication as a sentencing factor in this setting. The admission supports the application but is not a substitute for their statutory classification. These Justices acknowledge that McMillan and Watts do not alone resolve an increased ceiling; the distinct recidivism ground does that work. They would affirm without adopting unlimited legislative power to remove offense elements or resolving an unpresented subsidiary proof-burden question.

#### Source Notes

The applicable provision is the [1994 text of 8 U.S.C. §1326](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title8/pdf/USCODE-1994-title8.pdf), which increased the earlier fifteen-year aggravated-felony maximum to twenty years. The published account of the indictment, plea, preserved objection and sentence appears in [523 U.S. 227–229](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-523/pdf/USREPORTS-523-224.pdf). That account does not establish the exact charged found-in date or an intentional waiver of the indictment objection. No temporal challenge or broader waiver is decided.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fifth Circuit for resentencing. The enhanced sentence cannot remain in effect under the existing indictment and plea; the sentencing court must act within §1326(a)'s maximum. No new aggravated-offense proceeding or particular replacement term is ordered.

**End of entry: Almendarez-Torres v. United States, Merits decision, March 24, 1998.**

### College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board, No. 98-149

Merits decision, June 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, reviewing 131 F.3d 353 (1997), which affirmed dismissal of the federal Lanham Act false-advertising action on State-immunity grounds.

The questions concern Congress's Fourteenth Amendment §5 authority to remove State immunity for unfair-competition claims alleging false or misleading advertising of the State's own product, and whether continued interstate marketing after express statutory notice of federal suit supplies prospective Parden consent. The grant contains no reformulation. The separate patent action is not before the Court in this docket.

CSB, a New Jersey bank selling CollegeSure CDs, alleges that Florida Prepaid, an admitted arm of Florida offering competing tuition-prepayment products, misrepresented its own product in brochures and annual reports. This claim concerns neither appropriation of CSB's trademark nor false statements about CSB's product. The United States intervened to defend the statute. The alleged advertising violation and resulting loss remain unproved.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and the other eight Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The reversal removes only the federal Lanham Act immunity dismissal. No majority adopts a common affirmative ground for State amenability.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress expressly authorized federal suit and otherwise available remedies

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 1122(a)–(b) and 1125(a)(1)(B), (a)(2) unmistakably subject States and their instrumentalities to federal suit and otherwise available legal and equitable remedies for covered Lanham Act violations occurring on or after October 27, 1992. That enacted intent establishes neither constitutional power, State consent, a statutory violation nor entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** Part I, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 1122(a) expressly identifies State immunity and the federal forum. Subsection (b) separately provides remedies to the same extent as against nongovernmental defendants, preserving each remedy's conditions. Section 1125(a)(2) expressly includes States, while §1125(a)(1)(B) covers commercial misrepresentations about one's own or another's goods, services or commercial activities. The statutory clarity is not defeated by general coverage ambiguity. Seminole distinguishes Congress's unmistakable authorization from its power to impose the action; Lane, Blue Fox and West likewise keep substantive duties and actual remedial authority distinct in their own settings. Giving these provisions effect still requires a lawful basis for overcoming immunity and proof of the claim. The words do not find Florida's assent or turn an alleged violation into an award.

##### This own-product-advertising theory does not establish the required §5 basis

**Holding and operative rule:** The alleged loss of customers from Florida Prepaid's misdescription of its own product does not establish a constitutional entitlement to prospective customers or truthful competitive self-advertising, and the statutory tort and economic injury alone do not identify a constitutional deprivation to which this §5 remedy is fitted. Section 5 therefore does not sustain the asserted application on this theory; established business assets, goodwill and legal claims are not categorically excluded from constitutional property.

**Authority:** Part II, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stevens disagrees. The eight-Justice proposition is controlling despite the different judgment votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Roth and Paul require identification of the actual constitutional entitlement. Haddle permits statutory recovery for economic injury without equating that injury with deprivation of constitutional property. Sullivan preserves fair adjudication and established substantive entitlements while refusing to infer an unconditional payment right from a general statutory benefit. Those distinctions matter here: commercial injury and a federal remedy do not themselves establish the property deprivation that §5 enforces.

Boerne permits Congress to prevent constitutional injuries by reaching conduct that is not independently unconstitutional. The absence of a completed violation in each plaintiff's case is therefore not the objection. The defect is the absence of an identified constitutional deprivation to which the particular own-product-advertising remedy is fitted. Invoking a going business and goodwill does not make every lost sale caused by a competitor's self-description a deprivation of those assets without due process. Congress cannot supply the missing constitutional connection merely by creating the statutory tort.

The rule decides neither trademark appropriation, palming off, trade secrets, false statements about CSB's product nor every application of §1122. It creates no universal exclusion-right definition of property and demands neither formal legislative findings, a prescribed quantity of evidence, a violation in every jurisdiction, an individual constitutional violation for every prohibition, geographic limits nor a sunset. A properly supported preventive §5 remedy remains possible.

##### Continued ordinary commerce is not unequivocal consent to federal suit

**Holding and operative rule:** A State's continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking does not unequivocally submit it to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even when the undertaking is realistically optional. Congress's legislative intent is distinct from the State's voluntary decision to consent.

**Authority:** Part III, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer disagree.

**Controlling explanation:** Beers, Great Northern and Atascadero require the State's actual, unequivocal submission to the federal forum. Clear congressional language answers what Congress intended, not whether Florida chose to submit. A practical opportunity to leave ordinary commerce does not turn general regulation into acceptance of a specifically offered federal benefit or voluntary invocation of judicial authority. The relevant conduct remains operating the undertaking; the Court does not characterize the State as choosing to violate federal law.

The contrary prospective-condition argument gives proper weight to clear notice and realistic choice, but those features do not supply the missing act of assent in this commercial setting. Welch displaced the old inference from general FELA or Jones Act coverage. The express 1992 text cures a clarity problem without establishing a State waiver. The residual Parden inference necessary to this action therefore fails.

The Court does not adopt the Third Circuit's governmental-function classification. Nor does it disturb Hilton's independently retained FELA coverage and competent-state-court enforcement based on statutory continuity and reliance. Genuine voluntary waiver, properly conditioned federal benefits and interstate compacts remain distinct; no particular funding condition or coercion question is decided. Appearance and the dismissed counterclaim supply no holding here because the distinct litigation-waiver question is not reached.

##### The reversal has no common affirmative amenability rationale

**Judgment without controlling affirmative rationale:** Five Justices reverse the federal immunity dismissal. Stone-Zsela relies on Article I and an included-argument view of review scope; Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer rely on prospective commercial consent, and Stevens adds §5. These are distinct affirmative grounds, not five joins in any one of them.

**Authority and limit:** The Part V judgment binds the parties and permits the federal claim to return for ordinary adjudication. Parts I–III retain their direct majority support. No Marks rule makes the four-Justice consent position controlling: a participating majority expressly rejects that operative consent proposition, and consent and unilateral congressional displacement depend on different legal predicates. Stevens's §5 ground likewise cannot displace the eight-Justice rejection. No common constitutional or review-scope rule is extracted by combining unused alternatives.

The decision neither sustains nor rejects a general Article I abrogation power. It reaches no litigation-waiver question, particular accepted-benefit condition, new officer claim, underlying advertising merits or separate patent issue. A prospective officer action would require a connected officer, ongoing federal violation, proper claim and genuinely prospective relief; Edelman's retrospective-compensation boundary and Coeur d'Alene's actual limits remain.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Union Gas:** Its private damages judgment, statutory holding and five-vote constitutional conclusion remain effective within their decided scope. No majority here extends or overrules that conclusion, and Brennan's entire plurality does not become a majority rule.
- **Seminole Tribe:** Its clear-authorization distinction is applied. It established no controlling direct-State amenability rule or categorical Article I prohibition and did not overrule Union Gas.
- **Parden, Welch and Hilton:** The residual inference of federal-forum consent from ordinary commercial participation is rejected even under the new explicit enactment. Parden's independently retained substantive FELA coverage and Hilton's specialized state-court enforcement and reliance holding remain intact; no wholesale overruling of those retained propositions occurs.
- **Beers, Great Northern and Atascadero:** Their requirement of State consent supplies the distinction between unmistakable congressional intent and unequivocal State submission.
- **Boerne, South Carolina v. Katzenbach, Katzenbach v. Morgan and City of Rome:** The enforcement/redefinition boundary is applied while retaining real preventive legislation reaching nonviolations and each precedent's constitutional setting. No new numerical or formal legislative-record requirement is imposed.
- **Roth, Paul, Haddle and Sullivan:** Their distinctions between constitutional entitlement, statutory injury and fair adjudication are applied without denying established intangible property or converting every statutory tort into a constitutional deprivation. Logan's protection of a legal claim does not equate the underlying alleged commercial injury with a completed due-process violation.
- **Hans and Fitzpatrick:** Their existing force is unchanged. The separate federal-question/common-law account does not become a Court holding, and the distinct Reconstruction power remains available on its proper predicates.

Murphy, Kolstad and Olmstead retain their actual scope applications rather than creating an automatic rule for every alternative source of congressional power.
- **Hess, Lane, Blue Fox, Schacht, Cedar Rapids and West:** Their arm-status, remedy-specific consent, removal and accepted-funding distinctions remain confined to their actual settings. They establish neither Florida's commercial consent nor this claim's liability.
- **Ex parte Young, Edelman and Coeur d'Alene:** Their defendant, ongoing-violation and prospective-relief requirements remain; this judgment creates no officer defendant or substitute damages action.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 23, 1999, the federal immunity dismissal is removed in this action, but no common affirmative rationale supplies a general rule permitting other direct-State Lanham suits. The express statutory authorization remains distinct from constitutional power, State consent and entitlement to relief.

The Court adds a controlling rejection of the proposed explicit prospective commercial-participation consent theory and a confined rejection of the asserted own-product-advertising §5 basis. It preserves actual voluntary consent, fitted preventive §5 legislation and Hilton's retained FELA state-court coverage. Union Gas remains precedent at its decided scope; neither a categorical Article I permission nor prohibition emerges here. Lower courts must respect the direct majority propositions and the mandate without treating the four-Justice consent theory, the one-Justice Article I theory or Stevens's separate §5 theory as a unified holding.

**Stone-Zsela's nonmajority Part IV.** He would give effect to Congress's express federal remedy against a State selling competing investment products in interstate commerce. Union Gas supplies a retained Commerce-power conclusion; Seminole supplies no contrary categorical rule. On the federal-question account supported by Stevens's Union Gas concurrence, the Amendment's citizen-State diversity restriction does not prohibit this congressionally created federal action, and valid deliberate legislation may displace nontextual Hans immunity. That view does not depend on guessing Union Gas's corporate citizenship or adopting every statement of its plurality.

He regards the alternative source of congressional authority as an included legal argument for the same preserved federal immunity challenge, on the same established facts and for the same requested relief. He acknowledges the specifically framed §5 question but distinguishes the generic grant from a separate claim, new factual theory or expressly excluded question. Lebron supports the included-argument distinction; he does not rely on a prevailing-respondent defense or briefing alone. His position is confined to enforcement of this federal commercial obligation, not unlimited power to abolish every State immunity. He rejects constructive waiver and automatic constitutional property and finds no advertising violation or entitlement to relief. No other Justice joins these constitutional or scope grounds.

**Breyer, joined by Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg, concurring in part and in the judgment.** They find consent where Congress unmistakably and prospectively makes specified commercial conduct subject to private federal enforcement and the State continues an undertaking it can realistically choose not to operate. The relevant undertaking is competitive investment marketing, an optional way to further education, not education itself. The choice is whether to conduct that undertaking under the stated terms, not whether to violate the law. Clear advance notice, continuing covered activity and meaningful choice distinguish the condition from the displaced inference based on general statutory coverage.

They answer the objection that Congress alone imposed liability by treating this explicit prospective condition as a term accepted through continued optional activity. They do not assume a signed promise, federal subsidy, profit figure, exit-cost finding or withdrawal event. Nor do they revive original Parden, erase Florida Prepaid's arm status under Hess, or transfer Lane's federal consent and Cedar Rapids' accepted funding into an imaginary grant bargain. They would reverse and permit ordinary statutory adjudication without deciding direct Article I abrogation. Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join the Court's confined §5 rejection; Stevens does not.

Congress may protect against that category of constitutional injury through a defined unfair-competition remedy without requiring every covered advertisement itself to constitute a completed constitutional deprivation. He finds a reasonable preventive relationship, assessed under Boerne's congruence-and-proportionality rule and the presumption of legislative validity, between protection of the existing business interest and this private remedy.

He accepts the distinction between prevention and constitutional redefinition, rather than substituting general rational-basis review for Boerne. Haddle's statutory-injury distinction does not deny existing business property, and Sullivan's protection of substantive claims reinforces the need to identify and protect that interest. The same claim and the usefulness of an alternative do not themselves establish fair inclusion or an independently justified exception. Lebron concerns an included legal argument, while X-Citement Video permits a prevailing respondent to defend the same favorable judgment; CSB seeks reversal. Murphy and Olmstead reinforce attention to the actual grant; each leaves a presented but ungranted question undecided.

Their affirmance does not apply a categorical Seminole prohibition that the Court never adopted. Union Gas survives, and they leave the distinct Article I route undecided in this review. They preserve genuine voluntary waiver, valid §5 enforcement and properly available officer relief while finding neither of the presented routes sufficient for this claim. They would leave only the reviewed federal immunity dismissal in force and decide no underlying advertising violation, broader TRCA application or separate proceeding.

### Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank, No. 98-531

Merits decision, June 23, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 1999; decided June 23, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the Federal Circuit, reviewing 148 F.3d 1343, which affirmed the District of New Jersey's denial of Florida Prepaid's sovereign-immunity motion.

The question is whether Congress had power under §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment to abrogate State immunity and authorize federal patent-infringement suits through 35 U.S.C. §§271(h),296(a). CSB, the respondent, also defends the existing immunity denial on alternative grounds. The generic grant does not itself present every distinct constitutional source. The immunity denial is immediately reviewable before final adjudication of infringement.

CSB, a New Jersey bank, owns Patent No.4,722,055 and alleges that Florida Prepaid's competing tuition-prepayment program infringes it. Florida Prepaid is an admitted arm of Florida. Knowing and willful infringement and the requested declaratory, injunctive, and monetary relief remain allegations and requests. The Federal Circuit sustained §5 authority without deciding the preserved commercial- or litigation-consent theories.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and the other eight Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

The Federal Circuit's judgment sustaining denial of immunity remains in place. The case returns for ordinary adjudication of patent validity, infringement, causation, defenses, and lawful remedies. No liability, damages, trebling, fees, compulsory license, or injunction is awarded.

The four dissenters would vacate the immunity-denial judgment and remand for ordinary treatment of the preserved actual-litigation-consent contentions and any other properly available ground under retained law. They would not direct unconditional dismissal, find consent, order additional proof or a hearing, or decide the patent merits. Their limited vacatur/remand is distinct from the Court's affirmance and return to patent proceedings.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer as to Parts I and IV; Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer as to Parts II–III | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer support affirmance on each independent constitutional ground; the other four dissent from affirmance | Part I: common statutory clarity, patent-property/deprivation distinction, and proper respondent alternatives. Part II: Section 5 independently sustains the action. Part III: Article I independently sustains the action. Part IV: continued commercial participation is not consent, applying College Savings Part III; actual consent remains available, and litigation-conduct waiver is not decided. |
| Dissent in part | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas | Joins Parts I and IV; opposes affirmance and would vacate and remand on limited terms | Rejects affirmative §5 fit; opposes the Article I extension on the stated structural grounds while preserving the limited-remand nonreach of an independent Union Gas overruling; preserves actual-submission contentions without directing dismissal. |

Parts I and IV have all nine Justices' direct joins. Parts II and III each have five: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Stone-Zsela writes no separate opinion. Both constitutional grounds control independently; no Marks synthesis is needed, and commercial consent is not a premise of either holding.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Congress unmistakably authorized this federal patent action against States

**Operative proposition:** Sections 271(h) and 296 expressly subject States and their instrumentalities to private patent actions in federal court for covered violations on or after October 28, 1992. Section 296(b) makes the otherwise available patent remedies available on their own statutory terms; that authorization does not itself establish constitutional power, State consent, infringement, or entitlement to a particular award.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part I, joined by all nine Justices, directly adopts this statutory proposition and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress did not leave State coverage to inference from a general reference to persons or to federal-question jurisdiction. Section 271(h) identifies States and instrumentalities as persons subject to the patent obligations. Section 296(a) expressly addresses immunity, private claimants, and the federal forum; subsection (b) separately identifies available remedies. Seminole distinguishes this enacted authorization from the constitutional source of amenability, and Welch does not make general statutory coverage sufficient consent to federal suit. These provisions satisfy the distinct clarity inquiry. They still require a valid basis for proceeding against the State and proof of every element and remedial condition. No infringement, compensable injury, enhancement, fee award, or injunction follows from the text alone.

##### Patent property and unconstitutional deprivation remain distinct

**Operative proposition:** A patent is an established exclusive property interest; federal creation does not disqualify it from Fourteenth Amendment protection. Recognizing that property and alleging infringement do not alone establish deprivation without due process, compensable injury, or entitlement to an award; process, remedies, alleged conduct, and any applicable culpability requirement require separate treatment.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join this precise distinction in Part I. No additional patent-negligence rule or §5 fit conclusion receives that coalition's support.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 261 and the established patent-property authorities recognize an exclusive interest, rather than a mere expectation of continued competitive success. But a statutory infringement claim and a constitutional deprivation claim have different requirements. Available process and remedies cannot be assumed adequate or inadequate from the property's existence. Neither the allegation of knowing or willful infringement nor the request for damages adjudicates those additional matters. The Court does not decide whether Daniels's prison-negligence rule transfers wholesale to patent deprivation: Part II expressly reserves that extension. This common distinction supplies neither a judgment that §296(a) is valid §5 enforcement nor one that it fails substantive fit.

##### Appropriate respondent alternatives are legally available within their limits

**Operative proposition:** A respondent may defend an unchanged favorable judgment on an appropriately supported alternative that neither enlarges its rights nor reduces the adversary's rights under that judgment. CSB's posture permits consideration of its preserved defenses of the immunity denial, but preservation and briefing alone do not expand the §5 question or establish an alternative's merits.

**Authority:** Part I has all nine Justices' joins at this precise level. Their actual decisions on the alternative grounds remain distinct.

**Controlling explanation:** X-Citement Video permits an appropriately supported defense of an unchanged favorable judgment, while Izumi, Madsen, and Lebron preserve the boundary between included legal grounds and independent questions. Murphy leaves its two presented but ungranted questions undecided, while preserving the respondent-defense route for an unchanged judgment. Kolstad's inclusion of attribution within punitive relief and Olmstead's inclusion of the statutory duty's fundamental-alteration limit illustrate why the actual question and legal relationship matter; neither automatically includes a different constitutional source. CSB seeks to retain the denial of immunity, not obtain expanded relief. That posture permits consideration of properly supported alternatives without compelling every Justice to decide each one or establishing factual predicates by assumption. No general Article I reach rule, automatic consent finding, or exception for any preserved issue follows.

##### Section 5 independently sustains the authorized patent action

**Operative proposition:** Section 5 permits Congress to prevent unconstitutional property deprivations through congruent and proportional enforcement legislation reaching some conduct that is not independently unconstitutional. Sections 271(h) and 296 validly authorize this patent action on that basis; the plaintiff need not establish a separate constitutional violation to proceed under the Act.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, states an independently sufficient holding of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Fitzpatrick establishes Congress's authority to subject States to suit when enforcing the Fourteenth Amendment. Boerne requires congruence and proportionality between the threatened constitutional injury and the means of prevention, while permitting some reach beyond independently unconstitutional conduct. Patents are existing exclusive property interests protected by that Amendment. Congress enforced those obligations rather than redefining the constitutional right.

Exclusive federal infringement jurisdiction and uncertain alternative State remedies support Congress's judgment that immunity could leave unconstitutional deprivations without adequate redress. State use can cause completed loss that prospective relief cannot repair. Proof difficulties and uncertainty about effective compensation reinforce the need for prevention in this defined field. Ordinary infringement can occur without a constitutional violation, but that does not defeat a remedy fitted to the risk of unconstitutional deprivation. National uniformity and equal competition alone would not supply Section 5 power; the protected property and the remedial relationship do.

The Court does not find every State remedy inadequate or equate alleged willfulness with a constitutional wrong. Whether Daniels's negligence rule transfers to patent deprivations remains reserved. The Act's validity does not require each plaintiff to prove a constitutional tort, and allowing this action preapproves no particular award. Compensation, enhanced damages, fees, and injunctions retain their own conditions and properly presented constitutional limits.

##### Article I independently sustains the expressly authorized action

**Operative proposition:** Congress may authorize private enforcement against States when the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within its Article I authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes the State's exposure to suit. The Patent Clause, together with the Necessary and Proper Clause, supports this federal patent remedy as an independent ground for sustaining the immunity denial.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, states an independently sufficient holding of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress created a national patent system, concentrated infringement adjudication in federal court, and expressly included States in §§271(h) and 296. Uniform patent rights lose important content if a State may exploit a patented invention while withholding the ordinary federal remedy. The Patent Clause and Necessary and Proper Clause authorize enforcement of this valid federal obligation through the expressly enacted action.

Union Gas's retained five-vote constitutional conclusion supplies the Article I foundation. The Court extends that conclusion into the enforcement principle stated here without treating every proposition in its plurality rationale as binding. Seminole distinguished unmistakable statutory authorization from constitutional amenability and set aside the State-dismissal direction without a controlling constitutional rationale. It established neither a categorical Article I prohibition nor an overruling of Union Gas, and required no abrogation-before-officer sequence. The present holding resolves the constitutional question it left open.

The Eleventh Amendment limits the citizen-State diversity grants, not jurisdiction over this federal question. CSB's out-of-State citizenship does not change the claim's federal source. Hans does not establish an immunity that Congress cannot displace in this setting. The structural objection therefore cannot defeat an enforcement measure within Congress's enumerated authority and an unmistakable choice to permit suit.

X-Citement Video permits consideration of this preserved alternative source of power because CSB defends the same immunity-denial judgment without enlarging its relief. The holding is claim-specific: it removes immunity from this authorized action, finds no infringement or damages entitlement, and awards no relief. Neither this ground nor Section 5 depends on commercial participation constituting consent.

##### Continued commercial participation does not supply consent to this suit

**Operative proposition:** A State's continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes suit does not constitute consent: Florida Prepaid did not consent to federal patent suit by continuing its prepaid-tuition program after §§271(h) and 296 took effect. Actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as an express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available. Whether Florida Prepaid's litigation conduct waived immunity need not be decided, and no waiver finding is made.

**Authority:** Stevens's Part IV, joined by all nine Justices, applies College Savings Part III and states these limits.

**Controlling explanation:** College Savings Part III, announced earlier the same day, holds that a State's continued operation of an otherwise lawful commercial undertaking does not unequivocally submit it to federal jurisdiction merely because Congress clearly and prospectively subjects that activity to private federal enforcement, even when the undertaking is realistically optional. CSB's commercial-condition alternative rests on that inference: enacted notice, covered post-enactment activity, and Florida Prepaid's practical choice to keep marketing its competing tuition-prepayment program. Those circumstances establish Congress's intent, not Florida's voluntary submission. Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer did not join College Savings Part III, but they accept it as controlling; no changed premise or special justification supports departing from it here.

Express waiver and voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction remain distinct State choices that can supply actual consent; properly conditioned federal benefits and interstate compacts keep their own rules. CSB separately contends that Florida Prepaid waived immunity by appearing, seeking transfer under §1404(a), and requesting declarations of invalidity, noninfringement, and unenforceability. No Justice decides whether any of that conduct waived immunity, and no waiver finding is made.

##### Limits and questions not reached

The Court makes no finding of patent validity, infringement, willfulness, Florida-remedy adequacy, constitutional injury, or entitlement to relief. Section 296(b) retains its own conditions and any properly presented award-specific constitutional limits. Neither constitutional holding nor the judgment preapproves every compensation, injunction, fee, or enhanced award. Kolstad's Title VII punishment rules do not determine patent enhancement.

The appearance, transfer request under §1404(a), and request for affirmative declarations of invalidity, noninfringement, and unenforceability remain distinct litigation-consent contentions. The Court finds no waiver from any of them. Schacht does not settle their effect. No actual-violation exception, willfulness exception, State-remedy exhaustion, new constitutional damages cause, or Just Compensation Clause justification is adopted.

The action is not converted into an officer suit. Young and Coeur d'Alene require a connected officer, ongoing federal violation, proper cause, and genuinely prospective relief; Edelman and Pennhurst preserve retrospective-compensation and state-law boundaries. No Florida claims-bill or takings remedy is adjudged adequate or inadequate, and State Street supplies no invalidity finding about this different patent.

Its statutory holding and private third-party damages posture remain intact; its four-Justice complete rationale and absence of a Parden-waiver ground are not enlarged into historical majority holdings.
- **Seminole Tribe; Hans:** Seminole’s separation of explicit State-action authorization from constitutional amenability remains intact. #### Law After Decision

This immunity-denial judgment stands and the covered patent action proceeds to ordinary merits adjudication, without a new constitutional-tort prerequisite imposed by the mandate. The express federal-forum and remedy provisions satisfy the distinct statutory-clarity inquiry, and patent property remains distinct from unconstitutional deprivation.

Section 5 and Article I independently sustain this action. Lower courts apply the congruent-and-proportional preventive-enforcement holding without requiring each patent plaintiff to prove a constitutional deprivation. They also apply the Article I rule when both the underlying obligation and enforcement measure fall within Congress’s enumerated authority and Congress unmistakably authorizes State exposure to suit. Union Gas’s constitutional conclusion is extended to this principle, and Hans supplies no immunity Congress cannot displace in this setting. Under Part IV, for all nine Justices, continued commercial participation after Congress authorizes suit is not consent, as College Savings Part III holds; actual consent through a distinct State choice, such as express waiver or voluntary invocation of federal jurisdiction, remains available, and whether Florida Prepaid's litigation conduct waived immunity remains undecided. Hilton’s state-forum holding and actual-waiver and officer-relief doctrines retain their distinct boundaries. The action proceeds without an actual-waiver adjudication as a prerequisite, and all merits and award-specific conditions remain for ordinary adjudication.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas, dissents from affirmance while joining Parts I and IV.** Boerne permits prevention, but ordinary patent liability includes innocent infringement and circumstances with adequate process. Patent property and the usefulness of a federal remedy do not establish a sufficiently fitted response to deprivation without due process. The objection concerns substantive fit, not a mandatory quantity of evidence, violation in every jurisdiction, formal findings, geographic limits, or sunsets. The dissent does not find Florida's untested claims-bill or takings routes adequate and does not require exhaustion.

The dissent joins Part IV. On consent, a clear federal declaration states Congress's intent rather than Florida's unequivocal submission. Actual waiver, properly conditioned grants, and interstate compacts remain separate, including their genuine coercion questions. Appearance, transfer, and affirmative relief are not interchangeable or already adjudicated consent. The dissent opposes the Court’s Article I extension: substantive Patent Clause authority and unmistakable statutory intent do not, on its structural view, establish power to impose a nonconsensual direct-State suit. The surviving Union Gas conclusion cannot be silently displaced through the mandate; actual submission may resolve the case without that further constitutional decision. The dissent orders no discovery, hearing, new proof, amendment, or constitutional-infringement trial, and decides no patent merits.

### California Dental Association v. Federal Trade Commission, No. 97-1625

Merits decision, May 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 13, 1999. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 128 F.3d 720, which affirmed and enforced the Commission's cease-and-desist order. The questions are whether the FTC Act reaches this nonprofit association organized to benefit for-profit dentists and whether the actual price- and quality-advertising restrictions received sufficient rule-of-reason analysis.

The California Dental Association's (CDA's) roughly 75% statewide membership, commercial benefits and enforcement are described in the appellate record. The administrative law judge (ALJ) found practical restrictions on low-fee descriptions, broad discounts and quality claims without individualized truthfulness review. The Commission relied on per se treatment of price restrictions and alternatively on abbreviated analysis; the Ninth Circuit rejected the per se basis but sustained abbreviated review of price and quality separately. The ALJ and Commission differed on market power. Those positions are distinct from a Supreme Court factual finding.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy and scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FTC statutory coverage | Sustained, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Coverage is resolved; no final liability follows from it. |
| Price-advertising liability | Vacated and remanded, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | The existing abbreviated analysis is insufficient; further restraint-sensitive consideration is required. |
| Quality-advertising liability | Vacated and remanded, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Same formal relief, with the distinct quality-information analysis below. |

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit. O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas would leave that court to decide whether it can assess the Commission's decision on the existing record or should return it for more extensive agency analysis and an enhanced record. Stone-Zsela would require the Commission itself to conduct the fuller inquiry. Neither institutional direction commands five votes. The common judgment finds neither final liability nor reasonableness and directs no damages or new sanction.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Coverage includes an association serving its members' commercial profit

**Holding and operative rule:** The FTC Act reaches a nonprofit professional association organized to carry on business for its members' profit when its activities confer proximate, substantial economic benefits on their for-profit operations. CDA satisfies that condition through its insurance, financing, commercial support and other member-business activities; nonprofit form, tax status and absence of distributions to the association do not eliminate the members'-profit branch of section 44.

**Authority:** Souter's coverage opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, directly controls this proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory alternative concerning members' profit would do little work if only the association's own earnings counted. CDA materially advances its members' commercial practices, so the outside suppliers' role does not make those benefits merely incidental. This application does not require exclusive devotion to member profit, a substantial percentage of total activity devoted to it, or proof that each member makes a net profit from membership. The connection must be real and proximate; professional education's presumed or incidental benefit alone is not held sufficient. The Court does not decide coverage of every charity, educational organization, nonprofit with an annual surplus, or commercial competitor without for-profit members. Ordinary statutory interpretation suffices, without deciding deference to the Commission's jurisdictional view. Section 45's sector exceptions remain; none is established for CDA. Neither professional status nor an unverified guideline's description of state law supplies immunity. Coverage authorizes inquiry, not condemnation.

##### The inquiry must test the restraint's actual competitive tendency and material explanation

**Holding and operative rule:** An abbreviated rule-of-reason inquiry may suffice when experience and the restraint's actual character make its likely anticompetitive tendency sufficiently clear to assess both the restraint and its material competing explanation. Where professional-information disparities make those effects insufficiently obvious, fuller analysis fitted to the restraint is required; neither a fixed sequence of labels nor a complete industry or market-definition study is invariably compulsory.

**Authority:** Souter's five-Justice competitive-analysis opinion controls this rule and both applications below.

**Controlling explanation:** NCAA permits inquiry calibrated to an established competitive mechanism. Professional Engineers rejects the claim that competition itself is undesirable; it does not dispense with examining a plausible claim that a practice improves reliable comparison. Indiana Federation of Dentists permits direct analysis of an agreement withholding a desired service, but does not make every professional-information restriction identical to that agreement. Khan and NYNEX require attention to the actual restraint and mechanism without converting this horizontal association into a vertical arrangement. The FTC's concrete enforcement findings are adverse evidence, not a substitute for the missing analysis of information quality. The Court corrects legal sufficiency and does not reweigh supported findings under section 45(c), independently affirm every economic conclusion, or decide whether substantial evidence supports ultimate liability. It finds neither restraint reasonable and creates no professional immunity. Legitimate restraints on materially false or misleading advertising remain possible under their actual conditions; no categorical per se issue is independently decided.

##### Price restrictions require more examination on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** The practical restrictions on fee descriptions and broad discount advertisements cannot be sustained on the abbreviated analysis employed here without adequately examining their asserted effect on the reliability of price comparison. Further inquiry must test the actual enforcement and claimed information benefits; the mere reduction of advertising does not settle the net competitive tendency.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice competitive-analysis opinion directly adopts this application.

**Controlling explanation:** Price communication ordinarily helps consumers compare and dentists compete. Bates recognizes that value while preserving legitimate deception controls. But an across-the-board discount without an intelligible baseline or scope may be unreliable, and a disclosure condition might improve comparison, obstruct it or do both. The reported restrictions on truthful descriptions and practically prohibitive disclosures remain serious evidence against CDA. Their existence alone does not adequately test whether these particular conditions improve reliable information before demanding empirical justification on the assumption of an already established harmful net tendency. This is no finding that every advertisement misleads, that every condition helps, or that CDA's explanation ultimately succeeds. The lower proceedings must perform the missing analysis without automatically adopting the ALJ's no-market-power conclusion, a local-share threshold or mandatory comprehensive market definition.

##### Quality advertising is not equivalent to service output

**Holding and operative rule:** Quality-advertising restrictions whose effects depend on the reliability and verifiability of professional information require fuller consideration than equating fewer advertisements with a restriction of dental-service output. The actual conditions and competitive explanation must be assessed; possible effects on demand do not themselves establish a producer-imposed service-output restriction.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice opinion directly controls this distinct application.

**Controlling explanation:** Restricting useful quality information can impede service rivalry, yet patients may have difficulty verifying professional claims. A restraint addressing unverifiable or misleading claims may improve information. Bates recognizes that quality-claim problem, while Indiana Federation's established desired-service restraint provides no automatic answer to this different mechanism. The court below could not settle the matter merely by translating fewer advertisements into reduced dental output. The Court requires neither measured output decline in every antitrust case nor acceptance of a blanket ban. Supported enforcement against truthful information remains material. Fuller examination may condemn either or both restrictions, but the present analysis does not resolve their competitive explanation. The discontinued fear-allaying policy is not treated as a continuing restraint.

#### Precedent Treatment

- NCAA v. Board of Regents supplies flexible inquiry fitted to established competitive tendencies; it creates no compulsory full-market procedure.
- National Society of Professional Engineers v. United States remains a bar to defending restraints on the premise that competition itself is undesirable; an information-quality explanation must be tested on its actual competitive terms.
- FTC v. Indiana Federation of Dentists is distinguished at the level of the restraint and information mechanism; its direct-effects method and separation of supported findings from legal sufficiency remain.
- Bates v. State Bar of Arizona supplies the value of truthful price information and the distinct difficulty of unverifiable professional quality claims.
- State Oil Co. v. Khan retains its vertical maximum-price holding and horizontal distinctions; its attention to competitive mechanisms informs this inquiry without conferring immunity.
- NYNEX Corp. v. Discon retains its vertical supplier-substitution holding and distinct horizontal categories; labels or improper purpose do not replace analysis of the actual mechanism.

#### Law After Decision

CDA falls within FTC coverage, but the existing price- and quality-advertising liability determination cannot stand on the analysis used. The required inquiry is proportionate to actual restraints and live competitive explanations. The Court establishes neither final liability nor professional immunity, and imposes no universal market-definition trial or required proof of measured service-output decline. Supported agency findings retain their statutory review status. No majority specifies whether the Ninth Circuit may complete the examination on the existing record or must direct Commission reconsideration.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment:** The Chief joins coverage and the holdings requiring fuller, restraint-sensitive inquiry. The suppression of truthful comparison and the possible value of substantiation or discount disclosure must both be tested. He would direct the Commission itself to conduct that examination rather than permit the Ninth Circuit to complete it without Commission reconsideration. He requires no full market-definition trial in every case and grants no immunity for restraints on truthful, nonmisleading information.

**Souter's remedial discussion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas:** These four would leave the Ninth Circuit to decide whether it can adequately assess the Commission's decision on the existing record or should return it for more extensive analysis and an enhanced record. Correcting legal sufficiency neither authorizes judicial reweighing of supported facts nor mandates a new administrative hearing. They make no ultimate substantial-evidence determination.

**Breyer, joined by Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg, concurring in part and dissenting in part:** They join coverage and the flexible rule-of-reason method but find the existing analysis sufficient for both categories. Actual enforcement, rather than the Code's facial aspiration against deception, suppresses low-fee descriptions and practically excludes broad discounts. Truthful discounts can inform consumers without listing every undiscounted service price. The supported quality restraint suppresses useful information without examining whether particular claims mislead; the mechanism is interference with communicating qualities and matching dentists to patients, not a finding that the number of procedures fell. Verification problems do not justify the actual breadth once those effects are shown.

On that showing CDA bears the burden of establishing an offsetting competitive benefit. A merely possible advantage does not require the Commission to refute every conceivable theory. These Justices find the inquiry and record adequately answer the reliability claim. Membership, entry barriers, economic benefits and dentists' interest in remaining members support practical effect; the statewide figure is not a finding of equal shares in every locality. Indiana Federation supplies direct-effects analysis and respect for supported Commission findings, while NCAA calibrates depth and Professional Engineers requires a competitive justification. They would affirm the existing enforcement judgment without a new order, sanction or damages. They endorse neither every disclosure restriction nor all professional advertising, and find no need to decide the Commission's per se alternative.

The governing text is [15 U.S.C. §44](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title15/html/USCODE-1994-title15-chap2-subchapI-sec44.htm) and [§45](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-1994-title15/html/USCODE-1994-title15-chap2-subchapI-sec45.htm). No additional order term, exact local share, state-law command or new advertisement finding is decided.

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit with coverage resolved and the enforcement judgment vacated. Further proceedings must respect the five-Justice insufficiency holdings and section 45(c)'s treatment of supported findings. The four-Justice appellate option and the Chief's mandatory Commission direction remain separately stated, without either becoming a majority command. ### Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System v. Southworth, No. 98-1189

Merits decision, March 22, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 9, 1999. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 151 F.3d 717, decided August 10, 1998, Nos. 97-3510 and 97-3548.

The question is whether a public university violates the First Amendment by requiring students to pay an activity fee supporting organizations engaged in political, ideological and religious expression. The live dispute concerns speech and association and the objectors' allocable fees. The general fee power, nonallocable services, ordinary student government, nonideological organizations, nonobjectors' money, the student newspaper and Distinguished Lecture Series are outside the challenge.

In 1995–96 a full-time Madison student paid a mandatory $165.75 segregated fee each semester; nonpayment prevented receipt of grades and graduation. About 80 percent supported unchallenged nonallocable services. The challenged allocation program supported registered student organizations and qualifying services through student-government processes subject to University approval. The ordinary grant and General Student Services Fund routes were stipulated to operate without viewpoint discrimination or University advocacy of a particular view. Student organizations controlled their own messages, which included lobbying and other political advocacy; the University disclaimed their expression. Most funding reimbursed expenses. WISPIRG received lump-sum funding, and all uses of that money are not established.

The stipulation expressly excludes the referendum. Student government treated referendum outcomes as binding, permitting allocation, termination or veto of a group's funding. The record contains a WISPIRG funding vote and an October 1996 termination vote, without establishing a constitutional finding about each ballot's operation. The lower court had already confined relief to objectors' allocable fees and removed the district court's all-students injunction and prescribed detailed administrative mechanism. It nevertheless sustained the general compelled-subsidy liability rule challenged here.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|
| General compelled-subsidy liability for the stipulated ordinary program | Reversed, 9–0 in judgment | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | The general prohibition against using objectors' allocable fees for that program cannot stand. |
| Binding referendum | Remanded, 9–0 in judgment | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Determine its actual operation and any relief warranted by a defect. |

No damages, fixed refund, specific WISPIRG appropriation, universal opt-in procedure or order to fund every recipient is entered. The district court's already vacated all-students reach and detailed administrative design are not restored.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Ginsburg | Reversal of general compelled-subsidy liability; referendum-only remand | Entire opinion, including the educational private-expression framework, neutrality requirements, referendum treatment and all limits |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stevens | Souter, Breyer | Agrees with reversal and referendum remand | Actual program's sufficient justification; does not join the complete necessity framework |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The genuine educational private-expression program may be supported by a general fee

**Holding and operative rule:** A public university may require a generally applicable student activity fee to support a broad program of independent extracurricular student expression serving its educational mission when eligibility, allocation and administration are objective and viewpoint neutral. Within that program, a student's disagreement with a recipient's political, ideological or religious viewpoint does not require an ideological opt-out or pro rata refund.

**Authority:** Justice Thomas's opinion of the Court, with the six-Justice coalition identified above, directly controls this holding and its limits. Stevens, Souter and Breyer support the judgment on their separate ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Compelled financing implicates expression even without personal attribution. Abood and Keller protect against compulsory ideological support outside the representative duties that justify union and integrated-bar charges; Lehnert applies those distinct union principles. Here the common educational function is maintaining opportunities for independent and competing student expression. It does not appoint one organization to speak for every payer. Requiring courts to sort student ideas by agreement with an official educational orthodoxy would defeat that function.

Glickman recognizes expressive burdens while permitting a specified common expense within collective agricultural regulation. Its nonideological product-promotion holding does not itself authorize student ideological subsidies; this different program supplies its own justification. Rosenberger protects viewpoint equality in its controlled private-publication benefit and reserved the compulsory-fee question now presented. Independent student control, broad educational opportunity and viewpoint-neutral administration together answer that question here. Lack of attribution, common benefit, majority approval or enrollment alone would not suffice.

The ordinary SGAF/GSSF allocation and approval process is stipulated neutral, and the University does not advocate a particular viewpoint through it. Students need not adopt recipients' views, join their groups or surrender their own expression. The fee therefore supports a forum, not governmental endorsement of each message. The University may choose optional contributions or refunds, but the Constitution does not require ideological exemptions on these facts. No finding that opt-outs are impossible or every subsidy indispensable is made.

##### Neutrality governs the actual program, not every university expenditure

**Holding and operative rule:** The protection for a compulsory independent-expression program requires viewpoint-neutral eligibility, allocation and administration; neither an educational label nor popular approval permits selection by viewpoint. Reasonable neutral limits on program scope, financial accountability, performance and fraud prevention remain available; the rule does not require equal grants or funding every activity.

**Authority:** The same six Justices join Justice Thomas's opinion on this holding and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** Rosenberger distinguishes independently attributed student expression from official advocacy and forbids viewpoint exclusion within the benefit actually opened. A neutral program may define the work it supports and require an account of its performance; those conditions cannot conceal approval or rejection of ideology. The decision addresses the actual broad student-expression program and establishes no constitutional requirement that curricular choices, faculty instruction or governmental speech follow the same forum rule. It does not immunize those activities from other constitutional review.

This case establishes no particular devotional purchase. Agostini's unified Lemon inquiry, Jackson's funded-function rule and Strout's integrated-tuition application remain distinct from protected religious editorial viewpoint in Rosenberger. Neutrality alone does not authorize governmental worship or devotional instruction as such. The Court neither decides nor revives an Establishment, free-exercise or RFRA claim, and does not conclusively approve a particular external payment without its facts and proper claim.

##### The referendum requires separate examination

**Holding and operative rule:** A university cannot distribute access to its compulsory private-expression program according to majority approval of a group's viewpoint. The binding referendum is outside the ordinary-allocation neutrality stipulation, so the Seventh Circuit's general compulsory-subsidy judgment must be reversed and the case remanded only to determine the referendum's actual operation and appropriate relief.

**Authority:** The same six Justices join Justice Thomas's complete referendum rule. Stevens, Souter and Breyer independently support the referendum remand without adopting the complete necessity framework.

**Controlling explanation:** The referendum can allocate, terminate or veto funding, and the student government treats its result as binding. Ordinary committee neutrality does not establish referendum neutrality. Minority expression cannot depend on majority ideological consent. Yet the record does not decide every ballot's operation or establish that every possible voting arrangement is unconstitutional. The Court assumes no neutral final override and orders examination of the existing procedure.

The lower court already removed the district court's all-students reach and prescribed administrative scheme. Neither is restored. The late partisan-policy challenge is not reopened by a general reference to unresolved questions; the operative remand concerns the referendum alone and permits only relief its actual defect warrants. No damages, fixed refund, specified WISPIRG payment, universal opt-in procedure or order to fund every recipient is entered.

##### Limits and questions not reached

### Bradley v. Arkansas Department of Education

Merits decision, June 12, 2000

#### Chronology and Posture

Certiorari granted March 20, 2000; submitted on full merits briefing April 25, 2000. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, reviewing 189 F.3d 745 (August 31, 1999), appellate No. 98-1010. Review is confined to the Bradley IDEA immunity ruling; the accompanying appellate No. 98-1830, Jim C., is outside the grant.

Arkansas seeks reversal of the denial of immunity to the Department of Education and its employee Mike Crowley. The District Court's November 21, 1997 order in No. LR-C-96-1004 denied dismissal or summary judgment and stayed the merits during the immunity appeal. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the IDEA denial on accepted-funding terms and also discussed prospective officer relief. The questions concern clear federal-suit notice, actual acceptance and the scope of that consent, with independent abrogation grounds unnecessary if consent suffices.

Thomas and Dianna Bradley, on behalf of their son David, allege deficient procedures for review of his individualized education program and failure to perform IDEA responsibilities. The asserted hearing-timing and witness-process failures are allegations, not findings. They seek prospective compliance rather than compensatory or punitive damages.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The judgment denying sovereign immunity in the Bradley IDEA action is affirmed on the funding-consent ground. The case returns through the Eighth Circuit for ordinary adjudication in the District Court. The Court establishes no violation of a free appropriate public education obligation or hearing requirement and awards no damages, injunction, fees or other relief. The same sovereign-immunity defense asserted for Crowley in official capacity fails within the accepted condition; personal-capacity liability is not decided. No judgment is entered concerning Jim C., Section 504 or other ungranted claims.

#### Opinion of the Court

Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt the holding, application and limits below.

#### Holdings

##### Actual acceptance of the disclosed IDEA funding condition supplies consent

**Holding and operative rule:** A State that voluntarily accepts IDEA assistance after the effective express condition in §§1403 and 1415 consents to federal judicial enforcement of the obligations of that funded program, within the recipient, claim and remedy covered by the condition. Arkansas's acceptance under that notice removes sovereign immunity from the Bradley IDEA action, including the same sovereign immunity asserted for the named officer in his official capacity; it supplies neither a general waiver for Arkansas nor consent inferred from ordinary regulated activity.

**Controlling explanation:** The condition identifies the State, the federal forum and violations of IDEA. Section 1415 places federal judicial review among the procedures attached to the funded education program. Arkansas conceded both the spending-power validity of IDEA and its receipt of IDEA funds; its acceptance followed the express immunity condition. The present record supplies no retroactive surprise or coercive demand defeating that acceptance.

Atascadero requires unmistakable notice of federal suit. That requirement is satisfied here. Dellmuth identifies the earlier absence of the necessary statutory clarity; the express provision cures that notice defect. The heading and abrogation phrasing of §1403 do not obscure the consequence that the operative text discloses to a recipient. Congressional intent alone is insufficient, but the State's actual acceptance supplies the additional choice.

College Savings Bank rejects consent inferred from continued ordinary commerce and leaves particular accepted-benefit conditions unresolved. Accepting this identified assistance on disclosed enforcement terms is the different transaction that was absent there. South Dakota v. Dole requires a relationship between the condition and the funded undertaking; enforcing the duties of this very program satisfies that requirement. Cedar Rapids preserves an expressly disclosed IDEA obligation despite substantial compliance costs; cost alone does not erase the condition. None of these decisions dispenses with actual acceptance or establishes a universal waiver from federal funding.

The consent ground resolves the immunity appeal. The Court need not decide independent Article I or Section 5 abrogation. A difference between IDEA's protection and the constitutional minimum does not itself defeat preventive enforcement, as Boerne and Florida Prepaid establish, but neither does it establish IDEA's constitutional validity. No individual constitutional violation is an added prerequisite to proceeding on the consent ground.

**Operative qualifications and limits:** Section 1403(c) applies to violations occurring in whole or in part after October 30, 1990. Section 1403(b) makes legal and equitable remedies available only to the same extent as against another public entity; it creates no additional category of relief. The Bradleys seek prospective compliance, not compensatory or punitive damages. Lane's distinction between substantive coverage and consent to a particular remedy remains applicable; no unrequested damages question is decided.

Section 1415(i)(2) permits an aggrieved party's civil action concerning the presented complaint after the specified administrative decision and appeal route: a party aggrieved under subsection (f) or (k) without a subsection (g) appeal, or a party aggrieved by the administrative appeal decision, may proceed in a competent state court or federal district court without an amount-in-controversy requirement. The court receives the administrative record, hears additional evidence at a party's request, and grants appropriate relief on the preponderance of the evidence. Section 1415(l) preserves rights, procedures and remedies under the Constitution, the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, title V of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, and other federal laws protecting children with disabilities. Before a civil action under those laws seeking relief also available under IDEA is filed, it requires exhaustion of subsections (f) and (g) to the same extent as for an IDEA action. Immunity denial neither finds these predicates satisfied nor excuses them.

The Court decides no educational violation, hearing violation, exhaustion issue, individual liability or particular decree. An independent prospective-officer claim still requires a connected officer, an ongoing federal violation and genuinely prospective relief; a treasury claim for past injury cannot become prospective by naming an employee. The Court creates no new defendant or officer claim. Jim C. and its Section 504 claim are outside the grant; IDEA acceptance establishes no Section 504 waiver. That statute's own federal-suit notice, accepted assistance and remedial scope require separate consideration in a case properly presenting them.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon, 473 U.S. 234 (1985):** Applies its requirement of unmistakable notice of a federal-suit condition; actual IDEA acceptance under the express text satisfies that requirement rather than displacing it.
- **Dellmuth v. Muth, 491 U.S. 223 (1989):** Distinguishes the earlier statutory absence of a clear State/federal-suit provision; its clarity requirement remains, while §1403 supplies the notice missing there.
- **South Dakota v. Dole, 483 U.S. 203 (1987):** Applies relatedness to enforcement of this funded program and preserves the coercion limit; the record does not establish a coercive demand defeating the conceded program acceptance.
- **College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board (June 23, 1999):** Applies the distinction between congressional intent and actual State consent. Its rejection of consent inferred from ordinary commercial participation remains; the Court resolves the reserved accepted-benefit question only for the identified IDEA condition.
- **Cedar Rapids Community School District v. Garret F. (March 3, 1999):** Preserves the enforceability of a disclosed IDEA obligation despite continuity or substantial cost. Its service holding does not establish this claim's merits, immunity or a damages entitlement.
- **Lane v. Peña, 518 U.S. 187 (1996):** Applies the distinction between substantive duty and consent to the asserted remedy. This State-program holding supplies no general federal or State damages waiver.
- **Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida; Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank; Kimel v. Florida Board of Regents:** Preserve the distinct inquiries into express suit authorization and constitutional power. Florida Prepaid's Article I holding and Kimel's application remain controlling; this consent decision neither relies on nor rejects an independent application of that power to IDEA.
- **City of Boerne v. Flores; Florida Prepaid; United States v. Morrison / Brzonkala v. Morrison:** Preserve fitted preventive Section 5 legislation reaching some conduct not independently unconstitutional. No blanket IDEA invalidity, constitutional education right or mandatory individual-violation rule follows; IDEA's Section 5 validity remains undecided here.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 12, 2000, actual voluntary acceptance of IDEA assistance under the express federal-suit condition supplies program-specific consent. Lower courts must distinguish that acceptance from general federal receipts, ordinary regulated activity and congressional intent alone. This holding adds an application of accepted-benefit consent left unresolved in College Savings; its commercial-consent rule remains unchanged. Consent removes this immunity defense while preserving the statute's temporal and remedial limits and the ordinary prerequisites to relief. Existing Article I and preventive Section 5 law remains effective, without a new IDEA abrogation holding. No law concerning Section 504 immunity is adjudicated.

### Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez / United States v. Velazquez, Nos. 99-603 and 99-960

Merits decision, February 28, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 4, 2000. On writs of certiorari to the Second Circuit, 164 F.3d 757, which invalidated the challenged welfare-law condition and approved limited injunctive relief after the district court denied preliminary relief. The question is whether Congress may authorize representation of eligible individuals seeking specific welfare-agency relief while excluding efforts to amend or challenge the validity of existing welfare law. Section 504(a)(16) contains a broad welfare-reform litigation, lobbying and rulemaking restriction, an individual-benefits representation exception, and the challenged condition on that exception. The condition refers to law in effect when representation begins. LSC construed the broad reform ban to exclude both challenges to and defenses of reform law; that interpretation does not itself classify the distinct condition inside the individual exception. Recipient restrictions extend to non-LSC as well as LSC funds. The Government and LSC defend a limited service; challengers contend the condition disables independent advocacy within the authorized representation. The grant did not include severability, which was neither briefed nor argued.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed in Nos. 99-603 and 99-960, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Breyer | Affirmance in both dockets, 6–3 | Entire independent-advocacy holding and scope-limited preservation of existing excision |
| Dissent, common service-definition ground | Scalia | O'Connor, Thomas | Would reverse in both dockets | Program-specific defense accepting recipient-wide reach, withdrawal and joint-counsel limits |
| Same dissent, additional subsidy explanation | Scalia | Thomas | Additional explanation for reversal | Broader grant-selection account with independent-penalty limits; O'Connor does not join |

#### Holdings

##### I. The condition impermissibly disables independent client advocacy

**Holding and operative rule:** When Congress supports independent representation of eligible individuals seeking specific welfare relief, it may not condition that authorized representation on withholding otherwise relevant arguments challenging the validity of the law governing the claim. Section 504(a)(16)'s condition against efforts to amend or challenge existing law in effect when representation begins is unconstitutional within that individual-benefits service; Congress retains authority to define lawful subject, client and activity limits and need not fund every form of litigation.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion of the Court identified above directly adopts both holdings, their limits and the affirmance in both dockets.

**Controlling explanation:** Rosenberger and Southworth examine whose expression the actual program supports. Here the lawyer represents the client, including against government, while opposing counsel presents the government's position. The program's function is independent advocacy, not delivery of a government-authored message. A challenge to governing law may be the necessary legal basis for the very individual relief Congress permits. Finley's narrow approval of nonconclusive selection considerations does not authorize this categorical disability. Urofsky likewise rejects automatic governmental attribution from public resources and professional duties, without eliminating legitimate institutional control.

The condition permits facts, misreading, misapplication and agency-policy objections under existing law, but prohibits statutory and constitutional validity arguments, including federal preemption and facial or as-applied constitutional claims. The lawyer must then withdraw consistently with professional duties, even when independent counsel would present the validity argument and the recipient's lawyer would continue the other issues. Counsel must explain the withdrawal, may discuss views of the law and may refer the client; those permissions do not supply the missing representation. Separate affiliates are legally permitted, but their permission establishes neither available replacement counsel nor its impossibility.

The Government's program-boundary argument correctly recognizes authority to fund selected services. It fails because the condition disables a class of legal grounds inside the authorized client-directed service, including representation financed with non-LSC money. Symmetry of the broad reform ban concerns a different statutory component and does not neutralize this restriction inside the individual exception. Rust and Regan retain their lawful program applications; professional independence alone is not decisive, but the actual function and argument-based operation distinguish this program. Distortion of the court's receipt of legal arguments reinforces the speech violation without creating a separate structural cause of action, appropriation power or universal civil-counsel right.

##### II. The existing limited remedy stands without adjudicating an unpresented severability question

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court affirms the constitutional invalidation in both dockets and leaves the lower court's condition-only excision undisturbed because severability lies outside the limited grant and was not briefed or argued. Rule 24.1(a) permits discretionary consideration of an otherwise jurisdictionally proper plain error evident from the record, but the Court finds no sufficiently evident error warranting that enlargement here.

**Authority:** The same six Justices directly join this scope ruling and remedy; they do not adopt a new substantive severability conclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** Rule 14.1(a) ordinarily confines review to the questions presented and fairly included subsidiary questions. Rule 24 preserves a distinct discretionary exception; lack of briefing is neither an absolute bar nor automatic permission to invoke it. The dissent argues that the exception and its condition form a legislative bargain that cannot survive excision of the condition. That is a substantive severability question, not a necessary consequence of the speech holding.

Alaska Airlines requires lawful independent operation and inquiry into whether Congress would have enacted the remainder without the invalid provision. The lower court's report of unclear intent does not by itself prove that Congress would reject the remaining individual service. Conversely, the legal-assistance purpose does not itself prove Congress would enact that service without the condition. Removing the whole exception would itself withdraw an express permission. On this unbriefed question the Court does not find an evident error that justifies deciding between those substantive alternatives.

The limited excision therefore remains operative without a holding that the parties conceded expanded funding or established a legislative preference. The reviewed stage remains injunction litigation. The Court orders no new appropriation, whole-program invalidation, benefit award or relief against unrelated restrictions.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia: applies the distinction between independent expression and the government's message within the actual funded activity; reasonable program boundaries remain permissible.
- Board of Regents v. Southworth: applies attention to independent expression and viewpoint-neutral administration without converting every grant into a public forum.
- National Endowment for the Arts v. Finley: distinguishes its nonconclusive selective-grant criteria from this categorical restriction; its narrow controlling rule supplies no universal subsidy exemption.
- Urofsky v. Gilmore: applies the actual-function distinction against automatic governmental attribution from public means and professional duties; its employment framework is not mechanically transferred to all legal-service grants.
- Rust v. Sullivan: distinguishes its defined governmental program from the actual independent client advocacy here, without treating professional relationships alone as dispositive or displacing its lawful application.
- Regan v. Taxation With Representation: preserves legitimate funding selection at its actual scope; it does not answer the condition operating inside this independent representation.
- Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock: preserves the independent-operation and congressional-intent inquiry; no new severability merits determination is made here.

#### Law After Decision

Effective February 28, 2001, the challenged condition may not disable otherwise relevant welfare-law validity arguments within Congress's authorized independent individual representation. Ordinary factual, statutory-application and validity arguments remain distinct, and the decision creates no right to support for every subject, client or form of litigation. The lower court's limited excision stands through the Court's scope ruling; unrelated welfare-reform, lobbying, class-action and other LSC restrictions are not invalidated. Neither the dissent's conditional whole-exception remedy nor a new legislative-intent rule becomes law.

#### Separate Writings

**Justice Scalia, joined by O'Connor and Thomas on the common service-definition ground, dissenting.** Congress may subsidize routine enforcement of existing welfare law without assisting the recipient's validity-challenging representation. The dissent accepts that the lawyer advocates for the client, the restriction reaches non-LSC funds, and the lawyer must leave the whole case even if independent counsel could handle the prohibited issue. It nevertheless regards those terms as defining the selected recipient's welfare service, not penalizing independent speech outside it. The broad ban's symmetry, substantial permitted enforcement arguments, advice and referral, and legally separate affiliates support that classification without proving any particular substitute actually available. Rust's professional relationship shows why private professional judgment alone does not settle a program boundary. Their view creates no constitutional obligation to finance continuation when the case crosses that boundary.

Scalia and Thomas additionally rely on their Finley subsidy account and Urofsky grounds distinguishing assistance from an entitlement to public means, while preserving the limit against penalties on independent expression. O'Connor joins the particular service defense rather than a general immunity for conditions on nonfederal money. The common dissent neither denies the burden nor treats an affiliate as an established cure.

**The same dissent's conditional severability ground, joined by O'Connor and Thomas.** Even if the condition is unconstitutional, the three would reject preserving the individual-benefits exception without it. Rule 24.1(a) permits review of evident plain error within jurisdiction despite the question limitation, and they regard the expanded unconditional service as sufficiently evident error to exercise that discretion. Alaska Airlines requires more than a remainder capable of operating: Congress must have been willing to enact it independently. They infer from the expressly conditioned exception, legal-assistance purpose and political-pressure concern an integrated bargain, not willingness to provide the permission without its limit. They would remove the entire individual-benefits exception with the invalid condition, leaving the broad welfare-reform restriction according to its terms. That alternative neither invalidates the whole LSC program nor declares every ordinary benefits case reform litigation. It applies only if invalidity is adopted; under their preferred merits view the condition remains enforceable and nothing is severed.

#### Holdings

##### 1. Present-offense facts changing a binding punishment range receive element protections

**Holding and operative rule:** A fact concerning the commission or consequences of the present offense that is legally necessary to raise either the binding ceiling or the binding floor of punishment must be validly admitted as an element or proved beyond a reasonable doubt to a jury, unless a lawful jury waiver permits the judge to determine the element under that same proof standard. A larger outer statutory maximum does not make an enforceable inner Guidelines range discretionary; ordinary judicial assessment remains permissible within punishment independently authorized by the verdict or valid admissions.

**Authority:** Part I-A of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg; six direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The law's actual constraint determines whether the additional fact changes punishment authority. Apprendi supplies the principle that a present-offense fact authorizing greater punishment cannot escape element protections through a sentencing label. Its express reservation of general Guidelines classifications and unchanged-maximum minima left this application for decision. The mandatory Guidelines withhold sentencing choices unless an additional quantity fact is established. A still higher outer ceiling does not erase that legal consequence.

The traditional judicial role continues inside the range already authorized. Witte and Watts preserve that within-authority role; they do not hold every binding constraint constitutionally immaterial. Mistretta establishes the Commission's institutional validity, a different question. Limited departures likewise do not make the ordinary mandatory range advisory.

A mandatory floor changes the legal obligation as a ceiling changes permission: a formerly permissible lesser sentence becomes unavailable. McMillan's contrary maximum/minimum distinction cannot sustain that allocation and is overruled to this extent. The floor matters here because quantity compelled the bottom of Angle's 210-to-262-month range even though his 210-month term remained below the 240-month statutory ceiling.

The Guidelines remain binding subject to these constitutional conditions. Prior-conviction questions, a general advisory-system remedy and consecutive sentencing independent of the disputed range premise lie outside the questions resolved. The existing resentencing must identify the lawful range; no unsupported numerical replacement is supplied.

##### 2. Federal charging protection accompanies the covered aggravated fact

**Holding and operative rule:** In a federal prosecution, a present-offense fact performing the aggravating legal work identified in Part I-A must receive the applicable grand-jury charging protection as an element. A quantity-free charge and verdict, ordinary sentencing findings, or notice of possible exposure do not themselves establish that element; a valid element admission or lawful waiver must satisfy its own governing requirements.

**Authority:** Part I-B of Souter's opinion, joined by Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg; five direct rationale votes. Stone-Zsela joins the disposition on this component without adopting the separate charging proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Jones and Castillo supply the charging consequences of federal aggravated elements. Cotton confirms that omission of aggravating quantity is a genuine constitutional charging defect without withdrawing subject-matter jurisdiction. Part I-A identifies the additional fact by its binding legal consequence. Its placement in a Guideline does not eliminate the protection attached to that function. Here the indictment and verdict specified no quantity, and ordinary judicial calculations are neither grand-jury allegations nor valid admissions of an aggravated element. Proceedings on remand remain governed by existing charging, admission, waiver and sentencing law; this remand itself authorizes no new charging or jury procedure.

##### 3. Phifer's actual drug-count term is erroneous and plain

**Holding and operative rule:** Phifer's 292-month drug-count sentence exceeds the 240 months authorized by his quantity-free verdict and is constitutional error. Applying the established review-time rule in the settled-law-then-changed setting, the count excess is plain; substantial-rights effect and discretionary correction remain separate requirements.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Thomas and Ginsburg; five direct rationale votes. Scalia agrees that the count is erroneous and assumes plainness without joining the affirmative timing application.

**Controlling explanation:** Apprendi supplies the verdict-authorized count limit. Available maxima on other counts do not enlarge it. Johnson supplies review-time plainness where settled trial law changes during direct review, and Cotton applies that framework to an aggravating-quantity omission. This decision applies those authorities to the actual count excess. It does not declare the newly resolved Guidelines question settled at original sentencing or announce a rule for every initially unsettled trial-law question. Olano continues to require substantial-rights effect and the distinct discretionary condition before correction follows.

##### 4. An unsupported mandatory aggregate cannot dispose of the remaining plain-error inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** A demonstrated lawful and inevitable identical aggregate may bear on substantial-rights effect under Rule 52(b), but unused cumulative statutory exposure or a supposed mandatory total derived from the constitutionally disputed quantity findings does not itself establish that result. Because the categorical no-prejudice ruling depends on that defective range premise, the appellate affirmance of Phifer's sentence must be vacated for the remaining substantial-rights and discretionary-correction inquiry on the actual record.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Thomas and Ginsburg; six direct rationale votes.

**Controlling explanation:** The entered judgment imposes a 292-month drug term and concurrent 240-month money-laundering terms. A hypothetical judgment with different terms and consecutive service is not that judgment. The dissent's strongest point concerns mandatory inevitability: if a lawful total compelled identical imprisonment, correcting the count arrangement might cause no prejudice. But the total must first be valid under Part I. Section 5G1.2(d) does not independently establish the total whose legality is contested.

Olano keeps error, plainness, substantial rights and discretionary correction distinct. The defendant ordinarily bears the burden of showing that the error affected the outcome. Even when that showing is made, correction remains discretionary: a reviewing court may correct only an error that seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Vonn permits the relevant existing record to inform that inquiry. Cotton permits refusal of correction under the independent fourth condition on a properly supported record even while substantial-rights effect is assumed. Its particular overwhelming and essentially uncontested threshold evidence is not a finding about Phifer. A general assessment of sufficiency cannot replace that inquiry. Neder concerns its qualified preserved-error setting and does not transfer the burden to the Government here.

The court of appeals must consider the lawful range, actual evidence and any properly supported alternative. Vacating its insufficient rationale neither guarantees correction nor establishes harmlessness. Range calculation and the two remaining conditions are remand work under governing rules, not questions without governing law.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Apprendi v. New Jersey is extended to present-offense facts raising binding Guidelines ceilings or floors. Its earlier reservations are resolved to that extent; its within-authorized-range boundary remains.
- McMillan v. Pennsylvania is overruled insofar as ordinary judicial sentencing findings of present-offense facts may raise a mandatory floor merely because the maximum is unchanged.
- Jones v. United States supplies the federal aggravated-element charging consequences applied in I-B; its statutory holding retains its distinct scope.
- Castillo v. United States likewise supplies charging consequences for federal aggravated elements in I-B; its distinct statutory holding remains intact.
- Mistretta v. United States retains its institutional approval of the Commission; it does not settle allocation of aggravating factfinding.
- Witte v. United States retains its within-authority sentencing and later-prosecution holdings, without an exemption for facts first changing a binding legal limit.
- United States v. Watts / United States v. Putra retains ordinary consideration within constitutionally authorized punishment, subject to the new binding-range rule.
- United States v. Olano supplies the four distinct forfeiture conditions, ordinary defendant burden and discretionary correction. No condition is eliminated.
- Johnson v. United States supplies its settled-trial-law/intervening-law timing rule, applied to the count excess without a new general timing holding.
- United States v. Cotton preserves nonjurisdictional treatment of the charging omission and record-specific fourth-condition review. Its evidentiary result is not transposed to Phifer.
- United States v. Vonn permits relevant-existing-record review under Rule 52(b); its separate Rule 11 setting creates no burden shift here.

#### Law After Decision

Present-offense facts legally raising either end of a mandatory punishment range receive element protections, including binding Guidelines constraints below an outer statutory maximum. McMillan's contrary minimum rule is displaced. Applicable federal charging requirements attach under I-B's five-Justice holding. Judicial choice remains within the independently established range; the Guidelines are not made advisory.

Count error and appellate correction remain distinct. Phifer's count error and plainness are established while ordinary prejudice and independent discretionary correction remain. A lawful inevitable aggregate may matter to effect; theoretical exposure or the invalid range premise is insufficient. The mandate requires actual application and preserves the convictions.

He agrees the count exceeds its verdict-authorized maximum but assumes plainness instead of joining the affirmative timing application, preserving his Johnson and Cotton limitation. That difference supplies no objection to the range rule or reconsideration of the defective prejudice rationale. He treats neither evidence as a verdict nor sentencing error as automatically correctable.

**O'Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Kennedy and Breyer.** They preserve Angle's ordinary findings-based resentencing and would affirm Phifer. McMillan and their Apprendi position distinguish facts defining the statutory offense from rules regulating judicial choice within its authorized range. Making such rules binding does not, in their view, transform each aggravating direction into an element. Witte and Watts support the continuing judicial role. They acknowledge the Guidelines' legal force but reject the majority's classification and overruling of McMillan.

They accept that Phifer's actual drug-count term exceeds its limit but would deny relief at the substantial-rights condition. Under their constitutional premise, the judicially calculated total is lawful and section 5G1.2(d) requires enough consecutive service to reach it. That conclusion rests on an obligatory lawful total, not merely unused cumulative maxima. They reject automatic correction while retaining Olano's separate conditions.

### Harris v. United States, No. 00-10666

#### Chronology and Posture

Harris seeks sentence relief; the United States seeks affirmance.

The sentencing judge treated brandishing as a close question, found it by a preponderance and imposed the seven-year minimum, which determined the guideline sentence under §2K2.4. Although the judge considered seven years appropriate, he declined to commit to that term if the enhancement were reversed. The basic conviction and separate evidentiary rulings are outside the granted challenge. Harris and Ring are separately decided matters; neither supplies a prior holding for the other. #### Holdings

##### I. The amended statute uses a mandatory sentencing condition

**Holding and operative rule:** Amended §924(c)(1)(A) defines the basic firearm offense and assigns graduated mandatory minimums; brandishing is a sentencing condition under that structure, a classification that does not dispense with constitutional element protections. The opening exception applies to the extent a greater minimum is otherwise provided by §924(c) or another provision of law; brandishing requires displaying all or part of the firearm, or otherwise making its presence known to another person, for the purpose of intimidating that person, whether or not the firearm is directly visible.

**Authority:** Part I of Scalia's opinion, joined by all eight other Justices, supplies nine direct votes for this construction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress's completed offense clause and ensuing directions for how the offender shall be sentenced identify a basic offense followed by penalty conditions. The five-, seven- and ten-year clauses establish the structure; the present claim concerns the seven-year brandishing condition. Jones requires attention to text, structure, tradition and consequence. Its statutory analysis does not make the placement of a provision conclusive. Castillo construed the materially different former firearm-type provision and therefore does not decide this amendment.

Defined intimidating conduct and two additional mandatory years make the aggravated-element reading plausible. The enacted arrangement nevertheless supplies the better statutory account. Avoidance does not license rewriting an ascertainable penalty provision merely to avoid deciding its constitutional operation. The statute itself does not prescribe preponderance factfinding or dispense with charging protection. Applying the independent constitutional requirements thus gives effect to its words without treating the sentencing label as dispositive. The greater-minimum exception remains intact; no dispute concerning its application is presented. The distinct discharge condition is not independently adjudicated on this record.

**Precedent treatment:** Jones supplies the interpretive method and retains its carjacking construction. Castillo is distinguished because its former firearm-type text differs materially; its statutory-element holding remains intact.

##### II. Compulsory additional punishment requires element protections

**Holding and operative rule:** A present-offense fact legally necessary to raise a mandatory minimum must receive the charging protections applicable to the prosecution and must be validly admitted as an element or proved beyond a reasonable doubt to the proper guilt factfinder. A lawful jury waiver permits the judge to determine the element under that same proof standard; it neither enlarges the indictment nor converts an ordinary sentencing finding into the required adjudication.

**Authority:** Part II of Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplies six direct votes for the rule and its federal application.

**Controlling explanation:** Angle already holds that a present-offense fact changing either binding endpoint receives element protections and separately requires applicable federal grand-jury protection. The Court applies those holdings to brandishing. Before that additional finding, the law permits a sentence beginning at five years; afterward, it forbids a sentence below seven. The unchanged outer maximum does not erase that alteration of lawful punishment. Apprendi supplies the actual-authorization principle, and Gaudin and Winship supply the factfinder and proof protections once the aggravating fact is identified.

The Government and the dissent emphasize that seven years could have been selected within the outer range. That argument confuses discretionary selection with a new legal obligation. Judicial findings remain permissible for choosing punishment within a range already authorized; they cannot establish the fact that first changes the binding range. This is an allocation rule, not a policy judgment against mandatory sentences. Angle has already overruled McMillan's contrary unchanged-maximum distinction, and the Court adheres to that treatment. Legislative reliance does not sustain this pending sentence under a displaced rule; final judgments retain their own review doctrines.

Harris's indictment omitted brandishing, and his lawful jury waiver preserved the reasonable-doubt requirement. The contested preponderance finding supplies neither an element admission nor the missing aggravated adjudication. Prior-conviction facts, collateral retroactivity and a general redesign of sentencing are outside the present question; this decision creates no constitutional prior-conviction exception.

**Precedent treatment:** Angle is applied, including its prior overruling of McMillan to the stated extent. Apprendi supplies the punishment-authorization principle; Gaudin and Winship supply proper factfinding and reasonable doubt. Jones and Castillo retain their independent federal charging consequences.

##### III. This record satisfies all four plain-error requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming without deciding that Harris forfeited the constitutional theory, Rule 52(b) requires error, clarity at review in this settled-law-then-changed setting, the defendant's ordinary showing of outcome-affecting prejudice, and a separate determination that the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings before discretionary correction is warranted. Harris satisfies each condition on this record; the Court reverses the sentence-sustaining judgment, vacates the firearm sentence and remands for lawful resentencing while preserving the conviction.

**Authority:** Part III of Scalia's opinion has the same six direct joins as Part II, including its affirmative plainness application, independent prejudice finding and discretionary correction.

**Controlling explanation:** Olano separates forfeiture from intentional waiver and preserves the defendant's ordinary prejudice burden. The statutory objection and later constitutional argument need not be treated as identical: even the stricter forfeiture standard yields relief. Applying Angle, compulsory use of the uncharged, lower-standard aggravator is error. Johnson makes it plain now because settled contrary trial law changed during direct review; the Court decides no timing rule for initially unsettled law.

Harris demonstrates outcome-affecting prejudice. He contested brandishing; the judge found it a close question under a preponderance standard; and the resulting minimum supplied the sentence through §2K2.4. The carrying verdict did not decide the distinct intimidation purpose beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge's view that seven years was appropriate is contrary evidence, but his express refusal to select that term independently leaves a reasonable probability of a different lawful sentencing outcome. This is an affirmative inference from the actual record, not presumed injury from lost discretion or a burden shifted to the Government. Possible future departure authority is not an entered alternative judgment.

Correction is independently warranted because genuinely contested conduct, never adjudicated with the required protections, compelled additional imprisonment. That circumstance seriously impairs sentencing fairness and integrity. Substantial-rights injury alone would not suffice, and actual innocence is unnecessary. Johnson's clear-materiality setting and Cotton's overwhelming, essentially uncontroverted threshold record are materially different. Neder's preserved-error inquiry supplies neither automatic reversal nor a hypothetical brandishing verdict; clear-error affirmance cannot supply the missing reasonable-doubt adjudication.

Resentencing begins from the five-year minimum and its applicable guideline consequence. Any different sentence requires an independently lawful basis and reasons. A further aggravated proceeding requires independent charging, waiver, procedural and double-jeopardy authority. Neither an automatic enhancement trial nor a particular replacement term follows.

**Precedent treatment:** Olano's four conditions and ordinary burden are applied; Johnson supplies limited review-time plainness but its refusal of correction is distinguished. Cotton preserves nonjurisdictional treatment and record-specific discretion. Neder retains its distinct preserved-error burden; Vonn permits relevant existing-record consideration without a burden shift.

### Ring v. Arizona, No. 01-488

Merits decision, June 24, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 22, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Arizona, 200 Ariz. 267, 25 P.3d 1139, which affirmed Ring's death sentence. The question is whether the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments require the jury to find a present-offense aggravating fact necessary for death eligibility, notwithstanding Walton v. Arizona. Ring seeks relief from judicially authorized death; Arizona seeks affirmance and argues alternatively that the robbery verdict supplies harmlessness.

A jury convicted Ring of felony murder in an armored-car robbery but did not return a premeditated-murder verdict. The judge made the additional findings required for death eligibility and imposed death. The surviving aggravation included pecuniary gain; the appellate treatment of other aggravation supplied no jury finding. Arizona contends that pecuniary gain was implicit in the robbery verdict. The actual elements, instructions, required findings and relevant record control that claim. Harris and Ring are separate same-day decisions sharing the law entering June 24; neither supplies prior law for the other. Angle's June 20 decision did not itself overrule Walton.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**The Arizona Supreme Court's judgment sustaining the death sentence is reversed and remanded: seven votes to reverse, one to vacate, and one to affirm.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela would vacate the appellate affirmance and remand; O'Connor would affirm.

#### Holdings

##### I. The jury must determine the fact that first authorizes death

**Holding and operative rule:** A statutory present-offense aggravating fact necessary to make death legally available must be validly admitted as an element or proved to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt in a jury-tried prosecution. Walton is overruled insofar as it permits a sentencing judge, sitting without a jury, to supply that necessary aggravating fact; existing lawful jury-waiver requirements remain, and waiver does not lower the reasonable-doubt burden.

**Authority:** Part I of Kennedy's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg, supplying seven direct votes. Breyer's judgment vote rests on a different Eighth Amendment ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that capital eligibility turns on lawful punishment authority. Without an additional statutory aggravator, Arizona law does not permit death on Ring's felony-murder verdict. Finding that aggravator therefore enlarges the punishment the law allows. Apprendi supplies this actual-authorization principle; Angle reinforces the distinction between a binding legal condition and ordinary selection within existing authority. Neither earlier case decided Walton's reserved capital boundary. The Court decides it now.

The State's description of murder as generally punishable by death overlooks the additional statutory prerequisite. A sentencing label cannot do the constitutional work of an element. Winship supplies proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and Gaudin protects the proper factfinder's determination of the legally necessary fact. Judicial expertise or consistency cannot transfer that constitutional responsibility. Capital narrowing identifies the permissible class; it does not require that a judge find the fact establishing membership.

Walton's contrary allocation cannot coexist with this principle. Procedural reliance deserves respect in lawful implementation, but does not justify retaining the inconsistent rule for the fact that first permits death in a pending case. Judicial final selection and review are distinct from eligibility; Harris v. Alabama's adjudicated advisory-weight and override questions do not determine this allocation issue. Nor can appellate reweighing itself supply the missing eligibility verdict.

Ring's granted claim concerns the necessary aggravating fact. Mitigation, ultimate selection and complete weighing procedure are outside that question, as are state grand-jury incorporation, prior-conviction facts and collateral retroactivity. The holding supplies no constitutional prior-conviction exception or new general capital-trial procedure. Neither a valid admission nor a lawful jury waiver supplies the missing adjudication here.

**Precedent treatment:** Apprendi's rule is extended to the expressly reserved capital-eligibility boundary; Angle is applied as supporting range doctrine. Walton's contrary necessary-aggravator allocation is overruled. Winship and Gaudin supply proof and factfinder protections; Harris v. Alabama retains its distinct final-selection holding.

##### II. The omitted eligibility finding receives qualified actual-verdict review

**Holding and operative rule:** This omitted statutory eligibility finding is subject to the qualified Neder inquiry: the State must establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the omission did not contribute to the actual verdict, examining the actual instructions, the evidence they directed to the jury, required findings and material conflicts. Necessary equivalent jury findings suffice but are not exclusive; supported whole-record inferences may establish noncontribution only when reliably connected to the actual adjudication, without new credibility findings or substitution of sufficient evidence for a hypothetical verdict.

**Authority:** Part II of Kennedy's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Thomas, supplying five direct votes for the method and conditional remedy. Scalia and Ginsburg permit only genuine equivalent jury findings and do not join this broader method; neither Breyer nor O'Connor supplies an additional rationale vote.

**Controlling explanation:** Neder applies Chapman's burden to a preserved element omission through examination of the actual verdict. Its method is extended to this omitted statutory eligibility finding because the question remains whether the proper factfinder's actual adjudication makes the omission harmless, not whether an appellate court believes the aggravator probably true. Roy and Yates supply attention to the instructions, required findings and material evidentiary conflicts. Sullivan's invalid reasonable-doubt instruction remains distinct from omission of this particular aggravating element.

Arizona actually argues that the robbery verdict implicitly establishes pecuniary gain. The word robbery does not decide that contention. The reviewing court must compare the particular aggravator under Arizona law with the elements, actual charge and necessary jury findings, then evaluate any qualifying record inference. Strong or uncontested evidence alone is not an automatic cure; it must support noncontribution to the actual adjudication beyond a reasonable doubt. New credibility resolution is forbidden, and appellate reweighing of selection factors cannot substitute for the eligibility inquiry.

The narrower view would permit cure only where the jury necessarily made an equivalent finding. Such findings are sufficient, but Neder's qualified method also permits a reliable inference from the instructions and whole record without converting review into an initial factfinding trial. The burden remains the State's throughout. The argument's actual-record application was not decided below under the correct constitutional rule, so it returns for initial determination there. The Court determines neither harmlessness nor harmfulness here.

If the State fails its showing, the death sentence must be vacated; further sentencing requires independent state-law authority and compliance with constitutional and double-jeopardy limits. If the burden is met, a consistent sentence judgment may be reinstated. A remand for initial harmlessness review does not itself require unconditional sentence vacatur and creates no automatic enhancement trial.

**Precedent treatment:** Neder's qualified actual-verdict method is extended to the omitted eligibility finding; Chapman retains the State's burden. Roy and Yates supply the instruction-centered inquiry. Sullivan's distinct structural rule remains intact. Clemons retains lawful appellate reweighing but supplies no missing eligibility adjudication.

##### III. The appellate affirmance is reversed and the actual review contention returns

**Holding and operative rule:** The Arizona Supreme Court's judgment is reversed insofar as it sustains Ring's death sentence under judge-only eligibility factfinding, and the case is remanded for proper consideration of the State's actual harmlessness contention. The felony-murder conviction stands; the appellate reversal is distinct from unconditional vacatur of the death sentence.

**Authority:** Part III of Kennedy's opinion is joined by Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg. These six support the reversal rationale; Breyer adds a seventh reversal-and-remand vote on his independent selection ground. Stone-Zsela would reverse the judge-only constitutional ruling, vacate the appellate affirmance and remand, and therefore does not join Part III. His agreement with Parts I and II remains distinct from this formal disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Arizona judgment sustained the challenged allocation under Walton. The Court's eligibility holding removes that legal basis for affirmance. The State's alternative actual-verdict argument remains material and requires initial consideration below. A ruling about who must find the aggravator does not itself establish the outcome of that distinct review inquiry. Neder supports leaving initial harmlessness application to the lower court; agreement on this appellate remand supplies no additional join in Part II's broader method. The mandate preserves the existing guilt adjudication and leaves further sentence proceedings to the governing constitutional standard and independently available state law.

**Precedent treatment:** Walton no longer supports the challenged eligibility allocation. Neder supplies the distinction between reversing an erroneous legal premise and initially applying the proper harmlessness rule below.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Apprendi v. New Jersey: extended to the present-offense statutory aggravator necessary for death eligibility; its capital reservation is resolved at that boundary, without converting final selection into an element.
- United States v. Angle, June 20, 2002: supports examination of actual binding punishment authority; it did not already overrule Walton or decide capital selection.
- Walton v. Arizona: overruled insofar as it permits a sentencing judge without a jury to find the statutory aggravating fact necessary to authorize death. Its other propositions are displaced only to the extent inseparable from that allocation.
- In re Winship: requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the identified constitutional element; lawful jury waiver preserves that burden.
- United States v. Gaudin: protects the proper factfinder's determination of the legally necessary aggravating fact, subject to existing lawful jury-waiver requirements.
- Harris v. Alabama: its adjudicated advisory-weight and final-selection questions remain distinct from eligibility; the Court adopts no general Eighth Amendment jury-selection rule.
- Neder v. United States: its qualified actual-verdict method is extended to this omitted eligibility finding; its permission for supported whole-record inference retains the actual-instructions and material-conflicts limits.
- Chapman v. California: supplies the State's beyond-reasonable-doubt burden to show noncontribution to the actual verdict.
- California v. Roy: supplies instruction-centered examination of the omitted element and required findings; it does not permit a hypothetical appellate verdict.
- Yates v. Evatt: supplies attention to the actual instructions, relevant evidence and material evidentiary conflicts; it does not permit substitution of a hypothetical appellate verdict.
- Sullivan v. Louisiana: retains its structural rule for a vitiated reasonable-doubt standard; the present omission receives the distinct qualified inquiry.

- Atkins v. Virginia, June 20, 2002: retains its separate categorical disability restriction; it supplies no rule allocating aggravator findings.

#### Law After Decision

A present-offense statutory fact necessary to authorize death receives constitutional element protections. Walton's contrary allocation is displaced; the State's capital label and its reasons for narrowing the eligible class do not exempt that fact from the jury and reasonable-doubt requirements. The final choice among constitutionally authorized penalties remains distinct.

For this omitted eligibility finding, five Justices establish the State's qualified actual-verdict harmlessness burden and the conditional sentence consequence. The seven-Justice eligibility holding does not depend on Breyer's independent jury-selection theory or on combining inconsistent harmlessness methods. The State's actual-record application is remand work under the announced rule, not an unresolved legal standard. Preserved lawful state procedures and constitutional limits control any later sentencing.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stone-Zsela joins the eligibility rule and the qualified actual-verdict inquiry in Parts I and II. He would reverse the ruling that judge-only death-eligibility factfinding is constitutional, vacate the appellate affirmance of the death sentence, and remand for the State's actual harmlessness argument. The appellate affirmance should be set aside for that initial inquiry rather than reversed as in Part III. The State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt under the qualified Neder framework by examining the actual elements, instructions, findings and qualifying evidence, without resolving new credibility disputes or treating robbery as an automatic pecuniary-gain finding. If the State fails, the death sentence must be vacated; any later sentencing requires independent state-law authority and compliance with constitutional and double-jeopardy limits. If it succeeds, a consistent sentence judgment may be reinstated. He preserves the felony-murder conviction and supplies no universal structural-error rule or automatic new capital proceeding.

**Kennedy, concurring.** Kennedy joins the entire Court opinion while maintaining that Apprendi was wrongly decided. Its settled maximum rule nevertheless must be implemented in a principled way, and Walton cannot survive where an additional fact is indispensable to death eligibility. His disagreement with Angle's distinct floor extension does not justify withholding the jury protection required by the governing maximum rule here. Respect for state expectations counsels caution about other extensions and lawful implementation; it does not reconcile this judge-only eligibility allocation with Apprendi. He supports the qualified actual-record remand rather than a general jury-selection command.

**Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment; Ginsburg joins Part II.** In Part I, Scalia supports the eligibility holding despite his continued objection to the capital-narrowing requirements associated with Furman. Once the State makes an aggravating fact legally necessary, its origin does not remove the jury guarantee. Distinguishing factors adopted voluntarily from those responding to constitutional narrowing would not provide a reliable basis for dispensing with that protection. Gaudin and the punishment-authority principle underlying Apprendi and Angle require the fact to receive element safeguards. He preserves the distinction between that finding and the ultimate life-or-death selection, disagreeing with Breyer's independent Eighth Amendment approach. The concern that legislative sentencing labels could diminish the jury's constitutional role reinforces his allocation position; it is not a new factual finding about Ring.

In Part II, joined by Ginsburg, Scalia rejects the Court's broader whole-record inference method. Their Neder position permits harmlessness only through genuine equivalent findings the jury actually made. A court's assessment that the jury probably would have found pecuniary gain cannot supply an unasked element. They join Court Parts I and III and support reversal and remand, leaving the actual charge and findings to be examined, but withhold Court Part II. Ginsburg joins only this error-review limitation, not Scalia's separate account of capital-narrowing doctrine. Neither finds that robbery automatically establishes pecuniary gain or adopts a new appellate credibility determination.

**Breyer, concurring in the judgment.** Breyer continues to reject Apprendi's Sixth Amendment reclassification and joins neither the Court's eligibility rationale nor its broader harmlessness method. He nevertheless concludes that the Eighth Amendment requires a jury to take responsibility for the ultimate decision to impose death. He expressly reconsiders his own join in Harris v. Alabama and adopts the jury-responsibility view expressed by Stevens in that case and Spaziano. Gregg and Furman require special capital safeguards; Witherspoon identifies the jury's role in expressing community judgment, and proportionality requires attention to whether this defendant should suffer death. In Breyer's view, retribution is the principal justification for capital punishment, and a jury better expresses the community's moral judgment about its use in the particular case than a single official, even an elected judge. He treats death differently from ordinary guided sentencing. His ground reaches final selection, not simply an aggravator that makes death available: harmlessness of the omitted eligibility finding alone would not answer his objection to judge-only final selection. He concurs in reversal and remand, but his separate rule commands no Court majority.

**O'Connor, dissenting.** O'Connor would affirm against the allocation claim by retaining Walton and reconsidering Apprendi. She acknowledges that the two rules are incompatible; capital guidance does not harmonize them. Her Apprendi and Angle dissents defend legislative authority to distinguish offense elements from sentencing considerations and the historical judicial role in selecting punishment. She regards the Court's maximum-fact rule as constitutionally unwarranted and its extension as disruptive of settled state procedures. Those objections support her choice of which precedent to reconsider, not a finding that an aggravator is legally unnecessary. She does not join Breyer's Eighth Amendment rule, supply a vote for the Court's broader harmlessness directions, or sustain the sentence by making a substitute pecuniary-gain finding. Ordinary error review and collateral limits remain distinct from her preferred no-error ground.
