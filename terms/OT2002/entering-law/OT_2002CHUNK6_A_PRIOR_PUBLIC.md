### Kansas v. Hendricks / Hendricks v. Kansas, Nos. 95-1649 and 95-9075

Consolidated plenary merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 10, 1996. On writs of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Kansas.

The Kansas Supreme Court's March 1, 1996 judgment, In re Hendricks, 259 Kan. 246, 912 P.2d 129, invalidated Hendricks's commitment under the 1994 Sexually Violent Predator Act because it considered the statutory mental-abnormality definition constitutionally insufficient. It did not decide the preserved Double Jeopardy or Ex Post Facto claims, presented through Hendricks's cross-petition. Kansas seeks reversal and restoration of the commitment; Hendricks seeks affirmance on due process or the alternative punishment grounds. The granted questions concern the Act's substantive commitment criteria and whether this confinement is punitive. Both dockets concern the same commitment and judgment.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4, in both consolidated dockets.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would reverse and reject both punishment-dependent challenges.

The invalidation of Hendricks's commitment is sustained because this application imposes punishment for conduct predating the Act. He may not remain confined under that application. The disposition is as applied: it does not facially invalidate every prospective application or decide whether another independently lawful civil-commitment route is available. No new factfinding remand or release date is prescribed. The due-process discussion is not a separate reversal component; Double Jeopardy is not reached.

| Same lead opinion, Part I: non-dispositive due-process discussion | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Does not independently support affirmance or a separate reversal | Bounded rejection of a categorical diagnostic-vocabulary objection; Ginsburg reserves the issue |

#### Holdings

##### I. Civil intent remains rebuttable in this confinement scheme

**Holding and operative rule:** In assessing whether Kansas's civil commitment of Hendricks constitutes punishment for Ex Post Facto purposes, the Court first determines the Legislature's intended civil or criminal character; an intended civil scheme is treated as punishment only upon the clearest proof that its purpose or effect is punitive. The Act's civil designation is relevant but not conclusive, and neither confinement, a connection to crime, indeterminate duration, criminal-style procedural protection nor treatment difficulty alone decides that demanding inquiry.

**Authority:** Parts II–III of Breyer's opinion are the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. Those five Justices adopt each controlling proposition below, its application, qualifications and remedy. Part I's separate due-process discussion is not a ground for this judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted civil purpose must be tested against what this confinement does. United States v. Ursery supplies the civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry for the forfeiture statutes it considered; United States v. Ward supplies the distinction between legislative intent and the demanding showing necessary to overcome it. The same distinction applies here, but Ursery's conclusion about forfeiture does not answer a question about personal confinement. Kennedy v. Mendoza-Martinez permits examination of punitive purpose and effect without making a single indicator conclusive.

Kansas expressed a purpose of control, care and treatment, assigned custody to its social-services agency, and conditioned confinement on a present qualifying condition and danger. These are substantial civil features. The statute also commands care, review and release procedures. Their existence matters; it does not establish that the initial confinement operated as promised. The contrary claim that a civil label resolves classification would prevent the very inquiry Ward and Ursery require. Conversely, a court may not replace clearest proof with a demand for successful cure or treat each deficiency as a criminal penalty. The Court assesses the supported original operation as a whole, without a new exhaustive checklist or a general rule that every preventive confinement is punishment.

**Precedent treatment:** Ursery's method is extended to this confinement question without importing its forfeiture result; Ward's demanding intent-and-effect inquiry is applied; Mendoza-Martinez's purpose-and-effect analysis remains nonexclusive and no single indicator becomes dispositive.

##### II. This retrospective application imposes punishment

**Holding and operative rule:** The cumulative circumstances of Hendricks's original commitment—additional confinement after his sentence, selection tied to earlier sexual offenses, prisonlike restraint, and initially inadequate treatment sustained on the prospect of a future specialized program despite acknowledged possible treatment—provide the clearest proof of punitive operation in this application. Because the Act imposes that additional punishment for conduct predating its enactment, its application to Hendricks violates the Ex Post Facto Clause, and the judgment invalidating this commitment is affirmed.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above; this is the sufficient ground for the judgment in both consolidated dockets.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these circumstances must be considered together. At the October 1994 hearing, neither proposed specialist contract had been signed and the dedicated clinical program was not established. The trial court nevertheless left commitment in force on the possibility that the treatment program would become available. That is the initial treatment deficiency material here, rather than a finding that no clinician or treatment opportunity existed.

The contrary evidence is real: Kansas pursued procurement, had direct-care staff and hospital professionals available, anticipated individualized assessment, and had previously offered Hendricks a prison treatment program. His testimony included both a pay choice and an unexplained qualification concerning a parole-board member. These facts prevent an inference that all treatment was deliberately withheld. They do not remove the punitive significance of the additional offense-linked, prisonlike restraint and the prospective basis on which its treatment justification was sustained. The Court finds the demanding showing satisfied by their cumulative operation, not by a missing job title or an isolated contracting failure.

Kansas's independent interest in protecting the public and its statutory care and release obligations do not by themselves answer that showing. United States v. Sahhar permits custody beyond a hypothetical criminal maximum only on an independently lawful civil basis; it does not classify this commitment. No rejected meaningful alternative or sham release procedure is established or presumed. No reason for the failed bids, deliberate deferral of a particular effective therapy, complete absence of a clinical plan, or specified later treatment regimen is found. The additional punitive confinement follows a later enactment applied to earlier offenses. The Ex Post Facto Clause therefore forbids this application without requiring a separate decision about double jeopardy.

**Precedent treatment:** Sahhar's rejection of a hypothetical sentence ceiling is distinguished because its holding presupposes independently lawful civil custody; it does not answer the punitive-operation question here or supply Kansas with the federal statute's procedures.

##### III. Scope of the judgment and questions not decided

Hendricks may not remain confined under this application of the Act for conduct predating it. The Court affirms the invalidation of this commitment on the as-applied Ex Post Facto ground; it does not facially invalidate every prospective application, decide the validity of every later genuinely civil program, or decide whether another independently lawful civil-commitment route is available. No new release date, stay, hearing schedule, clinical protocol or alternative-custody authorization is prescribed.

The Double Jeopardy claim is not reached. Punitive classification does not by itself establish attachment of jeopardy or identity of offenses, and the Court does not decide those distinct elements. Part I's rejection of a categorical objection to the words mental abnormality is non-dispositive discussion, not an independently sufficient ground for affirmance or a holding that this commitment satisfies all due-process requirements. Ginsburg reserves that issue. Treatment-related due process, broader propensity-only definitions, a universal personality-disorder rule, a requirement of absolute inability to control conduct in every circumstance, and a new jury instruction are not decided. No Marks synthesis is needed for the five directly joined Ex Post Facto holdings.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Ursery; United States v. $405,089.23 in United States Currency, June 24, 1996:** Extends their rebuttable civil-intent and clearest-proof method from the specified forfeiture schemes to classification of this personal confinement for Ex Post Facto purposes. Their forfeiture result is not a classification of the Kansas commitment; their distinct Double Jeopardy limits remain.
- **United States v. Ward, 448 U.S. 242:** Applies the distinction between intended civil character and the demanding proof needed to establish punitive purpose or effect.
- **Kennedy v. Mendoza-Martinez, 372 U.S. 144:** Applies examination of punitive purpose and effect without making an individual indicator decisive or creating a mandatory exhaustive checklist.
- **United States v. Sahhar, December 16, 1996:** Distinguishes its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling for independently lawful §4246 custody. That decision does not establish that this Kansas confinement is civil; its federal placement, proof and release provisions are not transplanted into Kansas law.

No precedent is overruled. The non-dispositive Part I discussion draws on Foucha v. Louisiana's requirement of current lawful grounds and its reservation of materially narrower preventive regimes, and on Sahhar's insistence on independent civil authority. It does not change those holdings, Addington's ordinary civil-commitment proof protection, or Heller v. Doe's rejection of a particular unequal proof arrangement. The Court does not adopt a new due-process rule through that discussion. California Department of Corrections v. Morales's meaningful-parole-opportunity holdings remain confined to their subject; they supply neither a general speculative-risk rule nor a substitute for proving punitive operation here. Lynce v. Mathis's exhaustion disposition supplies no credit-withdrawal merits rule. Blockburger and the retained Grady rule are not applied because the independent Double Jeopardy elements are not reached.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 23, 1997, the civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry governs the Ex Post Facto classification of this commitment. The supported cumulative original operation establishes punishment as applied to Hendricks, and its imposition for pre-enactment conduct is unconstitutional. No single feature is made automatically punitive, and no new burden shift, mandatory alternatives inquiry, treatment protocol or release procedure is created.

The decision does not invalidate all prospective applications of the Act or all later civil programs. It leaves the availability of another independently lawful commitment route undecided. The substantive-due-process discussion creates no additional controlling rule, and Double Jeopardy's separate elements remain unresolved. Current lawful civil grounds and effective safeguards retain their independent importance under Foucha and Sahhar.

#### Separate Writings

**Part I's non-dispositive due-process discussion.** Breyer, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, rejects a categorical constitutional objection resting on the statutory words mental abnormality. The discussion is confined to the serious recognized condition, evidence of impaired control and connection to dangerous conduct shown here. A past offense and a prediction alone do not supply that substantive limitation; neither a statutory verdict nor a high proof burden substitutes for it. Foucha concerns custody after its conceded illness predicate ends and leaves narrower preventive regimes open; Sahhar requires a new lawful civil basis rather than making every legislative diagnosis sufficient. This agreement does not dispense with the constitutional safeguards needed for a lawful civil commitment. The discussion does not decide treatment-related due process or every application of personality disorder. It supports no separate reversal of the Kansas judgment and is not the ground of the Court's affirmance. Ginsburg does not join it and reserves the question.

**O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The dissent agrees with Part I's bounded rejection of a diagnostic-vocabulary requirement but would reverse the Kansas judgment and reject the punishment-dependent challenges. Applying the demanding civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry, it finds the supported original record insufficient to turn the commitment into punishment. The current condition and danger requirements, social-services custody, separate setting, care duty, procurement efforts, interim professional resources and actual review and release routes support an independent preventive purpose. The lack of a completed specialist program is serious, but does not establish punitive purpose or effect when the contrary original evidence is considered. Neither difficult treatment nor an indefinite duration alone defeats otherwise lawful civil custody.

The dissent does not find that treatment was adequate, that contracting failed for a benign reason, or that later treatment-hour representations cure the original deficiencies. It preserves proper challenges to unconstitutional conditions and requires genuine continuing predicates; a civil description would not protect a proved punitive operation. The dissent relies on the actual statutory sequence. Section 59-29a08 requires yearly examination, a report and judicial review. Probable cause that the condition has changed so the person is safe at large and will not commit sexual violence requires a further hearing; there the State must prove beyond reasonable doubt that the abnormality or disorder remains such that the person is unsafe at large and likely to engage in sexual violence if released. A full release trial does not occur automatically each year. Under section 59-29a10, the Secretary must authorize a petition upon determining that the condition has changed so the person is not likely to commit predatory sexual violence if released; a hearing and the State's reasonable-doubt burden follow. Section 59-29a11 permits petitions without permission, but a first or later unauthorized petition may be denied without hearing as frivolous. After an earlier unauthorized petition was found frivolous or insufficient condition change was found, a later petition must be denied unless its facts support sufficient change to warrant a hearing. These commands do not establish compliant administration.

On its civil classification, both Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy fail at their necessary punishment prerequisite. The dissent makes no unnecessary ruling on attachment, offense identity or every retrospective regulatory burden.

#### Source Notes

The original commitment and treatment record is reflected in In re Hendricks, 259 Kan. 246, 912 P.2d 129, and the Joint Appendix: procurement and administration at 60–66, the qualified prison-program testimony at 178–180, original staffing and anticipated individualized care at 366–380, and the October ruling at 386–387. The Act's distinct care and review provisions appear in Kan. Stat. Ann. §§59-29a08–11; the annual examination and review text does not command an automatic full annual release trial. Kansas's acknowledgment of potential treatment does not establish any particular treatment delivered to Hendricks.

The separate August 28, 1995 habeas proceeding and later treatment-hour representations are not findings about the original confinement; judicial notice of the separate proceeding was denied by the Kansas Supreme Court. The decision does not rely on them to supply actual later care. The cause of failed contracting, the complete interim clinical plan, a rejected adequate alternative and sham release administration are not established. The Court's punitive classification is its assessment of the cumulative supported original operation, with those limits.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Kansas Supreme Court's judgment stands at the scope necessary to invalidate Hendricks's commitment on the alternative as-applied Ex Post Facto ground. The Supreme Court merits review of both consolidated cross-petitions is concluded. Hendricks may not remain confined under this application of the Act for conduct predating it. No additional factfinding remand, new hearing, dated release command, stay or determination that another custody route is lawful is ordered.

### Hudson v. United States, No. 96-976

Merits decision, December 10, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Hudson v. United States, No. 96-976. Argued October 8, 1997; decided December 10, 1997. Certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 92 F.3d 1026, reviewing reinstatement of a criminal prosecution after civil banking sanctions.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument and decision. No Justice was recused or otherwise absent.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The criminal prosecution may proceed. The Court orders no additional accounting hearing and decides neither guilt nor the identity of the administrative predicates and charged offenses.

#### Opinion Topology

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I and III: classification framework, combined application, and disposition | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance | Parts I and III |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II: Halper's personal-penalty application | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg | Supports affirmance on the personal-penalty analysis | Part II |
| Concurrence in Parts I and III and the judgment | Stone-Zsela | Author alone | Joins Parts I and III and affirmance | Parts I and III and the judgment |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Scalia | Thomas | Agrees only in the judgment | Judgment only |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Civil intent remains rebuttable through examination of actual punitive operation.

Congress's civil designation of these banking monetary sanctions and the regulatory character of the occupational restriction establish the intended civil framework. That designation may be overcome by the clearest proof that the sanctions' purpose or effect is criminal; their supported design and operation must be assessed together, without treating deterrence, severity, occupational burden or overlap with crime as individually conclusive. Part I controls through Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

Ward supplies the intent-and-effect inquiry. Ursery preserves both its demanding override requirement and the distinct field of personal civil penalties; Hendricks requires meaningful examination of actual operation. The monetary provisions expressly impose civil assessments through administrative procedures. Their good-faith and gravity considerations affect amount and do not establish a universal willfulness element. The applicable original monetary authorization cannot be replaced by the increased tiers enacted after the February 1989 notice. Mendoza-Martinez informs punitive-effect examination without requiring a new exhaustive checklist. Civil wording is relevant, but it cannot make actual punishment immune from constitutional review.

##### 2. These monetary assessments survive Halper's retained personal-penalty inquiry.

A personal civil penalty remains subject to Halper's requirement of a rational remedial relationship, including reasonable approximation; that rule is not overruled or replaced by a universal statutory-face inquiry. On the supported record, the actual $44,000 in total monetary assessments, compared with the District Court's finding of $72,000 in government costs pursuing these defendants, permits a remedial characterization and requires no further accounting. Part II controls through Breyer, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

The Government may recover legitimate pursuit costs without establishing that deterrence played no part in the regulatory response. The objective relation answers the present claim; the agency's failure initially to calculate its costs does not itself establish punishment. The initial $200,000 demand is not the obligation ultimately imposed. Nor are alleged banking losses substituted for proved government costs. This application creates no automatic monetary ratio, immunity for every penalty below an asserted cost figure, or universal compensation ceiling for other sanctions.

##### 3. The combined sanctions do not bar this prosecution.

The banking restriction, which permits participation with written regulatory authorization, protects positions of trust in a regulated industry. Considered with the actual financial assessments, its supported operation does not provide the clearest proof of criminal punishment; the subsequent prosecution therefore is not barred on the presented multiple-punishment theory. Part III controls through Breyer, Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg.

The restriction's substantial occupational effect does not erase its protective function, and the Court considers that effect together with the monetary burden. Kurth Ranch involved an extraordinary arrest-linked assessment on confiscated contraband whose combined operation made it a second punishment; these sanctions have no comparable defining operation. Hendricks concerned prisonlike custody and the actual failure of a civil treatment structure, facts absent here. Those distinctions preserve both precedents. A different banking scheme or materially different operation could produce a different classification. Because criminal punishment is not established, the Court does not decide Blockburger or retained Grady offense identity, attachment in punitive civil proceedings, the Excessive Fines Clause, or independent due-process objections.

#### Precedent Treatment

Halper remains binding in its exceptional personal-civil-penalty field and is applied to the supported monetary obligations and costs. Ursery's rebuttable civil classification and preservation of that field remain intact. Hendricks supplies cumulative actual-operation analysis without transferring its confinement outcome. Ward and Mendoza-Martinez retain their intent/effect and punitive-indicator roles. Kurth Ranch's extraordinary-tax holding is distinguished, not narrowed. Dixon, Grady, Blockburger and Felix retain their offense-identity rules; none is applied to determine the identity of these charges. Austin's distinct Excessive Fines protection remains available in an appropriate case. No precedent is overruled.

#### Law After Decision

Effective December 10, 1997, the identified OCC sanctions supply no multiple-punishment bar to this prosecution. Personal monetary sanctions remain subject to Halper's reasonable-remedial-approximation inquiry, while actual combined operation remains material under the civil-intent/clearest-proof framework. Neither regulatory labeling nor a monetary comparison alone creates a categorical safe harbor. No same-offense rule, fines rule, settlement-waiver rule or confinement rule changes.

#### Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela concurs in Parts I and III and the judgment. He applies Ursery and Hendricks without alteration: the combined design and actual operation of these monetary and occupational measures do not establish the clearest proof of criminal punishment. Their accountability and protective functions differ from Kurth Ranch's extraordinary exaction and Hendricks's prisonlike application. He does not join the separate independent Halper-accounting analysis and does not reject Halper's continuing force. His ground leaves different operational facts, excessiveness and offense identity open.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, concurs in the judgment. Their account requires successive criminal prosecutions, and the genuinely civil banking proceedings do not supply the first such prosecution. They would reject Halper's freestanding civil multiple-punishment method, while allowing inquiry into whether a nominally civil proceeding is actually criminal. That theory does not command a majority and does not overrule Halper.

#### Source Notes

The Tenth Circuit opinions, 14 F.3d 536 and 92 F.3d 1026, supply the lower proceedings and the reported $72,000 cost finding. The published administrative record recitation gives consent assessments of $16,500, $15,000 and $12,500, totaling $44,000, and the written-authorization qualification on banking participation. The lower opinion's opening individual amounts conflict with that recitation; its operative comparison uses the same $44,000 total. Alleged banking or FDIC losses of approximately $900,000 are not a proved government-loss finding. Sections 101 and 103 of Pub. L. 95-630 and the official amendment notes to 12 U.S.C. §§93, 504 and 1818 establish the civil design and version limits. The complete original administrative instruments, indictment particulars and underlying cost-hearing documents are not reproduced. The available materials establish no additional statutory predicate, waiver or count-specific offense identity.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

This Supreme Court review ends. The reinstated criminal proceedings continue in the ordinary course, with properly remaining defenses preserved. The prior appellate nonparticipation ruling retains its procedural scope; consent supplies no newly found waiver or admission. No accounting, conviction, dismissal of individual counts, or scheduled return to this Court is ordered.

**End of entry: Hudson v. United States, Merits decision, December 10, 1997.**

### W.P. v. Verniero, Third Circuit No. 96-5416

Plenary merits decision, March 16, 1998

#### Holdings

##### I. The challenged notification is not punishment on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** For the class whose offenses preceded October 31, 1994 and who face Tier 2 or Tier 3 notification, New Jersey's civil public-protection purpose remains unrebutted by the clearest proof of punitive purpose or effect in the scheme's supported combined operation. The challenged notification therefore supplies no Ex Post Facto or Double Jeopardy ground for relief; the Court decides no same-offense question.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg and Breyer; six Justices directly adopt the common civil-classification ground. Scalia and Thomas support rejection on their separate grounds; Stevens dissents on Ex Post Facto punishment and does not reach independent Double Jeopardy identity.

**Controlling explanation:** Ursery supplies civil intent followed by demanding contrary proof, and Hendricks and Hudson require examination of actual combined operation. A civil label is insufficient. Here risk grading, individual exceptions and audience tailoring connect the warning to public protection. Tier 2 reaches qualifying schools, child-care facilities and organizations serving children or supporting women, subject to the state court's likely-encounter construction; Tier 3 reaches members of the public likely to encounter the registrant. The scheme directly imposes no residence, movement, employment or licensing prohibition.

The contrary shaming argument is substantial. Government actively distributes conviction and predicted-danger information, with potentially severe effects on housing, employment, relationships and safety. Those consequences cannot be dismissed categorically because some involve private acts. But the supported tailoring and causal record do not establish the clearest proof of punishment. The reported nineteen experiences, six state-compelled disclosures and separate 135-notification figure cannot be merged into one incidence calculation. Confidentiality instructions for Tier 2 and warnings against threats, vandalism and assault matter without guaranteeing perfect containment.

Hendricks found a different cumulative operation: prisonlike additional custody sustained on a future treatment structure. Its punitive result supplies no automatic classification of notification, just as its civil designation supplied no immunity there. Hudson and Kurth Ranch preserve meaningful aggregate examination; Halper's personal-monetary-penalty accounting is not a notification formula. The holding neither makes reputation irrelevant nor treats every public-safety measure as civil. Materially different punitive operation remains a different case. Registration alone, Tier 1, post-enactment offenses and every later registry are not decided.

##### II. The State must justify both tier and notification plan before ordinary notification

**Holding and operative rule:** In the ordinary circumstances presented, Tier 2 or Tier 3 notification may not proceed without a meaningful advance opportunity to contest both the risk classification and the proposed notification plan, with the prosecutor bearing persuasion for each. The protected interest rests on New Jersey's substantive restriction requiring disclosure to serve a legitimate and substantial interest with minimized invasion, rather than on reputation alone or a promise of procedures.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's opinion, joined by the seven other Associate Justices; eight Justices directly adopt this protected-interest, hearing and State-persuasion ground. Stevens joins conditionally because civil notification remains available under the Court's judgment. Stone-Zsela supports the disposition on his stated procedural grounds without joining this distinct state-entitlement explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Sandin directs attention to a real substantive constraint and meaningful deprivation; its prison setting supplies no universal noncustodial threshold. Paul v. Davis distinguishes reputation alone, which is not the sole interest here. The actual state disclosure restraint and irreversible distribution make erroneous classification or an excessive audience a protected deprivation.

Mathews requires consideration of the interest, error risk and practical burdens. The State selects the tier and audience and already assembles an initial case. Requiring the registrant ultimately to disprove that justification wrongly allocates the risk of an unsupported disclosure. An initial prosecutorial showing does not replace the State's ultimate persuasion on both determinations. Public protection and prompt warning remain substantial interests, but do not justify making the affected person disprove the affirmative basis for disclosure.

Individual review must permit challenges to factual accuracy and calculation, atypical cases and excessive plans. A credible supported indication of likely reoffense requires high-risk treatment regardless of score; a physical condition minimizing risk, including advanced age or debilitating illness, requires low-risk treatment regardless of score. The Scale is not conclusive. This individual proceeding does not authorize a new facial attack on the Scale or its weights. No individual tier or plan is approved, and no general federal informational-privacy right is decided. The distinct question of the standard of proof is addressed below.

##### III. Risk classification requires clear and convincing proof; the plan injunction is retained on a narrower authority basis

**Holding and operative rule — risk classification:** Before the ordinary Tier 2 or Tier 3 notification presented here, the State must establish the factual basis and risk classification by clear and convincing evidence. Preponderance alone is constitutionally insufficient for that classification in this setting; proof beyond a reasonable doubt and certainty of future conduct are not required.

**Authority:** Part III-A of Souter's opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer; five direct supporters. Stevens's join operates because the Court permits civil notification. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would hold State preponderance sufficient.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews requires a practical comparison of error risks, not automatic transplantation of every civil burden. Addington and Santosky show that a civil label does not end the inquiry and that sufficiently serious erroneous deprivation can require greater assurance. The classification here combines disputed historical facts, unadjudicated circumstances and predictive judgments with disclosure that cannot effectively be recalled. Overclassification can alter housing, work, safety and status before an error is repaired.

Underclassification can endanger potential victims, and that concern is not discounted. But continuing registration and law-enforcement monitoring remain protective measures, while the State already marshals its initial factual case. A prompt adversarial determination under the heightened standard reduces erroneous classification without demanding perfect prediction or disabling warnings justified by the evidence. The required high-risk and low-risk overrides and individual factual review give the additional assurance practical work. Existing hearing procedures do not answer the separate question of ultimate persuasion and its strength. Sahhar preserves different burdens for different legal operations; this holding derives from this notification setting, not from treating its initial-commitment and discharge provisions as interchangeable. The Court imposes no universal heightened burden for every civil prediction.

**Judgment without a controlling rationale for the plan's standard of proof:** The injunction's requirement of clear and convincing State proof for the notification plan is also affirmed, 5–4, by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Only Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer join Part III-B's plan-specific error-allocation reasoning. Stone-Zsela supports retention of that injunction component without adopting a plan-specific ground. O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would retain State persuasion but permit preponderance. The injunction binds this litigation; the judgment establishes no five-Justice rationale for the plan's standard of proof or broader general rule. The agreement in judgment without an accompanying rationale and the four-Justice explanation cannot be combined under Marks as broader and logically narrower grounds.

##### IV. The advance-notice emergency question remains unripe

**Holding and operative rule:** The present class challenge does not decide whether a concrete emergency may justify notification without ordinary advance notice because no representative alleges such notification and no prosecutor is shown to have done or planned it. The ordinary-process injunction is retained subject to that express reservation; it neither approves every emergency bypass nor prohibits every judicially authorized exception.

**Authority:** Part IV of Souter's opinion, joined by the seven other Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela agrees with the disposition of the separately reserved emergency component, adding no emergency rationale or new exception.

**Controlling explanation:** The described mechanism permits a prosecutor to seek a judicial order when release information arrives only after a Tier 3 offender's release or the registrant evades service despite every good-faith effort; after three days without completed service the prosecutor may apply. Permission to apply is not a command to bypass notice. Without a concrete use or planned use affecting the class, the Court cannot determine the constitutional adequacy of that exceptional procedure or any narrowing state construction. A later concrete application must stand on its own facts and legal basis. The ordinary holding therefore resolves neither that future controversy nor an imagined automatic emergency exemption.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current Law

- Ursery, Ward and Mendoza-Martinez supply rebuttable civil classification and combined purpose/effect examination; neither labels nor a single indicator is conclusive.
- Hendricks retains its as-applied punitive-confinement holding and actual-operation method; its outcome is distinguished, not replaced with an approval of that custody.
- Hudson preserves aggregate examination and Halper in its separate personal-monetary field. Halper is not extended into a notification accounting test.
- Kurth Ranch retains its extraordinary-tax punishment holding; its arrest-linked operation differs from the tailored warnings here.
- Sandin's substantive-constraint principle informs the protected state interest without transplanting a prison threshold into all civil settings. Paul v. Davis's reputation-alone boundary remains intact.
- Mathews supplies the context-specific process comparison. Addington and Santosky support attention to serious and irreversible error without creating a universal civil standard of proof.
- Sahhar preserves the distinct statutory initial-commitment and discharge burdens; neither is mechanically imported.
- Blockburger and retained Grady offense-identity questions are not reached. No precedent is overruled.

**Current law:** Civil classification remains rebuttable by clearest proof of punitive combined operation. A genuine substantive state privacy constraint supports ordinary advance review and State persuasion for both tier and plan. Clear and convincing proof for the factual basis and risk classification is a five-Justice rule. The plan requirement remains part of the affirmed injunction, with no five-Justice plan-specific rationale. The emergency question and separate E.B. jurisdictional judgment remain outside the merits resolution.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** Stone-Zsela joins the civil-classification and heightened risk-classification portions. Public protection and a civil structure do not remove the need for timely notice, disclosure of the basis, an opportunity to contest, a neutral decisionmaker, written reasons and clear and convincing State proof before the broad notification at issue. The State controls the evidence and proposes the classification; an erroneous high tier can irreversibly affect housing, work, safety and status. A prompt adversarial process can reduce error without defeating protection. His express affirmance includes retention of the notification-plan injunction, but he adopts no plan-specific explanation and does not join the separate state-entitlement analysis. On the unripe emergency component he agrees only with the disposition and adds no emergency ground. His stated safeguards supply no majority adoption of a new general written-reasons or disclosure code. Confidential registration and lower-tier treatment remain subject to their own law; every internet registry, lifetime classification without review and affirmatively punitive operation remain unresolved.

**Stevens, dissenting in part.** Stevens would reverse the denial of Ex Post Facto relief and require an injunction against retrospective Tier 2/3 notification for this defined class. The affirmative official broadcast of conviction and predicted dangerousness, its criminal-history trigger and enduring cumulative burdens resemble imposed shaming. Risk and audience limits deserve weight but do not alter that combined character on his assessment. Ursery's dissenting approach and the actual-operation holdings of Hendricks and Hudson support looking beyond the civil label. He does not attribute every private attack to the State or decide independent Double Jeopardy offense identity. Because the Court leaves civil notification available, he joins the indicated process portions, including clear and convincing proof for both tier and plan.

**Souter's Part III-B, joined by Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer.** These four would apply clear and convincing persuasion to the plan as well as the classification because unnecessary geographic or audience expansion causes the same irreversible disclosure. The State prepares the plan and its initial justification; factual disputes, atypical risks and the actual likely-encounter limit make stronger assurance useful without demanding certainty. Continuing registration and monitoring reduce the comparative cost of mistaken undernotification. This is the rationale of four Justices, not a controlling general rule for the plan's standard of proof.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas; O'Connor and Kennedy join the standard-of-proof portion only.** Scalia and Thomas reject the Ex Post Facto challenge because the particular risk-tailored warning scheme is genuinely civil and protective on this record. They separately reject the Double Jeopardy challenge because that civil warning process is not a successive criminal prosecution. They preserve their Hudson disagreement with a freestanding Halper civil multiple-punishment method; that view does not overrule Halper. All four Justices dissenting on the standard of proof accept the substantive state interest, advance opportunity and State persuasion for both tier and plan, but would permit preponderance. Mathews also accounts for potential victims, the danger of underclassification and existing safeguards. Noncustodial tailored warnings do not impose Addington's physical custody or Santosky's family-status severance; neither serious consequences nor a protected interest mechanically requires heightened certainty. Sahhar's different commitment and discharge rules demonstrate the need to preserve the actual legal operation. Their proposed modification would remove only the heightened standard of proof, preserving the remainder of the ordinary-process injunction.

The [amended Third Circuit opinion, 119 F.3d 1077](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/119/119.F3d.1077.html), supplies the separate E.B. and W.P. appeals, class limits, scheme, individual-review rules, reported effects, process injunction and unripe emergency issue. The operative statute and Guidelines are described through that published account. The nineteen experiences, six compelled disclosures and 135 notifications are distinct populations; no combined incidence or causation finding is made.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The W.P. injunction remains in force: ordinary Tier 2/3 notification for this class requires advance opportunity to contest tier and plan and clear and convincing State persuasion for each. The expressly unripe emergency question remains reserved. Further relief is denied within the reviewed class; no individual risk assessment or notification plan is approved.

### Jones v. United States, No. 97-6203

Merits decision, March 24, 1999

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 5, 1998. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit's 1997 judgment, 116 F.3d 1487 (table). The question is whether the serious-bodily-injury and death provisions of the governing 18 U.S.C. § 2119 define aggravated offenses or sentencing factors for the judge.

The December 1992 offense is governed by the original firearm-possession enactment: fifteen years for the basic offense, twenty-five years if serious bodily injury results, and up to life if death results. The indictment did not allege serious bodily injury, and the jury's general verdict did not establish it; the judge's injury finding supplied the asserted aggravated sentencing authority. The earlier Oliver appeal supplies the record recitation, not the judgment now reviewed. Death is relevant to the statute's structure, not a finding about this offense's result.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in the challenged aggravated-sentencing respect; sentence vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Kennedy and Breyer, who would affirm against the presented classification challenge.

On the existing carjacking indictment and verdict, punishment may not exceed the fifteen-year basic maximum. This is not an order to impose fifteen years, an aggregate-sentence calculation, an acquittal or automatic authorization of a new aggravated prosecution. Any further prosecution must satisfy its own procedural and constitutional requirements. The Court does not reinstate an earlier sentence or decide unrelated sentencing errors.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Exact scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Parts I–II | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Reversal, sentence vacatur and bounded remand | Statutory grades; ordinary element safeguards; existing-conviction maximum and remedy |
| Concurrence | Souter | Stevens, Scalia, Ginsburg | Joins the Court and advances an independent constitutional ground | Present-offense fact necessary to a higher statutory maximum; expressly bounded as stated below |
| Concurrence, Part I | Stevens | Scalia | Joins the Court and Souter's concurrence; states a broader constitutional position | Facts altering the legislatively prescribed penalty range, including minima; no categorical prior-conviction exception |
| Concurrence, Part II | Stevens | No other Justice | Additional broader position; no additional disposition | Death-eligibility facts and reconsideration of contrary Walton reasoning in an appropriate case |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court on its sufficient statutory ground | Maintains his broader constitutional position without making it an additional ground of this disposition |
| Dissent | Kennedy | O'Connor, Breyer | Would affirm against the presented classification challenge | Complete-offense and penalty-clause construction; independent constitutional defense of this injury allocation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Serious bodily injury and death define aggravated carjacking offenses

**Holding and operative rule:** The version of 18 U.S.C. § 2119 governing the December 1992 offense creates a basic carjacking offense and aggravated offenses when serious bodily injury or death results. Serious bodily injury is an element of the grade carrying a twenty-five-year maximum, and death is an element of the grade carrying a maximum of life imprisonment; the basic offense carries a fifteen-year maximum.

**Authority:** Part I of the six-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reads the additional injury and death conditions as facts defining aggravated crimes. The numbered clauses prescribe sharply different limits on lawful punishment and use harm to the victim, a traditional distinction among robbery offenses, to select the applicable grade. The Government emphasizes the complete opening description of firearm carjacking, the common punishment command, dependent clauses and penalty headings. Those features support its reading, but they do not establish that the additional facts merely guide sentencing: aggravated offenses may share the basic offense's elements and add the fact that permits greater punishment. Almendarez-Torres supplies a statutory analogue in which placement within a punishment provision did not dispense with an aggravated element. The fairly available graded-offense reading also avoids serious constitutional doubt about assigning to a judge a current-offense fact indispensable to punishment beyond the charged verdict's authority. Gaudin protects the jury's determination of actual elements; neither it nor Almendarez-Torres already establishes a universal rule classifying every sentencing fact. Monge permits renewed proof of its particular noncapital serious-prior qualification for double-jeopardy purposes and expressly reserves independent charging and jury claims. It does not resolve this statute's construction. The Court decides the original enactment, without adding the later intent-to-cause-serious-harm language or death-penalty amendment.

##### II. The existing indictment and verdict authorize only the basic carjacking range

**Holding and operative rule:** The aggravated injury or death element must be alleged in the federal indictment; absent a valid admission as an element of the charged offense or a valid waiver of jury determination, the Government must submit it to the jury and prove it beyond a reasonable doubt. An ordinary factual admission does not itself cure an indictment that omits the aggravated element. Because injury was neither charged nor established by Jones's verdict, the judge's later injury finding cannot authorize the aggravated range; the aggravated sentence must be vacated, and sentencing on the existing carjacking indictment and verdict may not exceed the fifteen-year basic maximum.

**Authority:** Part II of the same six-Justice Opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Hamling requires the federal charge to contain the offense's elements, and Gaudin places their determination beyond a reasonable doubt with the jury. Almendarez-Torres applies those safeguards to a statutory aggravator and holds that an admission to a fact cannot repair its omission from the charge. Here the indictment and general verdict establish only the basic carjacking offense. No valid admission or waiver supplies the missing aggravated charge and verdict. The judge's injury finding therefore cannot perform their work. Witte and Watts permit relevant-conduct findings to select punishment within authority supplied by the conviction; they do not supply additional statutory authority or remove an actual element from the jury. The Court reverses the Ninth Circuit's authorization of the injury-based aggravated sentence, vacates that sentence and remands for lawful proceedings. The fifteen-year ceiling concerns this carjacking count on the existing charge and verdict. It neither fixes the sentence at fifteen years nor determines an aggregate sentence. No acquittal, harmless-error finding or automatic entitlement to another aggravated prosecution is entered; any further prosecution must satisfy its own procedural and constitutional requirements. Monge's completed-insufficiency and renewed-proof question is absent.

**Limits and questions not reached:** The Court's ground is statutory construction and the protections of the resulting elements. It does not decide a general constitutional rule for all maximum-increasing facts, bare prior-conviction status, mandatory minima within an unchanged maximum, every Guidelines classification or capital sentencing. McMillan's unchanged-maximum holding and Walton's capital rule are not overruled. The death grade is part of the statute's construction, not a finding that Jones caused death.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Almendarez-Torres supplies the statutory aggravated-element and charging analogue, including the distinction between admitting a fact and properly charging the aggravated offense. It establishes no universal constitutional rule for maximum-increasing facts.
- Hamling requires a federal indictment stating the offense's elements. Gaudin requires the jury's determination of actual elements beyond a reasonable doubt; it does not itself classify every sentencing fact.
- Monge retains its particular noncapital serious-prior double-jeopardy classification and its express reservation of independent charging and jury claims. No renewed-proof or other independent remedy is drawn from it here.
- Witte and Watts retain their within-authority sentencing permission. They do not authorize punishment beyond the charged conviction's statutory range or remove an actual element from the jury.
- Holloway's attention to enacted offense conditions is consistent with this reading, but its construction of later statutory language does not amend the law governing this offense.
- McMillan's unchanged-maximum holding and Walton's capital rule are not overruled. Custis's limits on collateral attack remain undisturbed.

#### Law After Decision

The original section 2119 creates aggravated injury and death offenses whose added elements receive ordinary federal charging and jury-proof protections. The existing basic conviction cannot authorize the injury range. The Court adopts no general constitutional maximum-fact rule, prior-conviction exception, new mandatory-minimum rule or capital-sentencing rule. The distinct constitutional positions in the concurrences retain their stated, noncontrolling scope.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Souter, joined by Stevens, Scalia and Ginsburg.** These four join the Court's sufficient statutory ground and also conclude that a fact about the commission or consequences of the charged offense, indispensable to a greater statutory maximum than the charged elements and verdict otherwise allow, performs the constitutional function of an element. The federal charging, jury and reasonable-doubt safeguards follow, subject to valid admission and waiver rules; those rules do not repair the omitted charge by implication. Their Monge position treated an additional, previously unestablished personal-conduct qualification as an element for the finality question. They now apply that functional reasoning to injury in the present offense, with Gaudin supplying jury allocation. This is their constitutional conclusion here, not a claim that Monge already decided the jury question. Ordinary factfinding within existing conviction-authorized punishment remains distinct. The concurrence leaves bare prior-conviction status, unchanged-maximum minima, general Guidelines classification and capital sentencing unresolved. Four Justices adopt this independent constitutional ground; it is not the Court's holding.

**Stevens, joined by Scalia in Part I.** Their broader position protects facts that change the legislatively prescribed punishment range, including facts increasing the minimum, and recognizes no categorical exclusion merely because the fact is a prior conviction. Stevens relies on Winship's proof requirement, Mullaney's protection against shifting the burden on a fact determining punishment, his objections to Patterson's treatment of offense definition, and Duncan's protection of jury adjudication. Scalia agrees that the legislature cannot avoid the protection by naming a range-determining fact a sentencing consideration. His narrower Monge element-function ground supports that conclusion without having already established this universal range principle. Neither ordinary relevant-conduct sentencing under Witte and Watts nor an existing conviction's validity is the same question as whether a new fact legally authorizes a different punishment range. A charged prior-status element may be established by adequate admission or other lawful proof without relitigating the conviction's validity; Custis's collateral-review limits remain. These positions do not decide a mandatory-minimum application or overrule McMillan here. In Part II, Stevens alone also places facts necessary for death eligibility within his constitutional principle and favors reconsideration of contrary Walton reasoning in an appropriate case. No capital issue is decided, and Scalia does not join that recommendation.

**Stone-Zsela.** The Chief Justice joins the statutory construction and remedy as sufficient to decide this case. He maintains that a fact increasing authorized punishment must receive the jury protection owed an element, but does not make that broader position an additional constitutional ground of this disposition or join Souter's independent constitutional concurrence. Almendarez-Torres supplies a statutory analogue, not an already controlling universal constitutional rule. His maintained position preserves his view while the Court's decision rests on the enacted aggravated offenses. It supplies no decision of the separate questions concerning prior status, unchanged-maximum minima or capital sentencing.

**Kennedy, joined by O'Connor and Breyer.** The dissent reads the opening firearm-taking paragraph as a complete offense and the dependent injury and death clauses as graded punishment for its consequences. The sharp increases and the traditional use of injury to grade robbery support the Court's reading, but do not overcome that offense-first structure in the dissent's view. Patterson preserves a bounded legislative role in defining an offense; Gaudin protects the jury's determination once an actual element is identified. The dissent directly defends treating injury during this completed offense as a punishment condition, acknowledging that present victim injury differs from recidivist character. Monge accepted both an unproved personal-conduct qualification and a higher ceiling without treating those features as conclusive of offense status, while leaving the independent jury question open. The dissent therefore does not claim that Monge already resolved this claim or that the qualification was previously proved. Almendarez-Torres's statutory reentry holding remains binding but supplies no general constitutional rule either permitting or forbidding this allocation. McMillan's unchanged-ceiling setting and Witte's and Watts's within-authority sentencing do not themselves resolve the increased maximum. The dissent would sustain the challenged injury-based sentencing authority against this classification objection, without adopting unlimited legislative relabeling, resolving unrelated sentencing errors or directing reinstatement of an earlier sentence.
