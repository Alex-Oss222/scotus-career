## Event

Fitzgerald v. Racing Association of Central Iowa, No. 02-695. Argued April 29, 2003; decided June 9, 2003. On certiorari to the Iowa Supreme Court, which reversed the trial court and invalidated the racetrack/riverboat slot-revenue tax difference under common state-and-federal equal-protection reasoning. Iowa authorized racetrack slot machines with a graduated revenue-tax rate rising to 36 percent while retaining the 20-percent riverboat rate. The questions are whether an independent state ground bars review and whether the rate distinction violates federal equal protection. The treasurer seeks reversal of the federal invalidation; the racetracks seek preservation of the judgment and equal treatment.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or merits submission and in the decision.

## Public Action

The Iowa Supreme Court judgment is reversed and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with the federal equal-protection ruling.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Reverse and remand, 9–0. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The federal equal-protection ground for invalidating the rate difference is rejected; the remand preserves adjudication under genuinely independent state law.

## Holdings

**Authority.** Both holdings are adopted by all nine Justices in the opinion of the Court: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

### I. The interwoven state and federal judgment is reviewable

**Holding and operative rule.** When a state court expressly applies common state-and-federal constitutional reasoning and identifies no clear state ground independently sustaining its judgment, this Court may review the federal ground under 28 U.S.C. §1257. The Iowa Supreme Court's interwoven analysis permits that review here.

**Controlling explanation.** Michigan v. Long supplies the rule for identifying an independent state ground. The Iowa opinion treats the two equal-protection guarantees together; its reasoning does not establish that the same judgment would follow regardless of federal law. Review therefore answers the federal issue while leaving the Iowa court free on remand to decide an independently grounded state constitutional claim.

### II. Multiple legitimate economic purposes rationally support the different rates

**Holding and operative rule.** In ordinary economic taxation involving neither a suspect class nor a fundamental right, a classification satisfies equal protection when a reasonably conceivable legitimate purpose rationally supports it, and the challengers bear the burden of negating that basis. Iowa may authorize new slot-machine revenue for racetracks while taxing it at a higher rate than riverboat revenue to balance distinct legitimate industry and public-revenue objectives; every provision need not maximize aid to racetracks.

**Controlling explanation.** Nordlinger v. Hahn permits rational though imperfect tax classifications, and Beach Communications permits reasonably conceivable legitimate grounds without formal legislative findings or proof that each ground was the legislature's exclusive actual motive. The law gives racetracks a new revenue opportunity even at a rate rising to 36 percent. Keeping the riverboat rate at 20 percent can simultaneously protect a different industry and balance public revenues. Those aims concern the actual licensing-and-tax package, rather than the self-justifying claim that favored businesses deserve favor.

The racetracks argue that identical machines should produce identical taxes and that the disparity contradicts the legislation's assistance to them. But enterprises operating the same machines may stand differently within a lawful industry policy. A partial benefit remains a benefit; the Constitution does not require that every part of a compromise supply the largest possible advantage to one beneficiary. Allegheny Pittsburgh concerned arbitrary administration under a different state equality arrangement, not an enacted classification with the rational policy grounds present here. The federal invalidation therefore cannot stand.

## Precedent Treatment

- Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032: applied to permit review of the expressly interwoven state-and-federal reasoning while preserving genuinely independent state adjudication.
- Nordlinger v. Hahn, June 18, 1992: reaffirmed for rational but imperfect tax distinctions.
- FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., June 1, 1993: reaffirmed for reasonably conceivable legitimate grounds, the challenger's burden and the absence of a formal-findings requirement.
- Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission, 488 U.S. 336: distinguished because its arbitrary administration under the State's equality arrangement differs from this legislatively enacted classification.

## Law After Decision

The existing rational-basis standard continues to govern ordinary economic tax classifications. A legislature may combine a new benefit for one industry with different tax treatment supporting another legitimate industry or revenue objective. The Iowa classification survives the federal challenge; independent Iowa constitutional meaning and available state-law remedies remain for the Iowa courts.

## Event

Hillside Dairy, Inc. v. Lyons, No. 01-950, and Ponderosa Dairy v. Lyons, No. 01-1018. Consolidated merits decision, June 9, 2003. Argued April 22, 2003. On writs of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 259 F.3d 1148, which sustained dismissal on congressional-authorization and facial-citizenship grounds. The questions concern §144's application to California's milk-pricing and pooling rules and the individuals' Article IV claims.

## Participation

## Public Action

## Holdings

### Part I — Congressional authorization covers the enacted subjects

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 144 of the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 does not exempt California's challenged milk-pricing and pooling rules from Commerce Clause review. Congress may authorize a particular interstate burden, but permission must clearly encompass that burden; protection of composition and labeling authority does not extend to pricing and pooling merely because those measures operate within one regulatory program.

**Authority:** Part I of the Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer: eight direct joins. Thomas separately agrees that the lower court's statutory interpretation was erroneous, while dissenting from the Commerce disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 144 protects California's authority, against direct or indirect limitation under that Act or other law, to establish or continue requirements concerning the percentage of milk solids or solids not fat in fluid milk sold at retail or marketed in California, and labeling of such products concerning those characteristics. The broad introductory protection remains tied to those two enacted subjects. Neither pricing nor contributions to an equalization pool is among them.

South-Central Timber Development v. Wunnicke requires clear congressional permission for the state burden assertedly immunized. New York v. United States and Pic-A-State preserve Congress's authority to give such permission; they do not extend it past Congress's terms. California's strongest argument relies on the integrated operation of its marketing system and the statute's reference to indirect limits. Integration cannot erase the specified subjects. The composition statute and the separate pricing and pooling statutes reinforce that textual distinction.

The Commerce dismissal must therefore be vacated. West Lynn Creamery requires attention to a linked assessment and local distribution when the facts establish that connection; Tracy requires an actual competitive comparison rather than equivalence inferred from a label. Those principles govern ordinary adjudication of the remaining claim. The Court's retained Commerce doctrine supplies the basis for that adjudication despite Thomas's objection to its constitutional foundation.

### Part II — Express citizenship language is not required for Article IV review

**Holding and operative rule:** An individual's Privileges and Immunities Clause claim cannot be dismissed solely because the challenged rule does not expressly classify by residence or citizenship. The individual must establish covered discrimination against nonresident citizens in a protected pursuit; when that predicate is established, the State must show a substantial reason beyond noncitizenship and a close relationship between that reason and the discriminatory treatment.

**Authority:** Part II's affirmative rule and rejection of the categorical dismissal have nine direct joins. Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer additionally join Part II's reservation below; Stone-Zsela does not join that reservation and states his broader practical-operation rule separately.

**Controlling explanation:** Chalker v. Birmingham & Northwestern Railway Co. rejected a higher tax tied to an individual's out-of-state principal office because its operation discriminated against nonresident citizens, despite its lack of citizenship terminology. California's facial wording therefore cannot alone defeat the individuals' claims. Lunding supplies the substantial-independent-reason and close-relationship requirements once covered unequal treatment is proved; it preserves genuine relevant distinctions rather than requiring identical treatment in all respects.

California correctly observes that an interstate disadvantage to goods or firms need not establish discrimination against citizens. That point calls for the proper personal and substantive inquiry; it does not restore a categorical facial-language prerequisite. The individuals' covered status, practical disadvantage, appropriate comparison, and any justification require ordinary adjudication on remand. The corporations' separate Article IV exclusion was not challenged and remains undisturbed.

**Question reserved:** Whether Chalker reaches only classifications functioning as proxies for citizenship or every classification producing discriminatory practical effects lies beyond the categorical facial-ground question properly presented at this stage and is not resolved by the eight Justices joining this reservation.

## Precedent Treatment

- South-Central Timber Development, Inc. v. Wunnicke, 467 U.S. 82 (1984): applies the clear-congressional-authorization requirement to the particular interstate burden.
- New York v. United States, June 19, 1992: preserves Congress's power to authorize specified interstate burdens under its actual enacted conditions.
- Pic-A-State Pennsylvania, Inc. v. Reno, April 28, 1997: preserves bounded express congressional permission, without extending it beyond the category Congress authorized.
- West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy, June 17, 1994: preserves examination of a linked assessment and local competitive benefit; its established discriminatory operation is not presumed from this dismissal record.
- General Motors Corp. v. Tracy, February 18, 1997: preserves the need for an actual competitive comparison and its holding concerning materially different regulated service obligations.
- Chalker v. Birmingham & Northwestern Railway Co., 249 U.S. 522 (1919): applies its rejection of a facial-citizenship prerequisite. The broader proxy-versus-practical-effects question remains reserved at this stage by the stated eight-Justice coalition.
- Lunding v. New York Tax Appeals Tribunal, January 21, 1998: applies its substantial-independent-reason and close-relationship requirements at their established scope.

## Law After Decision

Section 144's protection is confined to its composition and labeling subjects; California's pricing and pooling operation remains subject to the established Commerce Clause rules. Eligible individuals may pursue Article IV claims based on actual citizen discrimination despite neutral wording, subject to proof of a protected pursuit, the relevant disadvantage, and the existing justification standard. The eight-Justice reservation preserves the broader proxy-versus-effects question. The remaining discrimination, comparison, justification, and lawful relief require adjudication under the applicable rules.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** The Chief Justice joins Part I, Part II's rejection of a facial-citizenship prerequisite and its established justification requirement, and both vacatur-and-remand judgments. He withholds assent to the proxy-versus-effects reservation. His rule requires an individual to establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, covered citizenship, a protected pursuit, a practical disadvantage to nonresident citizens, and its connection to the rule; the State must then establish a substantial reason beyond noncitizenship and a close relationship to the discrimination. Chalker makes operation decisive, and Lunding requires examination of the full treatment and the reasons offered. Interstate business activity alone establishes neither the individual's protected status nor the necessary disadvantage. Corporate Commerce claims remain distinct.

He would give further express instructions for remand. The court should identify each plaintiff's standing and claim, trace the transactions bearing the assessment and the competing producers receiving its linked benefit, and apply West Lynn Creamery if discrimination is proved. California then must establish a legitimate local purpose that adequate reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives cannot serve; protecting local producers from competition is insufficient. An evenhanded measure instead receives Pike's inquiry into whether the actual incidental interstate burden is clearly excessive in relation to putative local benefits. Existing adequate evidence should be used, with development confined to material unresolved facts; ordinary motions may resolve a claim without trial. Any decree must remove the proved unlawful operation consistently with lawful severability. A monetary claim requires payment or loss, an authorized remedial route, and consideration of preserved defenses. His fuller instructions and specified proof formulation are separate positions.

**Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Thomas joins Part II in full and the remand of the individuals' Article IV claims. He agrees independently that §144 does not support the Ninth Circuit's statutory analysis. He nevertheless would affirm dismissal of the Commerce claim because, for the reasons stated in his Camps Newfound/Owatonna dissent, the negative Commerce Clause has no basis in the Constitution's text and provides no lawful ground for invalidating state legislation. That foundational disagreement, not approval of blanket statutory immunity, explains his different Commerce disposition.

## Procedure After Action

Both cases return for ordinary adjudication of their respective Commerce and eligible individual Article IV claims. The lower courts must examine the actual challenged operation, the proper claimant and comparison, any justification, and lawful relief under the governing rules. The categorical grounds rejected here may not sustain dismissal again; the record and ordinary procedural rules govern whether additional factual development is required.

## Event

Nguyen v. United States; Phan v. United States, Nos. 01-10873 and 02-5034. Argued March 24, 2003; decided June 9, 2003. On certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed federal narcotics convictions entered in the District Court of Guam. The consolidated cases ask whether a panel containing two Article III circuit judges and the fixed-term chief judge of the District Court for the Northern Mariana Islands was authorized under the assignment statutes, and whether lateness, de facto service or a quorum preserves its judgments. Neither petitioner objected before decision or sought rehearing on composition; both seek a fresh lawful appeal.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated at argument or merits submission and in the decision.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit judgments in both consolidated matters are vacated, and the cases are remanded for appellate adjudication by a lawfully constituted panel.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Vacate both appellate judgments and remand, 6–3. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. **Opposing:** Scalia, Ginsburg and Breyer, who would affirm against the late composition challenge. Each consolidated docket has the same judgment and coalition.

## Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivers the opinion of the Court, in which Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas join in full. Scalia files a dissenting opinion, in which Ginsburg and Breyer join in full.

## Holdings

**Authority.** All three holdings are adopted by the six Justices joining the opinion of the Court: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. Scalia, Ginsburg and Breyer agree that the designation lacked statutory authority but dissent from correction and the judgment.

### I. The territorial judge was outside the statutory class eligible for this designation

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 292(a), read with Title 28's definitions and district-court structure, authorizes designation of the specified Article III district judges to a court of appeals; it does not include the fixed-term judge of the District Court for the Northern Mariana Islands. Participation by that judge therefore violated the statutory qualification for membership of this appellate panel.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the relevant judicial office must satisfy the statute's definition. Sections 451 and 132–134 place the district-judge designation provision within the organization of the Article III district courts. Mookini v. United States distinguishes territorial courts from that statutory category even when they exercise federal jurisdiction. The territorial judge's valid appointment to his own office and competence to decide federal questions cannot supply the different qualification Congress required for this assignment. The Government correctly concedes the statutory defect. That concession leaves the disputed consequence for the judgments to be decided.

### II. This substantial membership defect requires correction despite the late direct challenge

**Holding and operative rule.** On nonfinal direct review, participation by a judge whom Congress excluded from the eligible class for this appellate tribunal requires vacatur for fresh lawful appellate adjudication even though the litigants first objected in this Court. The litigants must establish the substantial membership defect, rather than prove that a properly constituted panel would have reached another result; the rule distinguishes a missing statutory qualification from a merely technical designation irregularity.

**Controlling explanation.** American Construction Co. v. Jacksonville, Tampa & Key West Railway Co. and United States v. American-Foreign Steamship Corp. give effect to substantial congressional choices about the composition of federal courts. Glidden Co. v. Zdanok recognizes direct-review correction of substantial adjudicator-qualification defects despite a late objection. Here the unauthorized member actually participated in the appellate decision. Treating that participation as de facto service would leave the very adjudication Congress withheld authority to perform as the defendants' only appeal.

Ryder v. United States and Landry v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation establish that a litigant entitled to lawful adjudication must receive the missing adjudicative function itself. Those decisions involved timely challenges; this case extends that remedial principle to the present late challenge because the substantive membership restriction protects the authority of the appellate tribunal. Olano and Johnson ordinarily require a separate justification for correcting forfeited error. The substantial composition defect and the court-composition precedents supply that justification here. Speculation about how the authorized judges might have decided cannot replace the prescribed tribunal. The broader constitutional claim concerning Article III is unnecessary to the statutory disposition of the complete challenge.

### III. A quorum does not validate the unauthorized member's participation

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 46's quorum rule permits qualified members of a lawfully constituted panel to act under the statute's conditions; it does not retrospectively convert this three-member judgment into a separate judgment by its two eligible members. Both appellate judgments must be vacated and the appeals heard afresh by a lawfully constituted panel.

**Controlling explanation.** A quorum concerns the number entitled to act for a legally constituted tribunal. It cannot supply the missing membership qualification or erase the unauthorized member's participation in the judgment actually rendered. The record establishes no independently rendered two-judge judgment. American-Foreign Steamship's enforcement of Congress's composition requirements and Ryder's insistence on the actual missing lawful adjudication foreclose the proposed substitute. Fresh appellate consideration supplies the remedy for both petitioners.

## Precedent Treatment

- Mookini v. United States, 303 U.S. 201: applied to distinguish territorial courts from the Article III district-court category in the designation statutes.
- American Construction Co. v. Jacksonville, Tampa & Key West Railway Co., 148 U.S. 372: applied to enforce a substantial statutory rule organizing an appellate tribunal.
- United States v. American-Foreign Steamship Corp., 363 U.S. 685: applied to require compliance with statutory appellate membership limits and reject retrospective validation through the eligible participants.
- Glidden Co. v. Zdanok, 370 U.S. 530: applied to support exceptional direct-review consideration of a substantial adjudicator-qualification defect.
- Ryder v. United States, June 12, 1995, and Landry v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, June 15, 2001: their requirement of the missing lawful adjudication is extended to this substantial statutory membership defect despite a late direct challenge; their distinct military and agency applications remain intact.
- United States v. Olano, April 26, 1993, and Johnson v. United States, May 12, 1997: their ordinary forfeiture and corrective-discretion rules remain in force; this substantial court-composition defect supplies the exceptional ground for correction.

## Law After Decision

Effective June 9, 2003, the right to the missing lawful adjudication extends to this substantial statutory appellate-membership defect on nonfinal direct review despite a late objection. The court must apply the distinction between a missing eligibility qualification and a technical designation irregularity. The quorum statute operates only within its lawful-panel premise.

## Separate Writings

**Scalia, joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, dissenting.** The dissent agrees that §292 did not authorize the designation but would affirm both appellate judgments because the defendants forfeited the objection. Under Olano and Johnson, which distinguish the existence of error from the discretion to correct it, even an assumed effect on substantial rights does not itself establish that correction is required to protect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of proceedings. The supplied record shows neither outcome prejudice nor bias or incompetence. The two qualified circuit judges and the territorial judge's lawful original office bear on that inquiry, although they do not authorize the assignment or establish a separate two-judge judgment. Ryder and Landry protect timely challengers and expressly leave the default question open. The dissent therefore rejects the majority's treatment of this violation as a sufficient reason for exceptional correction. Its position rests on the consequence of forfeiture, rather than an endorsement of future unauthorized designations or a rule that actual innocence is required.

## Procedure After Action

The court of appeals must designate a panel under valid statutory authority and provide fresh, independent consideration of both appeals. The designation record and replacement judgment must identify lawful composition and the new adjudication. The trial convictions remain subject to those appeals.
