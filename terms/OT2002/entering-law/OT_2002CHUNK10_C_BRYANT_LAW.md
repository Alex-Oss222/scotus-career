#### In re Baby K

**Docket or dockets:** No. 93-1673.
**Decided:** December 12, 1994.

##### The acute respiratory emergency triggers stabilization or lawful transfer

**Holding:** When a participating hospital determines that an individual has an emergency respiratory condition, §1395dd(b)(1) requires the examination and treatment within its available staff and facilities necessary to prevent likely material deterioration, within reasonable medical probability, resulting from or during transfer, or transfer complying with subsection (c). Inability to cure the patient's underlying anencephaly, an ethical or medical objection to continued life support, uniform withholding from comparable anencephalic patients, and the hospital's lack of an intention to transfer do not excuse this duty where the acute emergency and effective stabilizing capability are established.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies contextual reading and respect for expressed statutory coverage and limits; its military-leave rule does not decide a medical question.
- Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992): preserves separate inquiries into enacted duty, cause of action and remedy; its rejection of particular child-welfare claims supplies no categorical exemption from express hospital duties.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Screening under subsection (a) is requested examination of an individual coming to the emergency department within that department's capability, including routinely available ancillary services. The diagnosed-emergency duty under subsection (b) is distinct. Transfer includes hospital-directed movement and discharge, but excludes movement after declaration of death or departure without hospital-affiliated permission. The separate labor provisions are not decided; Congress remains free to enact a defined futility exception.

##### Unstabilized transfer remains a fully conditional alternative

**Holding:** An unstabilized transfer requires either a written request by the individual or legally responsible person after explanation of the hospital's obligations and transfer risks, or a physician-signed certification, based on then-available information, that expected medical benefits at the other facility outweigh increased transfer risks; if no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a qualified medical person may sign only after consulting a physician who makes that determination and later countersigns, and every certification must summarize risks and benefits. Every route also requires available treatment minimizing risk; receiving space, qualified personnel and acceptance of appropriate care; transmission of all available emergency-related records, the request or certification, and the name and address of a required on-call physician who refused or failed timely to appear; qualified transportation personnel and equipment with necessary appropriate life support; and the Secretary's applicable additional health-and-safety requirements.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): preserves the force of express statutory alternatives and conditions; protective purpose does not erase an enacted transfer route.

##### Refusal compliance requires an actual offer and informed refusal

**Holding:** Subsection (b)(2) deems the treatment requirement met when the hospital offers the required examination and treatment, explains its risks and benefits, the individual or representative refuses, and the hospital takes all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Subsection (b)(3) separately deems compliance when the hospital offers a transfer satisfying subsection (c), explains its risks and benefits, the individual or representative refuses, and the hospital takes all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal; the mother's opposition alone establishes neither route here.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), eight Justices; Stone-Zsela agrees in the disposition only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies the coordinated statutory structure, including the separate conditions attached to separate alternatives.
- Suter v. Artist M., 503 U.S. 347 (1992): preserves the distinction between a substantive duty and a claimed consequence; no broader family-authority or remedial rule is inferred.

##### The receiving-hospital route depends on appropriate transfer, required specialized capability and capacity

**Holding:** If a nursing-home movement is an appropriate transfer requiring the participating hospital's specialized capabilities or facilities, §1395dd(g) prohibits the hospital from refusing acceptance when it has capacity to treat the individual. The Court does not settle every movement's classification or presume future capacity; the conditional receiving-hospital route preserves those requirements independently of the ordinary arrival-and-diagnosed-emergency route.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), eight Justices; Stone-Zsela agrees in the disposition only.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies reading of related provisions together while retaining each provision's expressed conditions.

##### State medical law yields only to an applicable direct conflict

**Holding:** Under §1395dd(f), a state or local requirement remains effective except to the extent it directly conflicts with an applicable EMTALA requirement; state law cannot authorize withholding the stabilizing treatment required here. Virginia §54.1-2990, a limitation within an article concerning adult directives and surrogates, does not itself establish an infant-care exemption, and the Court invalidates no wider field of state medical law.

**Proposition-level authority:** Opinion of the Court (Ginsburg), unanimous.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- King v. St. Vincent's Hospital, 502 U.S. 215 (1991): applies the whole-enactment approach; the limited preemption clause and affirmative duty both retain effect.

**Operative remedy or transition:** The Fourth Circuit is affirmed. The declaration remains effective for the identified acute respiratory emergencies, with available-capacity, appropriate-transfer, informed-refusal and receiving-hospital predicates retained. No present transfer certification or accepting facility is established, and no indefinite post-stabilization care obligation is imposed.

#### Roberts v. Galen of Virginia, Inc.

**Docket or dockets:** No. 97-53.
**Decided:** 1999-01-13.

##### 1. Stabilization has no additional improper-motive element

**Holding:** A claim under 42 U.S.C. §1395dd(b) does not require proof that a participating hospital acted with an improper financial or other invidious motive. Liability nevertheless requires proof of the enacted stabilization or transfer duty and the applicable civil-action predicates; removing the extra motive requirement establishes neither breach nor injury.

**Proposition-level authority:** The per curiam opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. All nine adopt both holdings and their stated limits.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Subsection (b)(2) deems the treatment requirement satisfied when the hospital offers the required examination and treatment, informs the patient or representative of their risks and benefits, and that person refuses; the hospital must take all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Subsection (b)(3) separately addresses refusal of a transfer offered in accordance with subsection (c), after risk-and-benefit information, with the same reasonable-efforts duty concerning written informed refusal. Neither provision is a finding about this patient.

For an unstabilized emergency condition, subsection (c)(1) separately requires either a written transfer request made by the individual or legally responsible representative after receiving information about the hospital's EMTALA duties and the risks of transfer, or a physician's signed certification, based on the information available when transfer occurs, that the expected medical benefits of appropriate treatment elsewhere outweigh the increased transfer risks, including risks to the unborn child in labor; it also requires an appropriate transfer. If no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a medical person qualified under the Secretary's regulations may sign after consultation with a physician who makes the required determination and later countersigns. The certification must summarize its supporting risks and benefits. Appropriate transfer requires risk-minimizing treatment within the transferring hospital's capacity; available receiving space, qualified personnel and acceptance with appropriate treatment; transmission of all emergency-condition records available at transfer, including the condition, observed signs or symptoms, preliminary diagnosis, treatment, test results and written request or certification, together with the name and address of an on-call physician who refused or did not appear within a reasonable time to provide necessary stabilization; qualified personnel and transportation equipment, including necessary and medically appropriate life support during transfer; and any further applicable regulatory conditions. None is declared satisfied or violated here.

Subsection (d)(2)(A) permits the person directly harmed by a participating hospital's violation to sue that hospital for personal-injury damages available under the law of its State and appropriate equitable relief. The separate limitation is two years after the violation. This decision creates no personal action against an individual physician and no automatic liability for later deterioration.

##### 2. The motive-based summary judgment must be reversed; other predicates remain open

**Holding:** Summary judgment rejecting the stabilization claim because the guardian failed to establish improper motive rests on an element the statute does not contain and must be reversed. The hospital's actual-knowledge and completed-screening defenses remain open for lawful consideration on remand; neither is accepted or rejected on its merits by this decision.

**Proposition-level authority:** The same unanimous per curiam opinion controls this confined reversal and remand.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Whether an emergency medical condition existed, what the hospital determined or knew, duration of any duty, stabilization, authorized transfer, causal injury, damages and ultimate liability remain undecided. The treatment-refusal, transfer-refusal and transfer-certification provisions remain distinct; no exception is presumed.

**Treatment of earlier authority:**

- **National Organization for Women, Inc. v. Scheidler (January 24, 1994):** Applied by analogy for refusal to add an omitted motive element while preserving every enacted liability condition. Its RICO construction remains distinct from EMTALA.
- **Cleland v. Bronson Health Care Group, 917 F.2d 266 (6th Cir. 1990):** The extension of its screening-motivation analysis to impose an extra element under subsection (b) is rejected. The Court does not decide subsection (a)'s screening-motive question.
- **United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc. (November 29, 1994):** Its favorable-judgment defense rule is respected without treating an unestablished alternative factual premise as proved. Neither alternative defense is conclusively adjudicated here.

**Limits and questions not reached:** EMTALA stabilization litigation proceeds without an additional improper-purpose element. The statutory determination, treatment or lawful-transfer conditions, direct personal harm, causation, permitted remedies and limitations period retain their independent force. Screening, stabilization and the separate state-law agency claim remain distinct. The judgment changes no general medical-negligence rule and awards no relief beyond reversal and remand of the appealed stabilization disposition.

**Operative remedy or transition:** **Reversed and remanded, 9–0.** Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The reversal removes the improper-motive ground for summary judgment. The actual-knowledge and completed-screening defenses remain open, as do the statute's other predicates and available remedies. The separate state ostensible-agency ruling and other claims are not reopened; no liability judgment, damages award or mandatory trial is ordered.

## 2003-05-19 ? Harry-v-Marchant_merits_2003-05-19.md

## Event

Merits decision, May 19, 2003. On review of the Eleventh Circuit's en banc judgment, 291 F.3d 767. Review concerns whether EMTALA's stabilization duty can apply when an emergency patient is retained inside the hospital rather than transferred or discharged. The estate seeks reinstatement of its stabilization claim against Miami Beach Healthcare Group, Ltd., doing business as Aventura Hospital and Medical Center.

## Participation

All nine Justices participate.

## Public Action

Stabilization dismissal reversed and remanded, 9–0; separate screening dismissal and §1981 remand remain effective.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0, on the hospital stabilization claim.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The actual-transfer ground for dismissal is rejected. Further proceedings must adjudicate the preadmission allegations under the stabilization duty and distinguish them from any claim resting solely on later genuine inpatient care. The separate screening dismissal and the §1981 remand remain effective.

## Opinion Topology

Scalia delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. All nine join Parts I–II. Stone-Zsela files a concurring opinion.

## Holdings

### I. The stabilization duty does not depend on actual transfer or discharge

**Holding and operative rule:** When a participating hospital determines that a person coming to it has an emergency medical condition, §1395dd(b)(1) requires the examination and treatment within available staff and facilities necessary for stabilization, or a transfer complying with subsection (c). Stabilization means preventing likely material deterioration, within reasonable medical probability, resulting from or during transfer; that medical measure does not add actual movement outside the hospital as a prerequisite to the duty.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** In re Baby K already rejects the hospital's lack of an intended transfer as an excuse when an acute emergency and effective stabilizing capability are established. The statute offers stabilizing care or a compliant transfer; retaining the patient supplies no third option excusing both. Its transfer-related definition measures medical stability rather than postponing the obligation until departure. Roberts independently rejects an additional improper financial or other invidious motive requirement while preserving the enacted conditions.

The hospital argues that the estate's reading would federalize every negligent inpatient treatment decision. The argument does not justify the transfer prerequisite imposed below. The duty concerns an actually determined emergency, available stabilizing treatment and breach, with direct harm and causation required for this civil action. The estate alleges delay while Normil remained in emergency care before admission. Applying those bounded conditions to that phase gives effect to the statute; Part II addresses the distinct inpatient phase.

**Precedent treatment:** In re Baby K is applied to reject the no-transfer prerequisite. Roberts preserves the statutory predicates while rejecting an extra improper-motive element. Their bounded emergency duty remains distinct from general medical negligence.

**Operative qualifications:** A transfer is hospital-directed movement outside its facilities, including discharge; movement after declaration of death and departure without permission of a hospital-affiliated person are excluded. Internal movement to intensive care is not a statutory transfer. For unstabilized transfer, §1395dd(c) requires either the individual's or legally responsible person's written request after explanation of the hospital's obligations and transfer risks, or a physician-signed certification, based on information then available, that expected medical benefits of treatment elsewhere outweigh increased transfer risks. If no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a qualified medical person may certify only after consulting a physician who makes that determination and later countersigns. Every certification must summarize supporting risks and benefits. Every route also requires available treatment minimizing risk; receiving space, qualified personnel and agreement to accept the transfer and provide appropriate treatment; all available emergency records, including the condition, observed signs or symptoms, preliminary diagnosis, treatment, test results, request or certification and the name and address of any required on-call physician who refused or failed timely to appear; qualified transport personnel and equipment with necessary appropriate life support; and applicable additional regulatory conditions. The transfer alternative is permissive, not a command to move every patient.

Section (b)(2) separately deems treatment compliance when the hospital offers the required examination and treatment, explains risks and benefits, the patient or representative refuses, and the hospital takes all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Section (b)(3) separately requires an offered transfer satisfying subsection (c), the risk-and-benefit explanation, refusal and all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. These distinct alternatives retain their conditions. State medical law remains effective except to the extent it directly conflicts with an applicable federal duty under §1395dd(f).

### II. Later admission cannot erase an accrued preadmission violation

**Holding and operative rule:** An alleged breach of an already-triggered stabilization duty during emergency care must be adjudicated even if the hospital later admits the patient; later admission cannot extinguish a completed earlier violation. The estate must prove by a preponderance an actual emergency determination, necessary treatment within available capability, breach and direct causal harm; §1395dd(d)(2)(A) permits a directly harmed person to seek the State's available personal-injury damages and appropriate equitable relief against the participating hospital within two years of the violation.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join.

**Controlling explanation:** The complaint alleges a substantial delay in emergency care, a diagnostic notation involving pneumonia and possible sepsis, an unavailable scan required before admission, and ordered antibiotics allegedly not given. At dismissal these factual allegations receive their proper pleading effect. The notation and eventual death do not conclusively establish the statutory emergency determination, available treatment, breach or causal injury; those matters require ordinary adjudication under Part I.

The lower court must distinguish injury attributed to the preadmission phase from a claim resting solely on subsequent genuine inpatient care. The legal endpoint of an unperformed duty after bona fide admission remains open here because the character and timing of the actual admission and the alleged later breach have not been adjudicated; the present preadmission allegations independently defeat the categorical dismissal. The governing standard now supplied requires adjudication of the detected emergency, available stabilizing care, statutory performance and direct causal injury in the relevant phase. The private EMTALA action runs against the participating hospital. The separate screening dismissal and §1981 remand retain their existing status.

**Precedent treatment:** In re Baby K supplies the duty tied to the detected emergency and medically defined stabilization. Roberts supplies the distinct direct-harm, hospital-action, remedial and limitations requirements. The automatic actual-transfer premise of the judgment below is rejected.

## Precedent Treatment

In re Baby K governs the rejection of a no-intended-transfer exemption and retains the complete transfer and informed-refusal alternatives. Roberts removes an extra improper-motive element while preserving actual statutory determination, breach, direct harm and the hospital-only civil-action requirements. The no-actual-transfer rule applied below cannot survive those authorities. State medical law retains effect except for a direct conflict with an applicable federal duty.

## Law After Decision

Hospitals must meet the enacted stabilization or lawful-transfer duty after determining an emergency, even when retaining the patient. Later admission does not erase a completed emergency-care violation. The hospital's actual determination, available treatment, performance and direct causal harm govern the revived claim. The genuine-inpatient endpoint remains for a phase-specific case with the necessary facts; it does not defeat adjudication of the earlier violation here.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's no-transfer rule and reversal on the preadmission allegations. He would also hold that genuine inpatient admission itself cannot extinguish an already-triggered, unperformed stabilization duty. In his view, §1395dd(b) makes performance rather than location decisive: the hospital must stabilize the identified emergency through necessary available care, make a compliant transfer, or satisfy the applicable informed-refusal provision. Stability remains the medically bounded condition of reasonable probability, not guaranteed cure; an unknown condition or later unrelated deterioration after the duty ends would fall outside this proposed rule.

He relies on the statutory alternatives and Roberts's refusal to add an extra motive defense. He would reject Bryan's admission endpoint insofar as it terminates this unperformed duty, while retaining its distinction between EMTALA and general malpractice law. Baby K supplies neither a universal care entitlement nor a conclusive answer about every inpatient. The estate would still prove actual determination, necessary available care, breach and direct harm. The statutory hospital action and authorized remedies would remain separate from any action against individual clinicians, and the proposed rule would not establish a systemwide treatment failure from the pleaded course.

If adopted and applied, the construction would govern still-open direct-review claims within its scope under Harper, including earlier care. Reynoldsville would permit no reliance-only admission exception; genuine limitations, causation and remedy requirements would remain, and final judgments and time-barred claims would not reopen. With the admission endpoint left open on this record, he accepts the narrower ground: no actual transfer is required, and delayed admission cannot cure an earlier breach. The later phase should receive proper adjudication rather than a presumed absence of any preceding duty.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Eleventh Circuit. The lower court must determine the hospital's actual emergency determination, available stabilizing treatment, any lawful statutory alternative, the timing of any breach and resulting direct harm. It must separate the preadmission claim from any claim resting solely on later genuine inpatient care and apply the hospital-action, limitations and remedy requirements in Part II.
