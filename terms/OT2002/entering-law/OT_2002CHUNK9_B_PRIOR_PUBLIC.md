### Foucha v. Louisiana, No. 90-5844

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1991; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Louisiana Supreme Court, 563 So.2d 1138 (June 14, 1990), which affirmed continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity acquittal. The question is whether the State may continue that confinement on dangerousness and the acquittee's failure to prove safety after disclaiming present mental illness.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the judgment sustaining Foucha's continued psychiatric confinement and remands for implementation.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Continued custody under Louisiana's challenged insanity-acquittee provision | Reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | End confinement on that basis; remand for implementation. No indefinite continuation while seeking another rationale. Independently lawful custody requires its own authority and procedures. |

The equal-protection theory supplies no separate claim for additional relief and no majority holding. The Court does not find Foucha harmless or prescribe conditions for every possible alternative preventive statute.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Reversal and implementation remand | Parts I (record and posture), II-A (ended predicate), II-C (alternative dangerousness regime), IV (mandate); six Justices support each part |
| Opinion of the Court | White | Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Independent due-process ground for reversal | Part II-B (distinct psychiatric grounds and procedures); five Justices support this part |
| Plurality opinion | White | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Supports reversal; no controlling equal-protection holding | Part III (equal protection); four Justices support this part |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | O'Connor | No other Justice | Joins White's Parts I–II and IV and the judgment | Emphasizes reservation of narrower preventive schemes |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins White's Parts I, II-A, II-C and IV and the judgment | States the ground concerning the particular authority for custody and the immediate implementation limit |
| Dissent | Kennedy | No other Justice | Would affirm | Distinct authority arising from the acquittee's criminal adjudication |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would affirm | Acquittee distinction and adequacy of review procedures |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The insanity judgment no longer authorizes this psychiatric confinement

**Controlling proposition:** When the State concedes that an insanity acquittee is no longer mentally ill, it may not continue his psychiatric confinement under the existing insanity-acquittee authority solely because he cannot prove that he will be harmless. The original acquittal supports neither a suspended criminal sentence nor indefinite psychiatric custody after its illness predicate disappears.

**Authority:** White's Part II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this proposition and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Louisiana must identify a present lawful basis for the restraint it imposes. Jones v. United States permits initial commitment because an insanity verdict supports an inference of illness and dangerousness; its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling rests on those continuing conditions. O'Connor v. Donaldson establishes that a permissible commitment cannot continue after its supporting basis disappears. Jackson v. Indiana requires the nature and duration of custody to remain reasonably related to its purpose. Louisiana no longer claims that Foucha is mentally ill. His antisocial personality and the doctor's unwillingness to guarantee safety do not restore that conceded absence. The State's substantial interest in protecting the public therefore cannot perpetuate this particular psychiatric commitment. The original criminal conduct does not make the acquittal a conviction authorizing punishment. The Court neither finds Foucha harmless nor sets the maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment. Any separate lawful restraint must rest on its own established authority and procedures.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the continuing illness-and-danger basis of insanity commitment; initial verdict-based commitment and rejection of an automatic hypothetical-sentence ceiling remain intact.
- O'Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563 (1975): applied to the requirement that confinement end when its authorizing basis disappears; the Court does not equate a conditional-release recommendation with a finding of harmlessness.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the required relationship between confinement's nature, duration and purpose.

##### 2. Psychiatric custody requires appropriate grounds and procedures

**Controlling proposition:** Once the insanity-acquittal basis for holding Foucha as a mental patient has ended, Louisiana cannot preserve that psychiatric custody without constitutionally adequate proceedings establishing the grounds required for it. If the State invokes ordinary civil commitment, it must establish current mental illness and dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence; additional procedure cannot supply an absent substantive ground.

**Authority:** White's Part II-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: five of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this section's separate Vitek-based procedural characterization.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a hearing about dangerousness alone cannot authorize confinement as a mental patient after the State disclaims mental illness. Vitek v. Jones recognizes that placement in a psychiatric institution imposes a distinct deprivation requiring appropriate justification and procedure, even for someone already lawfully confined. Jackson v. Indiana requires a new lawful basis and the protections belonging to it when the original commitment purpose no longer supports custody. Addington v. Texas supplies the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment. Louisiana instead requires Foucha to disprove danger, although its conceded facts cannot presently establish the illness required for that civil route. The State's reliance on a counseled release hearing does not answer this defect: fairness in deciding the wrong statutory condition does not establish the missing condition. Baxstrom v. Herold reinforces that an expired original basis does not remove the safeguards of the new proceeding. The Court does not prescribe a universal burden for every possible preventive regime or disturb the distinct rules governing initial insanity commitment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Vitek v. Jones, 445 U.S. 480 (1980): applied to psychiatric institutionalization's distinct liberty deprivation and need for appropriate grounds and procedures.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the need for an independently authorized commitment proceeding after the initial basis expires.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979): applied unchanged to the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment; that burden is not made universal for every preventive custody channel.
- Baxstrom v. Herold, 383 U.S. 107 (1966): relied on for the ordinary protections attending a new civil commitment after the prior detention basis ends; no separate majority equal-protection holding is created here.

##### 3. Public safety does not sustain this unbounded dangerousness regime

**Controlling proposition:** Louisiana's scheme permitting indefinite psychiatric confinement of a no-longer-mentally-ill acquittee because he has not proved nondangerousness violates due process. A legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of a carefully limited substantive authority; the Court leaves materially narrower preventive regimes undecided.

**Authority:** White's Part II-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient rejection of Louisiana's asserted alternative preventive justification. The holding adopts no new general scrutiny tier or universal dangerousness proof standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exceptional power to prevent danger cannot be detached from the limits that justify the particular restraint. United States v. Salerno sustained a narrowly focused pretrial scheme with an identified threat, government proof, an adversarial hearing, review and strict temporal limits. Louisiana's rule instead permits confinement without an endpoint while requiring the acquittee to prove safety. The doctor's reluctance to certify Foucha as harmless is enough under that rule, although the State concedes that the psychiatric illness has ended. A fair procedure for predicting danger does not supply substantive authority for an otherwise unauthorized restraint. The original verdict supplies no punitive authority under Jones. If later conduct constitutes a crime, ordinary criminal proceedings remain available; the Court makes no new finding that a particular assault occurred or warrants conviction. A better hearing alone cannot cure this regime's substantive defect. The Court reserves narrowly drawn alternatives rather than converting today's holding into a universal prohibition of preventive detention.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): distinguished because its confined pretrial detention authority and safeguards do not authorize Louisiana's indefinite post-acquittal psychiatric restraint.
- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the absence of authority to punish an insanity acquittee as a convicted offender.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, Jones's distinct initial-commitment rule remains in force, but Louisiana cannot continue this psychiatric confinement after conceding the end of illness on the acquittee's failure to prove safety. Five Justices additionally require appropriate psychiatric grounds and procedures; ordinary civil commitment retains Addington's clear-and-convincing burden. Six reject the indefinite dangerousness alternative under this scheme. These are applied and clarified channel limits, not a new universal scrutiny tier or dangerousness standard. No majority adopts the plurality's equal-protection rule. Maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment, materially narrower preventive regimes, and the scope of state insanity defenses remain undecided. No Marks inference is needed because each controlling due-process proposition has at least five direct joins.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, concludes in Part III that Louisiana also denies equal protection by withholding ordinary commitment safeguards from a sane acquittee while not permitting comparable indefinite dangerousness confinement for other persons whose original custody basis has ended. The four would require a particularly convincing justification for that disparity; this is a plurality position, not law.

O'Connor emphasizes that the holding concerns this indefinite psychiatric regime. She leaves open detention carefully limited in nature and duration to substantial safety concerns and questions extended confinement beyond what a conviction could have permitted, without deciding that question. No constitutional requirement that a State provide an insanity defense is adopted.

Stone-Zsela agrees that the psychiatric channel's ended predicate requires relief. He reserves independent equal protection, a new general scrutiny tier, universal proof rules and narrowly defined alternative regimes. Implementation cannot allow indefinite continuation while the State looks for a new justification; separate custody requires its own established authority and procedures.

Kennedy would affirm because the insanity adjudication follows proof of criminal conduct and belongs to a distinct legal channel. He views state definitions of criminal insanity and incapacitation as constitutionally relevant and rejects automatic transfer of ordinary civil-commitment rules to this setting.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm because acquittees differ from ordinary civil committees, the State may attach continued safety-based custody to that status, and periodic counseled review is adequate here. They reject an expansive new substantive right against this confinement and distinguish its lack of a fixed endpoint from a refusal ever to provide release review. Neither dissent controls.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Louisiana courts must implement reversal by ending custody under the challenged insanity-acquittee basis. The mandate permits no indefinite continuation of that custody while the State searches for a substitute rationale. Any independently lawful custody requires its own established authority and procedures; the Court orders neither automatic release from a separate lawful restraint nor a new civil commitment. No future hearing date, factual diagnosis, new charge, compliance finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is created.

**End of entry: Foucha v. Louisiana, merits decision, May 18, 1992.**

### Reno v. Flores, No. 91-905

Merits decision, March 23, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 13, 1992; decided March 23, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit's en banc judgment of August 9, 1991, 942 F.2d 1352, affirming the district court's May 25, 1988 relief against the juvenile-release regulation. The questions concern statutory authority, substantive liberty, review procedures and related equal-protection objections. The government seeks reversal of the mandatory responsible-adult release and automatic-hearing requirements.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses and remands, seven to two. It sets aside facial invalidation and the resulting mandatory addition of every responsible adult as a release category and universal automatic-hearing requirement. The 1987 Juvenile Care Agreement remains in force.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory and substantive-due-process facial invalidation | Reverse, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens | Set aside mandatory responsible-adult release category; remand. |
| Universal automatic immigration-judge hearing | Reverse, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens | Set aside the facially grounded injunction; preserve properly supported implementation litigation. |
| Facial equal-protection objection | Rejected, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun and Stevens adhere to their dissent from the judgment | No independent basis to sustain facial relief. |

The disposition makes no finding that every detention, notice or delay is lawful. It leaves the conditions agreement intact and directs no particular child's detention or release.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Scalia | White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Supports all reversal and remand components | Entire opinion; six Justices support the statutory, substantive, procedural and classification analysis. |
| Concurrence | O'Connor | Souter | Both also join the Court's opinion and judgment | Entire concurrence; stresses children's substantial liberty interest and protective conditions. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins all reversal and remand components | No portion of Scalia's rationale. |
| Dissent | Stevens | Blackmun | Would uphold the release and hearing relief | Entire dissent. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. Statutory authority for the juvenile release regulation

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1252(a)(1) permits the Attorney General to use the family-custodian and discretionary-release arrangements in 8 C.F.R. § 242.24 for temporary juvenile custody pending deportation proceedings; the regulation is not facially beyond that authority because it does not mandate release to every willing responsible adult. Custody remains subject to the statute's reasonable-dispatch limitation, its habeas remedy upon the requisite conclusive showing of unnecessary delay, the regulation's release avenues and the existing conditions agreement.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion for the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six votes for this rule. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on a narrower statutory and facial-review ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress left room for reasonable arrangements governing detention and release while deportability is determined. Carlson v. Landon recognizes discretion within the immigration statute and requires a reasonable foundation for its exercise. The National Center for Immigrants' Rights decision permits general release conditions serving the immigration scheme, with individual safeguards, and does not confine every condition to physical attendance alone. Caring for a juvenile for whom an approved custodian is unavailable fits that statutory responsibility. The regulation permits parental designation and exceptional release, so it does not establish an absolute family-only barrier. The contrary reading would turn a discretionary custody statute into a command to entrust every otherwise releasable child to any willing adult. Neither the text nor the facial record requires that command. The Court does not decide the legality of particular prolonged custody, eliminate habeas review for unnecessary delay, or excuse breach of the conditions agreement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952): applies discretion bounded by the immigration statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody.
- National Center for Immigrants' Rights, December 16, 1991, decision: applies general immigration-release authority with preserved individual safeguards; does not enlarge that decision into universal detention authority.
- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): applies facial-review limits to the whole-regulation remedy.

##### 2. The facial substantive-due-process challenge fails

**Controlling proposition:** The Constitution does not give a juvenile without an available parent, guardian or close adult relative a fundamental right to placement with a willing unrelated adult instead of temporary, nonpunitive government care under this regulation and the binding conditions agreement. On this facial record, the family preferences and other release avenues reasonably serve care and attendance interests; individual unconstitutional confinement and enforcement of the agreement remain open.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. O'Connor and Souter also explain the substantial liberty interest in avoiding institutional confinement in a separate concurrence; Stone-Zsela does not join this formulation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the particular placement entitlement asserted here is not a fundamental constitutional right. Schall v. Martin recognizes that juveniles are subject to protective custody and that their liberty interests operate within the need for responsible adult care. Parham v. J. R. likewise rejects an assumption that a minor can simply choose complete freedom from adult custody. The challenged rule selects custodians and provides alternatives; it does not prescribe punishment. The conditions agreement supplies the protective setting against which this facial case must be judged, without establishing that every facility in fact complies. Youngberg v. Romeo preserves constitutional limits on institutional treatment, and Foucha requires a lawful ground for continued confinement after an authorizing predicate ends. Neither establishes a general right to unrelated-adult placement during lawful temporary immigration custody. The Court rejects the claim that the regulation must fall in all applications. Particular unlawful restraints and violations of the agreement remain subject to appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the distinction between protective juvenile custody and punishment.
- Parham v. J. R., 442 U.S. 584 (1979): applies the special circumstances of juvenile care; does not extinguish a child's liberty interest.
- Youngberg v. Romeo, 457 U.S. 307 (1982): preserves constitutional limits on institutional care.
- Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992, decision: distinguishes psychiatric confinement after its predicate ends; no universal custody test is inferred.

##### 3. Request-based review is not facially inadequate

**Controlling proposition:** The described examination, notice and opportunity to obtain immigration-judge review on request do not facially require replacement with an automatic hearing for every detained juvenile. That result depends on the available procedures and the absence of a record establishing general inability to invoke them; it does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela agrees with setting aside the universal automatic-hearing remedy but reserves a distinct Mathews-based implementation inquiry.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the facial record does not justify an automatic immigration-judge hearing in every case. For a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure, the examination and custody decision occur within twenty-four hours; a different officer examines unless no qualified alternative is available. The notice form provides English and Spanish notice of review, and a waiver can be withdrawn by requesting a hearing. Immigration-judge review, followed by administrative and judicial review, supplies a means to contest custody. Fare v. Michael C. recognizes that juveniles can knowingly waive even criminal protections; age alone therefore does not prove universal inability to request this review. The Japanese Immigrant Case supplies the underlying entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings. A proposed individualized comparison with every willing adult restates the rejected placement entitlement. Respondents have not established universal ignorance of the review option or inevitable excessive delay. The Court does not decide a supported claim that notice, assistance or delay makes review ineffective for a child or class.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fare v. Michael C., 442 U.S. 707 (1979): applies the recognition that juveniles can make a knowing waiver; no universal finding of comprehension follows.
- The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86 (1903): applies aliens' entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings.

##### 4. The challenged classifications do not establish facial unequal treatment

**Controlling proposition:** The regulation's distinction between juveniles with available family custodians and those without them survives the facial equal-protection objection because family availability bears a rational relation to safe placement and attendance. The separate federal juvenile-delinquency release scheme does not require identical release rules for immigration custody, and this ruling does not resolve unequal administration in a particular case.

**Authority:** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on this component and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the classifications challenged here do not make the regulation facially unconstitutional. Family members and unrelated applicants for custody stand differently in relation to the child's continuing care, and the government may use that difference when arranging temporary custody. The regulation does not make family status an absolute bar to other placement: it provides parental designation and unusual-and-compelling-circumstances discretion. The objection therefore cannot proceed as though every child without an available relative must remain confined regardless of the regulation's alternatives. Schall v. Martin supplies the relevant recognition that protective juvenile arrangements may account for responsibility and care. The existence of different release provisions for federal juvenile-delinquency proceedings does not erase differences between that statutory setting and deportation proceedings. The Court does not hold that all children in the former system are citizens, that aliens lack equal-protection safeguards, or that discriminatory administration would be permissible. Its conclusion concerns the challenged classifications on this facial record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S. 253 (1984): applies the legitimate relevance of protective care in juvenile custody; no general exemption from equal protection follows.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. National Center for Immigrants' Rights' individual-status and discretionary-relief safeguards remain controlling in their own channel. Foucha's psychiatric-custody holdings remain unchanged; the decision neither adopts a universal preventive-detention standard nor treats the conditions agreement as a finding of actual compliance.

Section 242.24 survives this facial attack. Subsection (b)(1) requires release in order of preference to a nondetained parent, legal guardian, or adult brother, sister, aunt, uncle or grandparent unless detention is required to secure timely appearance before the Service or immigration court, or ensure the juvenile's safety or that of others. If the only listed custodian is detained, (b)(2) provides discretionary case-by-case consideration of simultaneous release. Under (b)(3), a parent or guardian detained or outside the United States may designate another capable and willing person by sworn affidavit before an immigration or consular officer; that person must agree to care for the child and ensure presence at all future proceedings. Subsection (b)(4) permits other agreeing adult custodians only in unusual and compelling circumstances, at the district director's or chief patrol agent's discretion. These are distinct release avenues, not one undifferentiated exception.

Requested custody review and the enforceable 1987 conditions agreement remain. Section 1252(a)(1) makes the custody determination reviewable by habeas upon a conclusive showing that the Attorney General is not proceeding with the reasonable dispatch warranted by the particular facts and circumstances to determine deportability. No thirty-day legal cap is created. The twenty-four-hour examination and custody process described in the holding concerns a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure; the separate voluntary-departure safeguards are not adjudicated as universally sufficient here. No right to release to every willing unrelated adult and no automatic hearing for every detained juvenile is created. The Court does not decide particular excessive detention, defective notice, ineffective access to review or properly supported systemic implementation claims. Stone-Zsela's proposed means of remedying those defects, including necessary assistance or automatic review, remain noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

O'Connor, joined by Souter, emphasizes that institutional custody seriously restrains a child's liberty even when no parent is available. She accepts the Court's judgment because the governing agreement calls for care comparable to licensed juvenile facilities, with education, recreation and reunification efforts, rather than punitive imprisonment. The concurrence does not approve arbitrary or punitive confinement and rests on the difference between lawful protective care and institutional punishment. Both Justices join the entire Court opinion.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on the limited facial showing. He recognizes freedom from institutional custody as a core liberty interest and reads § 1252(a)(1), Carlson and Chevron to permit temporary care directed to attendance and safe placement. Lawful initial custody does not answer a properly presented narrower challenge to a mandatory barrier causing unlawful continued detention. Administrative convenience cannot authorize unnecessary or indefinite custody. Under Mathews, age, language, assistance, notice and actual ability to seek review matter. A supported individual or systemic defect can require effective assistance, automatic review where necessary, or another remedy fitted to that defect. The record does not establish universal inability to request review, justify prescribing a hearing schedule, or require placement with every willing adult. He leaves the 1987 agreement intact.

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, would affirm. The dissent treats freedom from needless institutional custody as the right at stake and argues that a willing, responsible adult can protect care and attendance interests without detention. Carlson, Witkovich and National Center for Immigrants' Rights constrain immigration discretion to legitimate statutory ends; administrative convenience does not itself supply those ends. Schall's demonstrated preventive need does not validate a categorical refusal to consider suitable nonrelatives. The dissent also regards a child's inability to invoke review reliably as a reason for the neutral hearing the lower court required. Its statutory and constitutional objections do not command a majority.

The [official United States Reports opinion and separate writings, 507 U.S. 292–348](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep507/usrep507292/usrep507292.pdf), and the [judgment below, 942 F.2d 1352](https://static.case.law/f2d/942/html/1352-01.html), support the dates, posture, regulation, 1987 agreement and existing record. The regulation's release alternatives are described from its reproduced text; no later Flores settlement is used. No unverified quotation is supplied.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete; the case is remanded through the Ninth Circuit for proceedings consistent with reversal. The facial injunction falls to the extent stated. The conditions agreement remains enforceable in the district court. No particular later hearing date, mandate-issuance date, release order or settlement is specified.

### Kansas v. Hendricks / Hendricks v. Kansas, Nos. 95-1649 and 95-9075

Consolidated plenary merits decision, June 23, 1997

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 10, 1996. On writs of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Kansas.

The Kansas Supreme Court's March 1, 1996 judgment, In re Hendricks, 259 Kan. 246, 912 P.2d 129, invalidated Hendricks's commitment under the 1994 Sexually Violent Predator Act because it considered the statutory mental-abnormality definition constitutionally insufficient. It did not decide the preserved Double Jeopardy or Ex Post Facto claims, presented through Hendricks's cross-petition. Kansas seeks reversal and restoration of the commitment; Hendricks seeks affirmance on due process or the alternative punishment grounds. The granted questions concern the Act's substantive commitment criteria and whether this confinement is punitive. Both dockets concern the same commitment and judgment.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4, in both consolidated dockets.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, who would reverse and reject both punishment-dependent challenges.

The invalidation of Hendricks's commitment is sustained because this application imposes punishment for conduct predating the Act. He may not remain confined under that application. The disposition is as applied: it does not facially invalidate every prospective application or decide whether another independently lawful civil-commitment route is available. No new factfinding remand or release date is prescribed. The due-process discussion is not a separate reversal component; Double Jeopardy is not reached.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signed lead opinion, Parts II–III: opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg | Affirms on the as-applied Ex Post Facto ground | Civil-intent and clearest-proof method, cumulative punitive application, retrospective punishment, remedy and limits |
| Same lead opinion, Part I: non-dispositive due-process discussion | Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Does not independently support affirmance or a separate reversal | Bounded rejection of a categorical diagnostic-vocabulary objection; Ginsburg reserves the issue |
| Dissent | O'Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse and reject both punishment-dependent challenges | Civil classification on the original record and resulting rejection of Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy; agrees with Part I of the lead opinion |

Parts II–III command five direct joins. Part I's due-process discussion is not a ground for the judgment or a separate judgment component. Ginsburg joins Parts II–III and reserves the due-process question.

#### Holdings

##### I. Civil intent remains rebuttable in this confinement scheme

**Holding and operative rule:** In assessing whether Kansas's civil commitment of Hendricks constitutes punishment for Ex Post Facto purposes, the Court first determines the Legislature's intended civil or criminal character; an intended civil scheme is treated as punishment only upon the clearest proof that its purpose or effect is punitive. The Act's civil designation is relevant but not conclusive, and neither confinement, a connection to crime, indeterminate duration, criminal-style procedural protection nor treatment difficulty alone decides that demanding inquiry.

**Authority:** Parts II–III of Breyer's opinion are the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter and Ginsburg. Those five Justices adopt each controlling proposition below, its application, qualifications and remedy. Part I's separate due-process discussion is not a ground for this judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted civil purpose must be tested against what this confinement does. United States v. Ursery supplies the civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry for the forfeiture statutes it considered; United States v. Ward supplies the distinction between legislative intent and the demanding showing necessary to overcome it. The same distinction applies here, but Ursery's conclusion about forfeiture does not answer a question about personal confinement. Kennedy v. Mendoza-Martinez permits examination of punitive purpose and effect without making a single indicator conclusive.

Kansas expressed a purpose of control, care and treatment, assigned custody to its social-services agency, and conditioned confinement on a present qualifying condition and danger. These are substantial civil features. The statute also commands care, review and release procedures. Their existence matters; it does not establish that the initial confinement operated as promised. The contrary claim that a civil label resolves classification would prevent the very inquiry Ward and Ursery require. Conversely, a court may not replace clearest proof with a demand for successful cure or treat each deficiency as a criminal penalty. The Court assesses the supported original operation as a whole, without a new exhaustive checklist or a general rule that every preventive confinement is punishment.

**Precedent treatment:** Ursery's method is extended to this confinement question without importing its forfeiture result; Ward's demanding intent-and-effect inquiry is applied; Mendoza-Martinez's purpose-and-effect analysis remains nonexclusive and no single indicator becomes dispositive.

##### II. This retrospective application imposes punishment

**Holding and operative rule:** The cumulative circumstances of Hendricks's original commitment—additional confinement after his sentence, selection tied to earlier sexual offenses, prisonlike restraint, and initially inadequate treatment sustained on the prospect of a future specialized program despite acknowledged possible treatment—provide the clearest proof of punitive operation in this application. Because the Act imposes that additional punishment for conduct predating its enactment, its application to Hendricks violates the Ex Post Facto Clause, and the judgment invalidating this commitment is affirmed.

**Authority:** The five-Justice opinion of the Court identified above; this is the sufficient ground for the judgment in both consolidated dockets.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these circumstances must be considered together. At the October 1994 hearing, neither proposed specialist contract had been signed and the dedicated clinical program was not established. The trial court nevertheless left commitment in force on the possibility that the treatment program would become available. That is the initial treatment deficiency material here, rather than a finding that no clinician or treatment opportunity existed.

The contrary evidence is real: Kansas pursued procurement, had direct-care staff and hospital professionals available, anticipated individualized assessment, and had previously offered Hendricks a prison treatment program. His testimony included both a pay choice and an unexplained qualification concerning a parole-board member. These facts prevent an inference that all treatment was deliberately withheld. They do not remove the punitive significance of the additional offense-linked, prisonlike restraint and the prospective basis on which its treatment justification was sustained. The Court finds the demanding showing satisfied by their cumulative operation, not by a missing job title or an isolated contracting failure.

Kansas's independent interest in protecting the public and its statutory care and release obligations do not by themselves answer that showing. United States v. Sahhar permits custody beyond a hypothetical criminal maximum only on an independently lawful civil basis; it does not classify this commitment. No rejected meaningful alternative or sham release procedure is established or presumed. No reason for the failed bids, deliberate deferral of a particular effective therapy, complete absence of a clinical plan, or specified later treatment regimen is found. The additional punitive confinement follows a later enactment applied to earlier offenses. The Ex Post Facto Clause therefore forbids this application without requiring a separate decision about double jeopardy.

**Precedent treatment:** Sahhar's rejection of a hypothetical sentence ceiling is distinguished because its holding presupposes independently lawful civil custody; it does not answer the punitive-operation question here or supply Kansas with the federal statute's procedures.

##### III. Scope of the judgment and questions not decided

Hendricks may not remain confined under this application of the Act for conduct predating it. The Court affirms the invalidation of this commitment on the as-applied Ex Post Facto ground; it does not facially invalidate every prospective application, decide the validity of every later genuinely civil program, or decide whether another independently lawful civil-commitment route is available. No new release date, stay, hearing schedule, clinical protocol or alternative-custody authorization is prescribed.

The Double Jeopardy claim is not reached. Punitive classification does not by itself establish attachment of jeopardy or identity of offenses, and the Court does not decide those distinct elements. Part I's rejection of a categorical objection to the words mental abnormality is non-dispositive discussion, not an independently sufficient ground for affirmance or a holding that this commitment satisfies all due-process requirements. Ginsburg reserves that issue. Treatment-related due process, broader propensity-only definitions, a universal personality-disorder rule, a requirement of absolute inability to control conduct in every circumstance, and a new jury instruction are not decided. No Marks synthesis is needed for the five directly joined Ex Post Facto holdings.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **United States v. Ursery; United States v. $405,089.23 in United States Currency, June 24, 1996:** Extends their rebuttable civil-intent and clearest-proof method from the specified forfeiture schemes to classification of this personal confinement for Ex Post Facto purposes. Their forfeiture result is not a classification of the Kansas commitment; their distinct Double Jeopardy limits remain.
- **United States v. Ward, 448 U.S. 242:** Applies the distinction between intended civil character and the demanding proof needed to establish punitive purpose or effect.
- **Kennedy v. Mendoza-Martinez, 372 U.S. 144:** Applies examination of punitive purpose and effect without making an individual indicator decisive or creating a mandatory exhaustive checklist.
- **United States v. Sahhar, December 16, 1996:** Distinguishes its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling for independently lawful §4246 custody. That decision does not establish that this Kansas confinement is civil; its federal placement, proof and release provisions are not transplanted into Kansas law.

No precedent is overruled. The non-dispositive Part I discussion draws on Foucha v. Louisiana's requirement of current lawful grounds and its reservation of materially narrower preventive regimes, and on Sahhar's insistence on independent civil authority. It does not change those holdings, Addington's ordinary civil-commitment proof protection, or Heller v. Doe's rejection of a particular unequal proof arrangement. The Court does not adopt a new due-process rule through that discussion. California Department of Corrections v. Morales's meaningful-parole-opportunity holdings remain confined to their subject; they supply neither a general speculative-risk rule nor a substitute for proving punitive operation here. Lynce v. Mathis's exhaustion disposition supplies no credit-withdrawal merits rule. Blockburger and the retained Grady rule are not applied because the independent Double Jeopardy elements are not reached.

#### Law After Decision

Effective June 23, 1997, the civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry governs the Ex Post Facto classification of this commitment. The supported cumulative original operation establishes punishment as applied to Hendricks, and its imposition for pre-enactment conduct is unconstitutional. No single feature is made automatically punitive, and no new burden shift, mandatory alternatives inquiry, treatment protocol or release procedure is created.

The decision does not invalidate all prospective applications of the Act or all later civil programs. It leaves the availability of another independently lawful commitment route undecided. The substantive-due-process discussion creates no additional controlling rule, and Double Jeopardy's separate elements remain unresolved. Current lawful civil grounds and effective safeguards retain their independent importance under Foucha and Sahhar.

#### Separate Writings

**Part I's non-dispositive due-process discussion.** Breyer, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, rejects a categorical constitutional objection resting on the statutory words mental abnormality. The discussion is confined to the serious recognized condition, evidence of impaired control and connection to dangerous conduct shown here. A past offense and a prediction alone do not supply that substantive limitation; neither a statutory verdict nor a high proof burden substitutes for it. Foucha concerns custody after its conceded illness predicate ends and leaves narrower preventive regimes open; Sahhar requires a new lawful civil basis rather than making every legislative diagnosis sufficient. This agreement does not dispense with the constitutional safeguards needed for a lawful civil commitment. The discussion does not decide treatment-related due process or every application of personality disorder. **O'Connor's dissent, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The dissent agrees with Part I's bounded rejection of a diagnostic-vocabulary requirement but would reverse the Kansas judgment and reject the punishment-dependent challenges. Applying the demanding civil-intent and clearest-proof inquiry, it finds the supported original record insufficient to turn the commitment into punishment. The current condition and danger requirements, social-services custody, separate setting, care duty, procurement efforts, interim professional resources and actual review and release routes support an independent preventive purpose. The lack of a completed specialist program is serious, but does not establish punitive purpose or effect when the contrary original evidence is considered. Neither difficult treatment nor an indefinite duration alone defeats otherwise lawful civil custody.

The dissent does not find that treatment was adequate, that contracting failed for a benign reason, or that later treatment-hour representations cure the original deficiencies. It preserves proper challenges to unconstitutional conditions and requires genuine continuing predicates; a civil description would not protect a proved punitive operation. The dissent relies on the actual statutory sequence. Section 59-29a08 requires yearly examination, a report and judicial review. After an earlier unauthorized petition was found frivolous or insufficient condition change was found, a later petition must be denied unless its facts support sufficient change to warrant a hearing. On its civil classification, both Ex Post Facto and Double Jeopardy fail at their necessary punishment prerequisite. The dissent makes no unnecessary ruling on attachment, offense identity or every retrospective regulatory burden.

### INS v. St. Cyr, No. 00-767

Merits decision, June 25, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 24, 2001; decided June 25, 2001. On writ of certiorari to the Second Circuit, 229 F.3d 406, affirming district-court habeas jurisdiction and the judgment preserving eligibility for former §212(c) consideration. The questions concern the independent habeas channel and the temporal operation of AEDPA and IIRIRA on an otherwise eligible preenactment guilty plea, not whether a waiver should be granted.

Enrico St. Cyr, a Haitian citizen and lawful permanent resident since June 17, 1986, pleaded guilty to selling a controlled substance under Connecticut law on March 8, 1996. He was then deportable but eligible to seek relief. Removal proceedings began April 10, 1997. The agency treated the 1996 changes as a legal bar and did not exercise waiver discretion. His independently filed habeas action challenges that conclusion. Calcano-Martinez, decided earlier in the coordinated consideration of these matters, supplies the controlling forum rule; its petitioners' individual histories do not supply St. Cyr's facts.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

**Opposing the eligibility disposition:** Scalia and Thomas.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Calcano permits adjudication of this actual habeas action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2241 supplies jurisdiction over St. Cyr's properly brought substantial legal claim that the agency erroneously denied authority to consider former §212(c) relief. The permanent review provisions and repeal of the separate immigration-specific habeas provision do not foreclose this action; the court may decide the governing eligibility law and afford effective relief without reweighing immigration discretion.

**Authority:** Part I of Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court has all nine joins. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas apply Calcano as controlling precedent while retaining their published disagreement with its original statutory construction; their present joins do not adopt that original reasoning anew.

**Controlling explanation:** Calcano-Martinez v. INS has resolved the permanent-scheme channel question. Reno v. Goncalves distinguishes eligibility from an exercise of discretion. St. Cyr challenges the Attorney General's conclusion that relief is legally unavailable, not the conviction or the agency's balancing of equities. Kolster and Calcano preserve a channel capable of deciding that objection and granting effective relief if it succeeds. Repeal of former §1105a's separate writ and comprehensive ordinary-review consolidation do not answer the retained §2241 claim.

The statutory ground makes a comprehensive Suspension Clause ruling unnecessary. General factual retrial and discretionary reweighing remain outside this claim.

##### II. The 1996 changes do not eliminate eligibility attached to this completed plea

**Holding and operative rule:** Absent sufficiently clear contrary temporal direction, AEDPA §440(d)'s new eligibility bar and IIRIRA §304(b)'s repeal of former §212(c) do not eliminate consideration under the former law for a lawful permanent resident whose deportable conviction resulted from a guilty plea entered before the relevant change while eligible for that relief. The protected consequence is the opportunity for lawful discretionary consideration, subject to the former eligibility conditions, not a favorable waiver or universal exemption for every preenactment conviction.

**Authority:** Parts II and III of Justice Stevens's Opinion of the Court are joined by the seven Justices who join the opinion in full. Their direct joins control the operative temporal rule and remedy; the opinion applies existing temporal law to this transaction rather than announcing a new general framework.

**Controlling explanation:** Landgraf v. USI Film Products requires examination of Congress's temporal direction and the legal consequences attached to completed events. Neither the new system's comprehensive character, its April 1, 1997 commencement nor the saving provision for earlier proceedings unmistakably directs withdrawal of eligibility attached to this completed plea. Those provisions determine which procedural system governs St. Cyr's April 10, 1997 removal proceeding. They do not themselves resolve the distinct substantive disability. Lindh v. Murphy supports attention to differentiated temporal provisions; it supplies no general exemption from AEDPA.

St. Cyr pleaded guilty on March 8, 1996, before both enactments, while eligible to seek the waiver. The plea completed an exchange of legal rights, including surrender of trial rights, while lawful discretionary consideration remained available. Eliminating that opportunity adds a new legal consequence even though a waiver was never guaranteed. A particular official's promise, subjective-reliance finding or proof that St. Cyr would have chosen trial is unnecessary. The Court makes none of those findings.

The government argues that removal concerns future presence and a present discretionary benefit, which a state plea cannot freeze. But the conviction obtained through this completed exchange supplies the very predicate for the new categorical disability. Protecting the existing opportunity does not bind the Attorney General to grant relief or freeze immigration law generally. Reyes-Hernandez protects a surrendered legal option without making its particular deportability defense indispensable. Kolster expressly distinguished a plea before eligibility; its different result does not defeat this eligible plea. Goncalves protected a pending application and left this later-application and repeal question open.

Both challenged changes are therefore inapplicable to this transaction. Ordinary statutory construction resolves their temporal meaning. Goncalves and United States v. Mead Corp. do not allow an agency interpretation to override that resolved meaning. No independent constitutional invalidation or discretionary entitlement is necessary.

**Limits and questions not reached:** Trial convictions, pleas after the relevant change, persons ineligible when they pleaded and unrelated later grounds of removal lie outside this transaction class. Their claims are not adjudicated adversely by implication. No new or later disqualification is adjudicated here. The former law's conditions remain operative, including seven years of lawful unrelinquished domicile and its bar for an aggravated-felony conviction with at least five years served for the felony or felonies; the decision waives no otherwise applicable former eligibility restriction.

##### III. St. Cyr receives consideration under the former law, not the waiver itself

**Holding and operative rule:** The Second Circuit's judgment recognizing habeas jurisdiction and preserving former-law eligibility is affirmed. The matter returns through the appropriate agency process for consideration of St. Cyr's request under the applicable former §212(c) criteria and, if those criteria are met, a lawful exercise of discretion; the 1996 changes cannot supply the categorical ineligibility ground rejected here.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice coalition directly adopts the eligibility disposition and remedy. Scalia and Thomas would reverse that relief while accepting present habeas jurisdiction under Calcano.

**Controlling explanation:** The agency stopped at a legal bar and did not perform the discretionary task the former law permits. A judicial determination of the lawful eligibility rule must have an effective consequence: the agency must entertain the request under that rule. Goncalves supplies the distinction between requiring lawful consideration and substituting a court's assessment of waiver equities.

The record does not supply the factual equities for this Court to grant relief, and the criminal conviction is not under review. Further factual and discretionary work is ordinary administration of the rule, not an unresolved answer to the temporal question. No favorable waiver, release, termination of removal proceedings, retrial of the conviction, extra hearing beyond lawful agency adjudication, new stay, dissolution of a stay or removal direction is ordered.

### Zadvydas v. Davis and Ashcroft v. Ma, Nos. 99-7791 and 00-38

Merits decision, June 28, 2001

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued February 21, 2001. On certiorari to the Fifth and Ninth Circuits. The Fifth Circuit, 185 F.3d 279, reversed Zadvydas's release order; the Ninth Circuit, 208 F.3d 815, affirmed Ma's release. The questions concern authority to detain previously admitted people beyond the removal period when removal is not reasonably foreseeable, retained habeas review, and effective custody relief. The underlying removal orders are not under review.

Zadvydas entered as a child, later incurred convictions, and received a final deportation order on May 2, 1994. Germany and Lithuania would not accept him as a citizen; efforts involving his wife's native country also failed. His September 1995 habeas filing arose under the earlier law, which provided a six-month removal period. The district court ordered release on October 30, 1997. That order remains effective because the Fifth Circuit stayed its mandate. The present controversy includes the government's asserted current authority to resume custody. It is neither continuous imprisonment through decision nor an automatically moot dispute.

Ma entered as a Cambodian refugee as a child, received a removal order following an aggravated-felony conviction, and was detained beyond the removal period. The district court released him; the Ninth Circuit affirmed, treating the absence of an existing or pending Cambodian repatriation agreement as decisive. Neither an agreement's absence nor ongoing negotiations alone establishes the actual removal prospect. Neither record establishes harmlessness, current acceptance, deliberate obstruction or compliance with every custody-review requirement.

##### Statutory and administrative setting

Section 1231(a)(1) ordinarily provides a ninety-day removal period beginning on the latest of administrative finality, the final judicial order when judicial review includes a removal stay, or release from nonimmigration confinement. Paragraph (2) requires detention during that period and separately forbids release of the specified criminal and security categories. Paragraph (6) permits detention beyond it for an ordered-removed person who is inadmissible under section 1182, removable under section 1227(a)(1)(C), (a)(2) or (a)(4), or determined to pose a community risk or be unlikely to comply with removal. If released, that person must receive paragraph (3) supervision. Permission to detain and mandatory supervision are distinct.

Paragraph (1)(C) separately extends the period and permits detention when the person fails or refuses to make a timely good-faith application for necessary travel or departure documents, or conspires or acts to prevent removal. A country's refusal establishes none of those triggers. The government's allegations about Lithuanian documents, reporting and citizenship possibilities, and the account of Zadvydas's renewed application, remain unresolved. Any reliance on obstruction requires its actual predicates.

The December 21, 2000 replacement section 241.4 governs the current administrative review described here and expressly covers relevant criminal cases under former section 242. It replaces the old clear-and-convincing release language with the detainee's satisfaction showing and specified findings: immediate removal unavailable, impracticable or contrary to the public interest; present and likely continuing nonviolence; no likely community threat; likely compliance with conditions; and no significant flight risk. Its existence does not establish compliance or satisfaction in either case.

The rule supplies written notice, records review and reasons; an optional district interview; headquarters review; and a personal panel interview if release is not recommended or an initial favorable panel recommendation is not accepted. Two panel members must agree; a split adds a third member and permits a majority recommendation. The final official is not bound by it. A person chosen by the detainee may assist at no government expense if able to attend the scheduled interview, subject to reasonable institutional and panel security concerns. The headquarters director provides translation when that director determines it appropriate; information submitted to the panel must be in English. Current custody decisions have no administrative appeal, except completion of Board cases already pending on December 21 under the transition provision.

The initial pre-expiration review may be waived by requesting additional preparation time. The district may retain responsibility for up to three months after the ninety-day review. On an earlier referral, headquarters review ordinarily occurs by expiration of the removal period or as soon thereafter as practicable; if district responsibility continues and removal has not occurred within three months of its decision, authority transfers and headquarters review ordinarily occurs at that three-month point or as soon thereafter as practicable. Approximately thirty days notice accompanies either headquarters review. Scheduling allows unforeseen circumstances or an emergent situation. Subsequent review ordinarily begins approximately annually; a material-change request may be made no more often than every three months and receives a written response approximately within ninety days. Shorter discretionary reviews and documented postponement for prompt proper removal or other good cause remain. Reasonable care must ensure review once the reason for delay is remedied or anticipated removal does not occur. For detainees already reviewed under the prior procedures, headquarters review follows a records-only review within six months, or is scheduled one year after a review that included an interview. Those transition reviews occur within those periods or as soon as possible thereafter, allowing resource limits, unforeseen circumstances or an emergent situation. The Board completes a case pending there on December 21, 2000; if it affirms continued detention, the next scheduled review occurs one year after its decision. These are not absolute deadlines or automatic annual release trials.

Travel-document prospects must be considered; efforts continue, likely or forthcoming documents may support detention, and the rule preserves impracticable or public-interest immediate-removal exceptions. It dispenses with this custody review when removal is ready to execute and denies release for failure or refusal to cooperate. Placement or sponsorship, when ordered, must be arranged; release conditions, discretionary employment authorization and withdrawal of approval before release remain distinct. Revocation may rest on served release purposes, a condition violation, appropriate enforcement or commencement of removal, or conduct or another circumstance making release inappropriate. It requires reasons and a prompt informal opportunity to respond. Further review evaluates contested facts, with an interview ordinarily expected within approximately three months and subsequent annual review under the ordinary review provisions. None of that administrative language supplies unlimited statutory custody authority.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision. These independently proper section 2241 custody proceedings supply the review channel, with certiorari review of their appellate judgments.

##### I. Habeas permits review of current executive custody authority

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 2241 permits these independently proper challenges to the legal authority for detention, including the government's asserted authority to resume custody, without reopening the removal orders. The court must be able to determine that authority and afford effective relief when warranted; the decision does not authorize general factual retrial of removability or reweighing of lawful immigration discretion.

**Controlling explanation.** Calcano preserves an independent habeas action addressing a substantial legal-authority objection when the actual custody, respondent, forum, exhaustion and other predicates are satisfied. St. Cyr applies that rule to an actual independently filed proceeding. These cases challenge custody's lawful basis, and no supported defect in the applicable habeas predicates is identified. They do not seek another merits review of the removal orders or a favorable discretionary waiver. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas apply Calcano as controlling law while retaining their published disagreement with its original construction. The statutory channel resolves review without a comprehensive new Suspension Clause holding.

##### II. Extended detention must remain reasonably necessary for removal

**Holding and operative rule.** For the previously admitted persons presented here, section 1231(a)(6) authorizes detention beyond the removal period only for a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal. When there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, continued custody under that provision is unauthorized; a final removal order, past crime, danger or continuing diplomatic effort alone cannot supply permanent preventive authority detached from removal.

**Controlling explanation.** The statute regulates removal, permits an extension of detention and expressly provides supervised release. Its listed criminal and risk categories identify people who may remain detained beyond the ordinary period while removal remains a genuine prospect; they do not clearly create lifelong preventive imprisonment. Congress's omission of an express endpoint does not make discretionary authority unlimited.

National Center confines delegated immigration discretion to the statutory scheme. Foucha and Jackson require the nature and duration of custody to remain connected to a continuing lawful ground; additional procedure cannot replace that ground. Flores concerns temporary juvenile care with preserved individual challenges, not universal detention authority. Those principles reinforce the serious physical-liberty concern supporting this available statutory construction. The Court does not independently invalidate the statute, impose a universal psychiatric requirement or dangerousness burden, or find these detainees harmless.

The replacement custody-review regulation strengthens the opportunity to contest danger, flight and continued custody. But considering travel prospects within a discretionary safety review differs from requiring release when the detention statute's removal ground ends. Its procedures cannot cure that missing authority, and no facial invalidation of the regulation is necessary to enforce the statutory limit. Hendricks and Seling keep punishment, present authority and effective relief distinct; no punishment finding is required for this statutory relief and none is made. Previously unadmitted entrants and distinct security or civil-commitment regimes fall outside the ordinary statutory custody question resolved here.

##### III. A rebuttable six-month presumption governs the removal-prospect inquiry

**Holding and operative rule.** Six months is a presumptively reasonable period of removal detention, within the removal process rather than added after the ordinary ninety days. After that interval, a detainee who provides good reason to believe there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future requires the government to furnish evidence sufficient to rebut that showing; six months alone neither compels release nor forecloses other properly supported unlawful-custody claims during the interval.

**Controlling explanation.** A uniform rebuttable interval gives the Executive a meaningful initial opportunity to arrange removal while making the statutory limit enforceable. The former six-month removal period supplies a legal reference point, and the current rule's ordinary transition from district review to headquarters after ninety days and up to three additional months supplies an administrative reference point. Neither establishes a statutory ceiling or a mandatory review deadline; the rule preserves extra preparation time, practicable scheduling, shorter review, documented good-cause postponement and its distinct transition provisions. The Court uses six months as a presumption, not as a finding about every removal's duration.

The detainee retains the initial supported showing. The government must then supply a sufficient factual response; the order alone, danger alone, good faith or an indefinite theoretical possibility does not answer it. As detention lengthens, the reasonably foreseeable future becomes correspondingly shorter. Courts consider actual efforts, foreign responses, negotiations, travel documents, receiving arrangements and material detainee conduct, giving appropriate leeway to supported executive judgments, administrative needs and foreign-policy expertise without surrendering the legal-authority inquiry.

No treaty is indispensable, diplomatic uncertainty is not automatically failure, and the detainee need not prove permanent impossibility. Detention may continue after six months until the absence of the required significant likelihood is determined. Where removal remains reasonably foreseeable, crime and flight risks may help justify custody within the reasonable removal interval. Where it does not, the court must afford effective supervised-release relief, subject to the actual posture and an independently lawful custody basis. Paragraph (1)(C)'s distinct obstruction extension requires its own actual predicates; foreign refusal alone does not establish them.

##### IV. Both records require the correct inquiry and effective lawful relief

**Holding and operative rule.** Both appellate judgments must be vacated so the two records receive the required reasonably-foreseeable-removal inquiry, instead of permanent-impossibility or agreement-only proxies. When paragraph (6) no longer authorizes custody and no independent lawful basis applies, effective relief is supervised release; release preserves the removal order and supplies no new admission, immigration status, waiver or immunity from removal.

**Controlling explanation.** The Fifth Circuit's requirement that removal be impossible permits more detention than the statute allows. The Ninth Circuit's reliance on a missing repatriation agreement may overlook a genuine practical prospect. Neither error can be corrected by assuming the facts that the proper inquiry must examine. Zadvydas's effective release and any proposed resumed custody remain distinct; Ma's actual prospects must receive appropriate attention to supported negotiations and elapsed confinement. The Court issues no automatic rearrest command and no new six-month detention entitlement.

Paragraph (3) requires supervision on lawful release: periodic identification, necessary medical or psychiatric examination at government expense, sworn information and reasonable written conduct restrictions. A violation may support return to custody only under actual lawful authority and process. The regulation's revocation grounds and review cannot revive unlimited custody on a rationale the statute no longer supports. Any criminal, civil-commitment or specialized security regime needs its own established authority, predicates and protections; none is invoked or adjudicated here. Questions about terrorism or other special circumstances potentially supporting different preventive authority or heightened deference are outside these ordinary records. The remaining removal-prospect and current-authority determinations are application of the binding standard, not unresolved law or permission to preserve unauthorized custody indefinitely.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Calcano-Martinez v. INS: applies retained independently proper section 2241 review of a substantial authority objection, with actual predicates and effective relief; no broader removal-merits review is added.
- INS v. St. Cyr: applies its use of Calcano in an actual habeas action; its plea-specific temporal holding does not decide detention under former section 242 or every earlier custody episode.
- INS v. National Center for Immigrants' Rights: applies the requirement that delegated immigration discretion remain within its statutory purpose, without converting its release-condition holding into universal detention authority.
- Foucha v. Louisiana: applies the distinction between a continuing lawful custody ground and a procedure reviewing an insufficient ground; its psychiatric setting does not create a universal diagnostic or dangerousness rule here.
- Jackson v. Indiana: applies the required relationship between custody's purpose, nature and duration.
- Reno v. Flores: distinguishes the temporary juvenile-care regime and preserves its individual unlawful-delay and implementation limits.
- Kansas v. Hendricks: preserves its actual as-applied punishment holding and the noncontrolling status of its diagnostic discussion; it supplies no controlling due-process approval of the Kansas scheme or these detentions.
- Seling v. Young: preserves distinct punishment, current-authority, conditions and custody-relief questions, with no inference of punishment from duration alone.

#### Current Law

No earlier precedent is overruled. Paragraph (6) now receives a controlling purpose-bound reasonable-duration construction for the previously admitted class presented, with the five-Justice rebuttable six-month implementation. Independent legality review must provide effective relief when its predicates are met. The statute's actual commencement, obstruction and supervision provisions remain distinct. The decision resolves neither initial-entry custody nor uninvoked security or civil-commitment regimes, and does not create an automatic release or rearrest date.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurrence in part and in the judgments.** Stone agrees with the removal-purpose limit, retained habeas review, both vacaturs and effective supervised relief when authority ends. He does not join a numerical presumption. Once the removal period has ended and the detainee presents concrete evidence of repeated refusals or comparable obstacles, he would require the government promptly to identify facts supporting a reasonably foreseeable removal prospect. The detainee must have a meaningful opportunity to answer that account. Duration, progress, receiving-country decisions, travel-document history and actual obstruction bear on individualized necessity; ongoing negotiations or theoretical possibility alone are insufficient.

His objection does not characterize the Court's rebuttable rule as an absolute safe harbor. He declines to make a calendar presumption part of his rule because custody may lose its purpose before six months, while genuinely imminent removal can justify custody after the ordinary period. He adopts neither automatic day-ninety-one release nor a mandatory six-month ceiling. Danger may inform custody within a live removal process but cannot preserve that source after its purpose fails. Other preventive detention requires its own authority and safeguards. His different trigger is noncontrolling; the Court's five directly adopt Part III.

**Kennedy, dissenting, Part I, joined by Scalia and Thomas.** The dissenters read paragraph (6) to authorize discretionary detention of the covered criminal and risk categories without a judicial foreseeable-removal condition. Congress expressly permitted custody beyond the ordinary period and selected categories serving protective as well as removal purposes, yet supplied no endpoint. Foreign refusal, absence of an agreement or inability to forecast a removal date therefore does not alone terminate authority. The common detention phrase also reaches inadmissible and removable categories, which weighs against implying different meanings, although these cases do not adjudicate every initial-entry setting. They reject both the statutory limitation and the numerical implementation, while applying the governing habeas channel and preserving actual regulatory requirements. Their dispositions would affirm the Fifth Circuit against this general challenge and reverse and remand the Ninth Circuit; neither automatically orders renewed custody.

**Kennedy, dissenting, Part II, for himself.** Kennedy recognizes protection against arbitrary or capricious detention and unlawful punishment for both removable and inadmissible people. Genuine danger or flight may sustain detention even if removal cannot be forecast. The replacement regulation supplies meaningful ways to contest continued custody: notice, reasons, records and interview review, headquarters responsibility and later review. Those requirements matter without establishing compliance in either case. Denial of prescribed process in an arbitrary or categorical manner is subject to habeas challenge.

He would reject this presented general duration challenge, without a new affirmative-current-justification holding or two new constitutional merits remands. A proper concrete case may require deciding the severity of danger needed, whether an actual danger or flight finding is unjustified, the adequacy of its review, or the operation of the burden. Neither the present alien-satisfaction language nor the availability of supervision alone proves such a defect. He imposes no universal hearing calendar, new government burden or automatic adversarial release trial. The distinction between general authority and an unlawful particular application preserves those questions without inventing unfinished claims in these proceedings.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissenting.** They reject the claimed general constitutional entitlement to supervised release into this country merely because removal after a valid final order is not foreseeable. Their Flores and Foucha positions make the precisely asserted right, adjudicated status and authorized custody channel material. Their use of Mezei as an analogy concerns that asserted right; it is not a finding that these people were never admitted. They accept the enacted discretionary channel and the opportunity for release review but do not adopt Kennedy's separate procedural-liberty explanation or a new affirmative-current-justification rule. Their general-challenge disposition supplies no indefinite remand to develop another theory. It does not authorize torture, unlawful punishment, sham authority or denial of a legally required procedure; no such particular violation is found, and no unpresented remedy is prescribed.
