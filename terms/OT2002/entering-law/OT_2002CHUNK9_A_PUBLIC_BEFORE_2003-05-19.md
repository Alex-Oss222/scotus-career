## 2002-11-04 — Early v. Packer; No. 01-1765.

Canonical source: `terms/OT2002/records/Early-v-Packer_summary-merits_2002-11-04.md`.

## Event

Early v. Packer, No. 01-1765. Summary merits decision, November 4, 2002, following the same-day grant of certiorari. On review of the Ninth Circuit judgment directing habeas relief on Packer’s jury-coercion claim. The questions concern a state court’s failure to cite Supreme Court cases and its rejection of coercion after the trial judge’s communications with a divided jury. Juror Radcliff sought release because of exhaustion or health concerns. The judge encouraged continued service, read the foreman’s criticism of her deliberation, learned of an 11-to-1 split without its direction, required deliberation and acknowledged the right to disagree. Further deliberation preceded the later verdicts.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit’s judgment directing a writ on the jury-coercion claim is summarily reversed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela.

The direction to grant a writ on this coercion claim is removed. The lower federal court must proceed consistently with that determination.

## Opinion Topology

Per curiam. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by no other Justice.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court opinion, Part I | Per curiam | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Governing-law agreement | Citation practice, contextual constitutional inquiry and common statutory limits |
| Court opinion, Part II | Per curiam | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Supports reversal | Actual reasonable application and disposition |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Agrees with Part I; opposes reversal | Cumulative coercion, unreasonable application and conditional relief |

## Holdings

### The governing rule turns on substance and the entire sequence

**Holding and operative rule:** A state court need not cite Supreme Court decisions for its adjudication to comply with clearly established federal law; its actual rule, reasoning and result control. Jury coercion is assessed from all surrounding circumstances, and federal supervisory rules do not independently establish a broader constitutional command for state trials.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Williams v. Taylor gives the contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses independent work. Clearly established law consists of this Court’s holdings at the relevant state merits decision; a general rule can govern without an earlier case having identical facts. Objective unreasonableness is more than disagreement, and no clear-and-convincing burden on legal error comes from the separate factual-review provisions. Lowenfield v. Phelps requires the complete course of deliberations and judicial intervention to be examined. Jenkins v. United States and United States v. United States Gypsum Co. address federal supervisory settings; their directions cannot simply be transferred into constitutional commands for state judges. Neither silence about case names nor a correct citation substitutes for examining the actual adjudication.

### The state adjudication satisfies that standard here

**Holding and operative rule:** On this complete sequence, the state court’s rejection of coercion is a reasonable application of the constitutional rule. Section 2254(d)(1) therefore precludes the writ direction on this claim.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela dissents from this application and disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The foreman’s accusation and the juror’s health concerns made the pressure serious, and the Court considers them together with the judge’s other communications. But the judge required continued deliberation, preserved the juror’s right to disagree, did not direct a particular verdict and did not know which way the numerical division ran. Deliberations continued afterward. Those combined circumstances permit the state conclusion even though a federal judge might assess the episode differently. A reassurance about independent judgment is not a safe harbor; nor does learning a split alone establish coercion or make pressure lawful. The question is whether the whole state assessment falls outside the permissible range, and it does not. The statutory threshold is thus unsatisfied, resolving this basis for habeas relief.

## Precedent Treatment

- Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362: applies its actual common rules on independent statutory clauses, holding-date limits and objective legal review. Neither competing exclusive allocation of the clauses gains controlling force here.
- Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231: applies its surrounding-circumstances inquiry to ordinary guilt deliberations; the rule is not reduced to any single reassuring phrase.
- Jenkins v. United States, 380 U.S. 445: preserves the distinction between federal supervisory correction and clearly established constitutional law for state trials.
- United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 438 U.S. 422: likewise preserves that supervisory/constitutional boundary without denying the relevance of the entire judicial course of conduct.

## Law After Decision

State-court citation omissions do not independently satisfy §2254(d)(1). Actual reasoning and the complete constitutional context remain reviewable; a general constitutional rule needs no factual twin. This jury-coercion claim fails the statutory threshold, without creating permission to pressure a holdout or a numerical-split safe harbor.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela agrees with the governing legal rules but dissents from their application. He regards the judge’s public repetition of the majority’s criticism as giving that criticism judicial force. Coupled with individualized pressure on an exhausted juror and disclosure of the 11-to-1 division, the intervention communicated a demand to yield even without naming the desired verdict. Permission to disagree could not neutralize that combined pressure.

Applying Lowenfield’s contextual rule, he finds actual coercion and separately concludes under Williams that the state assessment was objectively unreasonable, not merely less persuasive. He rejects both a constitutional citation requirement and reliance on Jenkins or Gypsum as independent state constitutional commands. His ground is the sustained exercise of judicial authority in this record, not a categorical ban on supplemental instructions, numerical knowledge or legitimate health inquiries. He would preserve a conditional writ allowing California a reasonable period to retry only the counts whose verdicts followed the coercive intervention, leaving earlier sealed verdicts and acquittals untouched.

## Procedure After Action

The summary reversal ends this Supreme Court review. The lower federal court proceeds with the jury-coercion writ direction removed.

## Source Notes

No. 01-1765; 28 U.S.C. §2254(d)(1); Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231. The jury’s numerical division was disclosed without identifying whether the eleven favored conviction or acquittal.

## 2002-11-04 — Woodford v. Visciotti; No. 02-137.

Canonical source: `terms/OT2002/records/Woodford-v-Visciotti_summary-merits_2002-11-04.md`.

## Event

Woodford v. Visciotti, No. 02-137. Summary merits decision, November 4, 2002, following the same-day grant of certiorari. On review of the Ninth Circuit judgment affirming penalty-phase habeas relief. The California Supreme Court’s 1996 habeas decision assumed deficient performance but found no reasonable probability of a different sentence. The questions are whether its occasional use of probable reflected an impermissible standard and whether its actual treatment of the omitted mitigation was objectively unreasonable. The record includes an abusive and unstable childhood, depression, neurological and learning difficulties, some guilt-phase brain-injury evidence, counsel’s damaging concessions, a robbery killing, attempted killing and earlier violent attacks.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Ninth Circuit’s penalty-phase habeas judgment is summarily reversed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela.

The sentence writ cannot stand on this ineffective-assistance claim. The convictions are unaffected.

## Opinion Topology

Per curiam. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by no other Justice.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Court opinion, Part I | Per curiam | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Governing-rule agreement | Whole-opinion reading and complete supported mitigation review |
| Court opinion, Part II | Per curiam | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer | Supports reversal | Reasonableness of this state assessment and resulting writ reversal |
| Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Agrees with Part I; opposes reversal | Unreasonable application, constitutional prejudice and conditional sentence relief |

## Holdings

### Read the whole opinion and assess the whole supported record

**Holding and operative rule:** Occasional shorthand does not establish a higher prejudice burden when the state opinion as a whole states and uses Strickland’s reasonable-probability standard. Courts must nevertheless examine the actual assessment of the complete supported mitigation, aggravation and counsel’s presentation; reciting the evidence or the correct formula alone cannot establish a reasonable application.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion, joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Strickland v. Washington requires a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the lawful outcome, not proof that a different result was more likely than not. The California opinion repeatedly states the reasonable-probability and confidence formulation. An abbreviated use of probable must be read in that context, not isolated as an alternative burden. Williams v. Taylor requires distinct contrary-to and unreasonable-application inquiries and substantive examination of the full sentencing record. Its common rules do not permit a factual clear-and-convincing burden to be imported into legal review. Nor does a correct quotation immunize an indefensible application. Strickland supplies the constitutional rule established when the State decided the claim in 1996; Williams supplies the governing interpretation of §2254. Deficiency remains assumed for this prejudice inquiry.

### The actual no-prejudice determination is reasonably supportable

**Holding and operative rule:** The state court’s substantive no-prejudice determination on this developed record falls within Strickland’s reasonable range. Neither a contrary governing rule nor an objectively unreasonable application is established under §2254(d)(1), so the sentence writ on this claim must be reversed.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela dissents from this application and remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The omitted family and neurological account has genuine mitigating force, and counsel’s concessions must be considered with its absence. The state adjudication nevertheless addressed that account in substance, and some brain-injury evidence had already reached the jury. The robbery killing, repeated shooting of the surviving victim and prior violent attacks supplied substantial aggravation. Considering that complete record, a reasonable adjudicator could find the additional mitigation insufficient to undermine confidence in the sentence.

The claim that a coherent mitigating presentation could alter a conscientious juror’s choice is substantial; the state decision did not make childhood or neurological evidence legally irrelevant merely because the crimes were grave. This separates the case from the material truncation and additional Fretwell requirement condemned in Williams. The jury’s deliberation time and questions do not themselves establish the necessary probability. There is no automatic no-prejudice rule for aggravated murder or automatic prejudice rule for omitted childhood evidence. The conclusion is the narrower statutory one: the actual state assessment is reasonably supportable, so federal disagreement cannot sustain this writ.

## Precedent Treatment

- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668: applies its reasonable-probability/confidence standard to the full supported penalty record; deficient performance remains assumed and is not newly adjudicated.
- Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362: applies its actual common statutory rules and complete-record requirement, while distinguishing its materially truncated mitigation assessment and unlawful additional prejudice demand. Its Virginia-specific unanimity and remedy rule does not govern this California proceeding.
- Lockhart v. Fretwell: preserves the distinction between a lost benefit of a rejected legal rule and ordinary prejudice from impaired presentation of lawful mitigation; the additional unfairness requirement rejected in Williams is not imposed here.

## Law After Decision

Section 2254 requires whole-opinion reading and real evaluation of the supported sentencing record. Neither imperfect shorthand nor nominally correct wording determines the result. This state no-prejudice assessment remains within the lawful range, leaving the sentence writ unavailable on this claim while preserving the full significance of mitigation in cases where the actual assessment is unreasonable.

## Separate Writings

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela joins the governing rules but dissents from the result. He agrees that occasional use of probable does not independently establish a contrary-to violation. His objection concerns what the state court actually did with the mitigating account. The missing coherent history of abuse, instability and functional impairment, combined with counsel’s damaging presentation, could affect a conscientious juror’s sentencing judgment even though it did not excuse the crimes. Scattered guilt-phase brain-injury evidence did not necessarily perform that explanatory work.

He gives the killing, attempted killing and prior violence their full aggravating force, but concludes that the combined impairment of the mitigation presentation undermines confidence in the sentence. In his view, treating the grave offenses as a sufficient answer to that impairment materially understates lawful mitigation and makes the state application objectively unreasonable under Williams as well as prejudicial under Strickland. The deliberations and questions reinforce the materiality of the missing account without proving any juror’s hypothetical choice.

He would preserve conditional sentence relief, allowing a reasonable period for constitutionally adequate resentencing or a lawful noncapital disposition. Any properly preserved unresolved defense that counsel performed adequately must be decided before implementing a writ dependent on deficiency. The convictions remain intact. He adopts no categorical prejudice rule for abuse, brain injury, lengthy deliberation or capital cases.

## Procedure After Action

The lower federal court must proceed consistently with reversal of the penalty-phase writ on this ineffective-assistance claim. The state convictions remain intact.

## Source Notes

No. 02-137; 28 U.S.C. §2254(d)(1); Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668. The pertinent state habeas adjudication occurred in 1996 and assumed deficient performance for its prejudice analysis.

## 2003-01-13 — Montiel v. Davis; district-court No. 01-0447-BH-S; October Term 2002.

Canonical source: `terms/OT2002/records/Montiel-v-Davis_merits_2003-01-13.md`.

## Event

Montiel v. Davis. Direct-appeal merits decision, January 13, 2003. On appeal under 28 U.S.C. §1253 from the three-judge District Court for the Southern District of Alabama, No. 01-0447-BH-S, which granted summary judgment for defendants on July 8, 2002, 215 F. Supp. 2d 1279. The questions concern population equality and racial electoral equality in Alabama's 2001 Senate and House plans. The congressional and education-board claims were severed and are outside this appeal.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the reviewed legislative judgment.

## Judgment & Remedy

Affirmed, 9–0. Supporting: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. The denial of relief on the population, constitutional racial, and statutory legislative claims stands within the plaintiffs' own electoral exposure. The requested invalidation and replacement of the legislative plans are denied. The severed congressional and education-board proceedings are unaffected.

## Opinion Topology

Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court. The complete-opinion coalition is Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer; Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer join the complete opinion. O'Connor and Kennedy additionally join Parts I and III. Scalia and Thomas additionally join Part I. Thomas filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment, joined by Scalia.

| Writing or division | Author | Justices adopting the reasoning | Scope and relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, Part I | Stone-Zsela | All nine Justices | Population equality; correct purpose rule; independently sufficient constitutional evidentiary disposition. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part II | Stone-Zsela | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Additional constitutional territorial-design ground. |
| Opinion of the Court, Part III | Stone-Zsela | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Independently sufficient statutory evidentiary ground. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Thomas | Scalia | Joins Part I; affirms the statutory judgment on noncoverage, without joining Parts II or III. |

## Holdings

### Population equality and prohibited purpose

**Holding and operative rule:** A state legislative maximum population deviation below ten percent does not alone establish a prima facie violation, but remains challengeable through proof of arbitrary or discriminatory weighting. Where the governing claim turns on discriminatory purpose, that purpose need only be a substantial or motivating factor; once that predicate is proved, the government must prove by a preponderance that materially the same decision would have occurred without it.

**Authority:** Part I, adopted by all nine Justices, directly controls.

**Controlling explanation:** Voinovich applies substantial equality without making smaller deviations immune; Reynolds and Brown reject a demand for mathematical exactness. Maximum deviation adds the greatest departure above and below the ideal district population. The Senate's 9.78% and House's 9.93% figures describe that plan-wide range, not a separate allowance for each voter. Below the threshold, plaintiffs must connect their evidence to unlawful weighting. County splits, demographics, and more equal alternatives can be relevant, but do not prove that connection merely by existing. The reported submissions add no material evidence establishing the claimed arbitrary or discriminatory operation. The lower court's suggestion that an improper purpose must be exclusive is incorrect under Arlington Heights, Brooks, and Page. That error does not require remand because the independent evidentiary deficiency remains under the correct rule, and no excluded material proof is identified. Political explanations receive no categorical immunity; they are assessed against the evidence. The challenged judgment stands on the absence of the necessary showing.

### Territorial design and independent constitutional injury

**Holding and operative rule:** Racial consideration or predominance in drawing a shared territorial district does not alone establish an equal-protection deprivation. An independently asserted exclusion, intentional impairment of electoral opportunity, material restriction on participation, or inferior civic status must satisfy its own elements; voters of every race receive the same protection.

**Authority:** Part II, adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, supplies an independently sufficient majority ground for the constitutional racial judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Shaw, Johnson v. Miller, Easley, and Robertson distinguish the composition of a common electorate from government action that impairs a protected civic interest. A design-only claim identifies motive, shape, racial composition, or district assignment without establishing that independent operation. Intentional dilution requires discriminatory purpose, actual material impairment of electoral opportunity, and causation. Unequal participation requires a real impediment to political activity, not merely placement in a less favorable district. Such an injury need not await a second governmental act or an electoral defeat. Here the population figures, boundaries, and suppositions establish neither the asserted unlawful weighting nor another concrete impairment. Race's correlation with party affiliation can be evidence; a partisan label neither proves a racial wrong nor excuses one proved. The asserted white-voter claim receives the same legal protection as any other racial claim, but does not acquire a different injury rule. This constitutional ground creates no additional prerequisite for an independent Voting Rights Act claim.

### Independent statutory proof

**Holding and operative rule:** A §2 district-dilution claim requires the relevant cohesion and opposing-bloc causal showing and unequal electoral opportunity under the statute; racial population figures and the availability of another map do not establish those requirements by themselves. The statutory claim here fails on its independent evidentiary deficiency, regardless of the disposition of the constitutional design theory.

**Authority:** Part III, adopted by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer, controls by seven direct votes. Scalia and Thomas support the statutory judgment on another ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Gingles identifies the causal voting conditions, Voinovich places the initial burden on challengers, and De Grandy requires an independent examination of actual opportunity without maximizing districts. Section 2 protects citizens of every race and uses a results inquiry distinct from constitutional purpose. The population and boundary evidence does not establish the requisite cohesion, opposing-bloc mechanism, and unequal opportunity. Current law permits one protected group to establish capacity in a lawful, geographically compact district either through a numerical majority or rigorous proof of dependable crossover support sufficient to elect its preferred candidates. Those routes remain available, but no renewed capacity ruling answers this separate deficiency. Balderas preserves a genuinely independent sufficient lawful ground rather than ordering remand for an immaterial error in another part of the analysis. Preclearance does not adjudicate these independent statutory or constitutional claims. The Court rejects the requested statutory relief on the record presented.

## Precedent Treatment

- Voinovich v. Quilter: applied to population burdens and independent statutory proof; deviations below ten percent remain challengeable.
- Reynolds v. Sims: applied to substantial legislative population equality; mathematical exactness is not required.
- Brown v. Thomson: applied to the rebuttable significance of smaller deviations; those deviations receive no categorical immunity.
- Arlington Heights: applied to prohibited purpose as a substantial or motivating factor; purpose need not be exclusive.
- Mt. Healthy: preserves the government's burden to prove materially the same decision without the prohibited purpose once the necessary predicate is established.
- Brooks v. Barnes: applies that claim-specific motivating-factor and same-enactment sequence to electoral claims; the sequence remains intact.
- Page v. Bartels: applied to the correct purpose rule and independent failure of constitutional proof; electoral success is not a universal answer to participation injury.
- Shaw v. Reno: its territorial-design boundary remains controlling; independent constitutional and statutory claims retain their own elements.
- Johnson v. Miller: applies that boundary even to predominant racial design; government-imposed participation or status injury need not await another act or loss.
- Easley v. Cromartie: preserves the distinction between design alone and a properly supported independent injury.
- Robertson v. Bartels: applies the same protection to voters of every race; an incumbent-prospect label does not itself establish a distinct civic deprivation.
- Thornburg v. Gingles: applied to the independently necessary causal voting conditions; population arithmetic does not replace proof.
- Johnson v. De Grandy: preserves independent totality without maximization or a proportionality safe harbor.
- Valdespino: its numerical-majority and dependable-crossover capacity routes remain intact; no new capacity determination is needed here.
- Balderas v. Texas: preserves a genuinely independent sufficient lawful ground; no remand follows from an immaterial error elsewhere in the analysis.

## Law After Decision

Smaller legislative deviations remain open to supported constitutional challenge. A prohibited purpose need not be exclusive, and independent constitutional injuries retain their own elements. The statutory opportunity inquiry remains separate. This record-specific affirmance creates no numerical safe harbor, partisan immunity, racial allocation, or different protection for white voters.

## Separate Writings

Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurs in Part I and the judgment. Consistently with the statutory construction he maintained in Holder, De Grandy, Page, and Balderas, he concludes that §2 does not authorize the asserted judicial allocation of districts. That antecedent construction supplies his reason to affirm the statutory judgment; it is not a join in the Court's Gingles and totality analysis. He also withholds assent from Part II's territorial-design rule. His agreement with the independently sufficient constitutional evidentiary disposition does not adopt that broader rule.

## Procedure After Action

The legislative summary judgment stands and this appeal is concluded. The severed proceedings retain their separate posture.

## 2003-01-14 — Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania; No. 01-7574.

Canonical source: `terms/OT2002/records/Sattazahn-v-Pennsylvania_merits_2003-01-14.md`.

## Event

Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania, No. 01-7574. Argued November 4, 2002; decided January 14, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court, reviewing its judgment affirming the death sentence after the second murder trial. The March 18, 2002 grant encompasses double jeopardy and due process. At the first trial Pennsylvania sought death, the penalty jury reported a hopeless nine-to-three division for life, and state law required a life sentence upon discharge. Sattazahn obtained reversal of the conviction for trial error. The State again sought death at retrial, including an additional aggravator, and obtained it. The questions concern renewed death exposure, not the legality of retrying guilt.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment is reversed insofar as it sustains the death sentence, the death sentence is vacated, and the case is remanded for lawful life sentencing.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed as to death, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The death sentence is vacated. The case is remanded for a lawful life sentence; the second murder conviction remains intact and no new capital penalty hearing is authorized.

## Opinion Topology

Stevens delivers the opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Stone-Zsela files a concurrence. O'Connor files a dissent, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Scalia files an additional dissent, joined by Thomas.

| Writing | Author | Direct supporters, including author | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Final-life double jeopardy and sentence-only remedy |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | Stone-Zsela | Additional due-process ground |
| Common dissent | O'Connor | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | No acquittal; rejection of independent due process; affirmance |
| Additional dissent | Scalia | Scalia, Thomas | Limited element explanation; no join from O'Connor or Kennedy |

## Holdings

### 1. A final mandatory life judgment ends the completed capital opportunity

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State completes a trial-like capital penalty proceeding and its law conclusively ends the jury's unresolved deliberations with a mandatory final life judgment, that disposition terminates the State's opportunity to obtain death for the offense. A successful appeal of guilt permits a new guilt trial but does not restore the extinguished capital exposure; this rule does not govern a penalty mistrial or an expressly nonfinal order lawfully leaving the capital question open.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer directly adopt this rule and sentence-only remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court extends Bullington's capital-finality protection to this mandatory final disposition. Bullington and Rumsey protected capital acquittals; the present jury made no unanimous finding rejecting an aggravator. The decisive event is instead the life judgment Pennsylvania required after the State completed its capital opportunity. The nine-to-three division is not a verdict.

The contrary position would make an acquittal on the merits indispensable and treat life as a default attached only to the first conviction. That account overlooks the different legal choices between an unresolved penalty mistrial and this statute's completed life judgment. Richardson permits renewed proceedings after an ordinary deadlock, but Pennsylvania concluded this penalty proceeding with a final punishment rather than leaving it pending. Poland involved no intervening life judgment ending the capital opportunity. Stroud's permission for a higher sentence after reversal does not govern this separate completed capital determination.

Jones gives a completed federal penalty deadlock a nondeath consequence and distinguishes a new hearing for penalty error; its statute does not itself decide this constitutional question. Monge's bounded noncapital recidivist-status rule remains unchanged. Ring and Harris regulate punishment-authorizing factfinding; they neither manufacture an acquittal nor answer finality. The State may retry guilt after trial error, but correcting that error does not entitle it to repeat the capital opportunity its completed process ended. The Court's sufficient double-jeopardy ground requires vacation of death and lawful life sentencing while preserving the second murder conviction.

## Precedent Treatment

- Bullington v. Missouri: extends capital-finality protection beyond an actual acquittal to the defined completed statutory-life judgment; the extension is made here.
- Arizona v. Rumsey: preserves protection of a genuine capital acquittal even when its legal premise is mistaken; no acquittal finding is attributed to this jury.
- Richardson v. United States: distinguishes ordinary unresolved deadlock; its retrial permission remains for a proceeding that has not ended in the final disposition held controlling here.
- Poland v. Arizona: distinguishes a capital proceeding with no intervening final life judgment; rejection of a particular aggravator remains distinct from acquittal of death.
- Stroud v. United States: confines its higher-sentence-after-reversal permission to circumstances lacking this separate completed capital determination.
- Monge v. California: preserves the noncapital serious-prior classification and renewed-proof rule without extending it to the capital finality question.
- Jones v. United States (federal capital sentencing): preserves the statutory nondeath consequence and separate penalty-error hearing rule; it supports the distinction but does not itself establish today's constitutional extension.
- Apprendi v. New Jersey; United States v. Angle; Harris v. United States; Ring v. Arizona: retain their present-offense element and factfinder rules and actual reservations; they supply no finding of acquittal or universal prior-conviction exception.
- Hicks v. Oklahoma: its protection of an actual state-created sentencing entitlement remains; the independent due-process ground receives no controlling resolution here.

## Law After Decision

Capital finality now protects the defined mandatory final life judgment after a completed capital opportunity, although the penalty jury made no unanimous acquittal finding. Ordinary guilt retrial remains available. The Court does not adopt a general finality rule for every sentence or change Monge's noncapital holding. The independent due-process theory lacks a majority: four majority Justices find it unnecessary, Stone-Zsela adopts it separately, and the dissenters reject it. No combination of opposing rationales supplies a controlling due-process or element-identity rule.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the double-jeopardy holding and would independently reach the same sentence-only result under due process. Pennsylvania made life the conclusive consequence of its completed unsuccessful capital opportunity. Requiring surrender of that final limit to correct guilt-phase error burdens meaningful appellate review and upsets the adjudicated liberty interest without a new lawful basis. Guilt retrial and life punishment remain available; the appeal alone cannot recreate capital exposure the completed process ended. Hicks supplies protection for an actual sentencing entitlement, while this application develops its operation in the distinct final-life setting. The position does not presume prosecutorial vindictiveness from a harsher sentence, preserve every favorable sentencing term after reversal, or convert the jury's division into an acquittal. The other four majority Justices rest on the sufficient double-jeopardy ground and do not join this independent theory.

**O'Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissenting.** They would affirm rejection of both federal challenges. Bullington and Rumsey protect an adjudication that death is unavailable on the merits. Here neither the jury nor the judge found the State's aggravation proof insufficient or selected life on the merits. The statutory consequence of deadlock therefore supplies no federal acquittal finality once the defendant obtains reversal of the underlying conviction for trial error. Poland preserves that distinction, and Richardson permits retrial of unresolved allegations. The State must still obtain the lawful findings and comply with the procedures governing the new capital proceeding. Hicks protects the entitlement state law actually confers, but the supplied law establishes no life ceiling independently surviving reversal of the conviction. Their due-process rejection concerns that asserted entitlement, not every possible state-created sentencing interest. O'Connor and Kennedy do not adopt the separate element-based explanation below.

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, additionally dissenting.** Even if a necessary present-offense aggravator performs the constitutional work of an element, a hung jury has not rejected it. Their Monge position concerns a completed failure of proof in an element adjudication; Apprendi, Angle, Harris and Ring supply present-offense factfinding protections without treating deadlock as acquittal. They therefore agree with the common dissent and explain why its result survives this limited element analysis. They adopt no universal prior-conviction exception, proposition that every aggravator is a separate offense, or rule that eligibility requires selection of death. O'Connor and Kennedy join no part of this additional writing.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Pennsylvania Supreme Court for lawful life sentencing consistent with the finality of the first capital disposition. The retrial conviction remains effective. This Supreme Court merits review is complete.

## 2003-04-22 — Women's Medical Center of Northwest Houston v. Bell; 248 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2001); No. 00-20037 below.

## Event

Women's Medical Center of Northwest Houston v. Bell. Merits decision, April 22, 2003. On review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit's interlocutory preliminary-injunction judgment, 248 F.3d 411, No. 00-20037.

The reviewed questions concern the equal-protection ground for enjoining Texas's annual-volume abortion-facility licensing threshold and the definiteness of three sanction-bearing regulations. The 1999 amendments cover offices performing more than 300 abortions in twelve months, while exempting those performing 300 or fewer. They replaced a primarily-used-for-abortions measure based on the majority of patients. The Fifth Circuit dissolved the injunction against that licensing classification, preserved preliminary relief against 25 Tex. Admin. Code §§139.51(1), 139.51(2) and 139.2(43), and remanded. Providers seek protection against the expanded obligation and challenged standards; officials seek enforcement. The providers did not appeal denial of preliminary relief on their separate access theory.

## Holdings

### I. The licensing-volume classification satisfies ordinary rational-basis review

**Holding and operative rule:** On the ordinary equal-protection challenge presented, Texas may require licensing of offices performing more than 300 abortions in twelve months while exempting offices performing 300 or fewer because the distinction rationally connects regular procedure volume to patient safety and the administration of oversight. The numerical line need not correspond to a sudden change in medical risk at procedure 301 or prior misconduct at every newly covered office.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Beach Communications permits reasonable, imperfect economic lines without formal legislative findings or proof that the selected number is optimal. The challengers must negate a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis. Here the activity being regulated has a direct connection to inspection, staffing and infection control; sustained volume provides a rational administrative basis for directing oversight while reducing regulatory burdens on smaller practices. Craigmiles preserves that public-interest inquiry without requiring an optimally tailored occupational scheme.

The comparison with substantially busier clinics, lack of prior substandard care and political compromise behind the threshold do not negate this actual connection. The absence of a medical discontinuity therefore cannot sustain the likelihood-of-success determination underlying this part of the injunction. Whether a particular operational burden impairs protected access presents a distinct claim and standard, addressed by Part III.

**Precedent treatment:** Beach Communications is applied to a numerical medical-practice licensing line. Craigmiles is applied to the relationship between regulatory duties and a legitimate public end. Moreno and Cleburne retain their prohibition of irrational or impermissibly motivated classifications.

### II. The three subjective sanctioning standards warrant preliminary restraint

**Holding and operative rule:** A sanction-bearing professional regulation must provide an ascertainable compliance standard and meaningful limits on enforcement. Sections 139.51(1), 139.51(2) and 139.2(43), as presented, fail that requirement by measuring compliance through enhancement of individual dignity, respect, self-esteem and self-worth, or satisfaction of each patient's expectations, without an objective measure or authoritative limiting construction; the supported findings on all preliminary-relief requirements sustain restraint against their enforcement.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** Grayned requires fair notice and workable enforcement boundaries. Hoffman Estates recognizes that professional context can supply clarity while requiring attention to the seriousness of sanctions. These provisions carry substantial civil fines and license revocation. The separate misdemeanor of operating without a required license does not change the character of those regulatory sanctions.

Professional care can be competent, truthful and appropriate while disappointing a patient or leaving her distressed. Those reactions supply no objective measure distinguishing compliance from violation. The drafting concession confirms the textual defect rather than creating it. The possibility of future administrative clarification is not an authoritative construction with present binding effect. A concrete, lawful clarification may receive examination when presented.

The providers show likelihood of success, and the preserved irreparable-injury, balance-of-harms and public-interest findings satisfy the remaining preliminary requirements. Severability is unchallenged. Leaving objective consent, confidentiality, competence and safety obligations distinct reduces the competing regulatory cost of the limited restraint. Legal conclusions receive independent review, factual findings clear-error review and the ultimate interim order abuse-of-discretion review.

**Precedent treatment:** Grayned and Hoffman Estates are applied to these subjective professional commands and their actual civil sanctions; contextual professional regulation remains permissible when it supplies an ascertainable standard.

### III. The unappealed access ruling retains its separate posture

**Holding and operative rule:** This appeal resolves the stated classification and vagueness grounds for preliminary relief; the unappealed denial of interim relief on the access theory remains undisturbed. A separately pending access claim must be adjudicated under its governing law and actual evidence, because rationality of the provider classification does not resolve the distinct constitutional effect of operational burdens.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court; this holding preserves the separate claim's law and procedural posture.

**Controlling explanation:** The providers did not appeal the access-based interim denial. Recasting Hansen's proposed withdrawal as a reason to reopen that ruling would enlarge this appeal. Conversely, the reasonable safety basis supporting the licensing category cannot substitute for the legal standard governing an access claim properly before the district court. Casey and the particular holdings of Owens and Newman continue to govern within their scopes. A health label or absence of established animus does not itself answer that inquiry.

Hansen's account may bear on practical access, but available capacity, delay, cost and clinical suitability of alternative care require their own proof. The district court must identify which claims remain properly pending and apply their governing standards in ordinary merits proceedings. Those proceedings concern facts and application under existing law; they do not convert this preliminary judgment into a final access adjudication.

**Precedent treatment:** Casey, Owens and Newman retain their distinct access standards and operative scopes. The ordinary classification ruling does not displace them.

## Precedent Treatment

Beach Communications permits the reasonable numerical licensing category, with Moreno and Cleburne's legitimate-purpose limits intact. Craigmiles supplies attention to a real public-interest connection without an optimal-fit requirement. Grayned and Hoffman Estates govern fair notice and enforcement limits in the actual professional and sanctions context. Casey's complete framework and Owens's necessary-care and Newman's established-added-encounter holdings continue to govern a properly pending access claim.

## Law After Decision

The annual-volume classification survives the reviewed ordinary equal-protection challenge. The three specified subjective regulations remain subject to preliminary restraint, while separable objective licensing requirements may operate. Protected-access claims retain their distinct governing law and must rest on the actual operation and evidence of the requirement challenged. Further merits proceedings preserve the difference between an interim likelihood determination and final adjudication.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** The Chief Justice joins the Court's classification, vagueness and procedural holdings. For the common standardless sanctioning defect, he would presumptively protect persons governed by the same demonstrated threat unless officials establish a narrower complete interim cure, subject to the actual parties, standing and supported preliminary findings. The decree should identify the three provisions and prohibit their use as grounds for penalties or licensing sanctions while separable objective rules remain enforceable. Officials may propose lawful clarification through the proper process; its actual binding effect must be tested before relief is dissolved. An assurance of future guidance does not cure present exposure.

He also explains the inquiry for any access claim properly pending at the later merits stage. Evidence must connect a particular requirement to delayed clinically necessary care or another concrete added burden. Under Owens, alternatives must actually provide timely suitable care, and protection cannot be reduced to imminent death. Hansen's account of medically indicated treatment makes that clinical fit material. Newman requires justification of a proved additional encounter and an answer to concrete adequate alternatives; ordinary prospective proof requires neither completed loss of care nor a universal causal percentage.

Casey's full framework governs: determine the actual duty and purpose, added interference and affected population; reject baseline violations, obstructive purpose or substantial obstacles; require compelling justification and narrow tailoring for remaining severe interference, and substantiated justification, fit and an answer to practicable substantially less burdensome alternatives for remaining meaningful interference; protect clinical judgment and truthful communication; and fit relief to the proved requirement and impairment. This conditional merits analysis preserves the unappealed interim ruling and requires the missing access facts to be established in their proper proceedings.
