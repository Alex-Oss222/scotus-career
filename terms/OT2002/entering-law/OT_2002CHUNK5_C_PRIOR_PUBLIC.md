### Henderson v. Norris

Merits decision, May 13, 2002

#### Chronology and Posture

On full merits submission. Review of the Eighth Circuit judgment, 258 F.3d 706, entered July 9, 2001 in No. 00-2203, reversing the denial of federal habeas relief and directing a conditional sentencing writ. The State seeks restoration of the habeas denial. The question is whether life imprisonment with no ordinary parole unless executive commutation first reduces the sentence to years is grossly disproportionate for this first conviction of delivering 0.238 grams of cocaine base, under the governing pre-AEDPA and nonretroactivity rules.

The completed twenty-dollar sale occurred April 23, 1993. Trial began August 29, 1994, and the Arkansas Supreme Court affirmed on November 13, 1995. Henderson's federal petition preceded April 24, 1996. The record includes evidence of two additional rocks of cocaine and an effort to prevent a prosecution witness from testifying by providing transportation and expenses to travel to another State. The Eighth Circuit reports that the sentencing jury was not instructed to use the additional acts in fixing punishment and declined to presume that witness interference supplied the sentence's basis. That is the appellate court's recitation of the charge, not a separate finding that the conduct did not occur.

The sentence is Arkansas life imprisonment, with ordinary parole available only if discretionary executive commutation first reduces it to years. It is distinct from the separately named capital-murder and treason sentence that precludes parole even after commutation. Clemency provides no assured release date. The comparison record concerns Arkansas maximum authorized penalties, later advisory guidelines subject to the statutory minimum and aggravating departures, and reported penalties elsewhere; authorization of a maximum is distinct from actual imposition.

All nine Justices participate at full merits submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Eighth Circuit judgment granting a conditional sentence-only writ is affirmed. Arkansas has ninety days after the Eighth Circuit issues its mandate implementing this judgment to resentence Henderson lawfully, subject to ordinary mandate administration. The conviction remains intact. The lawful replacement sentence is for the State to determine under the constitutional rule stated here; the affirmed conditional remedy gives it the stated opportunity before unconditional relief becomes operative.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Pre-AEDPA independent legal review and Teague remain distinct inquiries

**Holding and operative rule.** Henderson's noncapital federal habeas application, filed before April 24, 1996, is governed by pre-AEDPA law rather than amended section 2254(d); supported factual findings retain the treatment required by the former law, while the federal constitutional legal question receives independent review. Teague does not substitute general deference to every reasonable state legal interpretation for that review, but relief still requires a result dictated by precedent when the conviction became final or an actually applicable retroactivity exception.

**Authority.** Part I of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court is joined by all eight other Justices. All nine adopt this temporal and review distinction; only the five identified in Parts II–III adopt the separate old-rule and sentence-relief conclusions.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the filing date governs the statutory question. Lindh excludes the ordinary chapter 153 amendments at issue from this pre-enactment noncapital application. The later standard for unreasonable state adjudications therefore cannot decide this claim. Miller v. Fenton supplies the distinction between respect for supported facts and independent evaluation of the constitutional legal question.

The State invokes Teague to demand acceptance of reasonable state interpretations even in this earlier proceeding. Nonretroactivity performs different work. It bars a newly announced constitutional rule from supplying collateral relief unless an applicable exception permits it; it is not a general replacement standard for legal review. The published record does not establish a separately developed defense identifying some other specific new rule or an independent waiver of such a defense. The Court must nevertheless determine whether the relief it grants follows from finality-era law. It makes that determination below rather than treating rejection of the State's proposed deference rule as abolition of Teague.

##### II. Existing law dictates the sentence-specific result

**Holding and operative rule.** The finding of gross disproportionality for this first conviction, this exceptionally small completed sale and life imprisonment without ordinary parole applies the law already established by Rummel, Solem and Harmelin before Henderson's conviction became final. Relief therefore depends on no new categorical first-offender exemption, drug-weight ceiling or retroactivity exception.

**Authority.** Part II of Justice Souter's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg and Breyer. All five support the complete old-rule conclusion and its stated boundaries. The four Justices who reject relief do not join it merely because they accept independent pre-AEDPA review.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that identifying an old general principle is necessary but not sufficient: the result must follow from the earlier law at the relevant level of specificity. Henderson's direct-review affirmance occurred in 1995, after Rummel, Solem and Harmelin. No disputed exact subsequent finality date affects the availability of those precedents.

Solem already distinguished discretionary executive commutation from ordinary parole and required assessment of the actual offense and culpability. Rummel's repeated convictions and meaningful parole eligibility are absent. Harmelin preserves a narrow proportionality inquiry and sustained punishment for a quantity capable of extensive distribution; it does not make every retail sale equivalent to that large-scale offense. Together those settled distinctions require attention to the extraordinary combination present here. The comparisons corroborate the exceptional mismatch; they create no new sentencing schedule.

The contrary position would give legislative latitude conclusive force despite the surviving threshold. The Court gives that latitude substantial weight, but it cannot erase the actual offense or convert discretionary grace into parole. Neither disagreement about the application nor the absence of a prior case involving precisely 0.238 grams makes the result new. Graham's distinction between applying a dictated protection and extending it supports the inquiry; its capital-mitigation holding supplies no imprisonment rule. The Court changes no threshold and grants relief only for the existing constitutional violation.

##### III. Permanent imprisonment is grossly disproportionate on this offense and offender record

**Holding and operative rule.** A life sentence with no ordinary parole eligibility is grossly disproportionate for Henderson's first conviction of delivering 0.238 grams of cocaine base on the established record. The rule reaches materially equivalent first-offender conduct and punishment; it neither equates sale with possession nor invalidates every life sentence for a drug offense or fixes a maximum lawful replacement term.

**Authority.** The same five Justices join Part III of the Court's opinion, directly supporting this sentence-specific application and the conditional sentence-only remedy. The Court does not derive a new imprisonment formula from the distinct law of monetary forfeitures.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the severity of the punishment must be compared with this offense and offender. Solem supplies that individualized comparison and the distinction between parole and executive grace. Harmelin's narrow proportionality principle requires substantial legislative latitude and comparative inquiry only after the exceptional initial inference arises. Permanent imprisonment for this first conviction and tiny completed sale crosses that threshold.

The record concerns a completed twenty-dollar delivery, not innocent possession. It includes evidence of two additional rocks and interference with a witness. Even considering the additional rocks, it supplies no Harmelin-scale distribution predicate. The Eighth Circuit reports that the sentencing jury was not instructed to use the additional acts in selecting punishment. That is the appellate court's account of the charge; it establishes neither that the conduct did not occur nor that witness interference supplied the sentence's basis.

The comparisons confirm the mismatch at their supported scope. Arkansas authorizes life as a maximum for first-degree murder, rape and kidnapping. Later advisory guidelines do not govern Henderson; their three-and-one-half-year suggestion remains subject to a ten-year statutory minimum and permits aggravating departures, including for witness interference. Other-State evidence distinguishes authorizing life from actually imposing it and retains the uncertainty about Idaho parole. The reported federal ranges—ten to sixteen months, or fifteen to twenty-one with an obstruction enhancement—corroborate disparity without dictating a constitutional term. No complete nationwide survey or numerical ratio decides the case.

The State rightly emphasizes distribution's grave social harms. Those harms justify severe punishment, but they cannot substitute a hypothetical violent enterprise, recidivism or the entire drug trade for this individual's proved conduct. Arkansas retains the task of selecting a lawful replacement sentence in a lawful sentencing proceeding.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Lindh v. Murphy: applied to exclude amended section 2254(d) from this pre-April 24, 1996 noncapital petition; no exemption from every AEDPA provision is announced.
- Miller v. Fenton: applied to separate independent constitutional legal review from the former treatment of supported facts.
- Teague v. Lane: applied as a distinct nonretroactivity limit, not a general deference standard; relief rests on an old-rule application, without reliance on an exception.
- Rummel v. Estelle: distinguished because its recidivism and meaningful parole predicates are absent.
- Solem v. Helm: applied for actual-offense proportionality, corroborative comparison and the difference between ordinary parole and discretionary grace.
- Harmelin v. Michigan: its surviving narrow gross-disproportionality principle governs; its wholesale-quantity application does not decide every completed drug sale.
- Graham v. Collins: applied only for the distinction between dictated application and extension of old law; its capital-mitigation result does not govern imprisonment duration.
- United States v. Bajakajian: remains confined to its fines framework and actual-offense application; its monetary proportionality rule is not converted into a prison-term formula.

#### Law After Decision

Under the existing narrow proportionality rule, permanent imprisonment for this first conviction and materially equivalent tiny-sale conduct is unconstitutional. The conviction remains intact. The law establishes neither a general drug-weight schedule nor the replacement term. Pre-AEDPA independent legal review and Teague's separate finality requirement retain their distinct operation.

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Parts II–III, joined by O'Connor throughout the separate writing and by Thomas in the assumed-framework application only.** The dissent would restore denial of the sentencing claim. The tiny quantity, first conviction and lack of ordinary parole make the sentence severe, but the completed distribution offense and supported record do not meet the extraordinarily demanding threshold under Harmelin. Legislative judgments can account for distribution's preventive and social harms without measuring sentence length arithmetically by weight. The comparison evidence does not convert the exceptional severity into a constitutional prohibition. The dissent acknowledges the real difference from Harmelin's quantity and treats clemency as discretionary grace; it assumes neither a large enterprise nor prior convictions. Thomas's agreement assumes narrow proportionality for this disposition and does not adopt its constitutional foundation. Merits denial makes a separately unestablished new-rule bar unnecessary to these positions.

**Scalia, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Parts II–III.** Harmelin's distinct textual and historical account rejects prison-duration proportionality as an independent requirement of the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause. The actual sentence and comparisons may present a sentencing-policy objection, but they do not establish the asserted constitutional right on his ground. His acceptance of the Excessive Fines inquiry in Bajakajian supplies no prison-duration rule. He accepts the controlling temporal law and does not rely on amended section 2254(d) or a fictitious criminal history. His rationale is distinct from the other dissenters' conclusion that this sentence passes a narrow proportionality test.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Eighth Circuit judgment is affirmed. Its implementing mandate begins Arkansas's ninety-day period for lawful resentencing under the conditional sentence-only writ, subject to ordinary mandate administration. The conviction remains in force. The Court specifies no maximum replacement term; lawful state sentencing must comply with the constitutional boundary announced here.
