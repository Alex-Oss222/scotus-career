## 2002-11-04 ? Popovich-v-Cuyahoga-County-Court-of-Common-Pleas_merits_2002-11-04.md

Canonical source: `terms/OT2002/records/Popovich-v-Cuyahoga-County-Court-of-Common-Pleas_merits_2002-11-04.md`.

## Event

Popovich v. Cuyahoga County Court of Common Pleas. Merits decision, November 4, 2002. On review of the Sixth Circuit’s en banc judgment, 276 F.3d 808. The question is whether Congress validly authorizes the Title II state-court damages action for reasonable accommodation and protection against retaliation securing a hearing-impaired parent’s meaningful participation in custody proceedings. The court of appeals sustained due-process-related access and retaliation theories, excluded an equal-protection-based theory, set aside a $400,000 general verdict and directed retrial. The trial instructions encompassed unequal opportunity, exclusion and retaliation. The reasons for the long delay, effective assistance, injury and damages remain contested.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed in part, vacated in part and remanded for reconsideration of the general verdict and lawful trial scope.

## Judgment & Remedy

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Immunity for reasonable parental-custody access and related retaliation | Rejection of categorical immunity affirmed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | The expressly authorized statutory action may proceed under the governing duties and defenses |
| Exclusion of the same access claim because stated in equal-protection terms | Categorical exclusion vacated, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | Constitutional support for enforcement is not a separately pleaded cause of action |
| General verdict and trial-remedy direction | Remanded for reconsideration, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | Examine actual instructions and preserved objections under valid theories; determine whether the verdict can stand or retrial is required |

The $400,000 award is not automatically reinstated. Scalia and Thomas also would not sustain that general verdict, but would restrict any damages path to enforcement of an actual constitutional denial or penalty. No custody determination is altered.

## Opinion Topology

Kennedy delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer joined. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, J., joined. The entire Court opinion has seven joins; the dissenters agree only that §12202 clearly expresses Congress’s authorization and do not join the opinion.

## Holdings

### Clear authorization and valid preventive enforcement

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 12202 expressly authorizes the private federal action against the State. Under §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, Congress may require reasonable disability accommodation enabling a hearing-impaired parent to understand and participate meaningfully in custody adjudication, and may forbid retaliation for requesting that access, including applications exceeding the separately provable constitutional minimum. Those bounded duties are congruent and proportional enforcement of the right to a meaningful hearing.

**Authority:** The Court opinion is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Scalia and Thomas additionally agree with the clear-authorization proposition, but dissent from the preventive scope.

**Controlling explanation:** City of Boerne v. Flores permits fitted prevention while forbidding Congress to redefine constitutional rights. Garrett independently sustains a bounded accommodation regime under §5 and rejects a requirement that every claimant prove a separate constitutional tort; its reservation of Title II requires examination of this different application. A parent unable to understand material custody proceedings cannot effectively respond to evidence that affects the parent-child relationship. Mathews v. Eldridge connects the parental interest, risk of error, value of communication assistance and governmental burden. Boddie v. Connecticut supports indispensable initial access; Santosky v. Kramer and M.L.B. v. S.L.J. establish the gravity of parental adjudication without equating ordinary custody with permanent termination.

Reasonable assistance directly protects the hearing function, and punishment for requesting that assistance can defeat the same opportunity. The dissent’s concern that statutory accommodation exceeds what the Constitution independently requires does not defeat this close preventive fit. An effective alternative may supply meaningful participation; the accommodation duty does not require fundamentally altering the nature of the service or imposing an undue financial or administrative burden. A legitimate inquiry into disability and need is not itself retaliation, and delay alone proves no violation. Disability, statutory qualification, denied effective access, retaliation, causation, compensable injury and damages still require proof under their respective rules. The holding creates neither heightened disability scrutiny nor a right to preferred technology. Due process supplies the controlling enforcement basis; equality reinforces it without an independent equality-only holding.

### The statutory access claim and trial remedy remain intact

**Holding and operative rule:** A tried statutory access-and-retaliation claim cannot be divided into valid and invalid causes solely by the constitutional rationale offered for Congress’s enforcement power. The court of appeals must reconsider the general verdict and trial scope under the valid parental-custody access duties and the actual preserved objections; constitutional authority alone does not restore the damages award.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The plaintiff seeks the statutory remedy tried below, not a newly created unpleaded due-process damages cause of action. Once the relevant preventive duty is valid, the lower court cannot discard part of that same access claim merely because it was described in equal-protection terms. Its prior restrictive account of Garrett supplies no valid basis for that categorical exclusion. The actual instructions may nevertheless contain error, and the preserved objections and evidentiary findings determine whether the verdict survives or a new trial is required. That work must be performed under the correct statutory and constitutional framework. Broader Title II services and an Article I basis are outside the necessary ground of this disposition; the latter is not categorically barred but is not established here.

## Precedent Treatment

- City of Boerne v. Flores: applies congruence and proportionality to the particular preventive duty, without a quota of adjudicated violations or a power to rewrite rights.
- Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett: applies its independent affirmative §5 accommodation holding and respects its reservation of Title II; this parental-custody application receives its own fit analysis.
- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319: applies the procedural balance to effective communication in a binding custody hearing.
- Boddie v. Connecticut, 401 U.S. 371: draws on its protection of indispensable initial judicial access, without creating universal free civil process.
- Santosky v. Kramer, 455 U.S. 745: uses its recognition of serious parental interests without treating ordinary custody as permanent termination.
- M.L.B. v. S.L.J.: uses its meaningful-parental-process rationale by analogy while preserving the scope of its first-review permanent-termination holding.
- Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank: preserves the rule that Article I enforcement is not categorically barred; no Article I application is adopted here.
- Kimel v. Florida Board of Regents: preserves the same distinction between express authorization, valid substantive power and actual liability.

## Law After Decision

States may be sued through the expressly authorized Title II remedy for breaches of the bounded parental-custody access and related-retaliation duties sustained here, even when a particular statutory violation exceeds a separately provable constitutional tort. That authority does not establish liability or damages. The decision does not settle Title II’s application to every public service or expand the permanent-termination rules of M.L.B.

## Separate Writings

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Scalia, dissents. They accept the constitutional right to an adequate initial custody hearing and Congress’s clear authorization of suit, but find the preventive damages regime insufficiently fitted insofar as it reaches beyond an actual constitutional denial or penalty. Their Boerne and Garrett positions distinguish enforcement of a protected opportunity to be heard from substituting a broader statutory accommodation right for that constitutional standard.

Effective participation might already be supplied by counsel or other means even if a claimant could establish some additional statutory accommodation duty. The need to investigate disability, legitimate choices among effective assistance and reasons for delay therefore matter to the constitutional predicate. Their objection is not that all preventive legislation requires an individual constitutional tort or a numerical record of adjudicated violations. It concerns the fit of this damages regime. They would confine the remand to a properly preserved statutory application enforcing an actual denial of meaningful hearing participation or an unconstitutional penalty for requesting it, and would not sustain the existing general verdict.

## Procedure After Action

The court of appeals must reconsider the actual jury instructions and preserved objections under the valid access and retaliation theories. It must determine whether the general verdict can stand or a new trial is required. Any further trial must apply the distinct qualification, accommodation, retaliation, causation and damages requirements; the custody merits remain unaffected.

## Source Notes

Popovich v. Cuyahoga County Court of Common Pleas, 276 F.3d 808 (6th Cir. 2002) (en banc); 42 U.S.C. §§12131–12134, 12202 and 12203; U.S. Const., Amend. XIV, §§1 and 5. The stipulated choice between continuing without the accommodation motion and holding an inquiry into disability and need does not itself establish retaliation or explain the ensuing delay.

## 2003-01-15 ? Newman-v-Sathyavaglswaran_merits_2003-01-15.md

## Event

Newman v. Sathyavaglswaran. October Term 2002. Merits decision January 15, 2003. The Court reviews the Ninth Circuit's April 16, 2002 reversal of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal. The questions concern the parents' own property interests under California's 1997 corneal-removal scheme and the effect of state postdeprivation remedies when removal follows established governmental procedures. Robert Newman and Barbara Obarski allege removal of their deceased sons' corneas in October 1997 without notice or an attempt to obtain consent, and discovery in September 1999. The coroner and county seek dismissal; the parents seek adjudication of the reinstated retrospective claims.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer participated at submission and decision. All nine participate.

## Public Action

Judgment reinstating the complaint affirmed, 9–0; eight Justices adopt the contextual process remit and Stone-Zsela states a distinct ordinary-notice rule.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Judgment:** Affirmed, 9–0. **Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. **Opposing:** None. The Ninth Circuit's reversal of the pleading dismissal stands, and the action returns for the process, responsibility, defense and remedy proceedings specified in Part III. Stone-Zsela supports reinstatement on the distinct process rule stated in his concurrence.

## Opinion Topology

Breyer delivered the opinion of the Court, in which Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg joined, and in which Stone-Zsela joined as to Parts I and II and the specified Mathews and remand propositions in Part III. Stone-Zsela filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Same reinstatement judgment; distinct ordinary-notice rule | Parts I-II and identified III propositions joined; distinct ordinary-notice and necessity allocation |

## Holdings

### I. The retained disposition, gift and objection rights are property

**Holding and operative rule:** California's 1997 law gives the properly entitled next of kin a protected property interest in the retained powers to possess and direct disposition of remains, make anatomical gifts and object to corneal removal. The parents adequately plead that they hold those substantive rights; nonmarket character and the label quasi-property do not remove the interest from procedural due process.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Board of Regents v. Roth locates the entitlement in enforceable state law. California retained disposition, gift and refusal powers; former Government Code §27491.47 authorized removal during autopsy only when the coroner lacked knowledge of an objection. That condition recognizes a legally consequential objection, rather than transferring all control to the State. Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill separates the entitlement from the procedure for its deprivation. American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan requires an actual entitlement and preserves an existing one against procedural erasure: these parents allege presently vested control, unlike general eligibility for payment before substantive predicates are met. Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation confirms that lack of market value does not settle ownership; its takings remedy is not transferred here. The competing view treats retrieval authority as the substantive boundary of a burial duty. It fails to account for the same law's continuing refusal and gift powers. California remains free to define substantive rights, but omission of an effective opportunity to object does not itself extinguish the retained right. Health and Safety Code §7100 preserves contrary decedent directions and the priority of spouse, children, parents, next kin in intestate order and the public administrator if the estate has sufficient assets. The pleaded parents' priority is not defeated by an established superior claimant or donor direction. Autopsy authority and transfer of corneal tissue for another's use are distinct. The reasonable-contact provision for other tissue in §7151.5(a)(2) is not the text of former §27491.47; the later consent amendment does not determine 1997 process.

**Precedent treatment:** Roth and Loudermill supply the state-created-entitlement/procedure distinction; Sullivan is applied with its actual-entitlement qualification; Phillips supplies the distinction between ownership and commercial value. No immutable constitutional property category for human remains is adopted.

### II. An established procedure cannot rely categorically on later state remedies

**Holding and operative rule:** Available state postdeprivation remedies do not categorically defeat a §1983 procedural-due-process claim alleging a predictable, irreversible deprivation under established governmental procedures. Adequacy of process must be assessed under the actual circumstances, and exhaustion of state remedies is not a prerequisite to the federal action.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co. distinguishes an established state procedure from the random, unauthorized conduct addressed in Parratt v. Taylor and Hudson v. Palmer, where advance safeguards are impracticable. The complaint alleges a planned removal practice, not an unforeseeable departure from a valid system. The mere availability of a tort action therefore cannot end the federal claim. The former statute's liability shield also prevents assuming an effective compensatory remedy existed. Patsy v. Board of Regents separately rejects exhaustion; that rule does not itself prove that the available process was inadequate. The defendants may establish circumstances justifying action without advance protection, but the complaint cannot be dismissed by presuming such circumstances or treating every later remedy as constitutionally sufficient.

**Precedent treatment:** Logan is applied to established procedure; Parratt and Hudson retain their genuine random-and-unauthorized-act setting; Patsy preserves the distinct no-exhaustion rule.

### III. The actual process must be adjudicated under Mathews

**Holding and operative rule:** The reinstated claim must be adjudicated under Mathews by weighing the retained private interest and irreversibility of removal, the risk of mistaken disregard of an entitled person's objection and value of feasible safeguards, and the government's actual retrieval and health burdens. Ordinarily a planned irreversible deprivation requires a practicable opportunity beforehand, but actual urgency or impracticability may justify proceeding without advance process; the pleadings establish neither mandatory advance consent in every case nor adequate eventual money relief.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court, joined by the eight Associates: Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Stone-Zsela also joins the integrated Mathews framework in Part III, ordinary practicable-opportunity principle with its actual-urgency or impracticability qualification, and factual, responsibility, defense and remedy remit. He withholds assent only from leaving a more definite ordinary-notice requirement and necessity substantiation unstated. Eight full joins control Part III; the expressly identified common propositions have all nine joins, without synthesis of different rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews v. Eldridge requires one contextual inquiry, and Gilbert v. Homar illustrates why urgency can affect initial safeguards while continuing to require meaningful protection. Irreversible loss of an objection makes a practicable opportunity valuable; a real medical retrieval window may constrain the form and timing of that opportunity. Neither the value of donation nor the strength of the private interest eliminates the other side of the inquiry. The present record supplies no findings about available contact information, retrieval urgency, conflicting authorization or feasible safeguards. The Court therefore directs assessment under that standard without fixing a more specific ordinary-notice presumption or necessity-proof allocation on these pleadings; it does not decide that eventual compensation suffices. The rule answers the defendants' demand for categorical dismissal while retaining the factual work necessary to decide the actual process. On remand the courts must determine the parents' entitlement under the governing priority and donor rules, the procedure used and practicable safeguards, each defendant's responsibility, preserved defenses, causation, compensable injury and relief under the ordinary governing burdens.

**Precedent treatment:** Mathews is applied as an integrated process inquiry; Gilbert supplies the distinction between justified urgency and the continuing need for meaningful protection. The judgment makes no broader revision of either rule.

## Precedent Treatment

- Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564: applied; the identified California powers supply the property interest.
- Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill, 470 U.S. 532: applied; a retained substantive entitlement is distinct from its deprivation procedure.
- American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan (March 3, 1999): applied and distinguished; an actual vested control interest is alleged here, unlike general eligibility without a service-specific payment entitlement.
- Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation (June 15, 1998): applied to ownership despite absence of market value, without importing its takings question.
- Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422: applied to established governmental procedures.
- Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527, and Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517: distinguished from the pleaded established removal practice; their genuine impracticability principles remain.
- Patsy v. Board of Regents, 457 U.S. 496: applied against exhaustion as a prerequisite, separately from adequacy of process.
- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, and Gilbert v. Homar (June 9, 1997): applied to the actual private interest, error risk, feasible safeguards and urgency.

## Law After Decision

The retained California disposition, gift and objection interests are protected property for eligible holders under the 1997 statutory limits. The established-procedure claim survives a categorical state-remedy defense. Lower courts must apply the contextual Mathews rule stated in Part III to actual urgency, authorization, contact possibilities and safeguards. Those factual and remedial proceedings apply settled law; they are not a reservation of the protected interest. Stone-Zsela's more definite ordinary-notice presumption remains a separate position. The later consent regime is not displaced by adjudication of these retrospective claims.

## Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment, joins Parts I and II and Part III's integrated Mathews inquiry, ordinary practicable-opportunity principle with its actual-urgency or impracticability qualification, and factual, responsibility, defense and remedy remit. He would hold that an established procedure irreversibly defeating a retained objection ordinarily requires reasonable efforts at advance notice and a practicable opportunity to object. The parents retain the burden of proving deprivation without adequate process; the State must substantiate an asserted urgency, unavailable contact information or other necessity for bypassing that ordinary safeguard with actual circumstances. Mathews gives substantial value to advance protection of an irreversible choice, while allowing medical timing to constrain its form. Logan and Zinermon distinguish this foreseeable procedure from the random acts in Parratt and Hudson; Patsy separately rejects exhaustion. In his view the missing operational facts determine necessity and liability, and do not prevent stating the ordinary safeguard now. He requires neither a trial-type hearing nor an absolute veto of necessary autopsy, and honors valid donor directions and authorized consent. The lower court should develop contact opportunities, retrieval windows, donor directions, authorization and the decision procedure, then determine the practicable protection withheld and causal responsibility. The parents must prove their entitlement, deprivation, defendant responsibility and compensable injury by a preponderance; applicable immunity and other preserved defenses remain separate. The two retrospective removals establish no entitlement to a prospective decree against the later consent regime. Under Harper his rule would govern the parties and cases still open on direct review; Reynoldsville Casket preserves independent procedural, immunity and remedial limits without selective prospectivity based on reliance alone. Final judgments remain closed, and collateral relief requires its own authorization. The safeguard itself determines neither 1997 immunity nor institutional attribution or damages. His particular ordinary-notice presumption and necessity substantiation are not the Court's additional holding.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for further adjudication of the two 1997 removals under the stated entitlement and Mathews rules. The pleading-stage dismissal cannot be reinstated solely because state tort remedies existed. The later consent statute supplies no automatic answer to liability for the earlier removals.

## 2003-03-24 ? A-Womans-Choice-v-Newman_merits_2003-03-24.md

Canonical source: `terms/OT2002/records/A-Womans-Choice-v-Newman_merits_2003-03-24.md`.

## Event

A Woman's Choice–East Side Women's Clinic v. Newman. Plenary merits decision, March 24, 2003. On review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 305 F.3d 684, No. 01-2107, reversing the permanent injunction reported at 132 F. Supp. 2d 1150. The questions concern Indiana’s compulsory in-person communication of advance abortion information, review of trial and comparative evidence before enforcement, and preservation of separable informed-consent duties.

The statute’s medical-emergency exception excuses its informed-consent conditions. As construed by Indiana’s Supreme Court, it applies when the attending physician, exercising good-faith clinical judgment concerning the patient’s life and physical and mental health, finds pregnancy complications that make prompt therapeutic abortion medically necessary to address a significant threat. Severe but temporary conditions for which abortion is not medically necessary are excluded. The challenged added encounter burdens patients who remain subject to attendance after that exception operates.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

Reversed and remanded for restoration and implementation of the limited in-person-delivery injunction, 6–3.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

Restore and implement the permanent injunction against compulsory in-person delivery of the covered advance information. The independently remaining eighteen-hour interval is outside this reviewed injunction component; leaving it in place does not decide its constitutionality. Accurate material information, qualified communication, genuine clinical assessment and voluntary informed consent remain. A receipt certificate or dependent penalty cannot reinstate the enjoined physical-presence condition.

## Opinion Topology

Stevens delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. The complete coalition is Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. Kennedy files a dissenting opinion. Scalia files a dissenting opinion, joined by Thomas.

## Holdings

### I. The complete standard and ordinary review rules govern prospective proof

**Holding and operative rule:** A compulsory abortion-information delivery condition is governed by the complete Casey framework, including meaningful-interference review even when no substantial obstacle is proved. Supported historical trial findings receive Rule 52(a) protection, while constitutional sufficiency and application are independently reviewed; reliable comparative experience may establish prospective injury when material similarities and differences are considered.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court’s opinion; the six-Justice whole-opinion coalition identified in Opinion Topology joins this rule in full.

**Controlling explanation:** Casey requires identification of the legal duty and baseline, actual function and purpose, attributable interference and affected population, the corresponding substantive protection, clinical judgment and truthful communication, and fitted relief. Challengers prove duty, causation, affected population and triggering interference. Baseline violations, objectively proved obstructive purpose and substantial obstacles independently fail; other severe interference requires compelling justification and narrow tailoring. Remaining meaningful interference requires substantiated permissible reasons and fit, including an answer to an identified concrete, practicable, substantially less burdensome alternative. Below meaningful interference that heightened access inquiry ends, while independent constitutional requirements remain.

Fargo and Mazurek preserve this complete inquiry. Neither Salerno’s universal-invalidity condition nor a fixed numerical fraction displaces it. A court need not await completed local injuries, demand a national study or prescribe one regression method. Comparative evidence is not automatically conclusive either: geography, provider availability, income, transportation and clinical practice can affect transferability. Respect for underlying trial findings does not convert a contested prediction into an established Indiana effect. The Court resolves this case on the separate concrete extra-visit burden, without resolving the disputed projected percentage.

### II. This additional physical encounter lacks sufficient justification

**Holding and operative rule:** When compulsory prior physical attendance adds meaningful travel, work, childcare, scheduling or similar burdens beyond communicating accurate information, the State must substantiate the added encounter’s permissible function and answer a concrete, practicable, substantially less burdensome alternative adequately serving that function. Indiana’s uniform advance-presence requirement fails that inquiry on this record because remote communication followed by clinical examination and consent verification at the procedure visit adequately addresses the stated functions, and the State has not identified a material deficiency.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Casey distinguishes useful information from burdensome timing and delivery; Akron’s protection against unsupported attendance restrictions survives at that scope. Indiana may provide accurate information favoring childbirth, and legislative opposition to abortion alone proves no obstructive purpose. But patients who otherwise need only the procedure visit must make a concrete additional encounter. That added burden exists without assuming every projected decline in abortions reflects prevention rather than persuasion, travel elsewhere or voluntary choice.

The State identifies personal communication, nonverbal responses, misunderstanding and coercion. Those are legitimate concerns, but generalized claims do not explain why reliable advance remote communication, opportunities for questions and later individualized clinical review cannot adequately address them. The performing clinician remains responsible for actual consent and may delay care when consent is unresolved. The alternative therefore preserves clinical responsibility while removing an unsupported uniform trip. Reasonable experience and predictions may substantiate regulation; no new study or numerical benefit calculation is invariably required. Courts may not select a preferred policy once justification and fit are shown. Here that necessary answer is absent. Different restrictions supported by actual clinical need remain governed by the same rule, not a universal entitlement to remote medical treatment.

### III. The delivery-only injunction supplies complete relief on this appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** The compulsory in-person delivery condition and enforcement dependent on it must remain enjoined, while legally separable accurate disclosure and genuine consent duties remain enforceable. The independent eighteen-hour interval is outside this appeal’s reviewed injunction component and is not sustained by the Court’s limited judgment.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by the same six Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Casey’s remedy principle removes the obligation that fails and preserves lawful requirements. The existing decree does precisely that: it prevents the unnecessary advance physical encounter without requiring a procedure or a clinically necessary consultation to occur remotely, or forbidding an in-person visit requested by the patient. Lawful receipt certification may document information actually supplied; it cannot enforce physical attendance after that obligation is enjoined. The established meaningful-interference record is sufficient, so restoration does not require a new trial directed to the disputed statewide percentage. Any preserved challenge to the interval must receive the governing analysis in its proper posture.

## Precedent Treatment

- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey supplies the complete framework and separates lawful accurate content from unsupported timing and delivery burdens; its clinical and remedial qualifications remain.
- Fargo Women’s Health Organization v. Schafer confirms that absence of a substantial obstacle does not end review of meaningful interference.
- Mazurek v. Armstrong preserves concrete-burden proof and the full corresponding justification inquiry.
- Akron v. Akron Center for Reproductive Health retains its protection against unsupported waiting and delivery restrictions at the scope preserved by Casey, without barring accurate permissible information.
- United States v. Salerno does not impose universal invalidity or completed local injury as prerequisites to this Casey-governed relief; its treatment in other constitutional fields is unchanged.

## Law After Decision

Comparable compulsory delivery restrictions require justification of the added physical encounter against its established burden and concrete adequate alternatives. Accurate information and lawful persuasion do not themselves justify an unnecessary trip. Prospective proof remains available under ordinary evidentiary rules, without a universal causal percentage or demand that patients first lose access. Indiana’s separate interval receives no constitutional approval from this judgment.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, dissenting.** He retains protected choice but rejects the residual justification rule adopted in Casey and applied here. In his view, personal communication and an opportunity to observe misunderstanding are reasonably related to informed consent. Comparative abortion declines do not establish the number of women prevented from obtaining care, and the record does not justify overriding this reasonable delivery arrangement under his approach. He would affirm reversal of the injunction. He neither treats every decline as voluntary persuasion nor requires completed local injury in every prospective case.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They retain their Casey, Miller, Voinovich and Stenberg disagreement with the substantive constitutional abortion right. They would sustain this reasonable informed-consent regulation and affirm reversal of the injunction on that distinct foundation. Their position does not deny the extra burdens, replace supported trial findings or attribute the Court’s earlier contrary rule to a different Casey decision. It supplies no controlling law for lower courts.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for restoration and implementation of the delivery-only permanent injunction. The decree preserves the remaining duties at their existing scope and bars enforcement that would recreate compulsory advance physical attendance. No additional evidentiary trial is required to establish the already sufficient meaningful-interference ground.

## Source Notes

305 F.3d 684, Seventh Circuit No. 01-2107; 132 F. Supp. 2d 1150. Indiana Code §16-34-2-1.1; A Woman’s Choice–East Side Women’s Clinic v. Newman, 671 N.E.2d 104, 108–111 (Ind. 1996), construing the medical-emergency exception; Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a). The projected ten-to-thirteen-percent reduction is not a finding that all such procedures were prevented; the judgment does not depend on that inference.

## Event

Herring v. Keenan. Plenary merits decision, April 29, 2003. On review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, 218 F.3d 1171, No. 99-1263. The questions concern constitutional protection against a federal probation officer's disclosure of intimate HIV information to employers and family, and qualified immunity for conduct in December 1993 and January 1994.

The complaint alleges that Herring, on federal probation for driving while intoxicated on federal property, told Keenan he had taken an HIV test and thought he might be infected but had not received a result. Without authorization, she allegedly told restaurant employers he was positive, pressed for his termination under an asserted legal prohibition, and told his sister that he had been tested and/or was positive. Herring died in 1996, and his estate continues this individual federal-officer damages action. These are pleading assumptions, not findings of confirmed infection, actual purpose, transmission risk or injury. The district court refused to dismiss the informational-privacy claim; the Tenth Circuit reversed on qualified-immunity grounds. The estate seeks continuation of that claim, and Keenan seeks immunity.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate.

## Public Action

The appellate immunity judgment is vacated and the case remanded, 5–4.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The appellate immunity judgment is vacated. On the pleaded account of intimate disclosures without sufficient official justification, the asserted legal qualified-immunity defense is rejected, and the informational-privacy claim returns for ordinary further proceedings. That legal defense is not remanded merely for reconsideration on the same assumed facts. ## Holdings

### I. Probation does not erase protection against unjustified outside disclosure of intimate medical information

**Holding and operative rule:** The Fifth Amendment protects a probationer's objectively intimate medical testing and status information against intentional disclosure to an employer or family without sufficient lawful official justification. The claimant must establish the protected disclosure and the officer's personal responsibility; the asserted official need must sufficiently connect the information, recipient and supervisory function, with scope, use and safeguards fitted to that need.

**Authority:** Part I is the opinion of the Court, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five adopt the rule at the stated level of generality and its application to the pleaded account.

**Controlling explanation:** Sheets protects intimate information against unjustified official dissemination. Denius requires an actual functional connection and fitted safeguards for government access to medical information. Paul P. and Lawall require attention to the particular audience and use; lawful acquisition for a confidential task does not by itself justify an additional transfer. Sterling confirms the constitutional importance of intimate family exposure, while its coercive-disclosure trigger remains distinct from the actual disclosures alleged here. Doe v. SEPTA preserved a confined benefits use, which supplies no permission for an unrelated employer or family disclosure.

Probation permits genuine supervision, not every use of information acquired through that relationship. Voluntarily telling an officer about a test is not general consent to publication. Neither a limited number of recipients nor the public-safety label establishes a sufficient connection to these disclosures. Each employer or sister communication must be assessed separately; a justification for one does not justify the other. The allegations of unconfirmed status, termination pressure and absence of adequate official need therefore state the protected invasion, subject to factual proof by a preponderance. The privacy rule permits justified protective action and does not turn every administrative confidentiality breach into a constitutional violation. The guidance supplies relevant context rather than the constitutional right.

**Precedent treatment:** Sheets and Denius are applied to intimate medical disclosure in federal supervision; Paul P. and Lawall supply the recipient-and-function limits. Sterling's distinct compelled-disclosure holding is preserved. Doe's confined benefits application and Whalen's safeguarded administration remain permissible at their actual scope.

**Authority:** Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer reach that result under the existing objective notice defense. Stone-Zsela reaches it under his distinct proposed authoritative-reliance defense. Their common judgment binds this case; Part I independently supplies the five-Justice substantive rule. The immunity rationale remains fractured under Marks.

**Explanation of authority:** The four-Justice rationale finds that preconduct intimate-information and limited-supervision decisions gave sufficient warning on these allegations. One asks what a reasonable officer was objectively warned against; the other asks whether actual reliance on valid applicable permission is proved. **Precedent treatment:** Under Marks, these distinct predicates leave the immunity rationale fractured. Harlow and Anderson retain their existing force. Meyer preserves the separate question of an authorized cause of action, which recognition of the substantive right does not resolve.

## Holdings

### I. The stabilization duty does not depend on actual transfer or discharge

**Holding and operative rule:** When a participating hospital determines that a person coming to it has an emergency medical condition, §1395dd(b)(1) requires the examination and treatment within available staff and facilities necessary for stabilization, or a transfer complying with subsection (c). Stabilization means preventing likely material deterioration, within reasonable medical probability, resulting from or during transfer; that medical measure does not add actual movement outside the hospital as a prerequisite to the duty.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** In re Baby K already rejects the hospital's lack of an intended transfer as an excuse when an acute emergency and effective stabilizing capability are established. The statute offers stabilizing care or a compliant transfer; retaining the patient supplies no third option excusing both. Its transfer-related definition measures medical stability rather than postponing the obligation until departure. Roberts independently rejects an additional improper financial or other invidious motive requirement while preserving the enacted conditions.

The hospital argues that the estate's reading would federalize every negligent inpatient treatment decision. The argument does not justify the transfer prerequisite imposed below. The duty concerns an actually determined emergency, available stabilizing treatment and breach, with direct harm and causation required for this civil action. The estate alleges delay while Normil remained in emergency care before admission. Applying those bounded conditions to that phase gives effect to the statute; Part II addresses the distinct inpatient phase.

**Precedent treatment:** In re Baby K is applied to reject the no-transfer prerequisite. Roberts preserves the statutory predicates while rejecting an extra improper-motive element. Their bounded emergency duty remains distinct from general medical negligence.

**Operative qualifications:** A transfer is hospital-directed movement outside its facilities, including discharge; movement after declaration of death and departure without permission of a hospital-affiliated person are excluded. Internal movement to intensive care is not a statutory transfer. For unstabilized transfer, §1395dd(c) requires either the individual's or legally responsible person's written request after explanation of the hospital's obligations and transfer risks, or a physician-signed certification, based on information then available, that expected medical benefits of treatment elsewhere outweigh increased transfer risks. If no physician is physically present in the emergency department, a qualified medical person may certify only after consulting a physician who makes that determination and later countersigns. Every certification must summarize supporting risks and benefits. Every route also requires available treatment minimizing risk; receiving space, qualified personnel and agreement to accept the transfer and provide appropriate treatment; all available emergency records, including the condition, observed signs or symptoms, preliminary diagnosis, treatment, test results, request or certification and the name and address of any required on-call physician who refused or failed timely to appear; qualified transport personnel and equipment with necessary appropriate life support; and applicable additional regulatory conditions. The transfer alternative is permissive, not a command to move every patient.

Section (b)(2) separately deems treatment compliance when the hospital offers the required examination and treatment, explains risks and benefits, the patient or representative refuses, and the hospital takes all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. Section (b)(3) separately requires an offered transfer satisfying subsection (c), the risk-and-benefit explanation, refusal and all reasonable steps to obtain written informed refusal. These distinct alternatives retain their conditions. State medical law remains effective except to the extent it directly conflicts with an applicable federal duty under §1395dd(f).

### II. Later admission cannot erase an accrued preadmission violation

**Holding and operative rule:** An alleged breach of an already-triggered stabilization duty during emergency care must be adjudicated even if the hospital later admits the patient; later admission cannot extinguish a completed earlier violation. The estate must prove by a preponderance an actual emergency determination, necessary treatment within available capability, breach and direct causal harm; §1395dd(d)(2)(A) permits a directly harmed person to seek the State's available personal-injury damages and appropriate equitable relief against the participating hospital within two years of the violation.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices directly join.

**Controlling explanation:** The complaint alleges a substantial delay in emergency care, a diagnostic notation involving pneumonia and possible sepsis, an unavailable scan required before admission, and ordered antibiotics allegedly not given. At dismissal these factual allegations receive their proper pleading effect. The notation and eventual death do not conclusively establish the statutory emergency determination, available treatment, breach or causal injury; those matters require ordinary adjudication under Part I.

The lower court must distinguish injury attributed to the preadmission phase from a claim resting solely on subsequent genuine inpatient care. The legal endpoint of an unperformed duty after bona fide admission remains open here because the character and timing of the actual admission and the alleged later breach have not been adjudicated; the present preadmission allegations independently defeat the categorical dismissal. The governing standard now supplied requires adjudication of the detected emergency, available stabilizing care, statutory performance and direct causal injury in the relevant phase. The private EMTALA action runs against the participating hospital. The separate screening dismissal and §1981 remand retain their existing status.

**Precedent treatment:** In re Baby K supplies the duty tied to the detected emergency and medically defined stabilization. Roberts supplies the distinct direct-harm, hospital-action, remedial and limitations requirements. The automatic actual-transfer premise of the judgment below is rejected.

## Precedent Treatment

In re Baby K governs the rejection of a no-intended-transfer exemption and retains the complete transfer and informed-refusal alternatives. Roberts removes an extra improper-motive element while preserving actual statutory determination, breach, direct harm and the hospital-only civil-action requirements. The no-actual-transfer rule applied below cannot survive those authorities. State medical law retains effect except for a direct conflict with an applicable federal duty.

## Law After Decision

Hospitals must meet the enacted stabilization or lawful-transfer duty after determining an emergency, even when retaining the patient. Later admission does not erase a completed emergency-care violation. The hospital's actual determination, available treatment, performance and direct causal harm govern the revived claim. The genuine-inpatient endpoint remains for a phase-specific case with the necessary facts; it does not defeat adjudication of the earlier violation here.

## Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's no-transfer rule and reversal on the preadmission allegations. He would also hold that genuine inpatient admission itself cannot extinguish an already-triggered, unperformed stabilization duty. In his view, §1395dd(b) makes performance rather than location decisive: the hospital must stabilize the identified emergency through necessary available care, make a compliant transfer, or satisfy the applicable informed-refusal provision. Stability remains the medically bounded condition of reasonable probability, not guaranteed cure; an unknown condition or later unrelated deterioration after the duty ends would fall outside this proposed rule.

He relies on the statutory alternatives and Roberts's refusal to add an extra motive defense. He would reject Bryan's admission endpoint insofar as it terminates this unperformed duty, while retaining its distinction between EMTALA and general malpractice law. Baby K supplies neither a universal care entitlement nor a conclusive answer about every inpatient. The estate would still prove actual determination, necessary available care, breach and direct harm. The statutory hospital action and authorized remedies would remain separate from any action against individual clinicians, and the proposed rule would not establish a systemwide treatment failure from the pleaded course.

If adopted and applied, the construction would govern still-open direct-review claims within its scope under Harper, including earlier care. Reynoldsville would permit no reliance-only admission exception; genuine limitations, causation and remedy requirements would remain, and final judgments and time-barred claims would not reopen. With the admission endpoint left open on this record, he accepts the narrower ground: no actual transfer is required, and delayed admission cannot cure an earlier breach. The later phase should receive proper adjudication rather than a presumed absence of any preceding duty.
