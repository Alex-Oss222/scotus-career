QUESTIONS PRESENTED 


1. Whether absent class members are precluded from 
relitigating the issue of adequacy of representation through a 
collateral attack on a class settlement, after class members had 
a full opportunity to opt out, object, and appeal, and after both 
the trial court and the court of appeals, in the course of approv- 
ing the settlement, expressly determined that the class represen- 
tatives adequately represented the entire class. 


2. Whether, if collateral attack is permissible, the “ade- 
quacy of representation” issue is properly determined as of the 
time of the original litigation or in light of events and changes 
in the law occurring years after the settlement has become final 
and all of the settlement proceeds have been disbursed.

JURISDICTION 


The judgment of the court of appeals was entered on 
November 30, 2001, and a timely petition for rehearing was 
denied on May 8, 2002. On July 29, 2002, Justice Ginsburg 
extended the time for filing a petition for a writ of certiorari 
until August 20, 2002. The petition was granted on November 
4, 2002. This Court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1). 


CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISION AND RULE 
INVOLVED 


The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution 
provides: “No person shall be * * * deprived of life, liberty, or 
property, without due process of law * * * .” 


Rule 23(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides 
in relevant part: “One or more members of a class may sue or 
be sued as representative parties on behalf of all only if * * * 
(4) the representative parties will fairly and adequately protect 
the interests of the class.” 


STATEMENT 


This litigation involves a “mass tort” suit that was brought 
as a Class action nearly twenty-five years ago. In that case, the 
district court, after considering and rejecting numerous 
challenges to the appropriateness of class treatment, certified a 
class in 1983 upon finding that all prerequisites for class action 
treatment (including adequacy of representation) had been met. 
The parties settled the case in 1984, and a final judgment was 
entered that (1) required the defendants — now petitioners in this 
Court — to pay $180 million into a fund to be distributed by the 
district court for the benefit of the class and (2) permanently 
barred all class members from instituting against any of the 


2 


defendants actions that relate to the subject matter of the 
complaint. The court of appeals upheld the settlement and 
affirmed the judgment in 1987, specifically rejecting a 
challenge to the adequacy of the class representation. 
Petitioners paid almost $200 million for the benefit of the class 
members. 


In 1998, fifteen years after the class was certified, more 
than ten years after the judgment became final on appeal, and 
several years after all of the settlement funds had been 
disbursed, two members of the plaintiff class — now 
respondents in this Court — brought a new suit against 
petitioners, arguing that they had not been adequately 
represented at the time the class was certified and therefore 
should not be bound by the class judgment. In particular, 
respondents contended that class representation must have been 
inadequate because, as matters turned out, they did not receive 
benefits from the settlement as great as those received by 
certain other class members. The court of appeals agreed, 
holding (1) that class members who contest the adequacy of 
representation may collaterally attack class certification 
decisions long after the case is resolved and (2) that in such a 
collateral challenge the court should determine the adequacy of 
representation not as of the time of the original litigation, but in 
light of events and changes in the law occurring years after the 
settlement became final. The question now before the Court is 
whether those holdings were correct. 


1. Beginning in the late 1970s, suits were brought on behalf 
of Vietnam veterans and their families against petitioners, 
manufacturers of the herbicide Agent Orange, which petitioners 
supplied to the United States Government in compliance with 
military specifications for use as a defoliant. The suits alleged 
that exposure to Agent Orange had caused (and would cause in 
the future) a variety of illnesses. Despite the understandably 
“strong emotions these proceedings have generated” and the 
“grievous medical problems” experienced by many veterans 
and their families (Jn re “Agent Orange” Prod. Liab. Litig., 


3 


818 F.2d 145, 147, 149 (2d Cir. 1987) (“Agent Orange I’), cert. 
denied, 484 U.S. 1004 (1988)), the courts entertaining the 
Agent Orange suits consistently have indicated that “the most 
noticeable fact [about them] is the pervasive factual and legal 
doubt that surrounds the plaintiffs’ claims.” /d. at 149. 


The plaintiffs’ factual and legal position suffered from a 
number of serious and common weaknesses. Among them was 
(1) a complete absence of evidence that exposure to Agent 
Orange caused the plaintiffs’ illnesses, and (2) petitioners’ 
entitlement to complete immunity under the government 
contractor defense. See Agent Orange 1, 818 F.2d at 171-174. 
Then-Chief Judge Weinstein of the Eastern District of New 
York, who presided over the eventual settlement of the first 
round of Agent Orange litigation, thus “viewed the plaintiffs’ 
case as so weak as to be virtually baseless.” Agent Orange /, 
818 F.2d at 151. The Second Circuit echoed that belief when 
it affirmed the decision approving the settlement, noting “the 
pervasive difficulties faced by plaintiffs in establishing 
liability.” /bid. Indeed, Chief Judge Weinstein dismissed on 
the merits the claims of class members who opted out of the 
class to pursue individual actions (see /n re “Agent Orange” 
Prod. Liab. Litig., 611 F. Supp. 1223 (E.D.N.Y. 1985)), a 
decision that was upheld by the Second Circuit on appeal. /n 
re “Agent Orange” Prod. Liab. Litig., 818 F.2d 187 (2d Cir. 
1987), cert. denied, 487 U.S. 1234 (1988). 


2. a. The first Agent Orange suit was brought in New York 
state court in 1978, removed to federal court, and transferred to 
the Eastern District of New York. Other individual and class 
complaints followed. In 1979, the Judicial Panel on Multidis- 
trict Litigation consolidated the suits in the Eastern District of 
New York, where the litigation was assigned to then-District 
Judge Pratt. Ultimately, almost 600 class and individual actions 
became part of the Agent Orange MDL proceeding. See Agent 
Orange I, 818 F.2d at 152-153. The lead class action sought 
relief for a class of almost 2.5 million veterans who served in 
Vietnam between 1961 and 1972, including both veterans who 


4 


were then sick and the much larger group who were 
asymptomatic but assertedly were “at risk” of becoming ill 
because they hs been exposed to Agent Orange. See, e.g., Pl. 
Rep. Mem. oe Class Certification (Oct. 17, 1979), JA 47-49 
(taking the position that all Vietnam veterans “sustained some 
personal injury”). The class suit also sought relief for certain 
of the veterans’ family members. 


In 1980, Judge Pratt turned to the question whether the class 
should be certified. Although the defendants opposed 
certification, Judge Pratt, after a detailed analysis, conditionally 
granted the plaintiffs’ request to certify a class of “all persons 
exposed to Agent Orange and various members of their 
families” — a group that expressly was understood to include 
both ill and asymptomatic veterans. Jn re “Agent Orange” 
Prod. Liab. Litig., 506 F. Supp. 762, 787 (E.D.N.Y. 1980). See 
id. at 787-791. The court observed that, “[b]efore a class action 
may be maintained under Rule 23, the action must meet the 
prerequisites of Rule 23(a) and one set of the alternate 
requirements of Rule 23(b).” /d. at 787. Of particular 
importance here, Judge Pratt carefully considered the 
requirements of Rule 23(a)(4), recognizing that “[a]dequacy of 
representation depends on * * * the absence of antagonism or 
conflicting interests, and a sharing of interests between class 
representatives and absentees.” /d. at 788. Finding adequacy 
and the other elements of Rule 23(a) satisfied, Judge Pratt 
determined that class action treatment was appropriate under 
Rule 23(b)(3). 506 F. Supp. at 790, 791. See Agent Orange I, 
818 F.2d at 153. 


In 1983, after Judge Pratt was appointed to the Second 
Circuit, Chief Judge Weinstein of New York’s Eastern District 
assumed responsibility for the Agent Orange litigation. 
Because Judge Pratt had not entered a formal certification 
order, Chief Judge Weinstein revisited the question of class 
certification and — again over the defendants’ objections — 
ultimately concluded that “in the instant litigation * * * the 
desirability of class certification [is] even greater than it would 


5 


be in most mass tort litigation.” Jn re “Agent Orange” Prod 
Liab. Litig., 100 F.R.D. 718, 720(E.D.N.Y. 1983). Chief Judge 
Weinstein specifically noted that “(t]he four prerequisites [of 
Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(a)] have already been carefully analyzed by 
the court and found to exist.” /d. at 721. The court then 
concluded that certification was proper pursuant to Rule 
23(b)(3) because “the affirmative defenses and the question of 
general causation are common to the class” and “those - 
questions predominate over any questions affecting individual 
[class] members.” /d. at 724. 


Chief Judge Weinstein defined the plaintiff class broadly to 
include: 


those persons who were in the United States, New Zealand 
or Australian Armed Forces at any time from 1961 to 1972 
who were injured while in or near Vietnam by exposure to 
Agent Orange or other phenoxy herbicides * * *. The class 
also includes spouses, parents, and children of the veterans 
born before January |, 1984, directly or derivatively injured 
as a result of the exposure.

court and the parties to include both ill and unimpaired 
veterans; under the plaintiffs’ theory, all Vietnam veterans 
exposed to Agent Orange had suffered a legally cognizable 
“injury,” even if they had not manifested symptoms. '

b. As the trial date approached, the parties engaged in 
“arm’s length and adversarial, even fierce” settlement 
negotiations with the assistance of three special masters 
appointed by Chief Judge Weinstein. /n re “Agent Orange” 
Prod. Liab. Litig., 996 F.2d 1425, 1436 (2d Cir. 1993) 
(“/vy/Hartman’’), cert. denied, 510 U.S. 1140 (1994); see Agent 
Orange I, 818 F.2d at 155; P. SCHUCK, AGENT ORANGE ON 
TRIAL 144-166 (1987). The parties reached a settlement 
agreement early in the morning of May 7, 1984, on the eve of 
trial. Agent Orange I, 818 F.2d at 155. The settlement 
provided that the defendants would pay a lump sum of $180 
million into a fund to benefit the plaintiff class but did not 
provide for the formula to be used to distribute the settlement 
proceeds. See ibid. The settlement specified that “all members 
of the Class are forever barred from instituting or maintaining 
any action against any of the defendants * * * arising out of or 
relating to, or in the future arising out of or relating to, the 
subject matter of the Complaint,” and also made express that 
“(t]he class specifically includes persons who have not yet 
manifested injury.” Jn re “Agent Orange” Prod. Liab. Litig., 
597 F. Supp. 740, 864-65 (E.D.N.Y. 1984). 


Chief Judge Weinstein, in an unprecedented outreach to the

INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


The Agent Orange class was certified nearly 20 years ago. 
At the time, the courts directly confronted the question whether 
the class was adequately represented, with the issue vigorously 
contested first by the defendants and then by class objectors. 
Two district judges and the court of appeals, keenly aware of 
the judicial obligation to guard against intraclass conflicts, 
found the representation adequate. Faced with a certified class, 
petitioners settled the suit, making an unallocated $180 million 
payment to be distributed by the district court on the express 
condition that class members not initiate future Agent Orange 
litigation. 


Now, many years later, respondents have become 
dissatisfied with the district court’s distribution plan because 
they fell ill at a time when it was no longer possible for them to 
obtain direct payments from the settlement funds. Although 
respondents did not opt out of the class or object to the terms of 
the settlement prior to payment of the judgment — and although, 
like all members of the class, they benefited from the activities 
of the class assistance foundation — respondents insist that their 
current inability to receive direct cash payments shows that 
they were not adequately represented at the time of the 
settlement and that, as a consequence, they should not be bound 
by the class judgment. 


The implications of this argument are dramatic. 
Respondents’ approach would subject petitioners to new rounds 
of litigation and the prospect of additional liability after they 
already have irretrievably paid a substantial settlement on the 
express condition that they would not face future lawsuits. And 
more generally, respondents’ approach would substantially 
undermine the utility of the class action procedure by making 
it impossible ever to definitively determine that a class is 
adequately represented. 


I. This outcome is worse than unfair and unwise: it is 
legally insupportable, for several reasons. First, post-judgment 


18 


developments — here, the fact that, as matters eventuated, 
respondents became ill after direct payments from the 
settlement fund had terminated — may not be used to reopen 
litigation. After-the-fact dissatisfaction with the outcome of a 
lawsuit never has been thought to justify a collateral attack on 
a final judgment. The court of appeals nevertheless reasoned 
that, at the time of class certification, veterans who would 
become ill after direct payments from the settlement fund ended 
should have been put in a separate subclass. That conclusion 
suffers from a fatal failure of logic; at the time of certification 
all asymptomatic class members were in precisely the same 
position because it was not known or knowable when, if ever, 
any particular veteran would become ill. 


Second, and more fundamentally, barring respondents from 
relitigating adequacy of representation is fully consistent with 
the requirements of due process. The good-faith 
implementation of Fed. R. Civ. P. 23 — a rule that was applied 
by the courts in this case and that specifically requires, among 
other things, that the court itself assure the adequacy of class 
representation — suffices to “insure[] the protection of the 
interests of absent parties who are to be bound by [the 
judgment).” Hansberry, 311 U.S. at 42. At the same time, the 
balance of public and private interests that go into the due 
process equation shows that absent parties have not been denied 
due process when they are bound by determinations of 
adequacy made under Rule 23: (1) respondents’ interest in 
relitigation is limited by the Rule 23 protections themselves, 
which assure that the settlement is fair to absent parties; (2) the 
risk that the initial determination of adequacy was incorrect is 
low, as is the value of relitigation; and (3) the interests both of 
adverse parties (who have a right to count on finality) and of 
the public (which would see the class action mechanism sapped 
of much of its utility if relitigation is permitted) weigh heavily 
against allowing collateral attacks on determinations of 
adequacy. 


19 


II. Even if collateral challenges to long-settled findings of 
adequacy are permissible, the Second Circuit committed 
fundamental errors in the way it went about conducting the new 
adequacy inquiry. The court assumed that the analysis used in 
resolving a collateral attack is identical to the one that governs 
on initial direct review of the adequacy determination. But that 
plainly is wrong, because the due process balance is 
dramatically different in a collateral proceeding. The claimant 
who seeks to avoid the binding force of a final judgment and 
thereby to upset settled reliance interests must make a far more 
powerful showing of prejudicial intraclass conflicts than does 
a class member challenging adequacy of representation in the 
initial litigation. In addition, any redetermination of adequacy 
must be made on the basis of the facts and law as they existed 
at the time of the original judgment. When viewed under the 
proper standards, there is no basis for setting aside the finding 
of adequacy made in the Agent Orange — nearly two 
decades ago. 


ARGUMENT 


I. THE PRIOR FINAL DETERMINATION THAT ALL 
MEMBERS OF THE AGENT ORANGE CLASS 
WERE ADEQUATELY REPRESENTED IN THE 
ORIGINAL LITIGATION IS NOT SUBJECT TO 
COLLATERAL ATTACK 


A. Class Action Judgments Are Binding On All 
Members Of The Class And May Not Be Reopened 
On The Basis Of Developments Occurring After The 
Entry Of Final Judgment 


1. Preclusion principles apply in class litigation. The 
background principles that control this litigation are not in 
dispute. To begin with, “[a] fundamental precept of common- 
law adjudication, embodied in the related doctrines of collateral 
estoppel and res judicata, is that a ‘right, question or fact 
distinctly put in issue and directly determined by a court of 
competent jurisdiction ... cannot be disputed in a subsequent 


20 


999 


suit between the parties or their privies.’"” Montana v. United 
States, 440 U.S. 147, 153 (1979) (quoting Southern Pac. R. Co. 
v. United States, 168 U.S. 1, 48-49 (1897) (ellipses in 
original)). As a consequence, “the usual rule is that merits of 
a legal claim once decided in a court of competent jurisdiction 
are not subject to redetermination” (Kremer v. Chemical 
Constr. Corp., 456 U.S. 461, 485 (1982)), and such a judicial 
decision “precludes the parties or their privies from relitigating 
issues that were or could have been raised in that action.” 
Federated Dep't Stores v. Moitie, 452 U.S. 394, 398 (1981). 
See, e.g., Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore, 439 U.S. 322, 332- 
333 (1979). 


This preclusion of collateral litigation “is not a mere matter 
of practice or procedure inherited from a more technical time 
than ours. It is a rule of fundamental and substantial justice, of 
public policy and of private peace, which should be cordially 
regarded and enforced by the courts.” Moitie, 452 U.S. at 401 
(citation and internal quotation marks omitted). Indeed, rules 
of issue and claim preclusion are “central to the purpose for 
which civil courts have been established, the conclusive 
resolution of disputes within their jurisdictions.” Montana, 440 
U.S. at 153. The important social values those rules advance 
have often been rehearsed: barring collateral litigation protects 
parties against “the expense and vexation attending multiple 
lawsuits, conserves judicial resources, and fosters reliance on 
judicial action by minimizing the possibility of inconsistent 
decisions.” Jd. at 153-154. See, e.g., Kremer, 456 U.S. at 467 
n.6; Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90, 94 (1981); Parklane 
Hosiery, 439 U.S. at 326. This “‘public policy’” that “there be 
an end of litigation’” is so fundamental that even matters going 
to the court’s jurisdiction — and, thus, to its very authority to 
decide the case — once “fully and fairly litigated and finally 
decided” shall “‘be considered forever settled between the 
parties.’” Durfee v. Duke, 375 U.S. 106, 111 (1963) (citations 
omitted). See Chicot County Drainage Dist. v. Baxter State 
Bank, 308 U.S. 371 (1940). 


21 


There is no doubt that this principle of finality applies with 
full force in the class action context. “[U]nder elementary 
principles of prior adjudication a judgment in a properly 
entertained class action is binding on class members in any 
subsequent litigation.” Cooper v. Federal Reserve Bank of 
Richmond, 467 U.S. 867, 874 (1984). This means that “[a] 
judgment in favor of either side [in a class action] is conclusive 
in a subsequent action between them on any issue actually 
litigated and determined, if its determination was essential to 
that judgment.” Jbid. See, e.g., Phillips Petroleum Co. v. 
Shutts, 472 U.S. 797, 801 (1985); Hansberry, 311 U.S. at 41- 
42. In particular, in an action under Fed. R. Civ. P. 23, “‘[alll 
members of the class * * * are bound by the judgment entered 
in the action unless, in a Rule 23(b)(3) action, they make a 
timely election for exclusion.’” Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. 
Epstein, 516 U.S. 367, 379 (1996) (citation omitted). See 
Devlin v. Scardelletti, 122 S. Ct. 2005, 201 1 (2002) (“appealing 
the approval of a settlement is [a class member’s] only means 
of protecting himself from being bound by a disposition of his 
rights he finds unacceptable”). 


On the face of it, those principles would appear to dispose 
of this case. Respondents concededly are members of the 
Agent Orange class certified by Judges Pratt and Weinstein, 
and therefore are bound by the class judgment. The adequacy 
of class representation (and, more broadly, the propriety of 
class certification) was contested and decided both at the time 
of preliminary class certification in 1980 and at the time of final 
certification in 1983. See pages 4-5, supra. Adequacy of 
representation was contested and resolved again when objectors 
sought reconsideration of the settlement. See page 9, supra. 
And it was raised, contested, and expressly resolved by the 
Second Circuit on appeal in Agent Orange I. See pages 11-12, 
supra. The issue of adequate representation therefore should be 
“considered forever settled between the parties.” 


2. Post-settlement developments may not be used to reopen 
class judgments. The court of appeals nevertheless held that 


22 


respondents were not bound by the class judgment. In the 
court’s view, class members who did not manifest illness until 
after 1994 (the year direct cash payments from the settlement 
fund terminated) may not be treated as “proper parties” to the 
class action because “there has been no prior adequacy of 
representation determination” with regard to this limited 
subclass of veterans “whose claims arise after depletion of the 
settlement fund.” Pet. App. 14a. Starting from that hypothesis, 
the court concluded that the holdings of Amchem and Ortiz 
permitted reexamination of the prior judgment as to adequacy. 
There is however, a fatal error in this conclusion. 


a. The Second Circuit’s essential reasoning was that 
respondents must not have been adequately represented 
because, as events ultimately turned out, they did not become 
ill until after 1994 and therefore could not recover direct cash 
payments from the settlement fund. But this holding runs afoul 
of basic preclusion principles. It has never been thought that 
after-the-fact dissatisfaction with the outcome of litigation 
justifies a collateral attack on-a long-final judgment; after all, 
allowing such an attack “‘would result in creating elements of 
uncertainty and confusion and in undermining the conclusive 
character of judgments, consequences which it was the very 
purpose of the doctrine of res judicata to avoid.’” Moitie, 452 
U.S. at 398-399 (citation omitted). Cf. Rufo v. Inmates of 
Suffolk County Jail, 502 U.S. 367, 385 (1992) (modification of 
a consent decree ordinarily “should not be granted where a 
party relies upon events that actually were anticipated at the 
time it entered into [the] decree”). Indeed, the entire point of 
the preclusion principle described above is that parties do not 
get a second bite at the apple simply because they are unhappy 
about how the judgment played out over time. That is no less 
true of class actions than of other forms of litigation. 


The court of appeals compounded this basic legal mistake 
with a logical error. It concluded that class members who 
became ill after 1994 should have been put in a subclass (or let 
out of the class action altogether) because they necessarily were 


23 


disadvantaged by the settlement relative to those who 
manifested symptoms earlier. But this reasoning 1s unsound. 
The court did not say that a// asymptomatic class members had 
been inadequately represented, or that it was impermissible to 
certify a unitary class consisting of both currently ill and (some 
limited group of) asymptomatic veterans, to the contrary, the 
court accepted as correct its previous holding in Ivy/Hartman 
that class members who had not been ill at the time of

challenged on due process grounds by disgruntled class 
members. This is by no means a theoretical prospect, as this 
case shows; it will almost always be possible for class members 
to claim that they were differently situated from others in the 
class in some way that, in hindsight, could appear significant.

But this approach, too, is misguided. The determination 
that a case may proceed as a class action, if made in accordance 
with Fed. R. Civ. P. 23, binds all members of the defined class. 
Of course, any judgment may be attacked if enforcing it would 
violate due process. But “this Court is justified in saying that 
there has been a failure of due process only in those cases 
where it cannot be said that the procedure adopted{] fairly 
insures the protection of the interests of absent parties who are 
to be bound by [the judgment].” Hansberry, 311 U.S. at 42. 
And here, implementation of Rule 23’s procedures (of which 
Rule 23(a)(4) is a crucial component) provided respondents 
with all the process due by “fairly insur[ing] the protection of 
the interests of absent parties” so far as the determination of 
adequacy is concerned. 


1. Good-faith application of Rule 23 in a class action 
ordinarily ensures that binding absent class members to the 
judgment — and to the determination of adequacy that is a 
necessary part of that certification — comports with the Due 
Process Clause. It is, to be sure, a fundamental tenet of due 
process that a party may not be bound by a judgment in a 
representative suit if he or she was not “adequately 
represented” in the proceedings leading to that judgment. 
Richards v. Jefferson County, 517 U.S. 793, 794 (1996). See, 
e.g., Shutts, 472 U.S. at 812; Hansberry, 311 U.S. at 41-42. 
But having stated that principle, it remains necessary to 
determine what constitutes “adequacy” in a given context and 
to identify the considerations that make it fundamentally fair, 
and thus permissible, to bind an absent party. The Court’s 
decisions provide the answer to that inquiry: an absent party 
may be bound when the procedures in place provide a 
reasonable (or “adequate”) degree of assurance that his or her 
interests were taken into account by the decision-maker in the 
original litigation. That does not mean that the absent party 
necessarily must have received actual notice of the proceedings, 
or that the party must actually have appeared and been heard by 
the court in person, or that the party’s views must have been 


27 


presented in just the same way that they would have been 
argued had the party been able to appear in person. Instead, 
what is necessary, and what justifies binding the absent party, 
is a procedure that operates to protect the interests of that party 


in court.

and thus accorded absent class members all the process that was 
due. Rule 23 was written for the precise purpose of providing 
the procedural safeguards that would justify both the treatment 
of the case as a class action and the imposition of a judgment

counterparts.” Kahan & Silberman, The Proper Role for 
Collateral Attack in Class Actions: A Reply to Allen, Miller, 
and Morrison, 73 N.Y.U. L. REV. 1193, 1202 (1998).

C. The Balance of Public And Private Interests Further 
Demonstrates That Respondents’ Collateral Attack 
On Adequacy Should Not Be Allowed 


For the reasons we have explained, good faith compliance 
with the Rule 23 procedures, even viewed in isolation, must be 
understood fairly and reasonably — and thus permissibly — to 
safeguard the interests of absent class members regarding the 
determination of adequacy. But the case for preclusion here is 
Stronger than that. As the Court has indicated repeatedly, 
determining the process due in given circumstances requires 
achieving a “balance” of private and public interests. Mullane, 
339 U.S. at 313-314. See also, e.g, Dusenbery, 122 S. Ct. at 
701; Walters, 473 U.S. at 320-321; Mathews, 424 U.S. at 334- 
335. “At some point the benefit of an additional safeguard * * 
* in terms of increased assurance that the [governmental] action 
is just, may be outweighed by the cost.” 424 U.S. at 348. The 
factors that generally go into this balance are familiar: they 
include the nature and significance of the private interests at 
stake, the risk of error and the probable value (if any) of 
additional safeguards, and the competing interests of the public 
and other affected parties. Jd. at 334. See, e.g., Gilbert v. 
Homar, 520 U.S. 924, 931 (1997); Connecticut v. Doehr, 501 
U.S. 1, 10-11 (1991); Walters, 473 U.S. at 321. Here, given the 
specific circumstances of this case, and reviewing the full range 
of the relevant procedural due process considerations, there can 
be no doubt that binding respondents to the Agent Orange 

judgment conforms with due process.

in adequate class representation to ensure that their rights are 
safeguarded is important. But here, that interest is, in a 
significant respect, accounted for by the Rule 23 protections

A. Respondents’ Attacks On The Determination Of 
Adequacy Do Not Satisfy The Standards That Apply 
On Collateral Review 


1. The collateral nature of the review affects the due 
process inquiry. Any collateral attack on the adequacy of class 
representation, if permissible at all, must be addressed to the 
question whether it would deny absent class members due 
process now to bind them to the prior determination of 
adequacy. See, e.g., Shutts, 472 U.S. at 811-812; Kremer, 456 
U.S. at 482; Hansberry, 311 U.S. at 37. Under the Second 
Circuit’s approach, however, any showing that the class 
representation should have been held constitutionally 
inadequate on direct review also suffices to have the judgment 
as to adequacy set aside on a collateral attack. That approach 
is incorrect. Prior to final judgment, the due process question 
is whether the interests of the class representatives are closely 
enough aligned with those of the absent parties for it to be fair 
for the former to speak for the latter; after judgment, the quite 
different question is whether due process requires the reopening 
of long-closed litigation. 


Here, respondents brought their due process challenge more 
than ten years after the original Agent Orange judgment 
became final, after the adequacy of representation had been 
definitively resolved (twice, as it happens) by the district and 
appellate courts, and after petitioners had entered into a 
settlement and irretrievably paid the judgment in reliance on 
those seemingly closed judicial determinations. These changes 
in “time, place and circumstances” must weigh heavily in the 
due process balance. Mathews, 424 U.S. at 334 (internal 
quotation marks and citation omitted). Indeed, the Court has 
made just that point in the closely analogous context of a 
collateral due process attack on a criminal conviction that 
advances a claim of “actual innocence”: “because of the very 
disruptive effect that entertaining claims of actual innocence 
would have on the need for finality * * * and the enormous 
burden that having to retry such cases based on often stale 


43 


evidence often would place on the States, the threshold showing 
[to obtain relief on this ground] would necessarily be 
extraordinarily high” — far higher than the showing necessary 
to obtain relief by asserting “actual innocence” on direct 
review. Herrera v. Collins, 506 U.S. 390, 417 (1993). Cf. 
Factor VIII or IX Concentrate Blood Prod. Litig., 159 F.3d at 
1019 (emphasis added) (noting that “[t}he principle that 
jurisdictional defects may be noticed at any time is 
limited * * * by the equally important principle that litigation 
must have an end,” so that “[{a]fter a case has become final by 
exhaustion of all appellate remedies, only an egregious want of 
jurisdiction will allow the judgment to be undone”). 


The same conclusion — that the claimant must make a 
different and more powerful showing to establish a due process 
violation in collateral civil proceedings — is compelled by the 
factors customarily considered when striking the due process 
balance. First, for reasons suggested above (at 35, supra), 
respondents’ interest in complaining about the adequacy of 
class representation is limited by the Rule 23 procedures. 
Second, the risk of error is diminished in collateral proceedings 
because full appellate review already has confirmed that class 
representation was adequate. Third, and perhaps most 
significantly, petitioners’ interest in the finality of the Agent 
Orange settlement, and the more general public interest in the 
finality of judgments, does not come into play at all on direct 
appeal but is of overwhelming importance at the time of 
collateral review. See pages 37-39, supra. For all of these 
reasons, a class member who seeks to establish a due process 
“adequacy” violation in collateral proceedings must clear a far 
higher bar than does an objector who advances a similar 
complaint during the course of the original litigation.

B. If Adequacy Of Representation May Be Reexamined 
On Collateral Review, The Determination Must Be 


Made As Of The Time Of The Original Class 
Certification 


The Second Circuit committed an additional, fundamental 
error in applying the due process standard. The question on 
collateral review logically must be whether the certification 
decision was correct in light of the law and facts as they existed 
at the time of the original litigation: courts “do not, of course, 
judge the propriety of class certification by hindsight.” Falcon, 
457 U.S. at 160. See Uhi, 309 F.3d at 985 (rejecting argument 
that the class should be viewed “from an ex post perspective”). 
Here, the court of appeals departed from that principle by 
engaging in a hindsight review; the analysis below avowedly 
took account of post-settlement changes in the legal and factual 
landscape, including significant intervening changes in the law. 

The court reasoned erroneously that respondents must not 
have been adequately represented because, as matters 
ultimately worked out, they became ill after 1994 and therefore 
did not obtain direct payments from the settlement fund. As has 
been noted, however, at the time of certification and settlement 
all asymptomatic class members were in precisely the same 
position. It is only by using an after-the-fact review that it is 
possible to say that particular class members were (in a relative 
sense) disadvantaged by the settlement formula. Yet 
representation that was adequate at the time cannot later be 
rendered inadequate simply because, as events transpire, some 
class members obtain greater relief than others.

the effect on the settlement of Amchem and Ortiz (see Pet. App.
