STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


Respondents Joe Isaacson and Daniel Stephenson served 
in Vietnam, where they were exposed to “Agent Orange.” 
Isaacson volunteered for the Air Force, serving as a crewman at 
one of the airfields that supported Operation Ranch Hand, the 
code name for the “Agent Orange” missions. (JA at 291.) 
Stephenson, a helicopter pilot, was exposed to “Agent Orange” 
both at his base and in-country on the ground. (JA at 254.) 
In 1996 Isaacson was diagnosed with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma 
and in 1998 Stephenson was diagnosed with multiple myeloma. 
Both diseases are rare, deadly cancers which have been related 
to “Agent Orange” exposure. (JA at 319-325.) 


Respondents filed timely state law claims against 
Petitioners, who manufactured the chemicals which caused their 
cancers. Petitioners moved to dismiss these claims on the basis 
of res judicata, asserting that they had been settled years before 
Respondents’ cancers were diagnosed. They assert this 
settlement as a complete bar, even though it entitled Respondents 
to no compensation at its inception, no eligibility for 
compensation while it was being administered, and no potential 
for compensation after Respondents were diagnosed with their 
cancers. 


After the war ended, Respondents were fortunate to return 
to civilian life in good health. Although some other Vietnam 
veterans claimed injuries related to their exposure to Agent 
Orange, neither Stephenson, who became a civilian helicopter

B. The Settlement, The Settlement Class and Fairness 
Hearings 


Six days after the opt out deadline expired on May 7, 1984, 
Petitioners and class counsel reached a settlement of the class 
action. All of the representatives, who by that time had been 
designated, claimed significant present injuries resulting from 
“Agent Orange.” (JA at 80, 127, 129.); P. Schuck, Agent Orange 
on Trial (1987) at 139-140.’ 


5. Far from understanding that a// veterans who served in Vietnam 
would be part of the class, counsel for Petitioner Diamond Shamrock 
recognized that “half will not be a part of the class at all.”"(JA at 81.) 
Petitioners’ counsel were most concerned that the notice to persons 
“claiming injury” was too subjective to bind absent class members to an 
adverse judgment. (JA at 84-85.) They would have had no such concern 
if the class was meant to include all veterans exposed in Vietnam. 


6. This Rule 23(c)(2) notice was actually sent out on March 9, 
1984, 597 F. Supp. at 756, less than two months before the opt out 
deadline. The dissemination of the notice ran into innumerable problems. 
For instance, 57,096 mailed notices were returned as undeliverable with 
no follow up. The myriad problems encountered in giving out the notice 
are described in JA at 171-182. 


7. The Seventh Amended Complaint, filed on Feb. 17, 1984 
(Docket entry #1864), was the active pleading at the time of 
the settlement, and it defined the class as those “who claim injury.” 
Para. 25 It identified six representative plaintiff veterans, each of whom 
claimed “severe and permanent disabling injuries, diseases, physical 
disorders and disfigurement, and in some cases, death.” /d. Para.39.

. As part of the settlement, Petitioners agreed to pay $180 
million dollars to the class. The new class representatives agreed 
not only to release “present claims” but also all claims of “future”

to Agent Orange.* Class representatives agreed that no new 
23(c)(2) opt out notice need be given to “future” plaintiffs, and

P. Schuck, at 165; (JA at 88, 96.) The class representatives did

of any distribution plan before the settlement was given final 
approval. 


As a result, the court approved a notice pursuant to 
Rule 23(e) which included no right to opt out of the settlement 
and no information about what absent class members might 
recover under the settlement. The Rule 23(e) notice instructed 
veterans in bold letters that: “THE ONLY QUESTION TO BE 
DECIDED AT THIS TIME IS WHETHER THIS 
SETTLEMENT AS A WHOLE IS FAIR, REASONABLE AND 
ADEQUATE.” (JA at 109.) (All caps in original).

certification, this 23(e) notice was neither broadcast nor 
televised. Jn Re Agent Orange, 597 F. Supp. 740, 763 (E.D.N.Y. 
1984). It did not call attention to the fact that the settlement 
class now included “future” veterans who did not claim injuries.

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Neither due process nor Rule 23 empowers a court to 
predetermine the preclusive effect of its own judgment on absent 
class members. This can only take place in the setting of 
de novo collateral review. The right to collateral review is 
embodied in this court’s prior holdings, over sixty years of case 
law, the very notes to Rule 23, the comments of every 
contemporaneous commentator on Rule 23, the Restatement of 
Judgments, and virtually every court that has been asked to 
review whether a prior class action court has accorded an absent 
class member with adequate representation or notice. There is 
no reason for this Court to revisit this well-settled rule. 


To determine whether a prior judgment should bind an 
absent class member, a court is obliged to review whether due 
process protections were met: whether the class member was in 
fact adequately represented at all times in the litigation, was 
given constitutionally sufficient notice, had an opportunity to 
be heard, and had an opportunity to opt out from a class certified 
under Rule 23(b)(3). If he did not, then the absent class member 
was never properly before the court and no judgment can be 
final as to that absent class member. 


_Respondents were deprived of all of these basic rights 
during the course of the Agent Orange litigation. While a notice 
pursuant to Rule 23(c)(2) was sent out by the court, this notice 
gave Respondents no opportunity to opt out of the class, as they 
could not have known it was addressed to them. As “future” 
claimants, it is doubtful whether Respondents could ever be 
given constitutionally required notice. Nor could the Rule 23(e) 
settlement notice cure the deficiency in failing to give 
Respondents 23(c)(2) notice. Yet, even this notice failed to 
apprize Respondents of the most crucial fact they needed to 
know in order to assess whether to object or appeal the settlement 


16 


—— exactly what recovery they might expect to receive from the 
settlement. 


The Agent Orange judgment further lacked due process 
because Respondents had no separate representation, as 
required by Amchem Prods. Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591 (1997), 
and because they had no representation at all when the class 
was originally certified. When class representatives were 
subsequently designated, they all had very real present injuries. 
Their interests were neither aligned with nor could they be 
aligned with Respondents. The class representatives both wanted 
(and received) immediate payments for their injuries, while 
Respondents had no need to settle, particularly for nuisance value 
or no recovery at all, and could afford to wait for scientific studies 
in progress to be completed. 


Further, though a court’s Rule 23(a)(4) determination 
of adequacy of representation is made early in the litigation and 
is inherently tentative, “adequate representation” in the 
constitutional sense must be present at all times. In Agent 
Orange, the class representatives failed to protect Respondents’ 
needs from the day they were appointed through the day the 
settlement funds were exhausted. Until Respondents were 
stricken with their deadly cancers, they had no reason to be 
before any court. And in the absence of due process, they were 
never properly before any court. In this setting, Petitioners’ 
request to value “finality” over due process rights rings hollow. 
At the time Petitioners entered into the settlement, they knew 
Respondents, as “future” claimants, could seek collateral review. 


Indeed, Petitioners effectively invited this review by 
agreeing to “interpret” Respondents into the settlement class 
after the opt-out period expired. They remained deaf to pleas 
for separate representation of “futures,” while at the same time 
moving to deplete the last $10 million that could have been 
used for Respondents’ benefit. Under any standard for collateral 
review, the lack of notice and opportunity to be heard, the 
complete absence of representation, and the inability to opt out,

res judicata.

4. Petitioners’ Fears For The Legal System Are More 
Imagined Than Real; The Real Catastrophe Would 
Be If Collateral Review, An Essential Element in the 
Array of Due Process Protections For Absent Class 
Members, Is Limited. 


Petitioners and their amici cite a parade of horrors which 
will be visited upon the nation’s judicial system should 
Respondents prevail. Yet, they present no evidence that this 
has occurred in the past nor any likelihood that it will occur in 
the future., “[C]ollateral attack has been used relatively sparingly 
to attack the adequacy of representation.” Mollie Murphy, 
“The Intersystem Class Settlement; of Comity, Consent 
and Collision,” 47 U. Kan. L. Rev. #13, 469 (1999). 
One commentator reported that his research revealed only 44 
federal and state cases since the 1966 amendments to Rule 23 
in which absent class members collaterally attacked a judgment 
based on inadequate representation. Woolley, supra at 443, 
n. 268.

the outcome of litigation would likely sit back and refuse to opt 
out or object, just to seek collateral review. Opting out is simple. 
Even objectors in fairness hearings can challenge the fairness 
and adequacy of a settlement, whereas collateral attacks are 
narrow in scope and more costly to bring, requiring attorneys to 
engage in extensive litigation of res judicata questions before 
even getting to the merits of a case. G. Miller, Full Faith and 
Credit to settlements in Overlapping Class Actions: A Reply to 
Professors Kahan and Silberman, 73 N.Y.U. L. Rev. 1167, 1185; 
Woolley, supra, at 444. In this case, for example, it has been 
approximately four years since Respondents brought their cases
