BACKGROUND 


The Grand Jury charges that at all times material 
to this indictment: 


1. Charles Thomas Sell, the defendant herein, was 
a dentist practicing dentistry in St. Louis County, 
Missouri. 


2. Mary Sell, the defendant herein, was the office 
manager for Dr. Charles T. Sell. 


3.° The defendants directed their employees to 
place information in claim forms which they knew was 
false. 


4. The Missouri Medicaid Program (Medicaid) was 
jointly funded by both the federal government and the 
State of Missouri and was a state health care program 
as defined in Title 42, United States Code, Section 


8 


1320a-7(h). The Department of Social Services, Division 
of Medical Services (hereafter referred to as DMS) 
administered the Medicaid program in Missouri. 


5. Medicaid paid the cost of certain health care 
expenses for qualified individuals — Medicaid recipients. 


6. DMS contracted with GTE data services (hereaf- 
ter referred to as GTE), to serve as its fiscal agent to 
handle the receipt and processing of Medicaid claims in 
Missouri. The claims were received and processed in 
Jefferson City, Missouri. 


7. Upon application by a health care provider and 
approval by DMS, DMS assigned provider numbers to 
various health care providers, including dentists. The 
provider number allowed the health care provider to bill 
Medicaid directly for services rendered by the enrolled 
provider to patients who were eligible and received 
medical services under the Medicaid program. 


8. Enrolled providers agreed to abide by Medicaid 
policies and procedures. Medicaid policy, with respect to 
dentists, required that the enrolled provider only bill for 
the services actually performed by the provider. 


9. An authorized provider of dental services was 
reimbursed for furnishing dental services to Medicaid 
recipients upon submission of an application for pay- 
ment to the Medicaid program. 


10. Payment for dental services billed on an 
application are determined in part, by procedure codes. 
Procedure codes are numbers which are placed on the 
application for payment and identify a specific dental 
service.

INDICTMENT 
BACKGROUND 


The Grand Jury charges that at all times material to 
this indictment: 


1. Dr. Charles Thomas Sell, D.D.S., the defendant 
herein, was a dentist practicing dentistry in St. Louis 
County, Missouri. 


2. Mary Sell, the defendant herein, was the wife of 
and office manager for defendant Dr. Sell. 


Medicaid 

3. The Missouri Medicaid Program (Medicaid) was 
jointly funded by both the federal government and the 
State of Missouri and was a state health care program as 
defined-in Title 42, United States Code, Section 1320a- 
7(h). The Department of Social Services, Division of 
Medical Services (hereafter referred to as DMS), adminis- 
tered the Medicaid program in Missouri. 


13 


4. Medicaid paid the cost of certain health care 
expenses for qualified individuals, referred to on occasion 
as Medicaid recipients. 

5. DMS contracted with GTE data services (hereaf- 
ter referred to as GTE), to serve as its fiscal agent and to 
handle the receipt and processing of Medicaid claims in 
Missouri. The claims were received and processed in 
Jefferson City, Missouri. : 


6. Upon application by a health care provider and 
approval by DMS, DMS assigned provider numbers to 
various health care providers, including dentists. The 
provider number allowed the health care provider to bill 
Medicaid directly for services rendered by the enrolled 
provider to Medicaid recipients, who had received medical 
services under the Medicaid program. 


7. Enrolled providers agreed to abide by Medicaid 
policies and procedures. Medicaid policy, with respect to 
dentists, required that the enrolled provider only bill for 
the services actually performed by the provider. 


8. To obtain reimbursement, authorized providers 
were required to submit an application for payment, or a 
claim form, to the Medicaid program. Payments for dental 
services billed on an application or claim form were 
determined, in part, by procedure codes. Procedure codes 
are numbers that are placed on the application for pay- 
ment or claim form and identify a specific dental service.

9. Aetna, American Commercial Terminal, American 


Medical Security, Blue Cross/Blue Shield, Delta Dental, 
Provident Life & Accident, Prudential, and Western Union

were private health care benefit programs or insurance 
companies, that reimbursed providers for specified ser- 
vices rendered to persons insured by their respective 
companies. These Private Insurers only paid for those 
services actually provided to the insured patient. 


10. To obtain reimbursement, authorized providers 
were required to submit an application for payment, or a 
claim form, to the Private Insurers. Payments for dental 
services were determined, in part, by procedure codes. 
Procedure codes are numbers that are placed on the

specific dental service.

THE SCHEME TO DEFRAUD 


11. - It was the purpose of the scheme that the defen- 
dants would obtain money and property by means of false 
or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises. As 
part of the scheme, the defendants, and their employees at 
their direction, submitted false or fraudulent claims to 
Medicaid and Private Insurers to obtain payments for 
dental services which they knew defendant Dr. Sell had 
not rendered. 


12. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants, and their employees at their direction, created and 


15 


maintained patient files which reflected services which 
had not been rendered. 


13. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants, and their employees at their direction, provided 
false and fraudulent information, to Medicaid and Private 
Insurers, when Medicaid or Private Insurers asked for 
justification for the claims submitted by the defendants. 


14. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants altered x-rays to justify resin and amalgam fillings 
that defendant Dr. Sell had not provided, but for which the 
defendants billed Medicaid and Private Insurers. 


15. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants, and their employees at their direction, submitted 
the x-rays of one patient to justify services provided to 
another patient. 


16. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants, and their employees at their direction, routinely 
“maxed out” the insurance policies of patients within a 
short period of time by billing for services that defendant 
Dr. Sell did not render. 


17. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants, and their employees at their direction, billed 
Medicaid and the Private Insurers for covered or reim- 
bursable services when defendant Dr. Sell had actually 
provided a service that was not covered or was covered at 
a lower rate of reimbursement. 


18. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants, and their employees at their direction, submitted, 
or caused to be submitted, questionnaires, that had been 
sent to patients by the Private Insurers to verify that 
defendant Dr. Sell had provided dental services to the 


16

contained in the questionnaires was false at the time the 
questionnaires were submitted. 


19. It was further part of the scheme that the defen-

Insurers, letters, that the defendants knew falsely and 
fraudulently represented that defendant Dr. Sell had 
provided dental services. 


20. It was further part of the scheme that the defen- 
dants routinely waived co-payments from the patients of 
defendant Dr. Sell and concealed this fact from the Private 
Insurers.

AFFIDAVIT OF C. ROBERT CLONINGER, M.D. 


C. ROBERT CLONINGER, M.D., being first duly 
sworn upon his oath, states: 


1. I am over 21 years of age; I have personal know!l- 
edge of and am competent to testify to the matters set 
forth in this Affidavit. 


2. I am the Wallace Renard Professor of Psychiatry 
& Genetics at Washington University School of Medicine 
in St. Louis and the Director of the Center of Psychobiol- 
ogy of Personality at Washington University School of 
Medicine in St. Louis. A copy of my curriculum vitae is 
attached as Exhibit A. 


3. This Affidavit reflects my expert opinion as a 
psychiatrist regarding the treatment of Dr. Charles 
Thomas Sell who is diagnosed as having Delusional 
Disorder. 

4. Unlike Schizophrenia, Delusional Disorder is a 
rare condition, so few psychiatrists have extensive experi- 

ence with this. 

5. The basis for my opinion is that I have personally 
treated several cases of Delusional Disorder and have 
carried out a systematic review of the literature, including 
a computerized search of published literature through the 
National Library of Medicine from 1966 to present, and a 
review of psychiatric textbooks. See, for example, Chapter 
12 of the TExTBOOK OF PSYCHIATRY, Schizophrenia, 


31 


Schizophreniform Disorder, and Delusional (Paranoid) 
Disorders (Talbott, et al. eds.) attached as Exhibit B. 


6. The only systematic data that I could locate about 
the treatment of a large series of cases of Delusional 
Disorder have been reported by Opjordsmoen and Ret- 
terstol (PSYCHOPATHOLOGY, Volume 26, pages 90-94, 1993), 
a copy of which is attached as Exhibit C. 


7. Opjordsmoen and Retterstol reported “The effect 
of neuroleptics in the treatment of schizophrenia is well 
established. Some reports indicate that at least pimozide 
may be effective in delusional disorder as well. In this 
article a long-term follow up study of 72 first-admitted 
patients with delusional disorder is described. Outcome in 
the group admitted during 1946-1948 was compared with 
that of patients admitted during 1958-1961. We did not 
find a more favorable outcome in the group admitted after 
the introduction of neuroleptics.” 


8. In other words, there is no evidence that neurolep- 
tics are beneficial for patients with Delusional Disorder. 


9. Although neuroleptics are often recommended by 
physicians for control of agitation or psychotic symptoms, 
there are no data to justify this as a benefit to patients 
with Delusional Disorder. 


10. This finding is consistent with my own experi- 
ence; whereas neuroleptics are often beneficial in schizo- 
phrenia, they are not beneficial in Delusional Disorder. 


11. Dr. Sell himself has been treated with antipsy- 
chotic medication in the past by Mr. McKinney at Barnes 
Hospital. 


32 


12. Dr. Sell’s delusions did not respond as evidenced 
by his immediately discontinuing treatment with the 
medication and_never returning to see Dr. McKinney. 


13. Dr. Sell regarded that treatment as a terrible 
experience then and now. 


14. This would not constitute an adequate trial of 
the medication in a patient with schizophrenia, but nei- 
ther does it provide any basis to think he would be an 
exception to the rule that neuroleptics are not beneficial in 
Delusional Disorder. 


15. More generally, neuroleptics may be indicated 
for acute control of agitation or dangerousness, but they 
always are associated with risk of side affects. 


16. Their proposed use in Dr. Sell must be consid- 
ered from the point of view of benefit versus risk and with 
regard to his consent. 


—- 17. In my opinion, I do not see any justification for 
involuntary treatments that carry any risk at all in the 
absence of documented evidence of benefit to the person. 


18. Accordingly, in my opinion, the treatment of Dr. 
Sell should be limited to basic support and voluntary 
symptomatic treatment. 


19. This would indicate a safe supportive milieu with 
access to exercise and reading material. 


20. The voluntary symptomatic treatment should 
include access to continued pharmacotherapy with antide- 
pressants, which he has found helpful to his mood and 
energy level in the past.

O DELUSIONAL (PARANOID) DISORDERS 


Delusional (paranoid) disorders constitute a small but 
important group of disorders. The DSM-III-R includes 
persecutory, jealous, erotomanic, grandiose, and somatic 
subtypes, all unified by the presence of a dominant, well- 
systematized, and encapuslated nonbizarre delusion 
accompanied by affect appropriate to the delusion; person- 
ality deterioration is minimal. 


History 


The term paranoia has ancient roots. From the Greek 
para nous, meaning “a mind beside itself,” the term was 
originally used in Greek literature to describe insanity or 
“craziness.” The term fell into disuse but was revived in 
the early 19th century by German psychiatrists who were 
interested in disorders characterized by delusions of 
persecution and grandeur (Lewis 1970; Tanna 1974). 
Kahlbaum, in 1863, used the term to describe a chronic 
delusional condition. Kraepelin separated paranoia from 
dementia praecox and, like Kahlbaum, was concerned with 
longitudinal course. Kraepelin gradually altered his 
concept of paranoia, so that by the eighth revision of his 
Lehrbach der Psychiatric, he had restricted the term to 
describe those with systematized delusions, an absence of 
hallucinations, and a prolonged course without recovery 
but not generally leading to mental deterioration. He also 
identified paraphrenia as an intermediate group of para- 
noid disorders between dementia praecox and paranoia 


. 94 


characterized by unremitting systematized delusions and 
hallucinations without progression to dementia. The latter 
term is still used commonly in Britain. Bleuler agreed that 
paranoia was separate from dementia praecox but differed 
from Kraepelin in maintaining that hallucinations could 
occur in some cases. He was particularly interested in the 
psychological development of the disorder. 


Modern Scandinavian practice is to regard delusional 
disorders as reactive, arising in persons with vulnerable 
constitutions subjected to stress. Their term, paranoid 
psychotic reaction, would include contemporary American 
concepts of paranoia, but also schizophreniform and 
schizoaffective disorders. 


Contemporary American thought on the disorder is 
represented in DSM-III and is remarkably similar to the 
definition put forth by Kraepelin in 1912 and 1919. The 
ICD-9 defines paranoia similarly. Winokur (1977) sug- 
gested that paranoid disorder be renamed delusional 
disorder to rid the diagnosis of the ambiguity inherent in 
the term paranoia, which most persons construe to mean 
persecutory. Winokur and others have demonstrated that 
delusions found in paranoid disorders are not restricted to 
delusions of persecution or jealousy. These suggestions 
have now been incorporated into the revision of DSM-III 
(1987). Kendler (1980) has further proposed that in the 
absence of hallucinations, the term simple delusional 
disorder be used and that when hallucinations are present 
the term hallucinatory delusional disorder be used. 


Epidemiology 


Little is known about the true incidence and prevalence of 
delusional disorder. Much of our knowledge is based on 


95 


retrospective data of hospitalized patients and may not be 
generalizable. Kendler (1982) recently reviewed 17 reports 
on the frequency of paranoia for hospital admissions. He 
determined that delusional disorder constituted between 
one and four percent of psychiatric admissions and be- 
tween two and seven percent of admissions for functional 
psychosis. He calculated the incidence of first admissions 
for paranoia to be between 1 and 3 per 100,000 per year 
and the prevalence to be between 24 and 30 per 100,000 
population. From these reports, Kendler concludes that 
paranoia is an illness predominantly manifesting itself in 
middle to late adult life with a peak frequency of first 
admissions between 35 and 55 years of age. Patients are 
more likely to be female than male, with most reports 
showing that between 36 percent and 48 percent of first 
admissions are men. Sixty to 75 percent of patients first 
admitted are married, but up to one-third are widowed, 
divorced, or separated at the time of admission. Patients 
with the disorder are judged to be relatively socially 
disadvantaged, both in economic and educational terms, 
and are often immigrants. Kendler notes that these 
demographic characteristics are unlike those for schizo- 
phrenia or affective disorder and consistent with the 
hypothesis that delusional disorder is a distinct entity. 


These data differ from those presented by Winokur 
(1977) in a chart review of 29 paranoics admitted to a 
state psychiatric hospital over a 50-year period. The 29 
patients constituted 0.14 percent of all hospital admis- 
sions. Like Kraepelin, Winokur found that males pre- 
dominated 70:30. He also found that 94 percent had an 
age of onset between ages 20 to 49, that over half had been 
ill more than four years at the time of admission, and that 
most patients had a satisfactory work history. In his 
study, more than half had [Qs less than 90. 


96 


Etiology 


Little is known about the genesis of delusional disorders. 
As one would expect, most theories involve genetics, the 
environment, or psychodynamics. 


The weight of evidence suggests that delusional 
disorder is unrelated to either schizophrenia or affective 
disorders. In a recent review, Kendler (1980) found no 
increase in risk for affective illness in families of paranoid 
probands; two of three family studies reporting on the risk 
for schizophrenia showed a two-fold increased risk, while 
the other showed no increase. In a reanalysis of the 
Copenhagen sample of the Danish adoption study of 
schizophrenia, Kendler et al. (1982) found that cases of 
schizophrenia spectrum disorders (schizotypal personality 
and schizophrenia) were strongly -concentrated in the 
biologic relatives of schizophrenic adoptees but not in 
adoptees with delusional disorder. A recent family study of 
32 paranoics by Watt (1985) also found an increased risk 
for schizophrenia in first degree relatives of schizophrenics 
but not for paranoid or depressed probands. 


Apparently, patients with delusional disorder differ 
from schizophrenics premorbidly as well. In three relevant 
studies (Kendler 1980), they are noted to be more extro- 
verted, dominant, and hypersensitive, while schizophrenic 


patients premorbidly tend to be schizoid, introverted, or 


submissive. Unfortunately, there are no comparisons with 
affective disorders. 


It is possible that psychosocial stressors have a role in 
the etiology of delusional disorder. Certain rare paranoid 
conditions illustrate this possibility. Induced psychotic 
disorder (shared paranoid disorder), or “folie a deux,” occurs 
when delusions are transferred from one individual to 
another. Usually the dominant person in the relationship 


97 


develops a delusion; it is later passively adopted by the 
other. Migration psychoses are described in persons 
migrating to foreign countries. Certainly, persons in whom 
paranoia is prone to develop may be more likely to immi- 
grate than others, but it is also possible that the particular 
psychosocial stress associated with immigration plays a 
causal role in the development of the psychosis. Prison 
psychosis has been described in which isolation in prison, 
especially solitary confinement, may lead to paranoia. 
Querulent paranoia is a special form of paranoia charac- 
terized by litigiousness and is believed by Scandinavian 
investigators to be a psychogenic disorder in which 
unlucky personal experiences precipitate paranoia in 
persons with deviant personalities. How, or why, these 
situations should lead to the development of paranoia is 
unclear. 


The psychodynamic origin of paranoia stems from 
Freud’s analysis of the Schreber case (Freud 1958a). Freud 
believed persecutory delusions to stem from unconscious 
homosexual urges defended against by denial and projec- 
tion. However, there is apparently no increase in delu- 
sional disorder among homosexuals and vice versa. 


Clinical Features and Differental Diagnosis 


Delusional disorders, according to DSM-III-R (see Table 
7), consistent of nonbizarre delusions lasting at least one 
month and involving situations that occur in real life, 
behavior that is not obviously odd or bizarre apart from 
the delusion or its ramifications, absence of schizophrenic 
symptoms such as bizarre delusions or grossly disorgan- 
ized speech or behavior, no prominent hallucinations, and 
absence of an affective disorder or organic mental disor- 
der. The core feature, however, is the presence of a 


98 


well-systematized nonbizarre delusion — a fixed false belief 
in the presence of an otherwise clear sensorium. Winokur 
(1977) found delusions of persecution, reference, and 
jealousy to be the most common while hypochondriacal 
delusions and delusions of grandeur are less common. 
Sexual problems and depressive symptoms are frequent 
and patients may show overtalkativeness or circumstan- 
tiality, especially when discussing their delusions. In his 
study, the patients were not considered fussy or obses- 
sional by their families, but were considered to have been 
caronically jealous or suspicious. Interestingly, Winokur 
(1985) found these traits to be typical of family members 
as well.

Associated features may include anger, social isolation 
and seclusiveness, eccentric behavior, suspiciousness, 
hostility, and rarely violence. Many patients become litigious 
and end up as lawyers’ clients rather than as psychiatrists’ 
patients. 


According to DSM-III-R delusional disorder includes 
the following subtypes: persecutory, jealous, erotomanic, 
grandiose, and somatic. Unspecified type is a residual 
category for patients who cannot be subtyped in the 
previous groups. Patients ill less than one month would be 
classified as either having a brief reactive psychosis or an 
atypical psychosis. In DSM-III this would have been 
classified as an acute paranoid disorder. The DSM-III 
category shared paranoid disorder, or “folie a deux,” occurs 
when two or more persons share a delusion. This is a rare 
disorder and its incidence and prevalence are unknown. In 
DSM-III-R it is termed induced psychotic disorder and is 
grouped with psychotic disorders not elsewhere classified, 
rather than with delusional disorder. 


Several variants of delusional disorder have been 
described and include (in addition to those identified 
earlier): conjugal paranoia, which is limited to the delu- 
sion about a spouse’s perceived infidelity; erotomani (De 
Clerambault’s syndrome), wherein a person, usually female, 
develops the belief that another person, usually an older 
male of social importance, is in love with her; hypochon- 
driacal paranoia, wherein a patient has the unshakable 
belief of having a physical illness; Capgras syndrome, 
wherein a patient believes a person closely related to him 
or her has been replaced by a double; and Fregoli syn- 
drome, in which the patient identifies a familiar person in 
various other people he or she encounters. The patient 


100 


may maintain that although there is no physical resem- 
blance between the familiar person and others, they are 
nonetheless psychologically identical. It is important for a 
clinician to consider schizophrenia or an organic mental 
disorder as the cause of these variants. The following case

Case Example 


HM. is a 64-year-old electrician who lives with his wife of 
43 years. Although his father was alcoholic, and his family 
poor, H.M. was well adjusted as a child, achieved good 
grades in school, and participated in sports activities, 
although he was considered somewhat aloof and distant ~ 
his peers. After graduating from high school he ha 
married and joined the Army, serving honorably in World 
War II. After the war he worked as an electrician, eventu- 
ally opening his own business. Although hard-working, he 
was viewed as overly rigid and humorless by his family 
and had few friends. H.M. and his wife had three children, 
all of whom were healthy and emotionally stable. 


Several years after returning from the war, he began 
to suspect his wife of infidelity and over the ensuing decades 
continued to be convinced that his wife was constantly 
involved with other men. Although he had never seen her 
with another man, he was nevertheless convinced by 
trivial evidence such as frequently washed bed linen, spots 
on his wife’s undergarments, or tire tracks in the driveway 
that did not belong there. He had accused his wife of 
placing sleeping pills in his coffee at night 8) that she 
would be able to leave the house for a liaison with her 
lover after he had fallen asleep. He maintained that on 


101 


several occasions his wife actually had sexual relations 
with another man in the same bed where he lay sleeping. 
On one particular occasion, he noted awakening to find the 
bed sheets in disarray and his shorts pulled down to his 
knees in what he believed was his wife’s lover’s attempt to 
harass him. He had also claimed that neighbors had 
commented to him about the large number of men who 
visited his wife when he was away. 


His wife first became aware of his suspiciousness and 
jealousy early in their marriage and steadfastly main- 
tained that there was no basis for the accusations. No 
amount of reassurance could alter her husband’s convic- 
tions, however. She reported that during their marriage 
her husband’s beliefs would wax and wane, alternating 
with periods of relative normalcy. Although she continued 
to love and care for him, his wife admitted that the delu- 


sional beliefs had strained the marriage and had led to 
several separations. 


H.M. voluntarily presented for hospitalization report- 
ing that he was tired of his wife calling him “crazy” and 
wishing to settle the matter. During the hospital stay he 
was friendly and appropriate, but persisted with the 
unshakable belief that his wife had been unfaithful to him. 
There was no evidence of hallucinations. A trial of antip- 
sychotic medication did not alter his belief. 


Diagnosis 


Because delusional symptoms are common and frequently 
accompany both functional and organic illnesses, a careful 
diagnostic assessment is necessary. First, a thorough 
physical and history must rule out organic causes for 
delusional disorder. Delusional symptoms have been found 


102 


in a variety of organic illnesses including alcoholism; 
amphetamine-, cocaine-, and other-drug-induced states; 
dementia; and infectious, metabolic, and endocrine disor- 
ders (Manschreck and Petri 1978). 


Routine lab tests may be helpful in ruling out organic 
causes. If mass lesions are suspected, CT or MRI may be 
useful in selected cases but are not routinely necessary. 


The major differential remains in separating delu- 
sional disorder from affective disorder, schizophrenia, and 
paranoid personality. The chief distinction is that in 
delusional disorders, a full depressive or manic syndrome 
is absent, developed after the psychotic symptoms, or was 
brief induration relative to the duration of the psychotic 
symptoms,. Unlike schizophrenia, delusional disorder is 
characterized by nonbizarre delusions and generally either 
absence of hallucinations or hallucinations that are not 
prominent. Furthermore, patients with delusional disorder 
do not develop other schizophrenic symptoms, such as 
incoherence or grossly disorganized behavior, and person- 
ality is generally preserved in areas not involving the 
delusion. Persons with paranoid personality are suspicious 
and hypervigilant, but do not have delusions. 


Treatment (Pharmacology and Psychotherapy) 


There are no systematic data on treatment approaches 
and results in delusional disorder. Nonetheless, a combi- 
nation of psychosocial and physical measures seems 
prudent. The initial obstacle is getting the patient to the 
physician. Because of distrust and suspiciousness this may 
be particularly difficult, and might account for the low 
percentage of cases reported by physicians. Hospitaliza- 
tion is indicated if a potential for danger is present; 


103 


otherwise outpatient management is advisable. In some 
cases, involuntary hospitalization will be mandatory, if the 
threat of self-harm or harm to others is present. Unfortu- 
nately, involuntary hospitalization may increase distrust 
and resentment and perhaps fuel a patient’s persecutory 
delusions. 


Tact and skill are necessary to persuade a patient to 
accept treatment. It may help to first convince the patient 
that he or she should have treatment for associated 
depressive symptoms or anxiety and not his or her delu- 
sions. Once a therapeutic relationship has been estab- 
lished, the physician may then gently challenge the 
patient’s beliefs by showing how they interfere with the 
patient’s life. The physician must neither condemn nor 
collude in the beliefs. Also, the patient should be assured 
of confidentiality and the physician should not discuss 
matters with the patient’s family without the patient’s 
awareness. 


Insight-oriented psychotherapy and group therapy are 
generally not recommended because suspiciousness and 
hypersensitivity may lead to misinterpretation. Some 
clinicians, however, believe that dynamic approaches can 
be helpful (Meissner 1986). Needless to say, any psychoso- 
cial treatment recommendations are based on clinical 
observation, not empirical evidence. 


Antipsychotic medication may be useful, particularly 
for accompanying anxiety, agitation, and psychosis. 
Although many investigators are pessimistic, others 
suggest that specific forms of delusional disorder, for 
example, monohypochondriacal paranoia, may have a good 
response to antipsychotic medication (Munro 1978). Any of 
the standard antipsychotics may be used, the type and 


104 


dose of which depending on age and side effects. Because 
patients may be suspicious of medication, depot forms may 
be helpful, once it is determined that the patient benefits 


from the medication. 


Other treatments have been tried, including electrocon- 
vulsive therapy, insulin shock therapy, and psychosurgery, 
but results have not been encouraging, and these ap- 
proaches are not recommended. 


Course and Prognosis 


Kahlbaum and Kraepelin both recognized the importance 
of longitudinal follow-up and both believed patients with 
delusional disorder to have a chronic, unremitting course. 
Winokur (1977) found that 79 percent of hospitalized 
patients were discharged to the community (in contrast, 
only 26 percent of schizophrenics were), but on follow-up 
averaging 2.6 years, 69 percent were chronically um, 
though 31 percent were considered “socially recovered. 
Retterstol (1970) found on long-term follow-up that 79 
percent of his cohort [sic] were self-supporting and 74 
percent had had no major periods without work; compara- 
ble figures for schizophrenics were 31 percent and 30 
percent, respectively. Only 39 percent of delusional disor- 
der patients had been hospitalized since initial discharge 
and 27 percent were psychotic in follow-up, compared with 
90 percent and 80 percent of schizophrenics, respectively. 
Follow-up studies are consistent in suggesting that 80 to 
90 percent of patients with delusional disorder are diag- 
nostically stable over time. It is unlikely, for example, for 
these patients to develop schizophrenia. Prognostic fea- 
tures have not been systematically studied.

O AFTERWARD 


Substantial progress has been made during the past 
decade in our understanding of schizophrenia, schizo- 
phreniform disorder, and delusional disorder. While the 
introduction of the DSM-III criteria in 1980 narrowed the 
definition for schizophrenia, thereby creating a more 
homogeneous group for research, questions remain about 
whether the narrowing has gone too far. A reemphasis on 
the importance or the negative symptoms of schizophre- 
nia, or Bleuler’s “fundamental” symptoms, has emerged, 
balancing the perhaps too rigid emphasis on Schneiderian 
symptoms in the 1970s. Advances in classification and 
epidemiology have allowed us to reevaluate the distribu- 
tion of schizophrenia and its risk factors. Despite the 
drawbacks of the Epidemiologic Catchment Area project, 


this study has become the model for future community 
surveys. 


The development of new brain imaging techniques 
such as computerized tomography, magnetic resonance 
imaging, regional cerebral blood flow, positron emission 
tomography, and brain electrical activity mapping have 
enhanced our understanding of schizophrenia. This 
technology is allowing us to explore the nature and pat- 
tern of brain deficits and examine the possibility of symp- 
tom localization in schizophrenia. The development of 
“brain banks” as well as new techniques in histopathology 
have given renewed emphasis to postmortem research, 
permitting a more detailed investigation of abnormalities 
in neurotransmitter systems and in the neuropathology of 
schizophrenia. While the nosologists and neuroscientists 
have been clarifying the classification and pathologic 
mechanisms of schizophrenia, geneticists have been 


106 


amassing large family data sets and applying new meth- 
ods such as gene mapping that r-vmise to enrich the study 
of genetic factors in schizophrenia. 


While technological advances are helping us to explore 
the etiology of schizophrenia, knowledge about course and 
outcome has been enhanced through long-term studies. 
We have also learned that the best treatment approach to 
schizophrenia combines both pharmacologic and psychoso- 
cial measures. Although the pharmacologic treatment of 
schizophrenia has been hampered by the well-worn dopa- 
mine theory, investigators are now looking at other neuro- 
transmitter systems that may yield a more complex 
interactive model of neurotransmission and thus new 
pharmacologic approaches. meanwhile, research has 
highlighted the importance of family interaction models in 
schizophrenia, leading to more specific psychosocial 
interventions in the treatment of schizophrenia. 


Progress during the coming decade will build upon the 
foundation of current research. The future appears bright 
and, one hopes, will lead to a better understanding of the 
pathophysiology and etiology of schizophrenia. This should 
eventually allow physicians to offer more to those who 
suffer from schizophrenia, perhaps psychiatry’s greatest 
scourge.

Outcome in Delusional Disorder 
in Different Periods of Time 


Possible Implications for Treat- 
ment with Neuroleptics 


Abstract 
The effect of neuroleptics in the 
treatment of schizophrenia is well 
established. Some reports indicate 
that at least pimozide may be effec- 
tive in delusional disorder as well. In 
this article a long-term follow-up 
study of 72 first-admitted patients 
with delusional disorder is described. 
Outcome in patients admitted during 
1946-1948 was compared with that 
of patients admitted during 1958- 
1961. We did not find a more favour- 
able outcome in the group admitted 
after the introduction of neurolep- 
tics. This contradicts our earlier 
finding for patients with schizo- 
phrenic psychoses, indicating that 
neuroleptics generally may be more 
effective for schizophrenics than for 
patients with delusional disorder. 
Controlled drug trials in delusional 
disorder are strongly needed. 


133 


Several studies have demonstrated the effect of 
neuroleptics in the treatment of schizophrenics [1]. Re- 
cently especially pimozide has also been reported to be 
beneficial in the treatment of patients with monosympto- 
matic psychoses, meeting the criteria of delusional disor- 
der according to current classification [2-5]. These patients 
are, however, often difficult to treat because of denial, 
projection and reluctance to take drugs. We have earlier 
reported that some hospitalized patients with delusional 
order may recover without any specific treatment other 
than short hospitalization, and some may recover after 
antidepressive treatment as well [6-8]. In our large sample 
of patients with delusions as a prominent feature, most 
patients were admitted after the introduction of neurolep- 
tics, but a relatively large subsample was admitted before 
the neuroleptic era [9]. For the 141 patients with the 
diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizophreniform disorder 
according to DSM-III, it was found that average outcome 
was more favourable for those patients who were admitted 
after the introduction of neuroleptics. Moreover, the late 
clinical course tended to be stable in men, while women on 
average deteriorated. This is consistent with the hypothe- 
sis that estrogen may have postsynaptic antidopaminergic 
effects like neuroleptics, and protect female patients until 
age 40-45 [9]. We have also investigated the outcome 
according to gender in a sample of patients with delusional 
disorder, where the trend was the opposite as for the 
schizophrenic patients, namely that female patients did 
best at the follow-ups, and with the largest difference at 
last follow-up in the multivariate analysis [10]. The 
purpose of this study was to examine the difference of 
outcome between the group of patients with delusional 
disorder admitted before and after the introduction of 
neuroleptics.

Material and Methods 


The selection of patients and follow-up procedures 
have been described elsewhere [11-13]. The patients were 
first-time delusional cases consecutively admitted to the 
Psychiatric Department, University of Oslo, during 1946- 
1948 (long-term group) and 1958-1961 (short-term group). 
Bipolars and patients with confusional states were ex- 
cluded. One of the authors (N.R.) selected the sample and 
carried out a personal follow-up in 1963-1964 [11], and 
another follow-up of the short-term patients in 1966-1967 
[12]. About 20 years later the other author (S.O.) person- 
ally examined the patients still alive and willing to be 
interviewed [7, 13]. Diagnosis at discharge from index 
admission based on the records had been assessed before 
the last follow-up interviews. Of 301 patients, 71 met 
Kendler and Tsuang’s [14] diagnostic criteria for delu- 
sional disorder. These criteria are very similar to those for 
delusional disorder in DSM-III-R [15], but require an 
illness onset before age 60. Therefore, an additional patient 
who developed delusions at age 60 was included, so there 
were 72 patients, 49 men and 23 women. The mean age at 
admission was 40 years (range 16-61). The interviews at last 
follow-up were conducted semistructurally using SADS-L 
[16] in 42 patients; 24 had died, and 6 patients refused, 
had moved abroad or were untraceable. Additional infor- 
mation from the Norwegian Central Register for Psychoses, 
hospital records, family doctors, relatives and death certifi- 
cates was taken into consideration. The overall psychoso- 
cial functioning was estimated using Global Assessment 
Scale (GAS) [17]. Gas scores were also assessed retrospec- 
tively at earlier follow-up (first follow-up in the long-term 
group and short-term group when insufficient information 
at second follow-up, otherwise second follow-up). This 
means that we have GAS scores at 2 points in most 
patients, at 3-18 (mean 10) year follow-up in the total 


135 


sample, and at 22-39 (mean 27) year follow-up in the 
patients examined at last follow-up. Psychosocial stressor 
associated with outbreak of psychosis was estimated 
according to axis IV in DSM-III [18]. The interrater 
reliability for discharge diagnosis at index hospitalization 
was estimated based on the same information from the 
records for 2 raters. The kappa value [19] for Kendler’s 
aelusional disorder was 0.86. The reliability of axis IV 
score was estimated in the same manner, with an intra- 
class correlation coefficient (ICC) [20] of 0.72. ICC for the 
GAS ratings was 0.89 at earlier follow-up and 0.87 at last 
follow-up [13]. Statistical analyses used Student’s t test 
and the x’ test with Yates correction. Significance was set 
at 5%.

Discussion 


Contrary to what we found for schizophrenic patients 
[9], the subsample of patient with delusional disorder 
admitted in the late fifties and early sixties, after the 
introduction of neuroleptics, did not show a more favour- 
able outcome than the subsample admitted in the forties. 
The trend was rather that long-term patients did best at 
10-year follow-up. Certainly, the tendency was that short- 
term patients functioned best at last follow-up, but the 
long-term group then was very small because of attrition 
of patients. The results from the last follow-up must 
therefore be interpreted with caution. Gabriel and 
Schanda [21] compared the results from their follow-up 
studies of patients developing a delusional psychoses in 
midlife, who fell ill before and after the introduction of 
neuroleptics respectively. They did not find any difference 
between the two studies as to the frequency of uniform 
ve-sus polymorphous or episodic versus chronic course. A 
comparison with our study is difficult because of different 
methodology and at least partly different diagnostic 
categories. However, the results of Gabriel and Schanda 
[21] point in the same direction as ours. A conclusion of 
our studies is that the late clinical outcome in short-term 
schizophrenic patients admitted after the introduction of 
neuroleptics was more favourable than in long-term 
patients admitted before neuroleptics were available. No 
such difference was found in patients with delusional 
disorder. As regards gender differences there was a trend 
for male schizophrenic patients to do better in the long run 
than their female counterparts, while in delusional disor- 
der the trend was opposite. A possible explanation could 
be that neuroleptics generally may be of more important 
value for schizophrenic patients than for patients with 


138 


delusional disorder. Another explanation might be that 
there are more problems with compliance in delusional 
disorder than in schizophrenia. Paranoid patients are 
usually very sensitive to all side effects of drugs, and most 
reports on the effect of neuroleptics in delusional disorder 
are still anecdotal. Drug treatment in this disorder, 
therefore, still seems to be terra incognitia, and controlled 
drug trials are strongly needed. It also remains to be 
demonstrated if the effect of pimozide is specific. Because 
of the difficulties encountered in doing research in this 
field, trials should be carried out on a multi-centre basis.

With reference to the due process procedural issues 
related to your representation, limitation of witnesses, 
lack of objectivity by the hearing officer, appropriateness 
of grounds for decision-making relative to the utilization of 
psychotropic medication and the role of dangerousness in 
the decision-making process, the due process hearing was 
conducted and the decisions were rendered in accord with 
the provisions in the Code of Federal Regulations, Sections 
549.40 through 549.43. 


With reference to your due process procedural concerns, 
the regulation specifies that witnesses “should be called if 
they have information relevant to the inmate’s mental 
condition and/or need for medication, and if they are 
reasonably available. Witnesses who only have repetitive 
information need not be called.” The hearing officer 
properly adhered to these regulations. The hearing officer 
declined to call witnesses who would present repetitive 
testimony. Available witnesses were contacted and ap- 
peared. Testimony from unavailable witnesses who were 
above to be contacted was provided through your staff 
representative. Witnesses who could not be contacted did 
not testify. 


In accordance with the Code of Federal Regulations, the 
hearing was “conducted by a psychiatrist who is not 
currently involved in the diagnosis or treatment of the

Under the provisions of 28 CFR, Section 549.43, the 
hearing officer “shall determine whether treatment or 
psychotropic medication is necessary in order to make the 
inmate competent for trial or is necessary because the 
inmate is dangerous to self or others, is gravely disabled, 
or unable to function in open population of a mental health 
referral center or a regular institution.” This language 
succinctly addresses both your concerns about the basis for 
due process decision-making and the role of dangerous- 
ness. The language clearly indicates that restoration of 
competency is a criteria independent from the other 
specified criteria set forth in the regulation. The decision 
of the hearing officer was also based on specific evidence, 
(your delusional beliefs with respect to various agencies, 
departments, and individuals). In short, the hearing 
officer’s findings were not arbitrary or capricious, but were 
based upon specific evidence presented by witnesses who 
evaluated your condition and who have the added respon- 
sibility for planning a course of treatment for you.
