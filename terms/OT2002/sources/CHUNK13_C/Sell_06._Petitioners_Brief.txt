18 U.S.C. § 4241: Determination of mental competency to 
stand trial 


(a) Motion to determine competency of defen- 
dant. — At any time after the commencement of a 
prosecution for an offense and prior to the sen- 
tencing of the defendant, the defendant or the at- 
torney for the Government may file a motion for 
a hearing to determine the mental competency of 
the defendant. The court shall grant the motion, 
or shall order such a hearing on its own motion, 
if there is reasonable cause to believe that the 
defendant may presently be suffering from a 
mental disease or defect rendering him mentally 
incompetent to the extent that he is unable to 
understand the nature and consequences of the 
proceedings against him or to assist properly in 
his defense. 


(b) Psychiatric or psychological examination 
and report. — Prior to the date of the hearing, the 
court may order that a psychiatric or psychologi- 
cal examination of the defendant be conducted, 
and that a psychiatric or psychological report be 
filed with the court, pursuant to the provisions of 
section 4247 (b) and (c).

(c) Hearing. —- The hearing shall be conducted 
pursuant to the provisions of section 4247(d). 


(d) Determination and disposition. —- If ... the 
court finds by a preponderance of the evidence 
that the defendant is presently suffering from a 
mental disease or defect rendering him mentally 
incompetent to the extent that he is unable to 
understand the nature and consequences of the 
proceedings against him or to assist properly in 
his defense, the court shall commit the defendant 
to the custody of the Attorney General. The At- 
torney General shall hospitalize the defendant 
for treatment in a suitable facility — 


(1) for such a reasonable period of time, not

attain the capacity to permit the trial to pro-

(2) for an additional reasonable period of 

time until — 
(A) his mental condition is so improved 
that trial may proceed, if the court finds 
that there is a substantial probability that” 
within such additional period of time he will

ceed; or 


(B) the pending charges against him are 
disposed of according to law; 
whichever is earlier.

has not so improved as to permit the trial to

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 


I. Dr. Charles Thomas Sell. 


Dr. Charles Thomas Sell (“Dr. Sell”) is a fifty-three 
year old dentist who is currently confined in the United 
States Medical Center for Federal Prisoners (the “Cen- 
ter”) in Springfield, Missouri. (Record on Appeal (“Re- 
cord”) 1108, 1136; Appendix (“App.”) 339). Dr. Sell is a 
non-dangerous, pre-trial detainee, who has been confined 
either at the Center or other facilities for almost five 
years. (Record 559, 730-46, 1001-09; App. 349-50). Dr. 
Sell is accused of insurance fraud, but has been adjudged 
incompetent to stand trial.’ (App. 339, 359). Dr. Sell’s 
pretrial detention is already longer than the period of 
time that he would have served if convicted of the fraud 
charges.’ Dr. Sell has been diagnosed as suffering from 
delusional disorder, persecutory type. (App. 30). The 
government seeks to administer antipsychotic medication

stand trial. (App. 357). 


Dr. Sell is an intelligent, educated individual. He 
graduated from Saint Louis University High School and 
Saint Louis University, both Jesuit institutions. (Record 
1108). He completed his undergraduate degree in 1972 
with a major in biology/chemistry and was awarded a 
doctorate in dental surgery from the University of Mis- 
souri-Kansas City in 1976. (Record 1108). After gradua- 
tion, he obtained a dental license from the Missouri Dental 
Board (Record 1136) and opened a private dental practice 
in Des Peres, Missouri. (Record 1108). In private practice, 
he performed general dentistry, endodontics, oral surgery, 
periodontics, pedodontics, prosthodontics, and geriatric 
dentistry. (Record 1099). For several years, Dr. Sell’s 
dental practice thrived in a relatively affluent area of St. 
Louis County, Missouri. 


In 1977, Dr. Sell married his first wife, Deborah. 
(Record 1091). They had three children together. (Record 
1091). In 1983, Deborah left Dr. Sell, which caused him 
extreme emotional difficulty. (1999 Forensic Psychological 
Report (“1999 FPR”) at 3 (filed under seal)). In 1984, Dr. 
Sell fell in love with and later married his second wife, 
Mary, and the couple conceived Dr. Sell’s fourth child, 
Robert L. Sell, in 1987. (Record 1087). 


In 1982, Dr. Sell joined the United States Army 
Dental Reserve. (Record 1130). He received officer training 
at Fort Sam Houston in Texas. After training, the Army 
promoted Dr. Sell to the rank of Captain. (1999 FPR at 3). 
He excelled at his assigned tasks, and effective January 
11, 1990, the army promoted him to the rank of Major. 
(Record 1085). 


8 


During the 1980s and continuing into the 1990s, Dr. 
Sell occasionally suffered from recurrences of the emo- 
tional difficulties that had surfaced following his divorce 
from Deborah, and he periodically sought treatment from 
psychiatrists or psychologists. (1997 Forensic Report 
(“1997 FR”) at 3 (filed under seal)). He received psycho- 
therapy, and, at times, his clinician treated him with 
antidepressants. (See, e.g., 1997 FR at 4). Once, in the 
early 1980s, he was briefly treated with an antipsychotic 
drug, Haldol. (Record 589). Dr. Sell found the side effects 
of the drug to be intolerable. (Record 617 (Dr. James K. 
Wolfson testifying that Dr. Sell had suffered, at minimum, 
dystonic reaction from Haldol and that Dr. Sell had com- 
plained that Haldol gave him “all types of problems”)). In 
fact, Dr. Sell has likened taking antipsychotic drugs to a 
lobotomy, and he is opposed to antipsychotic medication. 
(Record 509). He expressed this opposition in his appeal 
of the Center’s administrative involuntary medication 
decision: “I have a God given right not to have [my brain] 
altered by the government’s antipsychotic, psychotropic 
medication.” (Record 511). ’ 


II. Criminal Proceedings. 


On May 16, 1997, the government filed a complaint 
alleging that Dr. Sell had committed insurance fraud by, 
among other things, submitting doctored invoices to 
insurers for the purpose of obtaining payment for services 
not covered by the policies. (App. 1, 7). On May 20, 1997, 
the government moved for a psychiatric examination to 
assess Dr. Sell’s competency to stand trial. (Record 3-5). A 
magistrate judge ordered that Dr. Sell be transferred to 
the Center for a psychological assessment (Record 8-10), 
and, on June 24, 1997, further ordered that a competency 


9 


hearing be held. (Record 11-12). The Center conducted a 
psychological assessment, and, on June 20, 1997, provided 
the magistrate judge with a Forensic Report setting forth 
the Center’s findings. (1997 FR at 1). The conclusions in 
the Forensic Report were tentative because Dr. Sell 
refused to discuss his case history with the evaluator. 
(1997 FR at 2). The evaluator, a neuropsychologist, pro- 
vided the following assessment to the court: 


It must be noted that the undersigned evaluator 
provides this opinion with less confidence than 
customary. ... [I]t is the undersigned evaluator’s 
opinion that Charles Thomas Sell does not cur- 
rently suffer from a mental disease or defect, and 
that he is competent to the extent he is able to 
understand the nature and consequences of the 
proceedings against him and assist properly in 
his defense. 


PROGNOSIS 


Currently, Dr. Sell evidences no obvious signs of 
psychosis. It is likely his mental status should 
stay consistent in the near future, but there is a 
possibility he could develop a psychotic episode 
depending on the nature of any past mental ill- 
nesses. It is impossible for the undersigned 
evaluator to be more specific given the lack of in- 
formation. He does have a [sic] paranoid person- 
ality characteristics which are pervasive and will 
color interactions between Dr. Sell, counsel, and 
the Court. 


(1997 FR at 8). At a hearing on July 15, 1997, the court 
considered the Forensic Report and determined that Dr. 
Sell was competent to stand trial. (Record 13-15). On July 
30, 1997, Dr. Sell was indicted for Medicaid and insurance 
fraud. (App. 7). 


10 


On October 14, 1998, counsel for Dr. Sell filed a notice 
of intent to introduce evidence of mental disease or defect 
(Record 285-86), and, in response, the government re- 
quested an additional psychiatric examination (Record 
287-88, 303-07), which Dr. Sell opposed. (Record 293-302). 
On November 25, 1998, the magistrate judge denied the 
government's request for a second competency determina- 
tion, but ordered a second psychological assessment based 
upon Dr. Sell’s interposition of the diminished capacity 
defense. (Record 308-17). 


Counsel for Dr. Sell then enlisted the services of a 
private psychiatrist, Dr. C. Robert Cloninger, to conduct an 
independent assessment of Dr. Sell’s competency. (Record 
326-27). Dr. Cloninger is the Wallace Renard Professor of 
Psychiatry at Washington University, a Professor of 
Genetics & Psychology at Washington University, and the 
Director of the Center for Psychobiology of Personality 
with an extensive educational background in the psychol- 
ogy of delusional disorder and personal experience in 
treating patients with the disorder. (Record 417-58 (Dr. 
Cloninger’s curriculum vitae)). He has over two hundred 
publications to his credit, and he was head of the Depart- 
ment of Psychiatry at Washington University from 1989 to 
1994. (Record 419). 


Dr. Cloninger examined Dr. Sell, who cooperated in 
the examination. (Record 326-27). Dr. Cloninger reviewed 
Dr. Sell’s medical records, spoke personally with Dr. Sell’s

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT 


Dr. Sell remains an innocent man — he has never been 
convicted of any crime — and the government has nonethe- 
less imprisoned him for almost five years without a trial. 
Even though Dr. Sell has not been found to pose a risk of 
harm to himself or others, the government seeks to violate 
his First, Fifth, and Sixth Amendment rights by injecting 
him —- against his will — with permanently mind-altering 
psychotropic drugs. The government wants to medicate Dr. 
Sell because it hopes that doing so will bring Dr. Sell into 
an artificially induced, drug-dependent competence, so 
that the government can bring him to trial on charges 
involving alleged economic crimes of fraud and money 
laundering. In other words, the government seeks to 
justify the deprivation of Dr. Sell’s rights on the chance 
that it will be able to prosecute him. Such a constitution- 
ally irreverent justification must be rejected. 


In the past, this Court has refused to allow the gov- 
ernment to violate an individual's right to bodily integrity 
when its only motive was a prosecutorial one. In fact, the 
Constitution only permits intrusions of this magnitude 
when the life or health of the individual or others is at 
risk. A citizen cannot be deemed to have forfeited such a 
fundamental, constitutional right simply based upon the 


24 


fact that he is charged with a crime. The government's 
unconstitutional course of action is particularly egregious 
because there is no medical consensus that the forced 
medication of Dr. Sell will result in his competency to 
stand trial. Even if Dr. Sell did become competent, trial of 
Dr. Sell in a drug-induced state will result in violations of 
his Fifth and Sixth Amendment trial rights. 


In sum, if the Court of Appeals’ decision is allowed to 
stand, an individual will lose his right to refuse medica- 
tion based solely upon the government’s unproven asser- 
tion that the individual is guilty of a non-violent crime and 
may be rendered competent if non-specified, mind-altering 
drugs are administered to him. Dr. Sell will also be sub- 
jected to potentially long-term and life-threatening side 
effects caused by the medication. At its core, this case 
presents the narrow question of whether an innocent 
individual loses his right to refuse psychotropic medication 
simply because the government has elected to charge that 
individual with a crime. Constitutionally, the answer to 
this question must be no.

Freedom of thought. This Court has long recognized 
that freedom of thought is protected by the First Amend- 
ment and recently reiterated that “the right to think is the 
beginning of freedom.” Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition, 
122 S. Ct. 1389, 1403 (2002). In fact, “the individual’s 
freedom of conscience [is] the central liberty that unifies 
the various clauses in the First Amendment.” Wallace v. 
Jaffree, 472 U.S. 38, 50 (1985); see also Stanley, 394 U.S. 
at 564; Olmstead v. United States, 277 U.S. 438, 478 
(1928) (Brandeis, J., dissenting) (recognizing that the 
Constitution protects “Americans in their beliefs, their 
thoughts, their emotions, and their sensations”). As this 
Court recognized, the scrupulous protection of constitu- 
tional freedoms prevents the “strangl[ing of] the free mind 
at its source.” West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette, 
319 U.S. 624, 637 (1943). 


In Stanley, the Court decided whether an individual 
hes a constitutional right to possess obscene material in 
his home. The Stanley Court did not base its decision on 


29 


the narrow First Amendment principle that an individual 
has a right to receive information. Instead, the Court 
recognized a broader principle: the government cannot 
control the minds of its citizens. Stanley, 394 U.S. at 565. 
It follows, a fortiori, that if the government is prohibited 
from inquiring into the contents of one’s library, it cer- 
tainly must be prohibited from “impermissible tinkering 
with the mental processes.” Mackey v. Procunier, 477 F.2d 
877, 878 (9th Cir. 1973) (recognizing that unconsented-to 
treatment with the drug succinycholine would be unconsti- 
tutional). “Our whole constitutional heritage rebels at the 
thought of giving government the power to control men’s 
minds.” Stanley, 394 U.S. at 565 (recognizing First 
Amendment privacy interest). This “heritage” compels the 
reversal of the opinions below. 


Lower courts have long held that the involuntary 
administration of drugs affecting a person’s mental proc-

First Amendment rights. See Scott v. Plante, 532 F.2d 939, 
946 (3d Cir. 1976); Mackey, 477 F.2d at 878; Lojuk uv. 
Quandt, 706 F.2d 1456, 1465 (7th Cir. 1983) (recognizing

even permanently affect an individual’s ability to think

Liberty and privacy interests. While the liberty 
guaranteed by the Fifth Amendment has not been defined 
with exactness, the concept has been recognized to include 
the right of an individual to be free from governmental 
intrusion into fundamental matters. Eisenstadt v. Baird, 
405 U.S. 438 (1972) (finding liberty interest in right of

Virginia, 388 U.S. 1, 12 (1967) (recognizing due process

the perceived fairness of the procedures used to implement 
them. In Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S. 266 (1994), this Court

bodily integrity.” 510 U.S. 266, 272 (1994) (citations

Ill. Dr. Sell Has Fundamental Trial Rights Under 
The Fifth and Sixth Amendments Not To Be 
Medicated Into Drug-Induced, Synthetic Sanity. 


Dr. Sell’s trial rights as guaranteed by the Fifth 
Amendment and Sixth Amendment include the rights (1) 
to present a defense, including a diminished capacity 
defense, see FED. R. CRIM. P. 12.2(b); (2) to not have the 
government manipulate his appearance in a way that 
prejudices him before the jury, /llinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 
337, 345 (1970); (3) to not be tried unless he is competent 
to “consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his 
defense,” Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); and 
(4) to testify and “present his own version of events in his 
own words,” Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U.S. 44, 52 (1987). 
Moreover, the government cannot suppress “evidence 
favorable to an accused . . . where the evidence is material 
either to guilt or to punishment.” Brady v. Maryland, 373 
U.S. 83, 87 (1963). All of these rights would be violated if 
Dr. Sell is tried while compulsorily medicated. 


Right to present defense. Antipsychotic medication 
affects the defendant’s ability to assist in his defense and, 
in essence, forces the accused to provide the jury with a 
coerced confession of his mental state. See Riggins, 504 
U.S. at 137; id. at 143 (Kennedy, J., concurring); Rochin, 
342 U.S. at 175 (Black, J., concurring) (finding person is 
compelled to be witness against himself when “incriminat- 
ing evidence is forcibly taken from him by contrivance of 
modern science”). In a case where diminished capacity is 
raised as a defense, the jury must assess their belief about 
defendant’s mental state at the time the crime occurred. 


44 


“(I]f the defendant appears calm and controlled at trial, 
the jury may well discount any testimony that the defen- 
dant lacked, at the time of the crime, substantial capacity 
either to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct or to 
conform his conduct to the requirements of the law.” 
People v. Hardesty, 362 N.W.2d 787, 796 (Mich. Ct. App. 
1984). The accused, sitting in the courtroom drugged and 
listless, becomes an instrument of his own conviction, with 
his calm and controlled demeanor rebutting his own case 
without a single word being uttered. 


Further, compulsory medication of a defendant makes 
it easier for the government to rebut the accused’s defense 
of diminished capacity by suppressing the most persuasive 
evidence of the individual’s demeanor at the time the 
crime occurred. Due process does not allow the govern- 
ment to suppress “evidence favorable to an accused ... 
where the evidence is material to either guilt or punish- 
ment.” Brady, 373 U.S. at 87. As this Court has recog- 
nized, “the most important witness for the defense in 
many criminal cases is the defendant himself.” See Rock, 
483 U.S. at 52. After antipsychotic drugs are administered, 
the defendant is deprived of the best evidence of his 
mental state at the time of the alleged crime — his own 
demeanor in an unmedicated state. Postmedication review 
of an individual’s Sixth Amendment rights may come too 
late to prevent impairment of that right. See United States 
v. Weston, 206 F.3d 9, 14 (D.C. Cir. 2000).

appearance. Further, the right to a fair trial also means 
that one accused of a crime “is entitled to have his guilt or 
innocence determined solely on the basis of the evidence 
introduced at trial.” Taylor v. Kentucky, 436 U.S. 478, 485 
(1978). If the government alters Dr. Sell’s outward appear- 
ance by medicating him with drugs that cause numerous

criminal proceedings against him. See Allen, 397 U.S. at
