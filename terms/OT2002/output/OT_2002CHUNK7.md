## Decisions and Dispositions

### Gerber v. Hickman

Merits decision, March 25, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed dismissal of Gerber’s amended complaint by the Eastern District of California. The question is whether confinement categorically extinguishes a married prisoner’s proposed privately financed, no-contact exercise of procreative choice, or whether the actual refusal must satisfy the standard governing restrictions on retained prisoner rights. At the pleaded events Gerber was 41 and his wife 44; he was serving 100 years to life plus eleven years without a parole date. He proposed a laboratory container and prepaid overnight mailer, or collection by counsel, with all costs reimbursed. The May 23, 2002 en banc judgment treated procreation during confinement as categorically unavailable.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

The preserved federal procreative-liberty claim returns for proceedings under Turner and Lewis. The state-law rulings and the rulings on amendment to add equal-protection and Eighth Amendment claims remain undisturbed. Gerber’s requested immediate permission to collect and transmit a specimen awaits the required factual and remedial determinations.

#### Opinion Topology

O’CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which SCALIA and THOMAS, JJ., joined as to Part II’s conditional §3626 requirements. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Complete Court opinion | O’Connor | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Vacates and remands the preserved federal claim | Retained-right inquiry, remand and conditional remedial requirements |
| Part II, conditional statutory requirements | O’Connor | The complete-opinion coalition, with Scalia and Thomas joining these requirements | Governs any eventual injunction; Scalia and Thomas oppose remand | All nine directly join every applicable §3626 requirement |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | | Supports vacatur and remand | Proposed heightened rule and additional remedial directions |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm dismissal | No asserted entitlement to institutional facilitation |

#### Holdings

##### I. A no-contact proposal requires the retained-right inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** Incarceration does not categorically extinguish a married prisoner’s claim to attempt conception through the alleged privately arranged, no-contact specimen transfer. The actual restriction must bear a reasonable relationship to legitimate penological interests under Turner, with substantial institutional deference.

**Authority:** Part I of O'Connor’s opinion of the Court; the seven Justices identified as the complete Court-opinion coalition directly join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the complaint cannot be dismissed simply by labeling all procreation incompatible with imprisonment. Turner preserves important marital attributes that can coexist with confinement. Skinner recognizes the constitutional weight of reproductive choice, although its prohibition on the sterilization classification does not itself require assisted reproduction. Lewis preserves inquiry into an actual reasonable relationship; invoking security is not a substitute for that relationship.

The alleged transfer requires no physical contact. Gerber’s sentence and the reported ages bear on whether later exercise is a meaningful alternative. Those allegations warrant adjudication, not a finding that the proposal is feasible or harmless. The warden’s strongest objection—that biological collection and transport require affirmative institutional assistance—must be assessed through the actual handling, security, staffing, punishment and resource interests involved. Private reimbursement does not eliminate those interests. The lower court must examine rational connection, remaining means of exercise, accommodation effects and ready alternatives; the last consideration does not require officials to disprove every imaginable accommodation or use the least restrictive constitutional means. Substantial deference remains part of that inquiry.

##### II. Proof and a fitted prison remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Further proceedings must determine the actual proposal and the justification for refusing it; any prospective relief requires a proved federal violation and the findings required by 18 U.S.C. §3626(a)(1). Relief must be necessary, narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means of correcting the particular plaintiff’s violation, with substantial weight given to adverse effects on public safety and criminal-justice-system operation; relief conflicting with state or local law or exceeding official authority must additionally be required by federal law, necessary to correct the violation and unavailable through another corrective remedy.

**Authority:** Part II of the same Court opinion. The seven complete-opinion members join the remand and its full explanation; Scalia and Thomas additionally join the conditional §3626 requirements, giving those requirements nine direct votes without adding their assent to remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Lewis links institutional relief to the proved wrong and preserves a genuine opportunity for officials to devise an effective cure. The amended statute governs any resulting prison-conditions injunction. The present allegations do not resolve collection arrangements, biological handling, staff time, actual costs or available safeguards. Ordinary proceedings must develop or resolve those matters under Turner before selecting relief. Gerber’s offer to pay cannot substitute for the statutory findings. His wife’s independent rights and the separate claims excluded from the grant remain outside this adjudication.

#### Precedent Treatment

Turner v. Safley supplies the retained-marriage and reasonable-relationship framework, applied to the pleaded no-contact proposal.

Skinner v. Oklahoma supplies the protected reproductive interest; its sterilization holding does not itself establish a duty to provide this procedure.

Lewis v. Casey supplies the actual institutional-fit inquiry, deference, violation-linked relief and initial governmental opportunity to devise a cure. The November 26, 1997 amendment supplies the current federal-requirement condition for relief conflicting with state or local law.

Washington v. Glucksberg remains confined to its asserted assisted-suicide entitlement and does not categorically resolve this retained marital interest.

#### Law After Decision

The pleaded class of privately arranged, no-contact marital procreation proposals receives Turner review rather than categorical dismissal as incompatible with confinement. Operational proof and justified prison restrictions determine the claim under that settled standard; an eventual injunction is governed by Lewis and the amended §3626 requirements.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the remand under Turner but would give stronger protection against complete prevention of a practicable, privately financed, no-contact method. Gerber would prove concrete feasibility by a preponderance at factfinding, with supported allegations accepted at the pleading stage. Officials would then substantiate an important penological objective and the actual operational need for complete refusal, including why workable safeguards do not address it. Routine handling restrictions would retain Turner review. Skinner’s protected choice, Turner’s surviving marital interests and Lewis’s insistence on actual institutional fit support that distinction, in his view. The practical permanence of the deprivation warrants more than ordinary deference to complete prevention; private payment still proves neither feasibility nor a violation.

He would require timely consideration of collection and transport arrangements, costs, handling risks and safeguards before delay defeats the choice. If an unjustified total refusal is proved, officials should have a genuine initial opportunity to propose an effective accommodation. Any injunction must satisfy every §3626 finding, specify the supported method, responsible officials and compliance arrangements, and set a practicable implementation schedule. An unsupported proposal warrants no compelled service. The claim supplies neither conjugal access, public financing, guaranteed pregnancy nor sentence modification; a broader policy remedy requires proof of the broader violation and the applicable findings.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** Preserving marriage and reproductive capacity does not, in their view, create an entitlement to the institutional assistance required for conception during confinement. Turner protects compatible marital interests, while Glucksberg distinguishes an antecedent liberty from a claimed right to assistance. They would affirm this dismissal on that confined ground. Their position preserves protection against forced sterilization and a general marriage ban; it does not eliminate Turner review of rights actually retained.

#### Source Notes

Gerber v. Hickman, 291 F.3d 617 (en banc), No. 00-16494; Turner v. Safley, 482 U.S. 78; Skinner v. Oklahoma, 316 U.S. 535; Lewis v. Casey, June 24, 1996; Washington v. Glucksberg, June 26, 1997; 18 U.S.C. §3626(a)(1); Pub. L. 105-119, §123. The operational facts are pleaded allegations. The reported ages describe the pleaded events.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit for ordinary proceedings on the preserved federal claim. The lower court must apply Turner to the actual restriction and, if a violation is proved, apply Lewis and each applicable §3626 requirement to relief. The ungranted rulings retain their existing effect.

**End of entry: Gerber v. Hickman, merits decision, March 25, 2003.**

### Brown v. Legal Foundation of Washington, No. 01-1325

Merits decision, March 26, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 9, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, sitting en banc. The questions concern taking and compensation under Washington’s mandatory pooled trust-account program, and whether the impracticality of litigating small compensation claims supports an injunction.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The judgment for the program’s defenders remains in force. These plaintiffs receive neither compensation nor the requested programwide injunction on the adjudicated zero-loss premise. The separately granted question whether litigation expense can justify an injunction for a small positive compensation entitlement is unnecessary to this judgment because no positive obligation is established here.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O’CONNOR, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., joined as to Part I’s ownership and appropriation rule and Parts II and III. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and in the judgment. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., joined. KENNEDY, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stevens, O’Connor, Ginsburg and Breyer throughout; Stone-Zsela as to Part I’s ownership and appropriation rule and Parts II and III | Affirms on the zero-loss premise | Five Justices join Parts I–IV, including initial placement and private error; six join public use and net owner loss |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | | Supports affirmance on the adjudicated premise | Supported positive-loss claims require compensation analysis; does not join the private-error limitation |
| Dissent | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Would vacate and remand | Market value and alternative factual inquiry; ownership, value and lawful relief |
| Separate dissent | Kennedy | | Joins Scalia’s dissent and adds a distinct concern | Reserves compelled-support objections for appropriate litigation |

#### Holdings

##### I. The property and the act of taking must be identified separately

**Holding and operative rule:** Interest actually generated from client principal belongs to the owner under Phillips; compulsory transfer of that owned interest is governed by the per se appropriation framework. Initial placement of qualifying principal is a separate act: on this record’s absence of adverse economic impact or interference with investment-backed expectations, it establishes no regulatory taking under Penn Central.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter’s opinion. All nine Justices directly adopt the ownership and appropriation distinction; Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer adopt the separate initial-placement analysis. The Court assumes the plaintiffs’ beneficial ownership, generation of interest and transfer for deciding compensation.

**Controlling explanation:** Phillips establishes that actual earnings follow the principal even when the owner could not earn a net return individually. Webb’s Fabulous Pharmacies prevents the State from converting those earnings into public property by assertion. The compelled transfer of an identified monetary asset therefore requires the appropriation analysis associated with Loretto, rather than treating every aspect of IOLTA as regulation. Conversely, depositing qualifying principal is distinct from confiscating subsequently earned interest. Penn Central evaluates economic impact and interference with investment-backed expectations; neither supplies a taking from the initial placement here. The imperfect individual record permits the ownership and transfer predicates to be assumed in the plaintiffs’ favor because the compensation analysis independently resolves their demands.

##### II. Public use does not determine compensation

**Holding and operative rule:** Providing legal services for the needy satisfies the public-use requirement for this transfer. Compensation nevertheless depends on the owner’s pecuniary loss; public benefit does not excuse a positive uncompensated loss, and personal or other nonpecuniary objections do not add to the compensation measure.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** The legal-service purpose supplies the public use. The compensation question remains distinct because the Takings Clause protects the economic position of the owner. Boston Chamber of Commerce directs attention to what the owner lost rather than what the taker gained. Marion & Rye Valley, Olson and Kimball Laundry likewise require compensatory valuation rather than an award based on public benefit or personal objection. The Foundation’s receipt therefore cannot alone establish the amount due each plaintiff.

##### III. Actual net owner loss is zero for these properly pooled deposits

**Holding and operative rule:** Just compensation measures the net value to the owner of the interest actually earned, taking proper transaction, administrative and bank costs into account. Under Washington’s eligibility rule, client-benefit investment is required whenever net earnings are possible, including feasible cost-effective subaccounting; on the adjudicated premise that these properly pooled deposits could yield no net client earnings, compensation is zero.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Ownership of actual interest and its compensable net value answer different questions. Phillips settled title while expressly reserving compensation. Boston Chamber and the related owner-loss authorities require restoration of the owner’s economic position, not transfer of the recipient’s economies of scale to the owner. The measure concerns actual earned interest after proper costs; it is not simply a hypothetical return from a separate account. Nor may the State confiscate value merely because a public program helped create it.

The authoritatively construed rules require client-benefit treatment whenever net earnings can be obtained, through individual accounts or feasible subaccounting, and eligibility changes as cost-effective technology changes. Separately, the District Court found these deposits incapable of net client earnings. The plaintiffs’ assumed title and appropriation therefore yield no positive compensable loss. The dissent’s treatment of the monetary asset as presumptively worth its face amount omits the proper costs of realizing its value to its owner. A classification label alone would not establish those costs or zero loss; the rule and the adjudicated premise are both necessary to the result.

##### IV. Private erroneous placement does not establish this program’s compensation liability

**Holding and operative rule:** When a limited practice officer mistakenly places funds capable of net client earnings in IOLTA contrary to Washington’s rules, the resulting private-error loss may support a claim against that officer; it does not establish compensation liability of the State or these respondents merely for administering the compliant program. That distinction defeats a remand based only on hypothetical erroneous placements in this action.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Court opinion, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; these five directly adopt this independently sufficient answer to the requested error-based remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The operative rules require profitable funds to be invested for the client’s benefit. A private officer’s contrary classification violates those rules. The dissent emphasizes the State’s later receipt of earned interest, but the loss posited by this alternative arises from the officer’s departure from the lawful eligibility command, not merely from these respondents’ administration of compliant pooling. A possible claim against the officer therefore differs from the compensation claim presented here. This ground does not approve a governmental command to pool profitable funds or decide materially different state-action facts. With no positive unpaid obligation established in this action, the granted question about injunctions for economically impractical small claims is unnecessary; its resolution in a positive-loss case remains open.

#### Precedent Treatment

Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation is applied on title and its reserved taking and compensation questions are resolved at the scope stated here; inability to earn independently remains irrelevant to ownership.

Webb’s Fabulous Pharmacies preserves interest-follows-principal and protection against conversion by assertion. Its reservation for reasonable actual-service charges remains distinct from this program’s diversion.

Loretto supplies the per se appropriation framework for identified property transferred to another.

Penn Central governs the distinct initial-placement analysis; its regulatory framework does not replace appropriation analysis for the subsequent transfer.

Boston Chamber of Commerce supplies the distinction between the owner’s loss and the taker’s gain.

Marion & Rye Valley supplies the requirement of actual pecuniary loss.

Olson supplies valuation by compensable economic loss rather than personal nonpecuniary value.

Kimball Laundry supplies the compensatory treatment of economic value and excludes nonpecuniary loss from this measure.

#### Law After Decision

Compulsory transfer of owned IOLTA interest and valuation of the client’s loss are distinct. Properly classified deposits incapable of net client earnings produce zero compensation under the actual-owner-loss measure; profitable funds require client-benefit investment, including feasible subaccounting as costs change. The five-Justice private-error holding confines the liability of these program respondents without treating a governmental command to pool profitable funds as lawful. A remedy for a small positive entitlement remains a separate legal question.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He joins the ownership and appropriation distinction, the public-use conclusion, and the actual-owner-loss disposition on the adjudicated premise. He emphasizes that a claimant asserting misclassification must establish actual net earning capacity, duration and necessary costs; a supported positive-loss claim must receive compensation analysis rather than automatic dismissal under this zero-loss judgment. Phillips makes actual generated interest the client’s property, while the Foundation’s aggregate gain does not measure what the client surrendered. He does not join the private-error limitation on respondents’ responsibility and supplies no categorical finding of State liability for a different deposit.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.** Market value at appropriation presumptively governs, subject to established exceptions when market value cannot practicably be determined or would produce manifest injustice. Neither exception is established here. The earned monetary asset ordinarily has its face value even if an individual account could not have created it. Phillips and Webb’s protect that asset; hypothetical account or extraction costs cannot erase its constitutional value. A legitimate, independently owed actual-service charge remains possible, but this diversion is not established as such a charge. Even on a net-value theory, inability to earn outside IOLTA does not itself establish the costs of receiving actual pooled earnings, so factual inquiry is required. A private officer’s initial error also does not excuse the later governmental appropriation. They would vacate and remand for ownership, value and lawful relief, including the granted small-claim remedy question if a positive unpaid entitlement is established.

**Kennedy, separately dissenting.** He joins Scalia’s dissent in full and adds that mandatory direction of pooled interest may prevent owners from supporting alternative legal-service viewpoints. Abood and Keller identify a potential compelled-support concern. He reserves that distinct First Amendment issue for appropriate litigation rather than resolving it in this takings case.

#### Source Notes

Brown v. Legal Foundation of Washington, No. 01-1325, 538 U.S. 216; judgment below, 271 F.3d 835; Washington trust-account and limited-practice-officer rules; Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation, June 15, 1998; Webb’s Fabulous Pharmacies, Inc. v. Beckwith, 449 U.S. 155; Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104; Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419; Boston Chamber of Commerce v. Boston, 217 U.S. 189; Marion & Rye Valley Railway Co. v. United States, 270 U.S. 280; Olson v. United States, 292 U.S. 246; Kimball Laundry Co. v. United States, 338 U.S. 1. Beneficial ownership, generated interest and transfer are assumed for the compensation decision, rather than quantified here.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment for the respondents remains in force and the requested programwide injunction is denied. Washington’s existing eligibility duties continue to require client-benefit treatment whenever net earnings are possible.

**End of entry: Brown v. Legal Foundation of Washington, merits decision, March 26, 2003.**

### People of Guam v. Guerrero

Merits decision, March 26, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Ninth Circuit’s review of the final territorial appellate judgment. The questions concern the Organic Act’s religious clauses, Congress’s authority to apply RFRA to Guam, and whether absence of a religious command to import marijuana conclusively defeats the statutory defense. Officials found five ounces of marijuana and ten grams of marijuana seeds in Guerrero’s belongings at Guam International Airport. The trial court found sincere Rastafarian adherence and sacramental use and dismissed the importation indictment on July 23, 1999. The Guam Supreme Court affirmed solely under the Organic Act in 2000; the Ninth Circuit reversed on May 28, 2002.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent broader Organic Act standard | Rejection affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Apply the federal constitutional analogue |
| RFRA’s territorial validity | Recognition affirmed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Permit the statutory defense under Congress’s territorial power |
| Conclusive rejection of the RFRA defense | Vacated and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Determine actual substantial burden and, if shown, Guam’s application-specific justification |

Remand runs through the Ninth Circuit to the territorial courts. The earlier dismissal is not automatically reinstated: its justification analysis did not separately determine the acquisition question.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O’CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

##### I. The Organic Act supplies the federal free-exercise analogue

**Holding and operative rule:** Sections 1421b(a) and (u) of Guam’s Organic Act provide the governing federal constitutional free-exercise protection and do not authorize a territorial court to substitute an independently broader test. On the reported neutral, generally applicable character of the importation law, the claimed broader protection must come from a valid separate source such as RFRA.

**Authority:** Part I of Stone-Zsela’s unanimous opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Organic Act is a federal enactment, so its interpretation presents a federal question rather than an adequate independent local ground. Its borrowed constitutional language and same-force provision carry the federal constitutional analogue. Smith governs genuinely neutral, generally applicable restrictions; Lukumi requires scrutiny of objective targeting and substantial comparable secular underinclusion. Tenafly confirms that actual administration matters to neutrality. The present defense establishes no such separate defect. A territorial charter’s local constitutional function cannot itself change these federal clauses’ meaning. Congress’s ability to provide additional statutory protection is a different question, answered here through RFRA rather than a substituted construction of the Organic Act.

##### II. Territorial power sustains RFRA’s express application to Guam

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress’s substantive authority over Guam under Article IV, §3 supports RFRA’s express territorial coverage notwithstanding Boerne’s invalidation of RFRA’s universal State and local command under Fourteenth Amendment §5. Guerrero may invoke that statutory defense, subject to RFRA’s substantive requirements and independent constitutional limits.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Boerne distinguishes enforcement of the Constitution from replacing its substantive standard. Its §5 holding leaves independent powers open. Congress’s legislative power over this territory supplies a substantive source for the additional statutory accommodation, and RFRA expressly includes Guam. Earlier statutory terminology calling a territory a State cannot alter its constitutional status. Dickerson forbids legislative reduction of a constitutional minimum; an additional protection within an independent power presents the converse situation. The Establishment Clause and other independent constitutional limits remain applicable. Other federal applications of RFRA and an independent Establishment Clause objection are outside this adjudication.

##### III. Actual religious burden precedes application-specific justification

**Holding and operative rule:** A restriction may substantially burden religious exercise by materially obstructing its practical means even when those means are not doctrinally commanded. Guerrero must prove actual exercise and a substantial burden from this importation restriction; if he does, Guam bears production and persuasion that applying the burden to him furthers a compelling interest through the least restrictive means.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Scientology and Swanner prescribe separate claimant and governmental burdens within RFRA’s valid field. The 2000 exercise definition rejects compulsion or centrality as a condition, and received religious-exercise principles independently reject judicial ranking of theological necessity. Sacramental use may require practical acquisition even when the act of importation is not a prescribed rite. Conversely, sincere adherence and religious use do not establish the purpose of every part of this shipment or the burden of restricting this means of acquisition.

The territorial courts must determine intended use, quantity and seeds, the actual connection to practice, and feasible lawful acquisition alternatives. A practical source matters if established, not if imagined. If substantial burden is proved, the general importance of drug control cannot substitute for Guam’s proof concerning this application and possible accommodations. The trial court’s earlier rejection of justification did not resolve the acquisition distinction. Its usable findings remain available, and any material preserved temporal objection to the 2000 definition must be resolved under the enacted temporal provisions and prosecution posture; the rejection of theological necessity does not depend solely on retroactive application of that amendment. If the burden is proved and Guam fails its justification, the statutory defense defeats the unjustified application; otherwise prosecution may proceed.

#### Precedent Treatment

Employment Division v. Smith supplies the neutral, generally applicable law rule incorporated by these Organic Act clauses.

Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye supplies protection against objective targeting and substantial comparable secular underinclusion.

Tenafly preserves scrutiny of actual discriminatory administration.

City of Boerne v. Flores retains its §5 limitation while its reserved independent-power question is resolved for Guam under Article IV.

Dickerson v. United States is distinguished: its constitutional-minimum rule does not prohibit added statutory protection supported by substantive legislative authority.

Church of Scientology Flag Service Organization v. City of Clearwater supplies RFRA’s claimant burden and the government’s application-specific production and persuasion requirements within a valid field.

Swanner v. Anchorage Equal Rights Commission applies that same sequence and preserves the need for an actual justification of the burden on this person; those statutory mechanics govern within this valid territorial field.

#### Law After Decision

The specified Organic Act clauses retain the federal constitutional free-exercise standard. RFRA independently applies to Guam under territorial authority. Its defense protects actual religious exercise without a theological-compulsion requirement, through the claimant’s substantial-burden showing followed by the government’s application-specific compelling-interest and least-restrictive-means proof. The remaining shipment, supply and accommodation facts are matters of application under those settled rules.

#### Source Notes

People of Guam v. Guerrero, 290 F.3d 1210, No. 00-71247; 48 U.S.C. §§1421b(a), 1421b(u), 1424-2; U.S. Const. Art. IV, §3; 42 U.S.C. §§2000bb-1 through 2000bb-3; Pub. L. 106-274, §§7–8. Sincerity and sacramental use were found and undisputed; the shipment’s complete religious connection and practical acquisition alternatives require determination.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Ninth Circuit must return the case to the territorial courts for the actual acquisition-burden inquiry and any necessary justification inquiry. Usable findings remain; a preserved material temporal objection is resolved under the enacted provisions. A successful RFRA defense requires disposition of the affected prosecution to the extent the application is unjustified; failure of the defense permits prosecution under ordinary law.

**End of entry: People of Guam v. Guerrero, merits decision, March 26, 2003.**

### Archer v. Warner, No. 01-1418

Merits decision, March 31, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 13, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. The question is whether releases, a settlement and a replacement promissory note categorically prevent the unpaid obligation from being a debt for money obtained by fraud under §523(a)(2)(A).

The Archers alleged fraud arising from a business sale. Their settlement included broad releases and a $100,000 promissory note; the first required installment was unpaid, and bankruptcy followed. The bankruptcy court and district court rejected the asserted exception on a novation rationale, which the Fourth Circuit affirmed. The settlement occurred without trial findings on the alleged fraud.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens and Thomas.

The bankruptcy proceedings must determine the §523(a)(2)(A) exception, the amount of the enforceable debt connected to a qualifying acquisition, and genuinely preserved independent agreement and preclusion defenses.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O’CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. THOMAS, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which STEVENS, J., joined.

#### Holdings

##### I. The federal character of a settled fraud debt

**Holding and operative rule:** A settlement, release and replacement note do not, merely by constituting a novation, foreclose proof that the resulting enforceable debt is for money obtained by fraud under 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A). Federal discharge characterization examines the surviving debt's origin even though state law has released the original causes of action.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins support this holding and Holding II.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the replacement promise can retain the federal character of the acquisition it settles. Brown v. Felsen permits examination of a debt's nature despite its embodiment in an earlier resolution. In re Cohen makes the relationship between a qualifying fraudulent acquisition and legally enforceable liability decisive. The settlement identifies the obligation the creditors may enforce; its form does not itself answer the federal discharge question. Warner's finality argument, and the dissent's distinction between a consent judgment and a substantive release, would make state-law substitution determine federal character even when the surviving note settles the very acquisition alleged to be fraudulent. The statute instead requires an inquiry into that connection. The Archers seek characterization of the unpaid settlement obligation, whose contractual amount and independently effective defenses remain relevant. Their allegations must be proved in the bankruptcy proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:** Brown v. Felsen is extended to a settlement and replacement note accompanied by releases; its source-of-debt inquiry is not confined to consent judgments. In re Cohen is applied to the legally enforceable liability arising from a qualifying fraudulent acquisition.

##### II. Proof, debt extent and independent defenses on remand

**Holding and operative rule:** For the actual-fraud exception presented, the creditors must prove by a preponderance actual fraud, actual and recipient-specific justifiable reliance, a qualifying acquisition, and the connection between that acquisition and the enforceable unpaid debt. The bankruptcy court must determine the fraud-derived liability under those requirements and separately resolve genuinely preserved agreement and preclusion defenses.

**Authority:** The same seven-Justice Court opinion directly adopts this rule and the reversal and remand it requires.

**Controlling explanation:** Grogan v. Garner supplies the preponderance burden. Field v. Mans requires actual and justifiable reliance measured by the recipient's knowledge, capacities and circumstances. There is no universal duty to investigate; known or personally obvious falsity defeats justifiability, and actually encountered warnings can require inquiry under that individualized standard. Objective imprudence can bear on whether reliance occurred, rather than supplying an independent prudent-person requirement. In re Cohen reaches valid liability arising from a qualifying acquisition, including amounts beyond the value initially obtained when legally connected to it. Subsection (A) excludes statements respecting the debtor's or an insider's financial condition; subsection (B) separately requires a materially false writing of that kind, reasonable reliance and debtor-caused making or publication with intent to deceive. The complaint, compromise and missed installment are not findings of fraud. A claimed distinct promise not to seek nondischargeability and an asserted issue-preclusive effect require their own preservation and merits analysis below: those defenses lie beyond the granted categorical-novation question and were insufficiently addressed. Claim preclusion and issue preclusion perform different work. The remand permits proof of the federal exception and determination of the enforceable amount under the actual bargain, rather than treating either settlement or default as conclusive.

**Precedent treatment:** Grogan supplies the unchanged proof burden. Field supplies actual and individualized justifiable reliance and its financial-condition distinction. In re Cohen supplies the acquired-by relationship and extent of connected, valid liability.

#### Precedent Treatment

Brown v. Felsen's examination of federal discharge character now applies to the genuine release-and-replacement settlement presented here. In re Cohen, decided June 30, 1997, retains its requirement of legally enforceable liability connected to a qualifying fraudulent acquisition. Field v. Mans and Grogan v. Garner continue to govern reliance and proof; the form of the surviving obligation supplies no exception to those requirements.

#### Law After Decision

A creditor may establish that an enforceable settlement debt remains within §523(a)(2)(A) despite the release of the original claims. Courts must distinguish the federal character of that debt from its contractual form and from independently operative defenses. The extent of nondischargeability follows the liability proved to arise from the qualifying acquisition under the existing fraud and reliance rules.

#### Separate Writings

Thomas, joined by Stevens, dissents. He treats these broad releases and the replacement promise as a substantive extinguishment of the earlier liability, rather than a change in its instrument. In re Cohen requires existing liability legally connected to the fraudulent acquisition; for the dissenters, the voluntary settlement severed that connection. Brown v. Felsen permits examining the nature of a debt embodied in a consent judgment but does not require disregarding this substantive replacement. They would affirm the discharge ruling on that construction of the bargain. Their position concerns the legal effect of the settlement, not a finding that the underlying fraud allegations are true or false.

#### Source Notes

Archer v. Warner, 538 U.S. 314, No. 01-1418; 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A)–(B); Brown v. Felsen, 442 U.S. 127; Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279; Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59; In re Cohen, decided June 30, 1997, Third Circuit No. 96-5155.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment is reversed and the case remanded through the Fourth Circuit for proceedings under the stated federal fraud, reliance and connected-liability rules and ordinary preservation requirements. If the exception is established, the bankruptcy court must identify the enforceable liability that survives discharge; otherwise the debt receives its ordinary bankruptcy treatment.

#### Holdings

##### I. The state plan lacked the required preclearance at the deadline

**Holding and operative rule:** A covered congressional plan cannot be implemented without §5 preclearance merely because a state court adopted it. A nonfrivolous, warranted request for omitted necessary information prevents the original submission from completing the statutory review period; the period begins upon receipt of the required response, including a permitted statement that requested information is unavailable.

**Authority:** Part I of Stevens’s unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** Georgia v. United States (1973) places on the submitting jurisdiction the obligation to supply information sufficient for review. The Chancery Court adopted its plan December 21, but Mississippi submitted it and Mauldin on December 26. The February 14 request concerned missing information bearing on the submitted judicial action, and the State supplied responses February 19 and 20. The request was warranted and nonfrivolous; it therefore prevented completion of a sixty-day period by the February 25 deadline. That conclusion does not require a final decision whether Mauldin itself was a covered voting change.

Lopez requires actual, submission-specific approval before use, and McDaniel distinguishes governmental policy adopted by a court from a genuinely judicial temporary remedy. State-law authority and asserted absence of discrimination cannot supply approval. The information-exchange chronology left the federal court without a usable state plan at its election-administration deadline. The statutory nonuse rule applies independently of a merits finding about the districts’ design.

##### II. Private appeals did not continue the State’s administrative review period

**Holding and operative rule:** An adjudicative state plan does not acquire automatic administrative preclearance merely through the passage of sixty days after the State ceases seeking to administer it. Here the State’s failure to appeal or continue seeking implementation after the injunction ended the running review period; private intervenors’ appeals did not constitute State action continuing that process.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices. Stone-Zsela concurs in rejection of this later-period contention without joining its rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 5’s administrative procedure concerns a covered jurisdiction’s proposed administration of a change. This plan rested on adjudication, rather than a legislative enactment remaining in force independently of the judgment. Once the State accepted the injunction and ceased pursuing administration, there was no continuing State request that could mature into approval simply by inaction. The private appellants could challenge the injunction, but their appeal did not act for the State in continuing administrative submission. The later-period argument consequently supplies no preclearance for this plan. That conclusion concerns the actual adjudicative plan and the State’s conduct; it does not replace the distinct initial-deadline analysis.

##### III. The alternative Article I holding must be vacated

**Holding and operative rule:** The adequate §5 ground sustains nonuse of the state plan, and the District Court’s alternative Article I objection is vacated as a basis for its injunction. That alternative binds neither officials nor later proceedings seeking to administer a renewed state-court plan.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The plan could not be implemented without preclearance. That statutory defect resolves the injunction’s operative basis and makes the broader question of state judicial power under Article I unnecessary to the judgment. Vacating the alternative preserves the distinction between a presently unavailable map and a constitutional restriction on the state judiciary’s general role. The Article I merits are left open because an independently sufficient statutory ground disposes of the requested implementation.

##### IV. Section 2c governs the feasible judicial remedy

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2c’s single-member requirement governs courts supplying a lawful congressional redistricting remedy as well as legislative districting. When a lawful single-member map can be implemented after legislative inaction, §2a(c) does not require an at-large election in its place; the available four-district federal plan therefore remains operative.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These seven directly adopt this proposition at the stated feasible-map scope.

**Controlling explanation:** The later statute addresses the election of the delegation through single-member districts, and its reference to districts established by law includes judicial action supplying a remedy. Legislative failure alone does not remove a lawful judicial plan from its command. The competing at-large construction would make that failure sufficient to displace an available arrangement complying with §2c. The Court instead applies the command to the remedy actually before it.

Lopez confines the federal court’s temporary authority: the plan must rest on independent lawful remedial power, comply with the Constitution and Voting Rights Act, displace state policy no more than necessary, and remain temporary until a lawful precleared arrangement becomes available. McDaniel prevents use of the court-order label to implement uncleared governmental policy. The District Court’s actual formulation and independent remedial authority support this federal map. A replacement requires actual lawful availability and review of preserved independent objections, rather than submission alone. The competing opinions’ broader accounts of §2a(c) do not alter their direct agreement on the feasible-map rule.

##### V. Constitutional review of this unenforceable plan was premature

**Holding and operative rule:** Constitutional examination of this state plan before it can legally be implemented under §5 is premature, including the challenge to the state court’s authority to adopt it. The Article I alternative must therefore also be vacated on that independent sequencing ground.

**Authority:** Part V of the Court opinion, directly joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Connor v. Waller requires constitutional review to follow the preclearance process for the plan that can actually become operative. Reviewing an unenforceable plan first risks passing on a measure the administrative process may alter or prevent from taking effect. It can also invert the proper sequence by making the administrative decision appear to revise a constitutional judgment. A challenge directed at the state court’s authority rather than the districts’ design does not cure that prematurity. This additional ground supports vacatur while preserving the statutory nonuse disposition.

##### VI. Section 2a(c) was not wholly repealed, but its complete surviving operation is divided

**Independently majority-supported proposition:** Section 2c did not wholly repeal §2a(c) by implication.

**Authority:** Scalia, Kennedy and Ginsburg expressly adopt that proposition in the retained-fallback concurrence; O’Connor and Thomas expressly adopt it in the partial dissent. These five direct positions support the same negative statutory conclusion despite their different remedies. This is not a combination under Marks.

**Controlling explanation:** Both writings preserve a field in which the older provision operates alongside the later single-member command. Their agreement is limited to rejecting wholesale implied repeal. They differ on the surviving trigger: three require an election so imminent that no competent legislative or judicial actor can complete lawful redistricting without disrupting it; two apply the older provision until the State completes its legally prescribed districting process, including preclearance. Neither complete operational rationale commands a majority. The independently controlling seven-Justice feasible-map rule above governs this remedy; the actual division, rather than a synthesized exception, describes the remaining question.

#### Law After Decision

Covered state-court plans require actual §5 approval, and the specified information and State-administration rules govern the competing automatic-approval contentions. The Article I alternative is vacated. Section 2c governs a feasible judicial single-member remedy after legislative inaction, subject to the existing lawful-remedy and replacement conditions. Five Justices reject wholesale implied repeal of §2a(c), but its full surviving operation remains divided between the stated incompatible constructions. The seven-Justice feasible-map holding supplies the governing rule here without a narrowest-ground synthesis.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Kennedy and Ginsburg.** They reconcile §§2a(c) and 2c by preserving constitutional applications of the older fallback only as a last resort: the election must be so imminent that no competent legislative or judicial actor can complete lawful districting without disrupting the electoral process. A feasible map must follow §2c and state policies insofar as consistent with superior law. Section 2a(c)’s reference to state law does not bar that judicial remedy, and Mississippi’s parallel fallback operates correspondingly. The actual federal map defeats both at-large demands. Paragraphs (1)–(4) ordinarily conflict with later population-equality requirements, though an unusual census requiring no constitutional district change is not impossible. They reject complete repeal and the partial dissent’s view that unfinished state process alone requires at-large elections.

**Stevens, concurring in part and in the judgment, joined by Souter and Breyer.** They join the feasible-map rule and would hold that §2c impliedly repealed §2a(c) and preempted Mississippi’s at-large fallback. Under Posadas, the categorical later command both conflicts with the older at-large regime and comprehensively replaces it. The 1967 enactment history and the express, expired exception reinforce that conclusion: a State entitled to more than one Representative that had elected its Representatives at large in every prior election could do so for the Ninety-first Congress. Only Hawaii and New Mexico qualified, and the permission covered only the 1968 election. They reject the retained emergency exception rather than accepting it as an additional rationale for their judgment.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment.** He resolves the statutes at the feasible-map scope: the later single-member command governs this available judicial remedy, while an unavoidable mapless emergency is beyond the required decision. He joins the statutory nonuse and adequate-ground vacatur but not the post-injunction administrative-clock rationale or the independent Connor ground. He would require a party seeking narrower relief than the operative election arrangement to demonstrate a complete lawful cure; a voter-by-voter exception cannot make an unlawful statewide plan usable. Officials should supply the operative map, actual submission and approval record and proposed replacement date. The court must prevent an election under an unapproved governmental change. A lawful precleared replacement should receive ordinary review of its actual effect and preserved independent objections, so the interim federal plan neither becomes permanent through inertia nor disappears on submission alone.

**O’Connor, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** They join preclearance nonuse, rejection of later automatic approval, constitutional vacatur and the Connor sequencing ground. They would reverse the single-member remedy. The majority’s feasible-map approach and the retained-fallback concurrence’s imminence restriction lack that textual condition, in their view. They preserve the distinct state-process and federal-remedy roles and invoke avoidance of the asserted commandeering concern without declaring §2c unconstitutional. On remand, the at-large arrangement must satisfy independent constitutional and Voting Rights Act requirements; cumulative voting or another lawful method remains possible rather than mandatory winner-take-all voting.

### Nassau County v. Shain

Merits decision, March 31, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Review of Shain v. Ellison, 273 F.3d 56. On review of the Second Circuit's October 19, 2001 judgment. The questions are whether the County may impose blanket strip and visual body-cavity searches on misdemeanor detainees without individualized reasonable suspicion, and whether the prohibition was clearly established for the responsible official's July 1995 conduct.

After a domestic-dispute arrest and Family Court remand, Shain underwent the jail's intake search. The police's pocketknife information was not shown to have reached the searching officer. The jury awarded no compensatory or punitive damages; the district court entered one dollar in nominal damages and declaratory relief. The appellate court affirmed the pertinent liability and immunity rulings and remanded the unresolved injunction request. That remand produced a separate September 17, 2002 injunction.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

| Constitutional liability for the proved policy and search | Affirmed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | The pertinent liability judgment stands. |

The three dissenters would reverse constitutional liability and remand for consequences for the reviewed relief dependent on it. They cast no vote granting or denying immunity on an assumed constitutional violation. The separate September 17, 2002 injunction remains in its existing procedural position outside this review.

| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Would reverse constitutional liability and reconsider dependent reviewed relief | Joins the conduct-date legal method; disagrees with the individualized-suspicion requirement and its application; does not reach the personal-immunity application. |

#### Holdings

##### I. Reasonable suspicion for this misdemeanor intake search

**Holding and operative rule:** A jail must have individualized reasonable suspicion that a misdemeanor-level detainee is concealing weapons or contraband before subjecting that person to the strip and visual body-cavity intake search at issue. The offense, actual personal characteristics, arrest circumstances and other properly available facts may establish that suspicion; admission to common custody or judicial remand alone does not justify this intensely intrusive practice.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six votes.

**Controlling explanation:** Bell v. Wolfish requires consideration of the search's scope, manner, place and justification, giving substantial weight to genuine institutional-security needs. The need to keep contraband from a shared facility is real, but the extensive compelled exposure here requires more than lawful detention and a general admission policy. Bell's contact-visit search rested on the security circumstances of that encounter; it does not make every jail admission sufficient justification for this intrusion. The dissent argues that the minor charge is an imperfect predictor of contraband and uniform screening protects shared custody. Those concerns receive weight in the balance, but the record does not justify dispensing with individualized reasonable suspicion for this intimate misdemeanor intake search. The requirement permits consideration of concrete security facts at a level below probable cause. Here the searching officer was not shown to have received the police's pocketknife information or other individualized contraband information. Judicial-remand status and the described file information supply no adequate suspicion. The County's proved policy and its actual application therefore violate the Fourth Amendment.

**Precedent treatment:** Bell's contextual balance is applied, while its contact-visit justification remains distinct from the blanket misdemeanor intake policy. The present rule concerns the identified strip and visual search and retains the relevance of its actual security setting, manner and scope.

##### II. The legal method for personal qualified immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Personal qualified immunity turns on whether the law when the official acted made the unlawfulness of the particular conduct apparent to a reasonable official. The reviewing court must consider all relevant preconduct authority and obvious applications of established rules, accounting for hierarchy, actual conflicts and factual context, without requiring a Supreme Court factual twin or treating a fixed count of cases as conclusive.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court, directly joined by all nine Justices. Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas join this methodology while dissenting from the constitutional judgment and leaving its immunity application unaddressed on their contrary merits ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Elder v. Holloway requires consideration of all relevant precedent on the preserved immunity question. United States v. Lanier rejects a Supreme-Court-only or identical-facts requirement while retaining particularized apparent unlawfulness. Hope v. Pelzer confirms that an established rule can supply fair warning through an obvious application, and McClendon v. City of Columbia retains that method in the present Term. The constitutional rule governing the Court's current decision and the sources of notice in July 1995 must be kept separate: decisions issued after the conduct explain today's reviewing method but cannot create earlier warning. The County's policy responsibility is distinct from the individual's personal defense; the County has no qualified immunity.

**Precedent treatment:** Elder, Lanier, Hope and McClendon are applied as rules of review; none supplies retroactive July 1995 notice. Harlow and Anderson retain the objective defense and particularized clarity inquiry.

##### III. The responsible official had fair warning in July 1995

**Holding and operative rule:** Weber v. Dell, Walsh v. Franco and Wachtler v. County of Herkimer gave fair warning before July 1995 that judicial remand did not authorize the suspicionless misdemeanor strip and visual body-cavity search presented here. The responsible individual therefore cannot obtain qualified immunity on the asserted distinction, and the reviewed liability judgment, one-dollar nominal award and declaration are affirmed.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; six votes for this application and the resulting relief.

**Controlling explanation:** Weber and Walsh identified the restriction on blanket searches of minor offenders. Wachtler addressed admission after arraignment and judicial remand, so the asserted difference between temporary detention and court-ordered jail custody did not remove the warning. Wachtler's protection for an official's reasonable uncertainty about individualized suspicious circumstances is different from permission to operate a suspicionless policy. The present record supplies no communicated facts that recreate that uncertainty. The circuit authorities gave sufficiently concrete notice under the legal method in Part II; disagreement about the ultimate constitutional balance does not itself erase their warning. The later September 17, 2002 injunction followed the already completed appellate remand and lies outside the specified review of the October 19, 2001 judgment. Its standing, scope and validity require properly presented review of that separate order.

**Precedent treatment:** Weber, Walsh and Wachtler supply conduct-date warning at their actual circuit authority and factual scope. The Court applies the ordinary fair-warning rule rather than treating these circuit decisions as earlier Supreme Court holdings.

#### Precedent Treatment

Bell v. Wolfish remains the governing contextual search framework; its contact-visit holding does not justify this blanket misdemeanor intake practice. Elder v. Holloway, United States v. Lanier, Hope v. Pelzer and McClendon v. City of Columbia retain the all-relevant-law, conduct-date inquiry. Weber, Walsh and Wachtler supply the actual pre-July-1995 circuit warning; their role in notice remains distinct from the Supreme Court rule announced here.

#### Law After Decision

For the identified intimate intake searches of misdemeanor-level detainees, individualized reasonable suspicion of concealed weapons or contraband is required, assessed from properly available facts within Bell's contextual balance. Judicial remand and general admission alone are insufficient. The responsible individual's fair warning is established on this July 1995 record, while the County's policy responsibility remains distinct from personal immunity. Prospective relief requires its own continuing controversy and lawful remedial scope.

#### Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Kennedy and Thomas, agrees that immunity review must consider all relevant conduct-date law, but dissents from the constitutional ruling. Bell's contextual balance, in their view, permits the reasonable visual screening of persons entering common custody because contraband risks do not depend on the seriousness of a charge. Their institutional-security ground requires a reasonable search for protection; it does not rely on attributing uncommunicated police information to the searching officer. They would reverse the reviewed constitutional liability and remand for the consequences for relief dependent on it. On that merits disposition they do not decide whether the individual would have immunity if the search were unconstitutional.

A challenge to the September 17, 2002 order must proceed on that order's own procedural footing, with current standing and remedial scope properly presented.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O’CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Holdings

##### I. Objective notice and assent to SmartDownload

**Holding and operative rule:** Downloading software does not accept license terms unless the user has actual or reasonable notice that the invited act manifests agreement to those terms. California's ordinary objective-assent rules apply to electronic transactions and to arbitration terms on the same basis as other contractual terms; the developed record establishes neither the requisite SmartDownload notice nor assent for these five users.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court, joined in full by all nine Justices; the same unanimous coalition directly supports all four holdings.

**Controlling explanation:** First Options requires independent judicial determination of antecedent agreement absent an established delegation. Howsam preserves that allocation for foundational consent, while assigning forum timing within agreed arbitration differently. Casarotto protects ordinary formation rules while forbidding an arbitration-specific notice disability. ProCD upheld acceptance through use after reasonable advance notice, meaningful review and practical rejection; those predicates distinguish it from the present downloads. Four users encountered a license reference below the download invitation, and Fagan's third-party route supplied still less notice. The developed evidence establishes no actual or reasonably conspicuous notice connecting the invited act to contractual acceptance. The notice assessment accounts for screen variation and rests on the developed evidence of these users' actual download routes. Netscape's proposition that an informed user can accept terms without reading them begins after the notice missing here. The rule requires objective assent, rather than a prescribed button design or special prominence for an arbitration clause. Common-law formation and Article 2 both require assent, so the same result follows under either suggested framework.

**Precedent treatment:** First Options and Howsam are applied to antecedent consent. Casarotto is applied to preserve evenhanded contract rules. ProCD is distinguished on its notice, review, rejection and acceptance predicates, not on electronic form.

##### II. The requested formation trial

**Holding and operative rule:** FAA §4 requires a trial when a preserved, genuine dispute about making the arbitration agreement is supported by evidence permitting a reasonable finding for the proponent of assent. Netscape's request for another formation proceeding fails because it sought decision on an uncontroverted record below and identifies no supported material dispute requiring such a trial.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion adopts this independently sufficient resolution of the trial request.

**Controlling explanation:** The record was developed through affidavits, depositions, screen images and a demonstration. Netscape asked the district court to decide that record, then sought a formation trial on appeal. Hypothetical screen differences and imperfect recollection do not furnish evidence from which the required notice and assent could reasonably be found. The FAA's trial procedure resolves genuine disputes; it does not create one from a possible different interface. First Options supplies independent review of legal conclusions and clear-error review of supported factual findings, with no added arbitral deference for the antecedent agreement question. The preservation posture and evidentiary insufficiency each support denying this requested remand. Ordinary proceedings on the statutory claims may resume.

**Precedent treatment:** First Options' ordinary legal and factual review rules are applied. Section 4's trial requirement retains its genuine-dispute predicate.

##### III. The accepted Communicator license's scope

**Holding and operative rule:** The accepted Communicator license's agreement-related arbitration clause does not encompass these claims of separate SmartDownload surveillance, which invoke no right or obligation under that integrated browser agreement. Technical cooperation between programs does not itself incorporate one program into another's contract.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion directly adopts this separate ground defeating compelled arbitration of the five users' claims.

**Controlling explanation:** Mastrobuono directs attention to the agreement as a whole, including genuine contractual limits. The Communicator license names Communicator and Navigator, contains an integration provision and exists alongside distinct SmartDownload terms. The complaints identify the browser's cookie as independently innocuous and attribute the challenged transmissions to SmartDownload. The claims therefore do not require construction of, or assert rights and duties under, the accepted browser license. A broad arbitration clause can cover statutory claims related to its actual bargain, but a scope presumption cannot supply the contractual relationship missing here. First Options protects the antecedent agreement inquiry, and Howsam retains judicial allocation of ordinary substantive scope absent clear delegation; these allegations concern that scope, rather than a procedural condition within agreed arbitration. This interpretation enforces the actual integrated contract.

**Precedent treatment:** Mastrobuono is applied to the integrated text and its genuine limits. First Options and Howsam retain the judicial inquiry into actual agreement and ordinary substantive scope.

##### IV. Specht's asserted indirect benefit

**Holding and operative rule:** An incidental or speculative commercial advantage from visitors' software use does not establish the direct contractual benefit asserted as a basis to bind a nonuser to their license's arbitration clause. The proponent must establish a predicate agreement and an applicable contractual or equitable relationship binding that particular person; Netscape has not done so for Specht.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion adopts this distinct ground defeating compulsion of the sixth plaintiff.

**Controlling explanation:** First Options requires attention to the particular person's obligation, and ProCD enforces an actual undertaking rather than imposing promises on strangers. Specht never acquired SmartDownload and invokes no performance due under a license. The five users' unaccepted terms supply no predicate agreement. Hypothesized agreements with other visitors likewise do not turn possible improved downloads, followed by possible subscriptions and disputed commissions, into a direct promised contractual benefit. Specht denies the commissions. The direct benefits illustrated in American Bureau of Shipping v. Tencara Shipyard and Deloitte Noraudit involved concrete contractual advantages that the asserted chain lacks. The claimed equitable basis therefore fails on its own predicates: the asserted visitor benefit supplies no necessary contractual connection to Specht.

**Precedent treatment:** First Options' person-specific agreement inquiry and ProCD's actual-undertaking limit are applied. Tencara and Deloitte Noraudit are distinguished at the scope of the direct-benefit examples on which Netscape relies.

#### Precedent Treatment

First Options governs personal assent, independent judicial determination and ordinary appellate review. Howsam's allocation for forum timing within agreed arbitration remains distinct from foundational consent and ordinary substantive scope. Casarotto preserves generally applicable formation rules without an arbitration-specific notice burden. ProCD's accepted software covenant remains enforceable under its notice, review and rejection predicates; the missing notice here distinguishes that bargain. Mastrobuono's integrated-contract method preserves the actual scope of the Communicator license.

#### Law After Decision

Electronic conduct can accept contractual terms when ordinary law supplies actual or reasonable notice of the act's contractual meaning. An arbitration proponent must establish each person's agreement or an applicable nonsignatory basis and show that the actual bargain covers the asserted claims. A formation trial requires a preserved genuine dispute supported by evidence. Those rules permit enforcement of informed electronic bargains while requiring the separate notice, scope and direct-benefit predicates presented here.

### United States v. Hambrick

Merits decision, March 31, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Fourth Circuit’s affirmance of the denial of suppression following a conditional guilty plea. The preserved question concerns privacy in subscriber-identifying and billing records obtained through conceded invalid process, and the distinct availability and causal scope of exclusion. MindSpring produced identifying, address, telephone, payment and Internet-address information in response to the March 19, 1998 subpoena. Later grand-jury process and the July 10, 1998 home warrant produced additional evidence. The Fourth Circuit affirmed the suppression denial on August 3, 2000.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Suppression disposition vacated and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Opposing:** O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The preserved challenge returns for application of Guest to the actual account and acquisition, followed by any necessary constitutional exclusion and causal inquiry. The conditional-plea conviction is not automatically reversed. The withdrawn sentencing issue remains outside review.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SOUTER, and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which O’CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined as to Part II. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion. O’CONNOR, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which SCALIA, KENNEDY, and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Breyer in full; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas as to Part II | Vacates and remands the suppression disposition | Complete joins cover the Guest remand and separate statutory and constitutional remedy rules. All nine join Part II: no statutory exclusion for a nonconstitutional violation alone; constitutional violation and causation remain separate. |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | | Supports vacatur and remand | Proposed ordinary-account extension and acquisition-specific inquiry |
| Dissent | O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Would affirm suppression denial | Failure to prove a protected relationship on these records |

#### Holdings

##### I. Guest requires examination of the actual restricted-account relationship

**Holding and operative rule:** A subscriber’s limited entrustment of nonpublic identifying records to a restricted-account provider does not by itself eliminate a reasonable expectation of privacy. The claimant must establish a personal protected interest through his actual expectation and the account’s terms, exclusion and identifying functions and practices, together with the governmental acquisition; the categorical third-party dismissal here must be vacated for that inquiry.

**Authority:** Part I of Ginsburg’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** Guest makes function and the actual relationship decisive. The fact that a provider possesses the information, or that the production contains no message content, does not alone resolve the privacy claim. Miller’s conveyed bank records and Smith’s dialed numbers retain their holdings, but Guest forecloses their automatic extension to every restricted-account identifier. Confidentiality alone is likewise insufficient.

The production included information connecting Hambrick’s identity to his account as well as addresses, telephone, payment and Internet-address fields. The lower court’s categorical treatment did not perform Guest’s account-specific inquiry. The government and the dissent identify ordinary service information, but that description cannot substitute for assessment of the actual protected relationship under the governing rule. Hambrick bears the proof burden. On remand, the courts must apply that rule to the supported terms, functions, practices, his personal interest and what the government actually acquired, preserving existing findings and ordinary procedural limits. Protection and a violation depend on that application; neither follows merely from invalid process.

##### II. Statutory disclosure violations and constitutional exclusion have separate remedies

**Holding and operative rule:** Under 18 U.S.C. §2708, the disclosure chapter’s specified remedies do not authorize the requested exclusion for a nonconstitutional statutory violation alone. A constitutional suppression claim requires a violation of Hambrick’s personal Fourth Amendment interest and the actual causal relationship to the evidence, subject to applicable preserved exclusion defenses; later process neither retroactively validates an unconstitutional acquisition nor automatically makes later evidence inadmissible.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, directly joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The conceded March 19 subpoena defect remains distinct from a constitutional search. The statute’s remedial structure does not supply an exclusion remedy merely because its disclosure restrictions were violated. If the account-specific inquiry establishes a constitutional violation, the courts must examine the later grand-jury process and home warrant, the origins of the evidence and applicable exclusion principles. A later valid signature is not itself an independent source or attenuation finding. Conversely, the initial defect does not dispense with the personal-right and causal requirements. Guest’s personal-damages immunity and Bach’s warrant-execution rule answer other questions. Any exclusion and its lawful effect on the conditional plea must follow the actual constitutional and causal determinations.

#### Precedent Treatment

Guest v. Leis governs the restricted-account privacy inquiry; its separate conduct-date damages immunity is not an exclusion holding.

United States v. Miller retains its conveyed-bank-record holding, subject to Guest’s rejection of automatic extension to every protected restricted-account identifier.

Smith v. Maryland retains its dialed-number holding with the same Guest limit on automatic extension.

United States v. Bach governs technically necessary provider execution of an actual warrant; that rule does not authorize or validate this conceded invalid subpoena.

Konop v. Hawaiian Airlines preserves conduct-specific statutory classification and the separation of statutory from constitutional claims.

#### Law After Decision

Existing Guest protection governs the account-specific question in this criminal case. The actual personal relationship and acquisition must be determined before constitutional authorization and exclusion. Section 2708 supplies no suppression remedy for a nonconstitutional disclosure violation alone; later evidence requires the separate causal and exclusion analysis.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the account-specific remand and would extend Guest to necessary entrustment of ordinary nonpublic identification and payment information. The claimant would identify the personal nonpublic relationship between the user and restricted account and the actual acquisition; public exposure or a materially different function could defeat that premise. The government would then establish applicable authorization or an exception, while suppression would still require its separate constitutional and causal predicates. Protection would follow restricted-account function rather than bargaining power over a provider’s boilerplate. Guest’s functional reasoning, and the limited bank-record and dialed-number holdings in Miller and Smith, support that extension in his view. It reaches the identifying relationship, not every provider file or a universal warrant requirement.

He would require an acquisition-specific account of what was produced and the origins of later evidence, with the government substantiating asserted authorization, independence or attenuation under the applicable rule. A later signature alone would be insufficient. The court would exclude only what the constitutional remedy requires and then implement the conditional-plea consequences through ordinary criminal procedure.

**O’Connor, dissenting, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** They accept Guest at its actual scope but regard the described identity, address, payment, telephone and Internet-address fields as ordinary service or business information rather than confidential access credentials or a proved protected identifying relationship. Hambrick has not carried the personal-interest burden on this record. Miller and Smith remain applicable at that limited scope. Invalid process and statutory confidentiality cannot themselves establish privacy, and the dissenters would affirm suppression denial without creating a universal provider-possession or noncontent exception.

United States v. Hambrick, 225 F.3d 656 (table), August 3, 2000; Guest v. Leis, February 27, 2002; 18 U.S.C. §2708; United States v. Miller, 425 U.S. 435; Smith v. Maryland, 442 U.S. 735; Konop v. Hawaiian Airlines, January 27, 2003; United States v. Bach, March 5, 2003. The March 19, 1998 process is conceded invalid. The challenged production contained subscriber-identifying and billing fields, not email or file contents.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fourth Circuit for application of Guest to the particular account records and acquisition. If a protected personal interest is established, the courts must determine whether the acquisition violated the Fourth Amendment, including any applicable authorization, and then resolve the necessary causal and exclusion issues and lawful conditional-plea consequences. The conviction’s status depends on those ordinary criminal proceedings rather than automatic reversal.

**End of entry: United States v. Hambrick, merits decision, March 31, 2003.**

### Kentucky Association of Health Plans, Inc. v. Miller, No. 00-1471

Merits decision, April 2, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued January 14, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit. The question is whether Kentucky's two any-willing-provider requirements are laws regulating insurance saved from ERISA preemption.

The Sixth Circuit affirmed the judgment rejecting the health plans' preemption challenge. Section 304.17A-270 concerns qualifying providers in the plan's geographic coverage area willing to accept the insurer's participation terms and conditions; §304.17A-171(2) concerns qualifying licensed chiropractors when the plan includes chiropractic benefits. The plans challenge the laws' effects on selective contracting and costs, their coverage of nonexempt self-insurers and multiple-employer welfare arrangements, and their coverage of HMOs supplying only administrative services to self-insured plans.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

All nine support rejection of the categorical preemption challenge to both statutory commands and both covered-entity objections. Kentucky may enforce the distinct requirements under their actual terms and applicable federal exemptions. Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment on the nonexempt self-insurer/MEWA definition and administrative-only-HMO components without adding a ground; his broader insurance rationale and limited joinder are stated below.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O’CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., joined as to Part VI. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela as to Part VI | Affirms the Sixth Circuit | Eight Associates directly adopt Parts I–VI; the general deemer and federal-enforcement rules in Part VI have nine direct joins. |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports affirmance | Retains UNUM's practical guideposts for insured arrangements; joins Part VI's deemer and enforcement boundaries; agrees without adding grounds on the two categorical covered-entity objections. |

#### Holdings

##### I. The insurance saving clause requires two conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** A state law regulates insurance within ERISA §514(b)(2)(A) when it is specifically directed toward entities engaged in insurance in their insurance activity and substantially affects the risk-pooling arrangement between insurer and insured. Both conditions are necessary; a material change in promised coverage, conditions of access or the participating-provider network can satisfy the second without a separately demonstrated new actuarial transfer of risk.

**Authority:** Parts I–V of the opinion of the Court are directly joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight votes. Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment on his different stated insurance rationale and joins Part VI only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court adopts these two conditions to identify what distinguishes insurance regulation from general rules that merely affect an insurer's ordinary expenses. UNUM and Rush Prudential establish that substantive coverage conditions matter to the insurance bargain even without proof of an independent transfer of risk. The former common-sense inquiry and nonconjunctive McCarran-Ferguson guideposts do not give the inquiry the same definite operative structure. The new conditions preserve those coverage holdings while replacing that method. A law directed at insurers' network arrangements remains insurance-directed although providers also experience consequences and cannot make contracts insurers are forbidden to make. An exclusive network and one admitting qualifying willing providers offer different access under pooled coverage. The plans' cost-control argument identifies that regulated feature of the bargain rather than an exemption from regulation. Royal Drug and Pireno classified practices under a different statute; their factors no longer supply a separate ERISA checklist. Contrary to the concurrence's preference for the existing guideposts, the two conditions make the necessary industry connection and substantial contractual effect explicit while preserving the practical coverage principles on which those earlier results depend.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM and Rush retain their substantive coverage and compatible-enforcement holdings, while their guidepost method is replaced. Metropolitan Life's substantive insurance-contract principle is applied. Royal Drug and Pireno retain their force under their own statutory inquiry rather than supplying a further ERISA test.

##### II. Section 304.17A-270's provider-participation command

**Holding and operative rule:** Kentucky's requirement that a health insurer refrain from discriminating against a provider within the health benefit plan's geographic coverage area who is willing to meet the insurer's participation terms and conditions is saved insurance regulation. Section 304.17A-270 includes the Kentucky state Medicaid program and Medicaid partnerships within its stated command and retains those actual participation and geographic conditions.

**Authority:** Part II of the same eight-Justice Court opinion applies both conditions in Holding I. Stone-Zsela supports affirmance for these insured arrangements under his separate existing-framework rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The provision directs the network-access obligation to health insurers and changes the provider access that they may promise as part of pooled coverage. It therefore regulates an insurance function and substantially affects the insured bargain. The insurer retains the offered participation terms and conditions, but may not exclude a qualifying provider within the stated area contrary to this command. The law's effects on providers and selective contracting follow from the insurance-network obligation; they do not turn it into general professional regulation. UNUM's recognition of coverage conditions and Metropolitan Life's treatment of substantive insurance terms support this characterization. The plan's economic preference for a closed network does not remove the State's saved regulatory authority.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM and Metropolitan Life are applied to the substantive access condition, using the two-condition rule announced in Holding I.

##### III. Section 304.17A-171(2)'s chiropractic command

**Holding and operative rule:** When a health benefit plan includes chiropractic benefits, §304.17A-171(2)'s requirement that it permit a licensed chiropractor accepting the plan's terms, conditions, reimbursement rates and quality standards to serve as a participating primary chiropractic provider for covered persons is saved insurance regulation. The command is triggered by existing chiropractic coverage and operates on the plan's listed conditions.

**Authority:** Part III of the same eight-Justice Court opinion applies both conditions in Holding I. Stone-Zsela supports affirmance for these insured arrangements under his separate rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** This provision determines how an existing covered benefit is available through the insurance network. The condition directly addresses the coverage arrangement and materially changes the provider choices offered under it. Its licensed-provider, benefit-coverage, terms, reimbursement and quality conditions define its reach. The geographic condition of §304.17A-270 belongs to that different provision. The chiropractic command does not require a plan to add chiropractic benefits or accept a provider's demanded reimbursement rate. Rush Prudential recognizes that a condition governing access to covered treatment can regulate the insurance bargain; the present application follows that coverage principle under Holding I's two necessary conditions.

**Precedent treatment:** Rush Prudential's substantive coverage principle is applied, preserving the difference between a covered-benefit condition and a new benefit or remedy.

##### IV. The specified self-insurer and welfare-arrangement coverage

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 304.17A-005(23)'s inclusion, for §304.17A-270, of self-insurers and multiple-employer welfare arrangements not exempt from state regulation under ERISA does not defeat insurance direction. Such arrangements can conduct the relevant pooling activity; the statutory exclusion for federally exempt arrangements remains effective under the federal law governing the particular arrangement.

**Authority:** Part IV of the same eight-Justice Court opinion. Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment rejecting this categorical covered-entity objection and adds no ground on that component.

**Controlling explanation:** The saving inquiry concerns the regulated insurance activity, not whether the actor is a separately incorporated insurance company. An arrangement that pools the pertinent risk can engage in insurance for this purpose despite being called self-insured. The actual definition preserves the exclusion of arrangements exempt under ERISA, so its inclusion of other self-insurers and welfare arrangements does not establish forbidden regulation of every self-funded employee plan. Applicable federal exemptions and provisions governing the actual arrangement determine the permissible regulatory reach. The plans' proposed classification would erase the enacted difference between exempt and nonexempt arrangements rather than apply it.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM's insured-coverage analysis and retained deemer boundary remain consistent with the actual federal-exemption distinction; this holding resolves the presented definition objection under the new insurance-activity rule.

##### V. Administrative-only HMOs and their separate plan customers

**Holding and operative rule:** The statutes retain their saved insurance character when applied to HMOs performing provider-network administration for self-insured plans, even if the HMO does not itself underwrite medical-cost risk. Regulating that separate actor's insurance activity does not itself deem its exempt plan customer an insurer; the plan's actual deemer protection continues to govern prohibited direct regulation.

**Authority:** Part V of the same eight-Justice Court opinion. Stone-Zsela agrees in the judgment rejecting this categorical administrative-only-HMO objection and adds no ground on that component.

**Controlling explanation:** Administration of the pooled coverage arrangement and its provider network is the relevant insurance activity. An administrator's lack of its own underwriting obligation does not convert the statutes into general business regulation or remove their substantial effect on the coverage bargain. Nor does a separate administrator acquire every exemption available to a customer merely because it performs services for that customer. The statutes' network commands and the deemer clause address different legal subjects, and the latter must be enforced where its actual prerequisites are met. Rush Prudential's earlier holding concerned insured coverage furnished by a risk-bearing HMO. It supplies the substantive coverage principle but did not decide this administrative-only objection. The Court resolves that presented objection here through the two-condition rule and the distinction between the separate administrator and the protected plan.

**Precedent treatment:** Rush Prudential is applied for its coverage principle at its actual prior scope; this decision adds the administrative-only-HMO application rather than treating it as already resolved there.

##### VI. Saving, the deemer clause and federal enforcement remain distinct

**Holding and operative rule:** Saving a substantive insurance requirement under §514(b)(2)(A) remains subject to §514(b)(2)(B)'s protection against deeming an exempt employee benefit plan an insurer or insurance business for state regulation. It also leaves ERISA's independent enforcement limits intact: benefits enforcement must use the applicable federal remedial channel, and a competing state enforcement scheme remains subject to its own preemption analysis.

**Authority:** Part VI of the opinion of the Court is directly joined by all nine Justices, including Stone-Zsela.

**Controlling explanation:** The general preemption clause, insurance saving clause and deemer clause perform separate tasks. Identifying a saved coverage condition does not resolve every plan's federal status or authorize a different remedial system. UNUM preserved the federal benefits action for its saved notice-prejudice condition. Rush Prudential likewise preserved enforcement through §502(a)(1)(B) for the covered medical-necessity determination, rather than an independent state damages action. Pilot Life addresses the separate conflict created by a competing state enforcement scheme. Those boundaries continue to govern Kentucky's enforcement of the provider-access commands under their actual conditions and applicable federal exemptions. For the covered insured arrangements, those boundaries do not sustain the plans' demand for an injunction on the ground that the challenged insurance requirements are categorically preempted.

**Precedent treatment:** UNUM and Rush Prudential retain their compatible-federal-enforcement and deemer limits. Pilot Life retains its separate bar against an incompatible state enforcement scheme.

#### Precedent Treatment

UNUM's saved notice-prejudice result and Rush Prudential's saved medical-necessity result remain governing substantive coverage decisions, with their federal-enforcement and deemer boundaries. Effective April 2, 2003, their nonconjunctive guidepost method is replaced by the two necessary conditions in Holding I. Rush's prior holding remains confined to the risk-bearing HMO and insured coverage then before the Court; the administrative-only-HMO objection is resolved in this decision. Metropolitan Life's recognition of substantive insurance-contract regulation remains. Royal Drug and Pireno no longer provide a separate checklist for ERISA saving, and Pilot Life retains its independent enforcement-preemption rule.

#### Law After Decision

Courts applying ERISA's insurance saving clause must ask whether the law targets entities engaged in insurance in their insurance activity and substantially affects the insurer-insured risk-pooling arrangement. Material coverage and network-access conditions can satisfy that rule without a separate actuarial transfer. Kentucky's two commands satisfy it under their distinct provider and coverage conditions, including the presented nonexempt-arrangement and administrative-only-HMO coverage. Actual federal exemptions, the deemer clause and ERISA's separate remedial requirements continue to control enforcement.

#### Separate Writings

Stone-Zsela concurs in part and in the judgment. He would apply UNUM's practical, nonmechanical guideposts to the insurer-directed changes in the actual coverage bargain. Network access affects promised care and the arrangement for furnishing and financing it; the plans' cost-control objection identifies the economic effect of insurance regulation rather than a reason to disregard the saving clause. Rush Prudential preserves saved coverage conditions and the federal benefits-remedy channel. He therefore supports saving the requirements within their lawful insurance scope but would retain the existing framework rather than replace it with two universal necessary conditions. He joins Part VI's deemer and independent enforcement boundaries. Particular disputed plan status must be established under applicable federal law. His agreement in judgment on the nonexempt self-insurer/MEWA definition objection and the administrative-only-HMO objection adds no separate rationale for those components.

#### Source Notes

Kentucky Association of Health Plans, Inc. v. Miller, 538 U.S. 329, No. 00-1471; ERISA §§502 and 514, 29 U.S.C. §§1132 and 1144; Ky. Rev. Stat. §§304.17A-270, 304.17A-171(2), 304.17A-005(23); UNUM Life Insurance Co. of America v. Ward, No. 97-1868, decided April 20, 1999; Rush Prudential HMO, Inc. v. Moran, 536 U.S. 355, decided June 20, 2002; Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724; Pilot Life Insurance Co. v. Dedeaux, 481 U.S. 41; Group Life & Health Insurance Co. v. Royal Drug Co., 440 U.S. 205; Union Labor Life Insurance Co. v. Pireno, 458 U.S. 119.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed. Kentucky may enforce §304.17A-270 and §304.17A-171(2) under their respective conditions and applicable federal exemptions. A particular enforcement dispute must identify the regulated actor, the arrangement's federal status and the applicable remedial channel under the preserved rules.

### Medical Board of California v. Hason, No. 02-479

Voluntary dismissal, April 7, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 279 F.3d 1167. The November 18, 2002 grant was limited to sovereign immunity from Hason's Title II licensing suit. The Board moved to dismiss on March 3, the United States consented March 4 and Hason objected March 6. The scheduled March 25 argument was removed from the calendar on March 7. Hason requests retention of review with an amicus, $85,020 in counsel fees and reimbursement of the Court's brief-printing expense.

The full Court participates in the motion and its disposition.

#### Public Action

The motion is granted and the writ dismissed under Rule 46 without damages and costs. The retention/amicus request and each asserted monetary remedy are denied. No tally or individual lineup is announced.

The Ninth Circuit judgment remains in force. The writ is dismissed without affirmance, reversal or vacatur of that judgment. Hason's counsel-fee request and separate printing-reimbursement request are denied on the grounds stated below.

The Court issued an unsigned order explaining dismissal and the disposition of the asserted retention and monetary incidents; individual joins are not announced.

#### Holdings

##### I. Dismissal and the request to retain review

**Holding and operative rule.** The Board's motion to dismiss its writ under Rule 46 is granted, and Hason's request to retain review with an appointed amicus is denied. Dismissal ends this writ and leaves the Ninth Circuit judgment in force; monetary incidents require a separate demonstrated legal and factual basis.

**Authority.** The Court's unsigned order states these procedural grounds. Its tally and individual joins are not disclosed.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Rule 46 supplies the procedure for ending this review. Under Rule 46.2, an adverse party's objection is confined to damages and costs in this Court or the movant's representation of the petitioners; an unresolved permissible objection comes before the Court for determination. Hason nevertheless asks the Court to retain the live underlying controversy because briefing is complete, a significant split is asserted and the Board has declined settlement. Those circumstances give him no entitlement to compel continued review. His proposal to appoint an amicus, drawing on Dickerson, addresses representation of a legal position; it does not establish a judicial need to refuse this proper dismissal request. The asserted timing and strategy establish no abuse warranting that course. The Court determines the actual monetary demands below, then completes dismissal. The limited immunity question is not reached after withdrawal; the separate substantive licensing question was outside the grant. The disposition rests on voluntary dismissal, not a finding that the underlying controversy is moot.

##### II. Rule 46 and ordinary costs

**Holding and operative rule.** Rule 46's provision for damages and costs resolves amounts payable under an applicable legal source; it creates no independent entitlement to reimbursement of all counsel work caused by review. The requested $85,020 in counsel fees and reimbursement of the Court's unquantified brief-printing expense are denied under the asserted Rule 46 and ordinary-costs grounds.

**Authority.** The Court's unsigned order supplies this controlling procedural determination.

**Controlling explanation.** Hason requests $58,400 for 146 hours at $400 and $26,620 for 48.4 hours at $550. Those amounts total $85,020, but expenditure and asserted rates do not themselves establish an obligation of the Board. Rule 46 provides a way to resolve a monetary objection, not a substantive fee entitlement. Rule 43.3 allows Clerk's fees and joint-appendix printing costs but excludes printing expenses for briefs. The request that the Board repay the Court for printing Hason's brief therefore fails as an ordinary taxable cost.

##### III. Attorney-specific excess expenses under §1927

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 1927 permits an award against an admitted attorney or other admitted representative who personally and unreasonably and vexatiously multiplies proceedings, limited to the excess costs, expenses and fees reasonably incurred because of that conduct. Seeking review and later withdrawing it on the circumstances shown here establishes neither that misconduct nor the claimed causal excess expense; the request under §1927 is denied.

**Authority.** The Court's unsigned order adopts this source-specific ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statute's actor, conduct and causation requirements must each be satisfied. It does not automatically place counsel's personal statutory liability on the Board. The timing of withdrawal after opposition and briefing does not by itself show unreasonable and vexatious multiplication, nor establish that all work preparing the opposition, the brief and the objection was excess expense caused by such multiplication. The statute cannot serve as a general respondent-compensation provision merely because a petitioner abandons review. The requested award thus lacks the predicates this asserted source requires.

##### IV. Inherent sanction authority

**Holding and operative rule.** A fee sanction under the Court's inherent authority requires bad-faith abuse of the judicial process and the restraint and procedural fairness described in Chambers v. NASCO, Inc. The facts asserted here do not establish that predicate; both counsel-fee compensation and printing reimbursement are denied on this alternative ground.

**Authority.** The Court's unsigned order adopts this distinct sanctions determination.

**Controlling explanation.** Chambers recognizes power to remedy proven abuse; it does not convert an available dismissal procedure into automatic responsibility for an opponent's expenses. Hason points to an unexplained one-sentence withdrawal after briefing, the absence of an announced change of circumstances and possible strategic selection of a better vehicle. Those circumstances do not establish bad faith. Ordinary expense in litigating a properly presented case is not made sanctionable merely because review ends before argument. Because entitlement fails, findings fixing reasonable hours, rates or the amount of printing expense are unnecessary to resolve the requested sanctions.

#### Precedent Treatment

U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership, 513 U.S. 18, supplies the distinction between termination of review and disposition of the lower judgment. Its settlement-vacatur holding does not turn this withdrawal into a settlement or an automatic right to vacatur.

Chambers v. NASCO, Inc., 501 U.S. 32, supplies the bad-faith requirement, restraint and procedural fairness for the asserted inherent-authority fee sanction. It remains available for proven abuse; the present showing does not establish that predicate.

Dickerson v. United States, 530 U.S. 428, is distinguished in answering the retention request: representing an unadvanced position with an amicus in a case the Court is deciding does not establish a right or need to continue this withdrawn review.

#### Law After Decision

Rule 46 dismissal, monetary entitlement and treatment of the lower judgment retain their distinct requirements. The dismissal adds no Title II immunity or licensing holding because those questions are not reached. The Ninth Circuit judgment remains operative, and Hason's action may continue in the position that judgment created.

Supreme Court Rules 43.3 and 46 as in force on April 7, 2003; 28 U.S.C. §§1927, 2106; No. 02-479; Hason v. Medical Board of California, 279 F.3d 1167. Hason's Objection to Motion to Dismiss (March 6, 2003), including page 8 footnote 1, presents the monetary and retention requests.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The writ is terminated. Hason's underlying action continues in the posture established by the Ninth Circuit judgment. Under Rule 46.3, no mandate or other process issues absent an order of the Court; this dismissal orders none.

### Virginia v. Black, No. 01-1107

#### Holdings

##### I. An intentional threatening method may be prohibited

**Holding and operative rule.** Virginia may prohibit burning a cross on another's property, a highway, or another public place with intent to intimidate a person or group through a serious threat of unlawful violence, when the prohibition operates without regard to the speaker's viewpoint. The State must prove the threatening communication and the statutory intent beyond a reasonable doubt in the circumstances of the particular act; an intent actually to carry out the threatened violence is unnecessary.

**Authority.** Part I of the lead opinion is an opinion of the Court: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia and Breyer directly join its rule and explanation. Thomas agrees that the confined offense is permissible on his distinct conduct and alternative First Amendment grounds.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the State may address this distinctive method of intimidation because its threatening force, rather than disapproval of an idea, defines the prohibited harm. The history of cross burning explains why its use can convey a grave threat, but the history does not establish the meaning or intent of every burning. A political rally, religious ritual or hostile ideological message falls outside the offense unless the actual communication and intent satisfy its elements. R. A. V. supplies the substantial-overbreadth rule while leaving distinctions wholly within an unprotected category open. The present confined permission answers the question about this threatening method. American Life League sustains defined harmful conduct while preserving protected advocacy; ACLA establishes that public dissemination can communicate an attributable contextual threat. Those principles answer the contention that either the symbol's expressive uses or its public setting categorically immunize intentional threatening intimidation. Conversely, the State cannot use its interest in safety to punish political or religious disapproval alone. Virginia's express intent requirement permits resolution without choosing the minimum culpability for criminal statutes lacking that element, a question outside this grant.

##### II. Black's specific instruction does not preserve the required distinction

**Holding and operative rule.** An instruction permitting the burning of a cross by itself to establish intent to intimidate cannot sustain Black's conviction where the charge leaves unresolved whether the jury evaluated the communication's context and rebuttal evidence or treated burning alone as sufficient. General directions to consider both sides' evidence and prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt do not dispel that specific constitutional ambiguity.

**Authority.** Part II is an opinion of the Court joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia and Breyer. They directly agree on the defect in Black's charge; their different positions on facial invalidity and the indictment are identified separately.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the actual charge allowed the jury to pass from an expressive act to criminal intimidation without a reliable determination of the feature separating a threat from protected advocacy. Instructions 6 and 8 required consideration of both sides' evidence and proof beyond reasonable doubt of burning and intent. Yet the additional by-itself instruction left the jury free to understand the symbol as sufficient despite evidence of a different meaning. Correct general directions do not show that the jury resolved that conflict constitutionally. ACLA requires contextual and attributable threatening meaning and separates the actual liability adjudication from findings supporting other relief; evidence concerning another respondent cannot repair Black's charge. County Court of Ulster County v. Allen distinguishes permissible evidentiary inferences from substitution for an element, and Carella v. California underscores the need to examine the instruction actually given. The State's argument that the entire charge necessarily preserved a permissible inference leaves the operative ambiguity unanswered. The ruling concerns that charge and supports leaving the conviction set aside.

##### III. Black's indictment and the facial question

Black's conviction vacatur and indictment dismissal are affirmed. Seven Justices support affirmance of the indictment dismissal, but no five adopt a common rationale for that part of the judgment. Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer rely on facial invalidity of the proof provision in its present model-instruction operation; Stone-Zsela joins that proof analysis but concurs only in the judgment affirming the indictment dismissal. Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg rely on invalidity of the selected prohibition as a whole. The four-Justice facial-proof analysis and the three-Justice offense-wide analysis are distinct grounds, not a controlling facial rule.

##### IV. The different Elliott and O'Mara judgments require separate review

**Holding and operative rule.** The judgments vacating Elliott's attempted-cross-burning conviction and dismissing its indictment, and vacating O'Mara's attempted-cross-burning and conspiracy convictions and dismissing their indictments, are vacated and remanded. Virginia's courts must determine the available authoritative construction or severance of state law and apply the constitutional requirements to Elliott's own trial record and O'Mara's conditional plea, including preserved objections and applicable restrictions on further prosecution.

**Authority.** Part IV is an opinion of the Court joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia and Breyer. Their agreement on this state-law and defendant-specific remand does not require agreement on the facial analysis in Part III.

**Controlling explanation.** Elliott's jury received no Black prima facie instruction, and O'Mara preserved his constitutional challenge by conditional plea. A defect in Black's actual charge therefore does not supply their complete disposition. Because the confined intent offense is constitutionally permissible, categorical invalidation of that offense cannot by itself sustain their judgments. The state court must first determine what law validly remains and what follows on the respective records. A saving application must actually remove the unconstitutional permission to use the symbol alone as sufficient proof; a nominal disclaimer retaining that permission does not suffice. If the offense cannot operate independently under state law, an inseverable prohibition cannot sustain further proceedings. The Court supplies no state-law severability determination. Griffith v. Kentucky applies the constitutional instruction rule to nonfinal criminal cases within its scope, subject to preservation and genuine error rules; final judgments require independently applicable collateral authority. Elliott's conspiracy acquittal remains untouched. Black's affirmed indictment dismissal ends proceedings under that indictment, while these other respondents receive the distinct remand their records require.

#### Precedent Treatment

R. A. V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, retains its substantial-overbreadth holding; this decision resolves its open content-selection question only for the defined viewpoint-independent method of intentional intimidation.

American Life League v. Reno (November 13, 1995) supplies the distinction between defined harmful conduct and protected advocacy. Its service-purpose law is distinct from the selected threatening method here.

Planned Parenthood of the Columbia/Willamette, Inc. v. Its purpose-or-knowledge rule continues to govern the civil liability it addressed; Virginia independently requires intent to intimidate.

County Court of Ulster County v. Allen, 442 U.S. 140, and Carella v. California, 491 U.S. 263, supply the distinction between permissible inference and an instruction that compromises the required determination of an element. They inform the presented First Amendment instructional question.

Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314, supplies application to nonfinal criminal cases. Preservation, ordinary error rules and separate collateral-review law retain their respective functions.

#### Law After Decision

The State may prohibit this viewpoint-independent method of intentional threatening intimidation, with proof of an actual serious threat and the statutory intent in context. Black's particular by-itself charge is constitutionally inadequate despite its general burden instructions. The Court's direct majorities establish those rules and the Elliott/O'Mara remand. The facial question remains fractured at the level described in Part III; the affirmed dismissal of Black's indictment binds the parties. Further proceedings for Elliott and O'Mara apply the constitutional requirements to the state law that validly remains and their different adjudicative records.

#### Separate Writings

**Part III of the lead opinion: Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer; Stone-Zsela joins its facial-proof analysis.** These Justices conclude that the proof provision in its model-instruction operation reaches substantial protected expression. Cross burning has political and ritual uses, and treating the act alone as sufficient permits conviction across a substantial identifiable range of those uses. The problem reaches beyond a single erroneous instruction because the model instruction remains undisavowed. General proof directions do not remove its specific permission. The state court has yet to supply a final construction, and construction or severance remains for the distinct Elliott and O'Mara remands. Stevens, O'Connor and Breyer affirm Black's indictment dismissal on that present facial ground. Stone-Zsela concurs in that part of the judgment without joining its indictment rationale.

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part; Thomas joins the facial-construction portion only.** Scalia accepts the confined offense, rejects present facial invalidation and would obtain an authoritative state construction. Prima facie evidence may be sufficient when unrebutted while leaving the jury obliged to weigh contrary evidence; possible misuse alone does not establish substantial overbreadth. Thomas joins that construction objection. Scalia separately concludes that Black's by-itself instruction leaves intolerable uncertainty whether the jury considered rebuttal notwithstanding Instructions 6 and 8. He would retain conviction vacatur, vacate the indictment dismissal and permit a retrial under a valid construction, subject to ordinary restrictions. Thomas does not join this instructional conclusion or retrial disposition.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment as to Black and dissenting as to Elliott and O'Mara, joined by Kennedy and Ginsburg.** They would resolve the open content-selection question against this prohibition. The combination of a symbol-specific offense and evidentiary privilege presents a realistic danger that suppressing the associated ideology partly explains the selection. A cross can convey ideology, intimidation or both. The proof provision tilts doubtful cases toward punishment, and a general intimidation law can protect safety without this selection. They would affirm every reviewed conviction vacatur and indictment dismissal. Later removal of the proof clause cannot validate prosecutions under a law constitutionally defective when the conduct occurred.

**Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas principally treats intentional cross burning as terrorizing conduct by a particular means. Alternatively, its history gives the permissive inference a rational basis under Allen, while the whole charge preserves the prosecution's burden and the jury's ability to reject the inference. He would reverse the reviewed constitutional grounds for vacatur and dismissal for each respondent and remand for remaining preserved matters, including O'Mara's plea consequences. His position requires statutory intimidating intent and does not make every political or ritual cross burning criminal.

### Stout v. Baxter Healthcare Corp.

Decision on plenary review, May 19, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Review of the Fifth Circuit's February 19, 2002 judgment concerns whether the Pregnancy Discrimination Act permits disparate-impact review of a uniformly administered probationary attendance rule and whether this evidence permits the claim to proceed. The circuit affirmed Baxter's summary judgments on disparate treatment and impact and denial of Stout's liability cross-motion. Baxter discharged Stout after documented early-labor and miscarriage-related absence exceeded its three-day limit during ninety days of probation. The record includes expert evidence that childbirth prevents work for at least two weeks.

All nine Justices participate.

#### Judgment

J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STEVENS, O’CONNOR, SOUTER, GINSBURG and BREYER, JJ., joined, and in which SCALIA, KENNEDY and THOMAS, JJ., joined as to Parts I and III. | Opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part | Scalia | Kennedy, Thomas | Agrees on common framework and affirmed components; would affirm the impact judgment for insufficient comparative proof | Entire separate opinion |

#### Holdings

##### I. Attendance practices remain subject to the enacted impact rule

**Holding and operative rule.** Uniform administration of an attendance practice does not defeat a disparate-impact claim under Title VII concerning pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions. A plaintiff must identify the particular practice and prove by a preponderance that it causes disproportionate exclusion of a protected group; the employer then must prove by a preponderance that the practice is job related for the position and consistent with business necessity, and liability may also follow from a proved available less discriminatory alternative serving the employer's legitimate needs that the employer refuses to adopt.

**Authority.** Part I is an opinion of the Court joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Pregnancy Discrimination Act's equal-treatment clause operates alongside §703(k), not as an exemption from its impact requirements. Griggs distinguishes a neutral practice from one justified despite its discriminatory effect. Guerra explains why the PDA's comparison language is not an absolute-neutrality ceiling. Watson requires identification of a practice and substantial causal disparity while rejecting a rigid mathematical formula; the enacted §703(k), rather than an earlier formulation of the defense burden, governs the present sequence. Thus regular attendance may be a legitimate employment need without establishing the necessity of this precise three-day total cutoff during ninety days of probation. The claimant must prove comparative causal effect; pregnancy or medical hardship alone supplies no unconditional leave entitlement. Those requirements answer Baxter's concern that impact review necessarily commands preferential leave. Necessity and alternatives determine whether a proved disparity is legally justified.

##### II. The medical evidence permits the impact claim to proceed

**Holding and operative rule.** Reliable medical and operational evidence may support a causal comparison without workplace statistics when a particular practice predictably excludes an independently defined protected group. Here the expert evidence that childbirth prevents work for at least two weeks, combined with the automatic three-day cutoff during probation and the supported individual related-condition injury, permits a reasonable inference sufficient to defeat Baxter's existing impact summary judgment.

**Authority.** Part II is an opinion of the Court joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the initial group is employees undergoing childbirth during probation, a condition Congress expressly identifies, rather than everyone selected by failure of the attendance rule. The medical evidence predicts essentially complete exclusion of that group. Otherwise relevant probationary employees not affected by childbirth do not face the same status-linked inevitable absence. That difference permits a comparative inference; it remains open to contrary proof concerning the actual groups and the rule's operation. Reeves requires reasonable inferences for Stout on Baxter's motion, without judicial credibility choices. The lower court accepted the predicted exclusion but rejected the nonstatistical route because it treated equal administration as sufficient for this attendance rule.

The contrary position demands more evidence of actual comparative absence patterns and stresses that Stout experienced miscarriage rather than childbirth. The comparison already rests on an independently defined statutory condition and medically necessary exclusion, not a circular group invented from the rule's failures. Stout's own early-labor and miscarriage absence of more than two weeks is independently documented, rather than inferred solely from the childbirth testimony. Her individual nexus to the proved impact theory and any proposed class's scope still require adjudication. The Court's sufficiency ruling removes the existing summary judgment; it does not resolve the ultimate statutory defenses or expand the childbirth medical proposition to every related condition.

##### III. Intentional discrimination and the competing liability motion

**Holding and operative rule.** Baxter's summary judgment on disparate treatment and the denial of Stout's liability cross-motion are affirmed. Each motion must be considered separately with reasonable inferences for its opponent: the record supplies no reasonable inference of intentional discrimination, and the impact evidence does not establish Stout's entitlement to a liability judgment on all required predicates and defenses.

**Authority.** Part III is an opinion of the Court joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that evidence of miscarriage-related absence does not itself establish that sex motivated discharge. The policy's terms and administration treat the reasons for absence alike; there is no supported unequal enforcement, preferential treatment of other absentees or sham physical requirement. Positive performance reviews and perfect attendance during the first two months do not contradict the actual absence-based explanation for the later discharge. Reeves permits a reasonable inference from supported pretext, not an inference from the protected condition alone. Swierkiewicz separates pleading from proof and leaves the Rule 56 evidentiary obligation intact.

The impact claim presents a different question, but defeating Baxter's motion does not satisfy Stout's own motion. Viewing each motion from the required perspective preserves the employer's opportunity to contest the comparison and the remaining statutory predicates and defenses. Denial of her requested liability judgment therefore stands while her impact claim returns for further adjudication.

##### IV. The governing remand

**Holding and operative rule.** The Fifth Circuit judgment is vacated only as to Baxter's disparate-impact summary judgment and is otherwise affirmed. On remand the court must apply §703(k)'s causal-impact, job-relatedness, business-necessity and refused-alternative sequence, determine Stout's individual nexus and any class predicates under ordinary procedure, and determine relief if liability is established.

**Authority.** Part IV is an opinion of the Court joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court's evidence ruling governs the existing impact motion; the remand is not permission to restore that same judgment solely because the attendance rule was uniformly administered or the supported medical evidence lacked workplace statistics. The parties may contest the inference and the relationship between the defined group, Stout's documented condition and any class properly proposed. Once impact is proved, Baxter must establish the need for this particular practice. A probation extension can be considered if supported as an available, equally effective less discriminatory alternative, rather than assumed to be an established accommodation. Certification, representative adequacy and eventual remedies follow their independent requirements. These are factual, liability and remedial tasks under the settled statutory framework, not reservations about whether pregnancy-related impact claims are legally cognizable.

#### Precedent Treatment

Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 401 U.S. 424, applies its distinction between facial neutrality and justified practices with discriminatory impact within the enacted §703(k) sequence.

Watson v. Fort Worth Bank & Trust, 487 U.S. 977, supplies identification of a particular practice, substantial causal comparison and flexibility in the evidentiary form; §703(k) controls the present burden of proving the employer's defense.

California Federal Savings & Loan Association v. Guerra, 479 U.S. 272, supplies the interpretation of the PDA's equal-treatment language as part of Title VII's equal-opportunity protection rather than an absolute-neutrality ceiling.

Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, supplies whole-record evidentiary review, reasonable inferences and the prohibition on credibility choices. Its pretext rule requires an evidentiary basis and does not infer discrimination solely from protected status.

Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A., 534 U.S. 506, retains its pleading rule; Rule 56 still requires evidence. Its rejection of an added complaint checklist does not replace the actual proof inquiry here.

#### Law After Decision

Title VII's impact sequence applies to attendance practices affecting pregnancy, childbirth and related medical conditions. Uniform treatment after employees violate a cutoff does not answer the challenge to the cutoff's comparative effects. Medical and operational evidence may establish the necessary comparison without a mandatory statistical format; the particular evidence here permits the claim to continue. The claimant's causal proof, the employer's necessity defense, an available refused alternative, class requirements and relief retain their separate burdens and stages.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Kennedy and Thomas.** They accept that §703(k) reaches a properly proved pregnancy-related impact claim, with its enacted necessity and alternative burdens. They would nevertheless affirm Baxter's impact judgment because this record establishes Stout's individual injury and predictable exclusion of childbirth during probation without adequately proving the relevant comparative effect. The lack of an inevitable childbirth absence among other employees does not establish their actual relative ability to meet the total-absence limit, and the broader related-condition claim requires a supported relationship to the childbirth subgroup. They do not demand statistics in every case: reliable medical, operational or statistical evidence could supply the missing comparison. Their ground is proof insufficiency before the employer's defense is triggered, rather than immunity for uniformly enforced attendance policies.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** Stone-Zsela joins the Court's evidence and statutory rulings. If a common unlawful cutoff is proved within a properly supported remedial claim, he would presume correction for the persons governed unless Baxter proves a narrower complete cure. That proposed remedial presumption would operate only after the independently applicable certification, individual-nexus and liability requirements are met. Relief would address causally supported injury, including lawful reinstatement or backpay, and prevent continued use of the proved unlawful cutoff within the appropriate scope. Baxter would first have an opportunity to propose a lawful attendance rule preserving proven job needs. A fitted decree would set a feasible implementation date and require the revised rule, employee notice and actual employment decisions to demonstrate compliance; failure to cure would permit enforcement. He treats a probation extension as a possible evidenced alternative and would permit judgment for Baxter if ordinary procedure ultimately leaves no genuine impact dispute or lawful further development. This conditional remedial approach is his separate position, not an additional holding of the Court.
