## Decisions and Dispositions

### Consolidated Edison Co. of New York, Inc. v. Pataki

Merits decision, January 14, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Second Circuit's affirmance of a permanent injunction against New York Chapter 190 and its limited remand to correct a clerical error. The questions are whether a corporation is protected against state bills of attainder and whether Chapter 190's targeted replacement-power cost disallowance and refund command imposes punishment for past wrongdoing without judicial trial. The underlying outage began February 15, 2000; the statute became law August 8, 2000; the appellate judgment issued June 5, 2002.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The permanent injunction bars enforcement of Chapter 190's cost-disallowance and refund commands in full. The limited remand to correct the clerical error in the judgment remains in place. Independent lawful PSC prudence review remains available; the judgment establishes neither that every replacement-power cost was prudent nor an entitlement to reimbursement. The approximately $250 million at stake is not a damages award.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Corporate protection against legislative punishment

**Controlling proposition:** A corporation may invoke Article I, §10 when a state enactment identifies it and imposes punishment for past wrongdoing without judicial trial. This protection concerns the corporation's legal and property interests under this guarantee; it does not confer every right belonging to a natural person.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court controls both holdings and the stated remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional restraint turns on the legislative act and the nature of its punishment. A State cannot avoid the prohibition merely because the property subjected to punishment is held corporately. United States v. Lovett supplies the prohibition against noncapital legislative penalties and the separation of punishment from legislative adjudication. Nixon v. Administrator of General Services requires punishment beyond mere identification. Ambassador Books & Video, Inc. v. City of Little Rock applies that distinction to identifiable businesses but leaves this corporate-coverage issue for decision. Historical punishments often affected bodily liberty or the occupations of natural persons. That history informs what counts as punishment; it does not create an exception permitting the same prohibited method against an entity's property. The guarantee therefore reaches this asserted punitive financial disability without resolving unrelated corporate claims to personal rights.

##### Chapter 190 imposes legislative punishment

**Controlling proposition:** A challenger must establish identification, punishment and the absence of judicial trial; historical treatment, functional operation and legislative purpose inform one punishment inquiry rather than three cumulative requirements. Chapter 190's legislative determination of Con Ed's completed wrongdoing, combined with its conclusive denial and refund of all replacement-power costs for the identified outage, imposes punishment without trial and cannot be enforced.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this law's combined operation supplies the constitutional defect. It names Con Ed, declares the company's completed operation unreasonable and makes that determination conclusively bar all replacement-power recovery for the outage, including repayment of amounts already collected. The Legislature itself fixes culpability and the complete financial consequence; the consequence does not depend on adjudicating which costs were imprudent.

Protecting ratepayers and allocating utility risk are legitimate regulatory objectives. Nixon and Ambassador Books establish that naming one operator, imposing a substantial burden, acting retrospectively or expressing hostility does not alone establish punishment. The Court accepts those limits. But describing who benefits from this command does not answer its operative character: a legislative judgment of past fault supplies the entire event-specific sanction without trial. That combination distinguishes this measure from genuine prospective rate regulation or an independently adjudicated cost disallowance. Neither an historically identical penalty nor a criminal conviction is necessary.

Selective Service System v. Minnesota Public Interest Research Group preserves the challenger's burden; the Court adds no motive presumption or least-restrictive-means test. The injunction fits the entire proved cost-disallowance and refund command. Ordinary lawful prudence review remains possible, and actual prudent costs and recoverable amounts remain matters for that process. The attainder holding supplies complete relief, so equal protection and takings do not furnish additional grounds for this judgment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Ambassador Books & Video, Inc. v. City of Little Rock:** Applies its separation of identification from punishment and its historical, functional and purpose inquiry; distinguishes genuine prospective business regulation from this completed-conduct penalty. Its protection for genuine regulation remains effective, while the present decision expressly establishes corporate coverage of this guarantee.
- **Nixon v. Administrator of General Services, 433 U.S. 425:** Applies the punishment inquiry and preserves the principle that identification alone is insufficient.
- **United States v. Lovett, 328 U.S. 303:** Applies the prohibition of legislative punishment without trial to a punitive financial disability rather than limiting the Clause to a criminal conviction or capital penalty.
- **Selective Service System v. Minnesota Public Interest Research Group, 468 U.S. 841:** Preserves the challenger's burden to establish punishment; no burden shift or required least burdensome alternative is added.

#### Law After Decision

Corporations may invoke the state Bill of Attainder Clause against punitive disabilities affecting their legal and property interests. The decision expressly resolves that coverage question and applies the existing punishment inquiry to Chapter 190. Specific regulation, ordinary prospective rates, neutral cost-allocation rules and independent prudence proceedings remain lawful when nonpunitive. The whole adjudged command is unenforceable; the decision creates no general remedial presumption, corporate entitlement to recover costs, or constitutional requirement to preserve an existing regulatory procedure.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Art. I, §10; 2000 N.Y. Laws ch. 190; Consolidated Edison Co. of New York, Inc. v. Pataki, 292 F.3d 338. The approximately $250 million figure describes the costs at stake rather than an adjudicated award.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The injunction remains effective. Upon the mandate, enforcing officials must omit Chapter 190's disallowance and refund command from implementation; regulatory orders and calculations may rest on independent lawful prudence findings. Reimposition of the same statutory command remains subject to enforcement of the injunction. A genuinely independent prudence determination proceeds through the ordinary regulatory review process. The lower court corrects the clerical error as already directed.

**End of entry: Consolidated Edison Co. of New York, Inc. v. Pataki, merits decision, January 14, 2003.**

### Craigmiles v. Giles

Merits decision, January 14, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Sixth Circuit's December 6, 2002 affirmance of an injunction against applying Tennessee's funeral-director qualification to the plaintiffs' existing retail businesses. The questions are whether requiring that qualification for merchandise-only retailers violates due process or equal protection, and whether protecting incumbents from competition alone supplies a legitimate governmental interest. The separate Privileges or Immunities theory is outside the grant.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.


**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela and Stevens.

The lower court must dissolve the injunction barring application of the funeral-director qualification to the plaintiffs' existing merchandise-only retail businesses. The challenged qualification survives the granted due-process and equal-protection challenges. Review remains confined to that existing-business injunction; the separate Privileges or Immunities claim is outside the grant.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. STEVENS, J., and STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed separate dissenting opinions, each unjoined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The actual merchandising and accountability connection satisfies ordinary review

**Controlling proposition:** An ordinary economic qualification that implicates neither a fundamental right nor a suspect class satisfies due process and equal protection when it has a reasonably conceivable rational connection to a legitimate public objective; the challenger must negate that connection without demanding empirical certainty, formal findings or the least burdensome system. Tennessee's funeral-director qualification may be applied to these merchandise-only retailers because its actual merchandise instruction and examination, together with professional discipline, rationally connect the qualification to competence and accountability in this specialized sales market.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice opinion of the Court controls this rule, its application to both granted constitutional grounds and dissolution of the injunction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the trial findings do not negate every reasonably conceivable legitimate connection. Beach Communications permits supported economic generalizations without proof that the legislature chose the best system. Lee Optical allows imperfect regulatory lines, and Nordlinger permits substantial disparities when an actual legitimate purpose rationally explains them. Heller and Romer retain scrutiny of the relationship to the disadvantage imposed; they do not require a separate, least burdensome scheme whenever one is imaginable.

These stores do not handle bodies, arrange funerals or burials, embalm or make advance-need sales. Much of the required preparation concerns services they never perform. But the curriculum includes merchandise instruction, and 37 of 250 examination questions concern funeral merchandising. A common professional qualification and disciplinary system can rationally serve informed selection, ethical sales and accountability when goods are sold in the funeral market. This is a permissible legislative connection, not a new finding that the qualification measurably improves casket quality.

The dissent's strongest objection concerns the entire entry barrier, not the mere existence of useful instruction. The Court nevertheless rejects its application because ordinary review does not impose proportionality between the curriculum and each specialty's daily work. The findings about casket quality, unrestricted burial choices and the substantial unrelated training defeat some proposed safety explanations; they do not eliminate the actual merchandise and accountability connection. Existing fraud remedies and a possible retail-specific license do not make the common qualification irrational.

Moreno and Cleburne continue to prohibit illegitimate ends and irrational distinctions. The Court does not decide whether incumbent protection standing alone is a legitimate end: the second question's premise that no independent rational public connection exists is not established in this record. The distinct Privileges or Immunities theory lies outside the grant.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307:** Applies the reasonably conceivable legitimate-basis rule, challenger's burden and absence of a formal-findings requirement; it supplies no immunity for an irrational or impermissibly motivated line.
- **Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc., 348 U.S. 483:** Applies legislative latitude in economic regulation without requiring an optimally tailored occupation or curriculum.
- **Nordlinger v. Hahn, 505 U.S. 1:** Applies its tolerance of substantial disparities supported by legitimate policy differences; protection of an existing business's profits alone is not decided.
- **Heller v. Doe, 509 U.S. 312:** Preserves the required relationship to the actual disadvantage and its separate holding that a less extensive alternative does not alone invalidate a rational classification.
- **Romer v. Evans, 517 U.S. 620:** Preserves real rational-connection review without empirical certainty or least-restrictive-means requirements; distinguishes an unexplained disability from this actual merchandising and accountability connection.
- **Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S. 528, and City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432:** Preserve the limits against bare harm and irrational prejudice; the Court adds no categorical rule that incumbent protection by itself is legitimate.

#### Law After Decision

The existing rational-basis standard governs the reviewed licensing requirement. A unified occupational qualification may cover a merchandise-only specialty when actual merchandise instruction, examination and common professional accountability supply a rational connection to legitimate consumer interests, despite substantial training concerning other professional services. The decision imposes no curriculum-proportionality test and adopts no fundamental occupational right. It leaves unresolved the legitimacy of naked incumbent protection as the sole end because the actual record supplies a distinct rational public connection; it does not approve an arbitrary credential merely because incumbents benefit.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stevens, dissenting.** Stevens would affirm the existing-business injunction under ordinary rational-basis review. His approach in Beach and Nordlinger asks whether an impartial legislature could pursue the asserted purpose and whether that purpose rationally explains the actual inequality. He accepts that the curriculum teaches some merchandising; he rejects the conclusion that this limited content explains exclusion from the entire retail activity until the seller completes the substantially unrelated professional qualification. The trial findings concerning the stores' limited work, casket quality and the entry burden supply the basis for that conclusion. A private benefit cannot justify itself merely because the statute creates it. His objection does not demand the least restrictive fraud law or an optimally efficient course: it concerns the relationship of the whole prerequisite to the actual trade. Bare preservation of incumbent rents supplies no independent legitimate justification in his view.

**Stone-Zsela, dissenting.** The Chief Justice would affirm on both due-process and equal-protection grounds. Beach and Lee Optical permit imperfect economic lines, but Moreno and Cleburne preserve a legitimate-public-end requirement. He would extend that limit to bare protection of incumbent sellers from harmless competition. Incidental incumbent benefits, consumer protection, redistribution, aid to a distressed industry and Nordlinger's protection of established commitments remain distinguishable legitimate objectives.

He accepts the merchandising instruction, the 37 examination questions and the possible value of professional discipline. On this combined record, however, the stores perform none of the defining services that principally occupy the education and apprenticeship; the burial choices and quality findings also undermine the proposed casket-safety explanation. Discipline's usefulness does not itself connect those unrelated entry prerequisites to the admitted retail activity. Challengers retain the burden of negating reasonably conceivable legitimate explanations; no empirical proof, least restrictive system or specially optimized curriculum is constitutionally required.

He would retain relief against this separable qualification and presume equivalent protection for materially equivalent merchandise-only activity unless the State establishes that a narrower decree fully cures the proved violation. The trial findings and remedial jurisdiction must define the covered activity. Bodily care, funeral arrangements, advance-need transactions and lawful sales regulation remain outside that invalidation. The State could not obtain narrower relief by merely promising discretionary nonenforcement against the named stores. His rule would govern still-open direct-review cases under Harper, subject to Reynoldsville's independent procedural, immunity and remedial limits; final judgments remain closed and collateral relief requires its own authorization.

#### Source Notes

U.S. Const., Amdt. 14; Tenn. Code §§62-5-101 and 62-5-201 and the related training and disciplinary provisions; Craigmiles v. Giles, 312 F.3d 220. The trial findings concern merchandise-only businesses with limited, rather than nonexistent, relevant training in the required curriculum.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for dissolution of the existing-business injunction. Officials may enforce the reviewed qualification against the covered retail activity consistently with the judgment. The disposition concerns these granted due-process and equal-protection challenges and the existing injunction; the rejected Privileges or Immunities theory remains outside this review.

**End of entry: Craigmiles v. Giles, merits decision, January 14, 2003.**

### Gibson v. County of Washoe

Merits decision, January 14, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Ninth Circuit's May 22, 2002 judgment reversing summary judgment for the County on supported mental-health policy claims and affirming judgment for the named deputies and supervising sergeant. The question is whether a County policy may cause an actionable denial of a detainee's serious medical needs despite the named officers' lack of the subjective awareness required for personal liability.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The supported County claims concerning screening/evaluation and medication handling return for ordinary further proceedings, including trial if the remaining procedural requirements are satisfied. Summary judgment for the named deputies and sergeant, rejection of the unsupported communications-policy theory, and the unappealed consortium and state-law dispositions remain intact.

#### Opinion Topology

STEVENS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The municipality's own policy may cause a constitutional deprivation

**Controlling proposition:** A municipality may be liable under §1983 for its own deliberately indifferent custodial policy that actually causes a denial of constitutionally required medical care, even when the named employees lack the personal awareness required for their liability. The claimant must prove an actual constitutional deprivation, an attributable municipal policy or legally effective custom, a deliberate municipal choice concerning a known or obvious constitutional need, and an identified defect closely related to and actually causing the injury; negligence, employment alone and pooled employee knowledge do not suffice.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court controls the rule, the summary-judgment application and the limited return for further proceedings.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a named employee's exoneration does not collapse the separate inquiries into the deprivation and its municipal source. Monell requires the municipality's own causal policy or custom. Collins separates the underlying constitutional wrong from attribution; the absence of a custody-based duty in its voluntary-employment setting does not govern a pretrial detainee's custody. Canton requires deliberate municipal choice and a close causal connection. Carter permits notice through a pattern or a sufficiently obvious need to perform a clear constitutional duty in known recurring circumstances; identical prior injuries are not invariably necessary.

The County correctly insists on an actual deprivation and more than negligent administration. But the named deputies' lack of relevant awareness establishes neither that all required care was furnished nor that a County policy could not disable that care. Farmer's individual-awareness rule does not permit combining employees' innocent information to manufacture personal culpability. The municipal claim instead must identify and prove the County's own deliberately indifferent policy and its injury-producing operation. This preserves the underlying constitutional-injury requirement rather than substituting institutional responsibility for it.

##### The supported screening and medication evidence permits further proceedings

**Controlling proposition:** At summary judgment, a claimant must identify evidence permitting a reasonable finding on every required municipal-liability element. The evidence concerning delayed mental-health evaluation and handling of psychotropic medication meets that standard here; the surviving claims return under the ordinary preponderance-of-the-evidence burden, while the unsupported communications theory and the preserved personal and unappealed dispositions remain excluded.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Court opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the evidence, viewed under Rule 56, supports a distinct policy inquiry. The jail regularly held mentally ill detainees, policymakers knew of that recurring population, and the evidence concerns practices postponing evaluation until an acutely disturbed person cooperated and treating relevant medication as property rather than a signal for assessment. General knowledge of mental illness alone would not suffice. These particular practices, combined with the recurring constitutional responsibility and medication evidence, permit a finding beyond hindsight that more training might have helped.

The County remains entitled to contest policy authority or effective custom, deliberate choice, deprivation and actual cause. Death during restraint does not itself establish a medical-care violation or that a different policy would have prevented the injury. The reasonable-force and personal-awareness rulings therefore survive, but they do not decide the independent medical-policy claim. The communications theory lacks the necessary policymaker-notice support and cannot survive merely by association with the supported policies. On remand, the family must prove the surviving elements and any compensable injury; ordinary factual and remedial adjudication under those rules does not make the municipal standard unresolved.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658:** Applies the municipality's own policy-or-custom and causation requirements; employer-based liability remains forbidden.
- **Collins v. City of Harker Heights, 503 U.S. 115:** Applies the separate inquiries into constitutional deprivation and municipal attribution; distinguishes voluntary employment from actual pretrial custody.
- **City of Canton v. Harris, 489 U.S. 378:** Applies demanding municipal fault, notice, identified deficiency and causal connection; no negligence or hindsight standard replaces them.
- **Carter v. City of Philadelphia, March 13, 2000:** Applies its pattern-or-obvious-recurring-need notice rule and actual-cause requirement without adding an invariably identical-prior-incident condition.
- **Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825:** Preserves individualized actual awareness and the protection for a reasonable response; neither collective awareness nor a failed reasonable response supplies personal culpability. The distinct municipal inquiry does not alter that rule.

#### Law After Decision

Existing municipal and custodial law governs. A municipality cannot obtain judgment automatically from the named employees' lack of personal awareness when supported evidence independently connects an actual medical-care deprivation to its own deliberately indifferent policy. The same demanding deprivation, attribution, fault and causation requirements control analogous custodial policy claims. The decision supplies neither institutional strict liability nor a new aggregate-awareness test. Section 3626's requirements for prospective prison-conditions relief remain unchanged.

#### Source Notes

42 U.S.C. §1983; U.S. Const., Amdt. 14; Fed. Rule Civ. Proc. 56; Gibson v. County of Washoe, 290 F.3d 1175. The policy and causal issues return on supported summary-judgment evidence, not findings of municipal liability.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for ordinary proceedings on the supported County screening/evaluation and medication-policy claims. The trial issues must identify those surviving policies. The family bears the ordinary preponderance burden on the required liability elements and must establish compensable loss under the applicable damages rules; the County may contest each. The affirmed personal judgments and the excluded communications, consortium and state-law dispositions remain effective.

**End of entry: Gibson v. County of Washoe, merits decision, January 14, 2003.**

### Johnson v. City of Cincinnati

Merits decision, January 14, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Sixth Circuit's September 26, 2002 judgment concerning Cincinnati's drug-exclusion ordinance, prospective relief and an entered $38,500 attorney-fee award. The granted questions concern ordinary local passage and the claimed intimate relationships involved in Johnson's parent-authorized care of her grandchildren and Au France's access to his lawyer. The separate district-court double-jeopardy ground for Au France is outside the grant.

All nine Justices participated.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Existing $38,500 attorney-fee judgment | Affirmed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | The existing award survives the challenge to its constitutional basis. |
| Prospective declaration and injunction | Remanded for component-specific jurisdictional and equitable review, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Assess Burnett, cessation and actual exposure; apply equitable vacatur if moot and fitted relief if a live controversy remains. |
| Requested damages | Remanded to identify procedural status and adjudicate any surviving claim, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Apply ordinary preservation, liability, causation, compensation and defense rules to any surviving claim. |

The existing prospective orders remain subject to lawful lower-court treatment; this disposition itself neither automatically vacates them nor directs enforcement to resume. The district court's independent double-jeopardy ground for Au France lies outside the grant and remains unadjudicated in this review.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and in the judgment. SCALIA, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg in full; Stone-Zsela, Scalia and Thomas as specified | Governs every component through its stated majority | I: collateral review, nine; II: movement, seven; III: caregiving, seven; IV: lawyer relationship, eight; V: fee affirmance, seven, and prospective/damages remand, nine |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | Unjoined | Supports fee affirmance and both remand components | Joins I, II, III and V; declines the attorney-intimacy determination and adds a conditional remedial position |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Scalia | Thomas | Would also vacate the appellate fee affirmance and remand entitlement and allocation | Joins I, IV and V's prospective/damages instructions; rejects II, III and V's fee affirmance |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The entered fee judgment presents a live collateral controversy

**Controlling proposition:** An appeal from an existing attorney-fee judgment remains a live monetary controversy requiring decision of the granted federal-right predicates necessary to review that award, even when prospective relief requires separate jurisdictional examination. That collateral jurisdiction does not establish a continuing basis for an injunction or decide the status of requested damages.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court, authored by Breyer, is joined by all eight other Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Cincinnati's appeal concerns an entered $38,500 award, not merely an interest in seeking fees after an underlying controversy has ended. The City contests the constitutional basis of that monetary judgment, and the Court must resolve the granted questions necessary to dispose of it. Laidlaw's statement that fees alone do not preserve an underlying controversy does not erase an existing collateral appeal. Arizonans permits correction of jurisdictional error and equitable vacatur when the relevant claim has become moot; its employment-departure and no-surviving-claim premises do not establish that this fee judgment or a damages claim has disappeared. Burnett and cessation must be addressed for prospective relief, but their examination supplies no substitute for adjudicating the monetary dispute. The remedies therefore require separate treatment.

##### II. Substantial neighborhood exclusion burdens ordinary public passage

**Controlling proposition:** Ordinary passage along public streets, sidewalks and public ways for lawful purposes is fundamental liberty. When a claimant establishes a personal exclusion substantially barring that passage throughout neighborhoods for months, the government must show a compelling justification and a close, individualized fit between the restriction's necessity and scope and the danger addressed; a past arrest or conviction alone does not discharge that burden.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion is joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven Justices adopt the rule and its application to both exclusion mechanisms.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that both the arrest-based and conviction-based mechanisms fail this standard. Drug prevention is compelling, but excluding a person from every public route in all designated zones for every lawful purpose is a direct restraint on ordinary movement. Kent v. Dulles informs the importance of movement to liberty; Papachristou protects innocent public activity from arbitrary displacement. Neither already decided this precise rule. Saenz concerns equal state citizenship after interstate arrival, while Morales controls enforcement guidance. Objective predicates satisfy neither the distinct substantive question nor the required fit.

An arrest establishes no guilt or ongoing danger. Administrative review by a preponderance determines the predicate offense, not whether future lawful presence requires broad exclusion. Conviction establishes past guilt more securely, but does not itself justify the year-long all-zone ban. General crime findings and recidivism concerns fail to explain the necessity of excluding each person's lawful passage.

Written variances protect qualifying preexisting residents and workers; social-service agencies may issue limited variances. Those exceptions omit ordinary passage and the demonstrated caregiving and consultation purposes. The 1998 amendment ends arrest exclusions after acquittal, dismissal or nonprosecution, but does not cure the remaining lack of fit; Johnson's earlier exclusion continued after nonprosecution under the version applied to her.

The dissent treats these predicates, durations and variances as sufficient crime-control limits. They constrain administration but do not justify the full liberty burden. This holding concerns substantial personal neighborhood bans, not ordinary route, hours or place-use management. Lawful arrest, custody, bail, probation and protective restrictions retain their own predicates. The Court prescribes no universal hearing, proof standard or procedure for every geographic regulation; the government must substantiate the necessity and scope of the serious restraint challenged here.

##### III. Johnson's established parent-authorized caregiving is protected

**Controlling proposition:** Government may not substantially interrupt an established parent-authorized family caregiving relationship through a categorical neighborhood exclusion without a compelling justification closely fitted to that interference. The claimant must establish the actual caregiving relationship and substantial disruption; an asserted past offense does not itself justify that disruption.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion has the same seven-Justice coalition as Part II and independently sustains Johnson's constitutional success.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Johnson's regular, mother-authorized care of five grandchildren, including school trips, is an actual protected family relationship. Moore v. City of East Cleveland protects the reality of extended-family life; Roberts v. United States Jaycees distinguishes intimate relationships from ordinary professional associations. Troxel protects parental direction. The grandmother here seeks to continue the arrangement the parent chose, rather than impose contact against that choice.

The exclusion substantially disrupted that demonstrated caregiving. Drug suppression is compelling, but the City supplies no adequate connection between these lawful caregiving trips and a danger requiring the interference. Its variance structure supplies no accommodation for the established relationship. The dissent characterizes the restriction as conduct-based regulation rather than regulation of family formation. That description does not remove its substantial interruption of the actual parent-chosen relationship. The holding creates no automatic grandparent entitlement, right to override a fit parent's refusal, or immunity from criminal law. Ordinary indirect inconvenience from lawful regulation does not equal the substantial interference shown here.

##### IV. Confidential professional representation alone is not intimate association

**Controlling proposition:** An attorney-client relationship is not constitutionally protected intimate association merely because professional representation is important and confidential. Those features alone do not establish the close personal relationship required by the intimate-association doctrine, so the alternative attorney-association rationale cannot support Au France's judgment.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Court's opinion is joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight Justices adopt this proposition. Stone-Zsela does not join this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Roberts requires attention to a relationship's nature and function. Professional service and confidentiality alone do not make an adviser a family-like intimate associate. The difficulty reaching Au France's lawyer's office establishes an actual geographic burden; it does not establish the relational characteristics necessary for this distinct theory. No equally convenient alternative meeting site is presumed. Counsel, petitioning and access-to-courts protections have separate sources and elements. Independent claims under those protections lie outside this intimate-association question and are not adjudicated here. Au France's successful movement ground remains sufficient for the reviewed judgment.

##### V. The supported fee award survives; continuing relief receives separate review

**Controlling proposition:** The existing $38,500 fee award is affirmed against the challenge to its granted constitutional predicates because both plaintiffs retain successful movement claims and Johnson also retains the caregiving ground; rejection of the alternative lawyer theory does not itself remove that success. Prospective declaration and injunction components require separate assessment of Burnett, cessation and present exposure; the court must identify the damages request's actual disposition and adjudicate any surviving claim under ordinary rules.

**Authority:** Part V's fee-affirmance proposition has the seven-Justice coalition in Parts II and III. Its distinct prospective-relief and damages-status instructions are joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the sustained constitutional grounds support the existing monetary award. No independent fee-amount or allocation challenge requires recalculation merely because an alternative theory fails. The district court's double-jeopardy ground for Au France is outside the grant and receives no new disposition.

For prospective relief, Laidlaw places the burden on a defendant invoking cessation to make it absolutely clear that the challenged injurious conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The lower court must determine Burnett's binding legal effect and actual remaining exposure. Plaintiffs need not show an announced plan to resume. Satisfaction of the standard for denying a presently unnecessary injunction does not itself establish Article III mootness. If a component is moot, equitable vacatur under Arizonans, Bancorp and Munsingwear must account for that component and the relevant chronology; vacatur is not automatic. If a live prospective controversy remains, relief must fit the controlling constitutional rules and present circumstances.

The existing prospective orders remain subject to that lawful lower-court treatment. Neither continuing enforcement nor present mootness is found. The damages request must receive its actual procedural treatment: any surviving claim proceeds on preserved grounds, causation, compensable injury and defenses. Silence supplies neither abandonment nor an established damages entitlement. This is ordinary jurisdictional, factual and remedial work under the stated law, not a reservation of the constitutional rules just decided.

#### Precedent Treatment

- **Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, January 12, 2000:** Applies the defendant's demanding cessation burden and the distinction between mootness and equitable entitlement; clarifies that the fees-alone limitation does not erase appellate review of an entered award.
- **Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, March 3, 1997:** Applies component-specific corrective jurisdiction and equitable vacatur; its absence-of-a-surviving-claim premise is not assumed for these monetary components.
- **U.S. Bancorp Mortgage Co. v. Bonner Mall Partnership and United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.:** Preserve equitable, chronology-sensitive treatment of an unreviewable judgment rather than automatic vacatur of every component.
- **Kent v. Dulles and Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville:** Extend their protection of movement and innocent public activity to the substantial neighborhood-exclusion rule stated here; neither is treated as having previously decided this precise restraint.
- **Saenz v. Roe:** Distinguishes equal state citizenship after interstate arrival from the local-passage protection now established.
- **City of Chicago v. Morales, June 10, 1999:** Preserves its controlling enforcement-guidance rule; the present substantive holding is an additional rule, not an application of vagueness to an ordinance with objective predicates.

#### Law After Decision

Substantial personal bans on otherwise lawful passage throughout public neighborhoods require compelling justification and a close individualized fit concerning necessity and scope. Both arrest and conviction mechanisms here fail that new controlling rule. Established parent-authorized family care receives protection against substantial geographic interference under the same compelling-and-fitted justification requirement; professional representation and confidentiality alone do not establish intimate association. Ordinary traffic and place management, lawful criminal restrictions on their own predicates and parental-refusal cases retain their distinct rules. No general policy-wide remedial presumption is adopted.

This proposal does not make the fee dispute a source of prospective jurisdiction. The court must first assess Burnett, actual legal enforceability, remaining exposure and the status of damages. If the prospective component is moot, its disposition must preserve live fee or damages controversies. ### Pierce County v. Guillen, No. 01-1229

Merits decision, January 14, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the Supreme Court of Washington. After a fatal intersection accident, Guillen sought highway and accident records through a public-disclosure action and a separate negligence action. The state court’s consolidated decision invalidated the collection protection in 23 U.S.C. §409. The questions are the scope of that protection and Congress’s authority to enact it; review first requires separate finality determinations for the two judgments.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interlocutory tort-discovery judgment | Writ dismissed for want of a final judgment, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | Review of this component is dismissed; its merits and discovery order receive no appellate mandate. |
| Public-disclosure judgment | Reversed and remanded, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | — | The federal-law basis for blanket production and its dependent fee determination are set aside. The state court must classify the holdings by purpose and custody, apply the remaining state disclosure law, and recalculate any fee entitlement. |

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Finality must be established for each judgment

**Controlling proposition:** Section 1257 requires a final state judgment in each action brought for review; consolidation does not transfer finality between actions. The public-disclosure judgment is reviewable because it conclusively determines entitlement to the records with only collateral fee calculation or implementation remaining, while the separate interlocutory tort-discovery judgment is not reviewable.

**Authority:** Part I of the unanimous opinion of the Court controls this rule and both component dispositions.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn’s practical-finality principles to the proceedings that actually remain. The disclosure determination conclusively resolves the federal question and effectively determines the requested production; further collateral calculation does not reopen that determination. The negligence action still requires substantive proceedings, and its discovery ruling can be reviewed through the ordinary final-judgment route. Jefferson v. City of Tarrant and Florida v. Thomas preserve that distinction between a conclusive federal ruling within an unfinished case and a judgment qualifying for review. Neither a consolidated caption nor the importance of the common statutory question supplies missing finality. Review of the tort component is therefore dismissed without passing on its discovery merits.

##### II. Section 409 protects purpose-qualified program holdings, not every copy of the information

**Controlling proposition:** Notwithstanding contrary law, §409 protects reports, surveys, schedules, lists, and data compiled or collected to identify, evaluate, or plan safety enhancement of potential accident sites, hazardous roadway conditions, or railway-highway crossings under §§130, 144, or 152, or to develop a highway-safety construction improvement project that may use federal-aid highway funds: those holdings are not subject to discovery or admission into evidence in a federal or state court proceeding, or consideration for other purposes in a damages action arising from an occurrence at a location they mention or address. The proponent must establish the qualifying purpose and custodial holding; obtaining preexisting material for that purpose is collection, but independently compiled or collected nonprogram records held by an independent nonprogram custodian do not become protected merely because a program agency obtains a copy.

**Authority:** Part II of the unanimous opinion of the Court controls the complete purpose, custody, covered-use and proof requirements.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court gives the addition of collected independent effect. A program can obtain existing accident information from another agency and assemble it for the safety task; protection is not confined to documents it newly creates. But the words defining the purpose continue to limit the privilege. Baldrige v. Shapiro supplies the rule of narrow construction for an evidentiary privilege, and the enacted purpose prevents collection of a copy from suppressing every independent source. The opposing newly-created-material construction erases the amendment; the information-wide construction erases its limiting purpose. The County must identify the statutory safety purpose and the holdings collected or compiled for it. Before production, the state court must resolve disputed purpose and custody from the records and an adequate agency explanation without disclosure that would defeat an established protection. It must withhold protected holdings from the barred uses and order disclosure of nonprotected records when state law requires. Classification of the actual documents is application of the settled rule on remand, not a finding that all requested records qualify.

##### III. This confined information protection implements Congress’s highway-safety power

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may protect purpose-qualified highway-safety information under the Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses when the protection reasonably implements safety administration of interstate transportation channels and instrumentalities. Section 409, including its collection amendment as construed here, satisfies that requirement because it addresses the supported disincentive to candid hazard collection while leaving independent nonprogram records outside the privilege.

**Authority:** Part III of the unanimous opinion of the Court controls this confined constitutional ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Lopez requires an identified interstate connection before aggregate consequences can establish power; Harris applies the direct transportation-instrumentality route. Here the relevant connection is the highway-safety program itself. Compulsory centralized disclosure can discourage candid hazard collection used to identify and correct dangerous locations. Protecting that collection reasonably serves the operation of interstate transportation channels and instrumentalities. Home Builders preserves the need for an identifiable connection and a fitted implementation of a valid interstate regime. Purpose and custody confine this measure to that function. The Court therefore rejects the objection that the protection is merely a general federal regulation of state evidence resting on remote litigation costs. The state court’s contrary constitutional ground cannot sustain blanket disclosure. The Commerce ground suffices without deciding an alternative Spending Clause ground. The separate Tenth Amendment dual-sovereignty contention was not addressed below and remains outside the question decided; the Court also does not reach the United States’ alternative objection to the private respondents’ standing to raise that federalism contention. Affirmative enumerated power does not decide either distinct question.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 U.S. 469: applied to the completed federal disclosure determination and collateral proceedings; consolidation supplies no additional exception.
- Jefferson v. City of Tarrant and Florida v. Thomas: applied to preserve §1257 finality and dismiss review of the unfinished tort action.
- Baldrige v. Shapiro, 455 U.S. 345: applied to confine the statutory privilege to its enacted purpose while giving collection meaningful effect.
- United States v. Lopez, April 26, 1995: applied through the direct transportation connection, preserving the prohibition on manufacturing power from remote downstream costs.
- United States v. Harris, April 27, 1995: its protection of transportation instrumentalities supports the confined highway-safety ground; the forcible-taking application does not itself determine this privilege’s scope.
- National Association of Home Builders v. Babbitt, June 8, 1998: applied for an identified interstate connection and fitted implementation; its independent externality route supplies no additional holding here.

#### Law After Decision

Effective January 14, 2003, §409 follows the qualifying act of compilation or collection and the holding in the program capacity. It protects obtained material as well as newly prepared material, without converting independently maintained source copies into privileged information. The limited federal protection is a permissible means of administering highway safety; state disclosure law governs the remaining nonprotected material. The ordinary §1257 finality rule and its established practical-finality routes remain unchanged. Document classification and the resulting fee calculation are remand work under these rules. The distinct Tenth Amendment contention and alternative private-plaintiff standing issue remain undecided on the grounds stated in Holding III.

#### Source Notes

537 U.S. 129; No. 01-1229; 28 U.S.C. §1257; 23 U.S.C. §§130, 144, 152, 409. The disclosure determination concerns four documents. Their purpose and custodial status require determination under the governing statutory rule.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The writ is dismissed as to the separate tort-discovery judgment. The public-disclosure case returns to the Washington courts for purpose-and-custody classification, application of §409’s covered-use protection and remaining state disclosure requirements, and recalculation of fees. The state court must protect qualifying holdings while resolving the statutory facts; program possession of a copy or a conclusory privilege label does not resolve an independently held original.

### Barnhart v. Peabody Coal Co., Nos. 01-705 and 01-715

Merits decision, January 15, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 8, 2002. On writs of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit. The court of appeals affirmed judgments setting aside initial Coal Act assignments made after September 30, 1993. The question is whether 26 U.S.C. §9706(a)’s direction to complete assignments before October 1, 1993 extinguishes the Commissioner’s authority afterward; separate statutory eligibility and premium disputes retain their own predicates. The consolidated matters include the related Bellaire judgment and the trustees' consolidated petition.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**The consolidated timing-based judgments are reversed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas.

Untimeliness alone cannot invalidate these initial assignments or require permanent unassignment. On remand, the challenged assignments and dependent premiums must rest on the actual statutory employment and covered-operator predicates; timely raised substantive, notice, review, and calculation objections remain for resolution. Any unperformed factual assignment judgment committed initially to the Commissioner must be returned to that officer unless law compels the result.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which O'CONNOR and THOMAS, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The mandatory completion date does not terminate initial-assignment authority

**Controlling proposition:** Section 9706(a) required the Commissioner to complete initial Coal Act assignments before October 1, 1993, but missing that mandatory date did not extinguish authority to make an otherwise lawful initial assignment later. This consequence follows from this statute’s text and structure; express expiration, private filing limits, prescribed sanctions, or other statutory indications that authority ends retain their different effects.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court opinion; its six-Justice coalition adopts the rule and its contextual basis.

**Controlling explanation:** Brock v. Pierce County distinguishes an obligation to act promptly from automatic termination of power, while preserving the possibility that ordinary statutory context establishes termination. James Daniel Good Real Property likewise gives mandatory administrative duties effect without an unstated dismissal sanction. The Coal Act prescribes a duty and date, but its continuing assignment task, correction machinery, and allocation structure do not make permanent unassignment the consequence of delay. The operators rely on the as-of baseline in §9704(f) and its specified later adjustments. Those accounting provisions can operate on the allocation that should correctly have been made; they do not make every initial omission permanently incurable. The mechanisms funding unassigned beneficiaries address interim or residual costs, rather than necessarily creating an irrevocable exemption for an otherwise assignable beneficiary. Cost allocation confirms this reading without creating power independent of text. The Commissioner breached the timely-performance duty, which remains obligatory. The ruling decides the consequence of that breach for assignment authority, not whether an independently grounded remedy for delay may be available.

##### II. Continuing authority preserves the actual assignment criteria and the proper initial decisionmaker

**Controlling proposition:** A late initial assignment remains lawful only if the Coal Act’s employment and covered-entity requirements are satisfied; direct asset succession alone does not authorize assignment, and the agency must substantiate contested statutory predicates before dependent premiums are imposed. Courts independently resolve preserved statutory objections and premium calculations, but must return a material factual assignment judgment entrusted initially to the Commissioner if it remains unperformed, unless the result is legally compelled.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion; the same six Justices adopt the eligibility limits and bounded remedial direction.

**Controlling explanation:** Sigmon Coal separates the enacted liability classes from an administrative interest in revenue. The Act’s related-person classes are the signatory’s §52(a) controlled-group members, including the signatory; a §52(b) commonly controlled trade or business; and a coal-industry partnership or joint-venture interest with a signatory employing eligible beneficiaries, except an interest held solely as a limited partner. The relationship is measured on July 20, 1992, or immediately before an earlier cessation of business. If a person becomes successor to an assigned operator after enactment, §9706(b)(2) permits that operator to transfer an eligible beneficiary’s assignment to the successor, who is then treated as the assigned operator; the transferring operator and its related persons remain benefit guarantors, and the operator must notify the Combined Fund trustees. The deadline ruling does not enlarge that provision. Ventura reserves an unperformed statutory factual task to the agency while preserving independent legal review. Bean addresses a different actual-denial prerequisite to §925(c) review and permits no original judicial fitness assessment; its gate is not a Coal Act deadline or a new review condition here. These distinct rules require a lawful assignment and calculation, not blanket validation of everything done late. Remaining preserved eligibility, notice, review, and amount disputes proceed on their own grounds. A separate delay claim requires its own preserved basis and is not decided by the timing question.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Brock v. Pierce County, 476 U.S. 253: applied to separate mandatory timing from automatic loss of authority, preserving its qualification that statutory context may establish expiration without a formula expressly declaring late acts void.
- United States v. James Daniel Good Real Property, December 13, 1993: applied for mandatory public duties without an unstated disabling sanction; its distinct limitations period and constitutional-delay protections remain intact.
- Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., February 19, 2002: applied without enlarging its related-person categories or asset-successor limit. A funding objective supplies no omitted assignment class.
- INS v. Ventura, November 4, 2002: applied to any still-unperformed factual judgment initially assigned to the Commissioner, subject to the legally compelled-result qualification; independent statutory review remains.
- United States v. Bean, December 10, 2002: distinguished as an actual-denial judicial-review gate under §925(c), not a termination rule for this assignment duty and not an added condition on Coal Act review.

#### Law After Decision

Effective January 15, 2003, the Coal Act’s initial-assignment authority continues after the missed October 1, 1993 deadline, although timely performance was mandatory. Lateness alone cannot permanently shift otherwise assignable beneficiaries to unassigned funding. Statutory eligibility, lawful calculation, meaningful notice and review, and any independently supported delay remedy retain their own requirements. Sigmon’s substantive classes are unchanged; Ventura governs unperformed agency factual tasks; Bean’s separate firearms-review prerequisite is neither displaced nor transferred to Coal Act litigation.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by O’Connor and Thomas, dissenting.** The dissent would affirm because §9706(a) grants initial-assignment authority in the same time-bounded mandate that requires its exercise. The fixed as-of allocation and express correction and reassignment provisions reinforce closure on October 1, 1993. An initial assignment of a previously unassigned beneficiary differs from a correction authorized under another provision, and the unassigned-beneficiary funding system supplies the statutory destination for those costs. Brock permits statutory context to establish expiration; Good involved separate continuing forfeiture authority and a distinct five-year limitations period. The dissent therefore does not rest on a universal rule that every mandatory agency deadline disables late action. It rejects the Court’s reading of this scheme and would preserve the timing-based judgments. Revenue or fairness cannot enlarge a source of authority Congress bounded by time.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Both consolidated timing-based judgments are reversed. Proceedings resume on properly maintained objections to assignments and dependent premiums. The Commissioner must substantiate contested statutory predicates; the reviewing court decides preserved legal objections independently, returning an unperformed statutory factual task to the Commissioner unless the outcome is legally compelled. ### Eldred v. Ashcroft, No. 01-618

Merits decision, January 15, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 9, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, which affirmed dismissal of the constitutional challenge. The granted questions concern the Copyright Clause and First Amendment validity of the Copyright Term Extension Act’s twenty additional years for subsisting copyrights. The petitioners’ challenge does not seek invalidation of the term for newly created works as such or restoration of works already in the public domain. The distinct petition question about arguments advanced only by an amicus is outside the grant.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**The judgment is affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Breyer.

The challenged application of the twenty-year extension to subsisting copyrights stands. The requested declaration and injunction against that application are denied. Stone-Zsela, Stevens, and Breyer would reverse and remand for relief confined to the existing-term challenge, on their distinct stated grounds.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court with respect to Parts I, II-A, and II-B. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. STEVENS and BREYER, JJ., each filed a dissenting opinion.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Ginsburg | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas in full; Stone-Zsela joins Part II-A only | Six Justices sustain the existing-term extension and reject the presented speech challenge | Parts I and II-B: six; Part II-A's severable rejection of categorical immunity: seven |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Stone-Zsela | Unjoined | Would reverse the existing-term application | Joins Part II-A; rejects Part I's application and the affirmance; does not join Part II-B |
| Dissent | Stevens | Unjoined | Would invalidate the challenged extension | Existing-bargain and progress ground; no independent First Amendment invalidity holding |
| Dissent | Breyer | Unjoined | Would invalidate the challenged extension | Copyright Clause read with expressive interests; no universal independent speech tier |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. A finite extension of existing copyrights may rationally serve the copyright system

**Controlling proposition:** The Copyright Clause permits a finite extension of subsisting copyrights when the extension bears a rational relationship to the promotion of progress through the copyright system; limited times requires bounded duration, not an irrevocable initial expiration date. The twenty-year extension challenged here satisfies that standard through reasonably supported systemwide coordination, dissemination, and preservation purposes, without requiring proof that the additional years newly induced creation of each already completed work.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court opinion; its six-Justice full-opinion coalition adopts the complete standard and its application. The three dissenting grounds are not aggregated into a competing controlling rationale.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that finite duration is necessary, while the promotion-of-progress requirement remains a real constitutional limit. A copyright system operates across works, time, and national markets. Congress may reasonably adjust the terms of existing rights as part of international coordination and future dissemination or preservation without claiming to induce completed creation retrospectively. Historical extensions support the conclusion that an initially stated endpoint is not immutable; repetition alone does not establish constitutional power. Feist’s originality boundary concerns what may receive protection and does not fix an unalterable duration. The petitioners correctly identify delayed public access and reject an incentive to create what already exists. Those points do not eliminate the distinct, forward-looking system purposes of a finite adjustment. The present extension rationally serves those purposes; constitutional review does not require the optimal policy or a new work-for-work exchange. Private reward alone would not establish progress, and the decision does not authorize indefinite evasion of limited times. The existing-term application is sustained. The unchallenged new-work term and restoration of public-domain material are outside this case.

##### II-A. Copyright safeguards do not create categorical First Amendment immunity

**Controlling proposition:** Fair use and the distinction between ideas and expression do not categorically immunize copyright-duration legislation from the First Amendment. A challenger must identify the actual expressive burden of the attacked provision, and a court must assess the continuing safeguards in relation to that burden; the petitioners’ delayed reproduction and dissemination of protected expression identify a real burden requiring that inquiry.

**Authority:** Part II-A of the Court opinion; its six-Justice full-opinion coalition and Chief Justice Stone-Zsela directly support this severable proposition. The Chief Justice's join does not reach Part II-B's constitutional conclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** Harper & Row explains the legal work of copyright’s accommodation with expression. Campbell preserves actual fair use through its integrated statutory inquiry, not an owner’s veto over qualifying criticism. Neither makes the copyright label conclusive. Corley separately assessed a demonstrated access-circumvention function and a tailored posting-and-purposeful-linking injunction; it did not remove constitutional review or approve perpetual control of public-domain material. The present burden concerns twenty additional years of exclusivity over expression petitioners wish to reproduce, preserve, or disseminate. Availability of facts, ideas, and some fair uses does not equal the unrestricted uses allowed after expiration. The Court therefore examines that burden rather than treating safeguards as a reason to dispense with inquiry. The different conclusions about the present extension appear in Part II-B and the separate opinions.

##### II-B. The actual duration change does not violate the First Amendment

**Controlling proposition:** The challenged finite extension does not violate the First Amendment because it leaves facts and ideas unprotected, preserves qualifying fair use of expression, and neither compels expression nor imposes a separately content-selected restriction. In this ordinary duration setting those operative safeguards suffice after examination of the actual burden, and the First Amendment does not require additional heightened review of the legislative term policy.

**Authority:** Part II-B of the Court opinion; its six-Justice full-opinion coalition controls. Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Breyer withhold assent; their Copyright Clause grounds do not supply affirmative votes for a separate speech-invalidity rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court recognizes that petitioners’ desired uses are expressive and that fair use will not authorize every reproduction they could make in the public domain. Section 102(b) nevertheless leaves facts and ideas available, and §107 retains a genuine defense for qualifying use of protected expression. Campbell’s four factors remain an integrated inquiry, with the defense burden unchanged and criticism injury distinguished from cognizable market substitution. The term adjustment removes neither safeguard and demands no endorsement or carriage of another’s message. Those features answer the presented burden without a further heightened review of the length Congress selected. Corley’s content-neutral scrutiny addressed the executable circumvention function and its separate injunction; that different restraint does not mechanically prescribe a universal duration test. Quality King concerns exhaustion of distribution rights in particular domestic copies, not reproduction, and Tasini creates no blanket archival privilege. These limits matter to what remains available, but neither reduces the present extension to a categorical immunity theory. The Court rejects the actual speech challenge on this examined statutory setting.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co., 499 U.S. 340: its constitutional originality boundary is preserved and distinguished from duration; it does not fix the initial expiration date.
- Harper & Row, Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539: applied for the relationship between copyright, expression, and fair use; it supplies no categorical immunity from review of a duration burden.
- Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., March 7, 1994: its integrated four-factor analysis, defense burden, and distinction between criticism injury and cognizable substitution remain intact. It supplies an operative safeguard, not unrestricted public-domain use.
- Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Corley, April 23, 2002: distinguished as review of a demonstrated access-circumvention function and a tailored injunction. Its requirement to examine the particular speech burden is preserved; its function-specific scrutiny is not transposed into a universal duration test.
- Quality King Distributors, Inc. v. L’anza Research International, Inc., March 9, 1998: its domestic-copy distribution limit is preserved without creating a reproduction entitlement or deciding term-setting power.
- New York Times Co. v. Tasini, June 25, 2001: its right-specific collective-work rule remains; archival value alone creates neither a statutory reproduction privilege nor a duration rule.

#### Law After Decision

Effective January 15, 2003, Congress may make a finite adjustment to existing copyright terms when the adjustment rationally serves copyright’s progress function at the system level; the initial expiration date is not constitutionally immutable. The challenged twenty years remain enforceable. The Court also rejects categorical speech immunity, while holding that this duration change’s actual burden is constitutionally accommodated by the retained safeguards without additional heightened term-policy review. Originality, finite duration, the availability of facts and ideas, and genuine fair use remain operative limits. There is no controlling retrospective-immutability rule, no general First Amendment scrutiny tier for all copyright provisions, and no adjudication of the unchallenged new-work term or restoration of public-domain material.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins Part II-A’s rejection of categorical First Amendment immunity but would invalidate the challenged existing-term increment under the Copyright Clause. An extension must genuinely and rationally serve progress; challengers must show that asserted purposes do not connect the additional exclusion to that function. He does not demand empirical proof of a new work-for-work bargain, optimal policy, or immutable original endpoints. Here completed creation cannot be induced; longer lives do not justify added postdeath protection for those works; and generalized harmonization and preservation claims do not explain the undifferentiated increment, which imposes no new dissemination or preservation obligation. Feist supplies a real constitutional boundary; Sony and Graham distinguish the exclusive right from the public end it serves. A supported systemic transition or conditioned preservation program could differ. Delayed verbatim dissemination is a real speech burden, but his Copyright Clause ground supplies complete relief without a universal independent speech tier. He preserves Campbell’s actual fair-use analysis.

He would reverse, declare the added twenty years invalid within the challenged application, and remand for severability and prospective relief against federal enforcement. Relief would presumptively cover the proved unconstitutional existing-term extension unless the defendant establishes that a narrower cure fully remedies it, within severability and the court’s authority over that defendant. The decree would use preextension expiration dates: lawful earlier terms survive, and works with unexpired earlier terms do not immediately enter the public domain. He would leave the new-work term intact and award no damages or personal judgments against absent owners. Under Harper the rule would govern the parties and cases still on direct review; Reynoldsville preserves independent immunity, procedural, and remedial limits, while reliance alone would not justify selective prospectivity. Final judgments would remain closed, and collateral relief would require its own authorization. Private infringement disputes would apply the constitutional rule through their own parties, defenses, and procedures.

**Stevens, dissenting.** He would reverse as to existing copyrights because completed creation cannot be induced by prolonging an existing federal bargain at the public’s expense. General parity, dissemination, and preservation rationales do not justify a blanket further restriction on the access originally promised. Initial establishment of the federal copyright system differs from enlarging an existing federal grant, and repeated legislation cannot itself establish constitutional power. Feist’s real constitutional boundary and Campbell’s and Quality King’s operative access limits support this objection. He would remand for relief confined to the existing-term application, without independently deciding First Amendment invalidity, a permissible new-work term, public-domain restoration, or remedies for older extensions. His existing-bargain ground is distinct from Stone-Zsela’s acceptance of genuinely justified later adjustments and Breyer’s assessment of this increment’s burdens and benefits.

**Breyer, dissenting.** He would reverse the challenged existing-term application under the Copyright Clause read in light of expressive interests. Meaningful legislative leeway remains, but a substantial additional restraint on use of already-created material must have an adequate copyright-related public justification. The reasons offered do not sufficiently connect this increment of exclusion to progress. Corley’s attention to functional benefit and actual expressive burden and Quality King’s preservation of operative limits support that assessment. His ground does not require a measured new-creation incentive for every work and does not impose a universal independent First Amendment tier. He would remand for relief confined to the existing-term challenge. This ground adopts no new economic calculation, aggregate royalty estimate, orphan-work count, or empirical preservation finding.

#### Source Notes

537 U.S. 186; No. 01-618; U.S. Const., Art. I, §8, cl. 8; First Amendment; 17 U.S.C. §§102(b), 107, 302, 304. The challenge concerns the additional twenty years for subsisting copyrights, not adjudication of any particular infringement or fair-use defense. Historical legislative extensions are legislative practice, not prior judgments deciding this constitutional challenge.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment dismissing the constitutional challenge is affirmed. The existing-term extension remains enforceable, and the requested declaratory and injunctive relief against that application is denied. The unchallenged new-work term and the distinct treatment of works already in the public domain receive no disposition.

### Newman v. Sathyavaglswaran

Merits decision, January 15, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

The Court reviews the Ninth Circuit's April 16, 2002 reversal of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal. The questions concern the parents' own property interests under California's 1997 corneal-removal scheme and the effect of state postdeprivation remedies when removal follows established governmental procedures. Robert Newman and Barbara Obarski allege removal of their deceased sons' corneas in October 1997 without notice or an attempt to obtain consent, and discovery in September 1999. The coroner and county seek dismissal; the parents seek adjudication of the reinstated retrospective claims.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's reversal of the pleading dismissal stands, and the action returns for the process, responsibility, defense and remedy proceedings specified in Part III. Stone-Zsela supports reinstatement on the distinct process rule stated in his concurrence.

#### Opinion Topology

BREYER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Breyer | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; Stone-Zsela as to I-II and the identified III propositions | Affirms reinstatement and governs the return | All eight Associates join I-III; Stone-Zsela also joins the III framework, qualified ordinary-opportunity principle and factual, liability, defense and remedy remit |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | Unjoined | Same reinstatement judgment; distinct ordinary-notice rule | Parts I-II and identified III propositions joined; distinct ordinary-notice and necessity allocation |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The retained disposition, gift and objection rights are property

**Controlling proposition:** California's 1997 law gives the properly entitled next of kin a protected property interest in the retained powers to possess and direct disposition of remains, make anatomical gifts and object to corneal removal. The parents adequately plead that they hold those substantive rights; nonmarket character and the label quasi-property do not remove the interest from procedural due process.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court opinion; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Board of Regents v. Roth locates the entitlement in enforceable state law. California retained disposition, gift and refusal powers; former Government Code §27491.47 authorized removal during autopsy only when the coroner lacked knowledge of an objection. That condition recognizes a legally consequential objection, rather than transferring all control to the State. Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill separates the entitlement from the procedure for its deprivation. American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan requires an actual entitlement and preserves an existing one against procedural erasure: these parents allege presently vested control, unlike general eligibility for payment before substantive predicates are met. Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation confirms that lack of market value does not settle ownership; its takings remedy is not transferred here. The competing view treats retrieval authority as the substantive boundary of a burial duty. It fails to account for the same law's continuing refusal and gift powers. California remains free to define substantive rights, but omission of an effective opportunity to object does not itself extinguish the retained right. Health and Safety Code §7100 preserves contrary decedent directions and the priority of spouse, children, parents, next kin in intestate order and the public administrator if the estate has sufficient assets. The pleaded parents' priority is not defeated by an established superior claimant or donor direction. Autopsy authority and transfer of corneal tissue for another's use are distinct. The reasonable-contact provision for other tissue in §7151.5(a)(2) is not the text of former §27491.47; the later consent amendment does not determine 1997 process.

##### II. An established procedure cannot rely categorically on later state remedies

**Controlling proposition:** Available state postdeprivation remedies do not categorically defeat a §1983 procedural-due-process claim alleging a predictable, irreversible deprivation under established governmental procedures. Adequacy of process must be assessed under the actual circumstances, and exhaustion of state remedies is not a prerequisite to the federal action.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co. distinguishes an established state procedure from the random, unauthorized conduct addressed in Parratt v. Taylor and Hudson v. Palmer, where advance safeguards are impracticable. The complaint alleges a planned removal practice, not an unforeseeable departure from a valid system. The mere availability of a tort action therefore cannot end the federal claim. The former statute's liability shield also prevents assuming an effective compensatory remedy existed. Patsy v. Board of Regents separately rejects exhaustion; that rule does not itself prove that the available process was inadequate. The defendants may establish circumstances justifying action without advance protection, but the complaint cannot be dismissed by presuming such circumstances or treating every later remedy as constitutionally sufficient.

##### III. The actual process must be adjudicated under Mathews

**Controlling proposition:** The reinstated claim must be adjudicated under Mathews by weighing the retained private interest and irreversibility of removal, the risk of mistaken disregard of an entitled person's objection and value of feasible safeguards, and the government's actual retrieval and health burdens. Ordinarily a planned irreversible deprivation requires a practicable opportunity beforehand, but actual urgency or impracticability may justify proceeding without advance process; the pleadings establish neither mandatory advance consent in every case nor adequate eventual money relief.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court opinion is joined in full by all eight Associate Justices. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela also joins its integrated Mathews framework, ordinary practicable-opportunity principle with its actual-urgency or impracticability qualification, and factual, responsibility, defense and remedy remit. He withholds assent only from leaving a more definite ordinary-notice requirement and necessity substantiation unstated. The eight full joins control Part III; the expressly identified common propositions have all nine joins, without synthesis of different rationales.

**Controlling explanation:** Mathews v. Eldridge requires one contextual inquiry, and Gilbert v. Homar illustrates why urgency can affect initial safeguards while continuing to require meaningful protection. Irreversible loss of an objection makes a practicable opportunity valuable; a real medical retrieval window may constrain the form and timing of that opportunity. Neither the value of donation nor the strength of the private interest eliminates the other side of the inquiry. The present record supplies no findings about available contact information, retrieval urgency, conflicting authorization or feasible safeguards. The Court therefore directs assessment under that standard without fixing a more specific ordinary-notice presumption or necessity-proof allocation on these pleadings; it does not decide that eventual compensation suffices. The rule answers the defendants' demand for categorical dismissal while retaining the factual work necessary to decide the actual process. On remand the courts must determine the parents' entitlement under the governing priority and donor rules, the procedure used and practicable safeguards, each defendant's responsibility, preserved defenses, causation, compensable injury and relief under the ordinary governing burdens.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564: applied; the identified California powers supply the property interest.
- Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill, 470 U.S. 532: applied; a retained substantive entitlement is distinct from its deprivation procedure.
- American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. Sullivan (March 3, 1999): applied and distinguished; an actual vested control interest is alleged here, unlike general eligibility without a service-specific payment entitlement.
- Phillips v. Washington Legal Foundation (June 15, 1998): applied to ownership despite absence of market value, without importing its takings question.
- Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422: applied to established governmental procedures.
- Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527, and Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517: distinguished from the pleaded established removal practice; their genuine impracticability principles remain.
- Patsy v. Board of Regents, 457 U.S. 496: applied against exhaustion as a prerequisite, separately from adequacy of process.
- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, and Gilbert v. Homar (June 9, 1997): applied to the actual private interest, error risk, feasible safeguards and urgency.

#### Law After Decision

The retained California disposition, gift and objection interests are protected property for eligible holders under the 1997 statutory limits. The established-procedure claim survives a categorical state-remedy defense. Lower courts must apply the contextual Mathews rule stated in Part III to actual urgency, authorization, contact possibilities and safeguards. Those factual and remedial proceedings apply settled law; they are not a reservation of the protected interest. Stone-Zsela's more definite ordinary-notice presumption remains a separate position. The later consent regime is not displaced by adjudication of these retrospective claims. No immutable constitutional property category for human remains is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment, joins Parts I and II and Part III's integrated Mathews inquiry, ordinary practicable-opportunity principle with its actual-urgency or impracticability qualification, and factual, responsibility, defense and remedy remit. He would hold that an established procedure irreversibly defeating a retained objection ordinarily requires reasonable efforts at advance notice and a practicable opportunity to object. The parents retain the burden of proving deprivation without adequate process; the State must substantiate an asserted urgency, unavailable contact information or other necessity for bypassing that ordinary safeguard with actual circumstances. Mathews gives substantial value to advance protection of an irreversible choice, while allowing medical timing to constrain its form. Logan and Zinermon distinguish this foreseeable procedure from the random acts in Parratt and Hudson; Patsy separately rejects exhaustion. In his view the missing operational facts determine necessity and liability, and do not prevent stating the ordinary safeguard now. He requires neither a trial-type hearing nor an absolute veto of necessary autopsy, and honors valid donor directions and authorized consent. The lower court should develop contact opportunities, retrieval windows, donor directions, authorization and the decision procedure, then determine the practicable protection withheld and causal responsibility. The parents must prove their entitlement, deprivation, defendant responsibility and compensable injury by a preponderance; applicable immunity and other preserved defenses remain separate. The two retrospective removals establish no entitlement to a prospective decree against the later consent regime. Under Harper his rule would govern the parties and cases still open on direct review; Reynoldsville Casket preserves independent procedural, immunity and remedial limits without selective prospectivity based on reliance alone. Final judgments remain closed, and collateral relief requires its own authorization. The safeguard itself determines neither 1997 immunity nor institutional attribution or damages. His particular ordinary-notice presumption and necessity substantiation are not the Court's additional holding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns for further adjudication of the two 1997 removals under the stated entitlement and Mathews rules. ### Norton v. Ashcroft

Merits decision, January 15, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Sixth Circuit's July 31, 2002 judgment affirming dismissal. The questions are whether Congress may regulate local, noncommercial force, threats and obstruction directed at reproductive-health services and whether FACE's service-related distinction violates the First Amendment. The challengers seek facial declaratory and injunctive relief. The warning letter and disputed driveway conversations concern June 2000; no individualized obstruction adjudication is before the Court.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed on the two granted facial claims, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

Facial declaratory and injunctive relief on those grounds is denied. The separate as-applied ripeness and ungranted declaratory dispositions remain undisturbed. This judgment is not an endorsement of each direction in the warning letter.

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Congress may protect the actual interstate services market

**Controlling proposition:** Congress may prohibit the challenged class of force, threats, physical obstruction and facility destruction intentionally directed at, and directly interfering with, the provision or receipt of reproductive health services in an interstate medical-services market. Local conduct and ideological motive do not remove that class from federal power, and no separate interstate journey by each actor is required.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine participating Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies American Life League v. Reno, which sustained this direct market-interference mechanism. The service market includes interstate patients, personnel and supplies, and the regulated acts impair access to the services themselves. United States v. Lopez requires an identifiable constitutional connection before aggregate magnitude can matter; the connection here is the conduct's object and operation on that actual market. United States v. Morrison preserved that distinction while rejecting a private-assault remedy resting on more remote economic consequences. The challengers' lack of profit motive does not change the object or operation of the regulated conduct. Congress regulates interference with the market, not the commercial occupation or political beliefs of the person interfering. The two facial questions therefore require no revision of the existing commerce rule.

##### II. The service connection regulates defined interference without suppressing advocacy

**Controlling proposition:** FACE's challenged reproductive-service restrictions survive the facial First Amendment challenge: they require the enacted prohibited means, intentional harm or attempt, and service connection, while preserving protected advocacy and individual attribution. Expressive conduct satisfies intermediate scrutiny because the substantial access-and-safety interest is unrelated to suppressing a message, the burden is no substantially greater than necessary, and usable nonobstructive communication remains.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine join. The discussion of the existing civil-threat culpability rule applies its present binding force and does not reopen the earlier proposition-level joins.

**Controlling explanation:** American Life League supplies the defined-conduct rule and intermediate scrutiny; O'Brien and Ward require fit to the access-and-safety interest. Section 248(a)(1) requires force, threat of force or physical obstruction; intentional injury, intimidation, interference or attempt; and action because a person is or has been obtaining or providing reproductive services, or to intimidate that person, another person or a class from doing so. Interference restricts movement; intimidation concerns reasonable apprehension of bodily harm to oneself or another; obstruction makes ingress or egress impassable or passage unreasonably difficult or hazardous. Section 248(a)(3)'s service branch separately addresses intentional facility-property damage, destruction or attempt because the facility provides services. Those predicates answer the objection that the statute selects a political viewpoint: the service connection identifies the activity whose access is protected, while persuasion, political offense and a listener's voluntary stop are not themselves obstruction. Section 248(d)(1) preserves protected expression; subsection (d)(2) separately creates no new outside-facility speech or religion remedy and preserves existing remedies. A parent or legal guardian is excluded from every FACE penalty and civil remedy insofar as the activity is directed exclusively at that person's own minor; that exclusion supplies no immunity under other law. Claiborne Hardware and American Life League require each defendant's legally attributable violation, rather than shared belief, membership or reduced demand through persuasion. In enforcement, the prosecution must prove liability elements beyond a reasonable doubt; a civil claimant must prove them by a preponderance. Planned Parenthood of the Columbia/Willamette v. American Coalition of Life Activists additionally requires purpose or knowledge of threatening meaning in its reviewed civil-threat category. Context may communicate a threat but cannot eliminate that culpability requirement. Its finding-specific relief supplies no finding or injunction against Norton. The civil holding does not establish a universal criminal-threat mental-state rule.

#### Precedent Treatment

- American Life League v. Reno (November 13, 1995): applied and reaffirmed on direct market interference, expressive-conduct scrutiny, protected advocacy and individual attribution; its actual definitions and exclusions continue to govern.
- United States v. Lopez (April 26, 1995): applied; a constitutionally identifiable connection precedes aggregation.
- United States v. Morrison (May 15, 2000): distinguished; its private-assault rationale does not displace FACE's direct medical-market connection.
- United States v. O'Brien, 391 U.S. 367, and Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781: applied for the fit of expressive-conduct restrictions to an interest unrelated to suppressing a message.
- NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co., 458 U.S. 886: applied; association and lawful persuasion cannot substitute for attributable unlawful conduct.
- Planned Parenthood of the Columbia/Willamette, Inc. v. American Coalition of Life Activists (December 10, 2002): applied within its civil-threat culpability and finding-specific remedial boundaries; no universal criminal-threat rule is added.

#### Law After Decision

The existing facial commerce and speech rules remain unchanged. FACE's actual conduct, intent, service-connection, expression and own-minor limits govern covered enforcement. Civil threats within Planned Parenthood v. ACLA require purpose or knowledge of threatening meaning. Later enforcement must establish an attributable violation in its own proceeding; this affirmance resolves neither the ungranted warning-letter application nor the disputed encounters.

#### Source Notes

18 U.S.C. §248(a)(1), (a)(3), (c), (d), (e). Norton v. Ashcroft, 298 F.3d 547 (6th Cir. 2002). The disputed driveway encounters have not been adjudicated as violations in this facial proceeding.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The facial claims are concluded by the limited affirmance. The distinct lower-court dispositions outside the grant stand. Any later enforcement proceeds under the statute and controlling liability and remedy rules.

### United States v. Jimenez Recio, No. 01-1184

Merits decision, January 21, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 12, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. Police secretly seized drugs from a truck and used cooperating drivers to arrange a controlled delivery. Jimenez Recio and Lopez-Meza later arrived to collect the vehicles. Following two trials, the court of appeals reversed their second-trial convictions because the Government had not sufficiently proved joinder before the seizure under its automatic-termination rule. The granted question is whether unknown governmental frustration of a conspiracy’s objective itself terminates the conspiracy; it does not independently submit all sufficiency, instructional, or double-jeopardy issues.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

**The judgment is reversed insofar as it rests on automatic termination, and the case is remanded, 8–1.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stevens.

All nine Justices agree that unknown government frustration does not automatically terminate a genuine conspiracy. Stevens would preserve the defendants’ appellate victory because of the Government’s accepted trial burden and untimely challenge. The remand requires consideration of remaining properly maintained appellate grounds from the trial record before reinstatement or further trial proceedings. Rejection of automatic termination does not itself authorize an additional trial or a new factual record to supply missing proof; any resulting trial remedy must rest on its own lawful predicate, including the applicable double-jeopardy limits.

#### Opinion Topology

THOMAS, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STEVENS, J., filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela joins Parts I and III; Stevens joins Part I | Rejects automatic termination unanimously; directs limited reversal and remand by eight votes | Part I: nine; Part II: seven; Part III: eight |
| Concurrence in part and dissent in part | Stevens | Unjoined | Would preserve the appellate reversal on the procedural ground | Joins Part I; rejects Parts II and III |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Unknown government frustration does not itself terminate a conspiracy

**Controlling proposition:** A drug conspiracy under 21 U.S.C. §846 does not automatically terminate when government action unknown to its members makes the intended transaction impossible; pre-seizure joinder is therefore not a categorical statutory element. The prosecution must still prove beyond a reasonable doubt an actual unlawful agreement and the defendant’s knowing participation: mere presence, association, awareness, or an arrangement solely with government agents is insufficient, and actual withdrawal, abandonment, termination, and entrapment retain their independent requirements.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court opinion, joined by all nine Justices. Unanimous agreement on this proposition does not establish unanimous agreement on the judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Shabani construes §846 as an agreement-based offense requiring no additional overt act. Salinas supplies the bounded distinction between agreeing to advance an unlawful undertaking and personally completing its acts, within its different RICO setting. Secret seizure changes the plan’s prospects without necessarily changing the private agreement or a participant’s knowing commitment. The Ninth Circuit’s Cruz rule incorrectly makes objective attainability a continuing legal condition and pre-seizure joinder an additional requirement. The defendants’ concern about manufactured liability identifies an actual evidentiary question: a police-only arrangement is not a genuine private conspiracy. The answer is proof of the unlawful agreement and knowing joinder, together with applicable defenses, rather than legal extinction of every agreement when police secretly remove its object. Actual abandonment or withdrawal must be distinguished from an unknown external obstacle. The Court rejects the categorical rule without supplying the factual agreement necessary for these convictions.

##### II. Correction of the appellate legal rule preserves the consequences of the trial proceedings

**Controlling proposition:** The Court may correct the court of appeals’ automatic-termination rule in deciding the granted statutory question without thereby granting relief for an unobjected-to trial instruction or extinguishing the legal consequences of the Government’s accepted trial burden and procedural choices. Those consequences remain for resolution on properly maintained appellate grounds, rather than being treated as irrelevant or conclusively resolved by the substantive correction.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, supported by the seven Justices who join the opinion in full. Stone-Zsela does not join this additional procedural rationale, and Stevens dissents from it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Government did not object before jury retirement to the second-trial instruction, accepted pre-seizure joinder as its burden in closing, did not initially attack the circuit rule on appeal, and first challenged it on rehearing. The then-applicable Rule 30 objection requirement and the consequence of that accepted burden are real procedural questions. Stevens would treat them as sufficient to preserve the existing insufficiency reversal. The Court instead distinguishes the granted correction of a court of appeals’ statutory rule from a ruling authorizing relief for instructional error. Shabani’s limited appellate return and Quality King’s separation of a legal correction from unadjudicated grounds support leaving distinct questions to their proper disposition. This correction neither assumes government plain-error authority nor decides that nonobjection establishes intentional relinquishment. It also does not select a sufficiency benchmark that disregards the Government’s accepted instruction. The court of appeals must give the procedural history its proper effect on genuinely remaining claims.

##### III. The limited reversal requires resolution of remaining proper appellate questions

**Controlling proposition:** The appellate judgment is reversed only insofar as it rests on automatic termination, and the court of appeals must address remaining properly maintained sufficiency, instructional, and other trial objections from the existing trial record before reinstating the convictions or directing further proceedings. If legally required proof was insufficient, the remedy must respect the bar on another opportunity to supply missing proof; any other trial remedy requires its own lawful predicate.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court opinion, supported by its seven-Justice full-opinion coalition and Chief Justice Stone-Zsela. Stevens dissents from this remedial disposition while joining Part I.

**Controlling explanation:** Shabani’s remand preserves actual appellate objections after rejection of an added statutory element. Here the legal error concerns automatic termination; the convictions’ ultimate treatment also depends on claims with distinct preservation and remedial requirements. The remand preserves only arguments properly maintained, not a new right to revive forfeited issues. Both actual statutory elements and the legal consequences of the trial and appellate history remain relevant within those claims. Correcting the termination rule supplies no missing evidence and does not itself authorize a third trial. The court of appeals must resolve the remaining proper grounds and apply the corresponding sufficiency, instruction, and double-jeopardy consequences. That is ordinary appellate work under the governing rules, rather than an unresolved definition of the §846 agreement offense.

#### Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Shabani, November 1, 1994: applied to preserve actual agreement, knowing participation, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the absence of an additional overt-act requirement. Its limited remand supplies the model for resolving remaining appellate grounds.
- Salinas v. United States, December 2, 1997: applied only by analogy for agreement rather than personal completion; its distinct RICO elements are not substituted for §846.
- Quality King Distributors, Inc. v. L’anza Research International, Inc., March 9, 1998: the seven-Justice procedural portion applies the distinction between correcting a categorical legal premise and deciding remaining independent grounds; its copyright rule is not extended to conspiracy liability.
- United States v. Cruz, 127 F.3d 791: the lower court’s automatic-termination rule is rejected. Unknown police frustration no longer supplies its categorical pre-seizure-joinder requirement.

#### Law After Decision

Effective January 21, 2003, unknown governmental frustration of the unlawful object does not automatically terminate a §846 agreement. Genuine agreement and knowing joinder remain indispensable, with their ordinary proof and defense requirements. The Court adds that legal application to Shabani without changing the offense to punish mere presence or an agreement only with agents. Correction of the appellate rule leaves the independent legal consequences of accepted instructions, preservation, sufficiency, and double jeopardy for proper appellate treatment. The statute’s rule is settled; the remaining work concerns these convictions under that rule and the applicable procedural protections.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He agrees that §846 does not make a conspiracy end automatically upon secret government seizure. He would nevertheless preserve the defendants’ appellate victory. Rule 30 required a timely instructional objection before jury retirement, but the Government made none, affirmatively accepted pre-seizure joinder as its closing burden, and first attacked the circuit rule on rehearing after not challenging it in its initial appellate presentation. For Stevens, characterizing the requested relief as correction of appellate doctrine does not justify removing an insufficiency reversal reached under the very trial standard the Government accepted. His own procedural objection, also reflected in his Williams dissent, supplies the ground for leaving that judgment in place now; it neither endorses automatic termination nor finds that these defendants could never join a real conspiracy. He rejects the Court’s limited reversal because he regards the established procedural history as already sufficient to preserve the result.

537 U.S. 270; No. 01-1184; 21 U.S.C. §846; Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 30 as applicable at trial; United States v. Cruz, 127 F.3d 791. The second-trial instruction appears at petition appendix 75a–76a; the Government’s closing acceptance of pre-seizure joinder appears at opposition appendix 34a. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns to the Ninth Circuit after reversal only of the automatic-termination basis. That court must resolve remaining properly maintained appellate grounds from the trial record, including the proper consequences of the instruction and the Government’s procedural conduct, and then determine the lawful treatment of the convictions. Neither reinstatement nor another trial follows automatically from this mandate; a remedy for insufficient legally required proof must preserve the applicable bar against a second opportunity to supply it.

### Meyer v. Holley, No. 01-1120

Merits decision, January 22, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 3, 2002. The Court reviews the Ninth Circuit's reversal of a Rule 54(b) summary judgment for Meyer on the surviving FHA theory, following an earlier pleading disposition of the officer-capacity theory. Claims against Triad and Crank remained pending. The question is whether corporate ownership, office or designated-broker control alone creates personal vicarious liability for a corporate employee's alleged discriminatory refusal to sell. The Holleys allege a racially discriminatory interference with their attempted 1996 purchase. Meyer's presidency and broker designation are established, but ownership after his asserted 1995 transfer was disputed; direct participation was not alleged. The review concerns compensatory attribution.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The Ninth Circuit's special personal vicarious-liability rule is removed. The lower courts must apply ordinary attribution law to genuinely preserved alternatives as directed in Part III. The pending corporate and salesman claims remain distinct.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Ordinary agency and corporate principles govern compensatory attribution

**Controlling proposition:** For the compensatory vicarious-liability question presented, the Fair Housing Act incorporates ordinary agency and corporate principles: an employee's qualifying conduct may be attributed to the corporate principal, but office, ownership or control of that corporation alone does not make the individual officer, shareholder or designated broker a second principal. Personal liability requires a legally sufficient individual agency relationship, the individual's own actionable wrongdoing, or an applicable basis for disregarding corporate separateness, with the relevant theory properly preserved and proved.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden supplies the interpretive principle that Congress ordinarily incorporates common-law agency where the statute supplies no different rule. The Court uses that method by analogy, not Darden's ERISA employee checklist as an FHA test. General Building Contractors Association v. Pennsylvania distinguishes agency responsibility from an inferred nondelegable duty. United States v. Bestfoods preserves corporate separateness while recognizing supported derivative liability and a genuinely independent direct statutory duty. The FHA's important antidiscrimination purpose cannot alone create an omitted individual principal or impose a nondelegable personal guarantee on every controlling officer. Corporate-employer liability and the actor's own liability remain effective remedies under the actual relationships. Faragher and Ellerth likewise require actual agency-based attribution in their employment settings; their material-aid rules do not make a corporate supervisor the individual employer of all corporate employees. The Holleys must establish the alleged discrimination and a relationship or conduct legally attributing it to each defendant by a preponderance. Here Meyer was president and designated corporate officer/broker, but ownership at the 1996 transaction was disputed after his asserted 1995 transfer. No direct participation was alleged, and the evidence did not establish his direction or approval. Those facts cannot be replaced by a status-based liability rule. Punitive vicarious damages lie outside this reviewed compensatory question.

##### II. The regulation and broker designation do not impose liability from control alone

**Controlling proposition:** Former 24 C.F.R. §103.20(b) requires discrimination by the directing or controlling person's employee or agent acting within the scope of that authority; direction or control alone is insufficient. The later notification rule and California's designated-broker supervisory obligations do not themselves displace the ordinary federal attribution requirements.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The complete former regulation qualifies direction or control by an actual employee-or-agent relationship and conduct within its scope. The circuit's approach detaches the control language from those limits. The 1999 provision, §103.202(b), addresses notification of persons who direct or control, while §103.203(a) separately preserves defenses available in court. Notification is not a substantive expansion of responsibility. A state licensing obligation can be relevant to a properly presented agency theory, but the title and right to supervise do not alone establish that the corporate salesman was Meyer's individual agent. The actual relationship must satisfy the applicable ordinary elements. The statutory purpose cannot supply the missing relationship that neither statutory nor regulatory text establishes.

##### III. Vacatur removes the special rule while preserving only genuine alternatives

**Controlling proposition:** The Ninth Circuit's judgment is vacated and the case remanded to determine whether any traditional veil-piercing or other ordinary attribution theory was actually raised and preserved and, if so, whether its elements are established. Rejection of the circuit's special rule neither proves a personal theory nor extinguishes a legally sufficient corporate or employee claim.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The challenged appellate rule governed reversal of a Rule 54(b) summary judgment for Meyer after an earlier pleading disposition of the officer-capacity theory; Triad and Crank claims remained pending. Leaving that broader rule in place would misdirect further proceedings. The opposing request to end all claims against Meyer rests on a contested preservation premise: the United States' argument identifies a claimed prior presentation of veil piercing, while Meyer disputes preservation. The Court resolves neither account by assumption. The return permits only genuinely preserved theories under ordinary procedure. Ownership must be resolved only as material to such a theory; sole ownership, inadequate assets or office alone is no veil-piercing determination. A general rule of personal responsibility for one's own wrongdoing cannot invent a direct-participation allegation or revive an unappealed dismissal. The choice between state and federal veil-piercing law is not reached because the question presented concerns the special FHA attribution rule and preservation of an ordinary theory remains undetermined; Bestfoods likewise left that choice open. The lower courts must apply the governing elements to the evidence and resolve defenses and corresponding relief. Ordinary proceedings may dispose of a preserved theory without trial or new discovery; this remand grants no independent entitlement to either.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, 503 U.S. 318 (March 24, 1992): applied by interpretive analogy; its ERISA checklist is not an FHA holding.
- General Building Contractors Association, Inc. v. Pennsylvania, 458 U.S. 375: applied to reject an inferred nondelegable duty in place of an actual agency relationship.
- United States v. Bestfoods (June 8, 1998): applied; ordinary ownership does not collapse corporate separateness, and supported derivative liability remains distinct from an independently established direct duty. Its state-or-federal veil-piercing choice remains open in this posture.
- Faragher v. City of Boca Raton (September 8, 1997) and Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth (June 26, 1998): their actual-agency and material-aid principles support the attribution distinction without importing their employment-specific applications or reserved defenses as an FHA test.

#### Law After Decision

Ordinary agency and corporate principles govern the presented FHA compensatory attribution. Status and supervisory control alone create no personal nondelegable guarantee. Corporate and direct-actor responsibility remain independent, and properly preserved individual-agency or corporate-disregard theories receive their ordinary elements and defenses. The regulation's relationship-and-scope limits and distinct notification provisions retain their force. This recurring attribution rule is settled; preservation, evidence and relief on the actual surviving theories remain ordinary adjudication.

#### Source Notes

Meyer v. Holley, No. 01-1120, 537 U.S. 280; Holley v. Crank, 258 F.3d 1127 (9th Cir. 2001). 42 U.S.C. §§3604, 3613; former 24 C.F.R. §103.20(b); 24 C.F.R. §§103.202(b), 103.203(a). Brief for the United States as Amicus Curiae, No. 01-1120 (September 2002), pt. II & n.6. Ownership at the alleged transaction and preservation of ordinary alternatives remain disputed; no direct-participation allegation is established.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The vacated appellate judgment returns for further proceedings under ordinary agency and corporate law. The lower courts first identify the properly preserved personal theory, then apply its elements and defenses to the evidence. Existing claims against the corporation and salesman continue in their own posture.

**End of entry: Meyer v. Holley, merits decision, January 22, 2003.**

### Roe v. Texas Department of Protective and Regulatory Services

Merits decision, June 2, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Fifth Circuit's July 17, 2002 interlocutory immunity judgment, 299 F.3d 395, No. 01-50711. The questions concern a child-protection worker's nonurgent intimate examination and photography, conduct-date fair warning, the mother's scheduled-interview entry claim and overlapping substantive-due-process theories. The search occurred July 10, 1999. The Court accepts the supported plaintiff-favorable account for the legal appeal. The worker, Beverly Strickland, claimed neither probable cause nor exigency; consent to the intimate search remains disputed. The child's representatives seek federal damages relief; Strickland seeks immunity. The state-law claims had been remanded, and other defendants' dismissals were not appealed.

All nine Justices participated at submission and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The agreed interview authorized entry within its own scope

**Controlling proposition:** An objectively understood, voluntarily arranged home interview permits the identified worker to enter for that interview when she arrives as agreed, absent withdrawal or an expressed limitation. The absence of a renewed express doorstep invitation does not revoke that antecedent permission, but the interview agreement does not authorize intimate bodily examination or photography.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court opinion; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court accepts the mother's account that she did not expressly invite Strickland inside again at the doorway. Her acknowledged earlier agreement to an appointment at home, after learning the worker's identity and welfare purpose, nevertheless supplies affirmative authorization for the interview. The result rests on that agreement and its objective scope, not on silence as consent. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte supplies voluntary consent, and Ohio v. Robinette requires attention to the actual circumstances rather than an indispensable verbal formula. Johnson v. Jones and Behrens v. Pelletier permit assessment of the legal effect of the accepted account, not a credibility choice in the worker's favor. The mother's separate entry claim therefore fails, while the child's distinct search claim requires its own justification.

##### II. This nonurgent intimate examination required consent or judicial authorization

**Controlling proposition:** A nonemergency investigative visual examination and photography of a child's genital and anal areas in these circumstances requires voluntary consent or appropriate judicial authorization supported by probable cause; genuine exigency is an independent exception. The claimant must establish the governmental search, and the official must substantiate consent or another invoked exception; cooperation with official directions does not conclusively establish voluntary consent.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court opinion, supported by the seven Justices who join the opinion in full. Scalia and Thomas do not reach the constitutional merits; they neither join this rule nor vote that the search was constitutional.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court examines the actual operation, as Ferguson v. City of Charleston requires, and the demonstrated need, as Chandler v. Miller requires. A protective purpose is substantial, but the challenged operation was intimate exposure, manipulation and photography by a worker without training in that photography, rather than an ordinary interview or superficial observation. The ten-day response classification, absence of claimed urgency or probable cause, and acknowledged medical-examination route supply no necessity for immediate intrusion without ordinary authorization. Griffin and Vernonia allow justified protective arrangements; they do not make a general protective mission sufficient for every investigative method. Statutory reporting duties or possible police contact do not categorically defeat special needs, and Ferguson's coordinated prosecution program is not a fact of this case. Good v. Dauphin County Social Services applies the Fourth Amendment in child-protection investigation; Darryl H. v. Coler likewise retains limits on scope and necessity under its different approach. Consent remains a distinct question. Under Schneckloth and Robinette, the mother's assistance in removing clothing and positioning the child, in response to official direction, must be considered with her questions about necessity and counsel, distress and lack of advance explanation of the eventual scope. These facts permit a finding of submission rather than permission; the Court requires no particular warning or verbal formula and assumes no threat to remove the child. On the supported plaintiff-favorable account of no voluntary consent, this search lacked the required justification. Actual consent remains for ordinary proceedings.

##### III. Pre-July 10, 1999 law supplied fair warning on the accepted account

**Controlling proposition:** Qualified immunity does not protect the assumed unconsented, highly intimate and nonurgent search because preconduct law made its lack of justification apparent under either plausible search framework. The legal inquiry uses supported plaintiff-favorable facts and law effective before July 10, 1999; materially different established facts remain subject to their own defenses, and later decisions supply no earlier notice.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court opinion, supported by the same seven Justices. Scalia and Thomas dissent from reversal of immunity. The seven direct joins control; no synthesis of fractured rationales is required.

**Controlling explanation:** Harlow v. Fitzgerald and Anderson v. Creighton require objective, particularized conduct-date notice. Wilson v. Layne distinguishes violation from personal immunity without demanding a factual twin; Elder v. Holloway requires all relevant preconduct law. Hope v. Pelzer explains how obvious application can give warning, but is not itself July 1999 notice. Before the search, Camara and New Jersey v. T.L.O. subjected civil officials and children's searches to the Fourth Amendment. Bell v. Wolfish required examination of scope, manner, justification and place. Vernonia and Chandler required real contextual justification, while Schneckloth and Robinette required voluntary consent rather than submission. Good specifically rejected a general child-protection exemption. The contrary authorities do not disappear: Darryl H. rejected a categorical warrant requirement and granted immunity despite substantial necessity and consent doubts. Yet it retained reasonableness limits, declined to treat maternal assistance as conclusive consent and identified medical examination for suspected sexual abuse. Landstrom involved a nurse's examination of complained-of soreness and a March 1987 notice date; Wildauer concerned an investigative home visit. Doe v. Louisiana granted Fourteenth Amendment immunity and expressly reserved the Fourth Amendment question. That reservation warrants care but does not affirmatively authorize this distinct nonmedical photography beyond the shared scope, necessity and consent limits. The Court's conclusion therefore does not depend on a settled warrant rule for every protective examination. It rests on the particular intrusion's obvious excess under either approach. Scalia and Thomas regard the immunity outcomes and unresolved local question as dispositive uncertainty; the Court instead finds the shared limits sufficiently concrete for the accepted lack of consent, probable cause and urgency. Ferguson, Wallis, Calabretta, Tenenbaum, Saucier and Hope create no July 1999 warning. Neither actual reading of precedent nor proof of actual reliance is required. No compulsory merits-first sequence is imposed.

##### IV. The same entry and search do not create duplicate substantive-due-process claims

**Controlling proposition:** The mother's home-entry claim and the child's bodily-search claim are governed by the specific Fourth Amendment protection applicable to those acts, not a duplicate substantive-due-process damages theory. Failure to recover on a specific claim, including because of personal immunity, does not supply a separate substantive-due-process remedy for the same intrusion.

**Authority:** Part IV of the Court opinion; all nine Justices join this rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** Graham v. Connor and the specific-provision rule preserved in County of Sacramento v. Lewis direct the identified intrusions to their express constitutional source. The record contains neither removal of the child nor a separate coercive displacement of a parental decision beyond the challenged examination. The mother's emotional injury does not itself establish that additional governmental act. This conclusion does not erase a parent's distinct family-liberty interest when a different supported act infringes it. It rejects these duplicative theories concerning the entry and examination, whose actual scope and justification are addressed by the Fourth Amendment.

##### V. The restored federal claim and state-law claims return in their actual posture

**Controlling proposition:** Texas official immunity must be assessed under its own law, independently of federal qualified immunity. With the child's federal search claim restored, any decision about supplemental jurisdiction must take account of that surviving claim; the unappealed dismissals of other defendants remain intact.

**Authority:** Part V-A, the independence of Texas immunity, is joined by all nine. Part V-B, the restored-federal-claim remit and its supplemental-jurisdiction consequence, is joined by the seven Justices who join the opinion in full; Scalia and Thomas would instead leave the federal dismissal in place and preserve the independent state-law return.

**Controlling explanation:** The federal notice determination does not decide a Texas defense. The Fifth Circuit's state-law return must be conformed to the federal claim's restored posture, rather than implemented on an assumption that all federal claims have ended. Ordinary proceedings determine actual consent, personal responsibility, causation, injury, liability and remedy. Strickland must substantiate an invoked consent justification on the actual circumstances; the child must prove the actionable search and resulting compensable injury by a preponderance. If the proved facts materially differ from those assumed for the legal appeal, the court applies the governing defenses to those facts. A lawful discretionary refusal of supplemental jurisdiction remains governed by its actual predicates, and a jurisdictional dismissal is without prejudice. The department and other officials whose dismissals were not appealed are not restored by this mandate.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, and Ohio v. Robinette (November 18, 1996): applied to voluntary, circumstance-sensitive consent without a mandatory verbal formula.
- Johnson v. Jones (June 12, 1995), and Behrens v. Pelletier (February 21, 1996): applied; interlocutory legal review uses supported assumed facts and cannot decide credibility or evidentiary sufficiency.
- Ferguson v. City of Charleston (March 21, 2001): applied to current merits; actual operation requires its own justification, without a categorical mixed-purpose bar or retrospective notice.
- Chandler v. Miller (April 15, 1997): applied; a demonstrated need must justify the intrusion.
- Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 U.S. 868, and Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton (June 26, 1995): their actual protective arrangements are distinguished from this unsubstantiated intimate method; the search and no-waiver principles remain.
- Camara v. Municipal Court, 387 U.S. 523; New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325; Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520: their preconduct civil-search, child-search and scope/manner/justification rules inform the particular warning.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, and Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635: applied; objective, particularized legal notice remains the defense.
- Elder v. Holloway (February 23, 1994), and Wilson v. Layne (May 24, 1999): applied; all relevant preconduct law and the violation/immunity distinction remain material without a factual-twin requirement.
- Hope v. Pelzer (June 27, 2002): applies the obvious-application method with attention to contrary authority and hierarchy; it does not create 1999 notice.
- Saucier v. Katz (June 18, 2001): preserves separate objective immunity analysis; it creates neither compulsory merits-first sequencing nor earlier notice.
- Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386, and County of Sacramento v. Lewis (May 26, 1998): applied to these acts under the specific Fourth Amendment source, without eliminating independent family-liberty claims.
- Wallis v. Spencer (April 2, 2001): its independent-examination reservation supplies no resolution or conduct-date warning here.
- Good v. Dauphin County Social Services, 891 F.2d 1087: applied to Fourth Amendment protection in child-protection investigations.
- Darryl H. v. Coler, 801 F.2d 893: distinguished in scope and safeguards; its different approach retains limits on necessity and reasonableness and does not make maternal assistance conclusive consent.

#### Law After Decision

The bodily-search holding requires voluntary consent, probable cause with appropriate judicial authorization, or genuine exigency for the nonurgent intimate investigative operation addressed here. It does not impose one procedure on every protective inquiry or make police reporting alone decisive. The scheduled interview authorizes only its objective scope. On the accepted no-consent, no-probable-cause and no-urgency account, pre-July 10, 1999 law gave sufficient warning for the child-search claim to proceed. Actual liability remains subject to the proved facts and ordinary defenses. The same intrusions do not support duplicate substantive-due-process damages theories. Texas official immunity remains independent, and jurisdictional treatment must reflect the restored federal claim.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurs in Parts I, IV and V-A and dissents from the reversal of child-search immunity and the consequential federal-claim remit. They affirm the interview-entry and duplicative-due-process rulings and preserve the independence of Texas immunity. They would affirm dismissal of all reviewed federal claims and return the state-law claims for evaluation of Texas immunity or a lawful decision declining supplemental jurisdiction, with a jurisdictional dismissal without prejudice. They do not decide the child's Fourth Amendment merits. In their view Anderson's particularized-notice requirement is not satisfied by the general gravity of this intrusion. Darryl H. rejected a categorical warrant-and-probable-cause requirement and granted immunity despite serious questions about necessity and consent; Landstrom likewise found insufficient clarity for an intimate examination. Doe v. Louisiana's local Fourth Amendment reservation left the application unsettled. Nonmedical photography is a material distinction, but the preconduct authorities had not made its personal-damages consequence sufficiently definite by July 10, 1999. A factual twin is unnecessary, all relevant other-circuit authority remains relevant, and child-protection workers have no blanket immunity. Nevertheless, the present Court's stronger merits interpretation cannot cure the earlier uncertainty. Ferguson, Wallis, Calabretta, Tenenbaum, Saucier and Hope cannot supply retrospective notice. Their position finds neither voluntary consent nor constitutional approval of the search.
