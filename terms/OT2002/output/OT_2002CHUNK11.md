### Adler v. Duval County School Board

Plenary decision, May 27, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

The Court reviews the Eleventh Circuit's May 23, 2001 en banc judgment sustaining Duval County's graduation-message policy. The question is whether the policy delegates an official religious choice or permits independent student expression. The facial challenge is the granted question; the separate intervention dispute and disputed as-applied claims lie outside this review. The 1993 policy permits the senior class to choose whether to have a short opening or closing message and to select its student speaker, whose words school officials may not review. The challengers seek declaratory and injunctive relief. Continuing jurisdiction must be determined first.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

O'CONNOR, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** A plaintiff seeking prospective relief against a school policy must establish a continuing personal injury that relief in the action can redress. When the record establishes a live controversy at filing but does not establish whether it continues at review, the court must determine the remaining injury and available relief before deciding the constitutional merits; neither assumed continued enrollment nor the mere passage of time supplies that determination.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court controls the rule, its application and the conditional jurisdictional remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Altman requires the court to identify the actual present stake before adjudicating a school-religion claim. Ingebretsen permits prospective review on supported imminent school-event exposure, and Tanford requires a concrete continuing expectation rather than abstract disagreement with school policy. These rules require inquiry into the plaintiffs who seek relief now.

The 1998 allegations concerning parents and younger enrolled children established a concrete controversy at filing. They establish neither those children's enrollment in May 2003 nor some other continuing personal injury. Conversely, the mootness of the earlier graduates' claims and the intervening years do not show that every claim in the 1998 action has ended. The record establishes neither a certified class nor a developed damages claim independently supporting the present facial adjudication. The policy's continued operation and its constitutional importance cannot replace these plaintiffs' personal stake. The appropriate disposition is therefore a jurisdictional remand. The facial Establishment Clause question remains undecided because the facts necessary to establish continuing merits jurisdiction have not been determined.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Altman v. Bedford Central School District (May 19, 2003) is applied: an earlier live-school finding establishes neither continuing enrollment nor universal mootness at later review.
- Ingebretsen v. Jackson Public School District is applied for a supported imminent personal exposure supporting prospective school-event relief.
- Tanford v. Brand is applied for a concrete continuing expectation of exposure rather than abstract objection or assumed perpetual enrollment.

#### Law After Decision

Existing Article III law requires a plaintiff-specific determination of current injury and available relief before the facial school-policy merits. A continuing policy does not by itself establish a continuing claim for every former plaintiff.

#### Source Notes

Adler v. Duval County School Board, 250 F.3d 1330. Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(a)(2). The 1998 allegations concerning younger children do not establish their enrollment at the present review; the earlier graduates' claims belong to distinct litigation.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The lower courts must identify, plaintiff by plaintiff and claim by claim, the remaining injury and effectual relief as of this review, using the existing record and any necessary ordinary jurisdictional proceedings. A live preserved facial claim proceeds under governing school-function law. If no live claim remains, the court must dismiss it for mootness and separately determine the equitable treatment of the remaining judgments. The remand is confined to the reviewed facial claim.

The case returns to the lower courts for the jurisdictional determination and conditional proceedings specified in the mandate.

**End of entry: Adler v. Duval County School Board, Plenary decision, May 27, 2003.**

### Black & Decker Disability Plan v. Nord, No. 02-469

Merits decision, May 27, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 28, 2003; decided May 27, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. The question is whether ERISA requires special treating-physician deference or a heightened explanation burden for crediting reliable contrary medical evidence.

The district court granted summary judgment to the plan after finding no abuse of discretion. The Ninth Circuit reversed and directed summary judgment for Nord under its treating-physician rule. Nord's treating physician and an orthopedist described degenerative disc disease and chronic pain preventing work. An examining neurologist concluded that medication would permit sedentary work. MetLife recommended denial, which Black & Decker's administrator adopted. Nord seeks benefits under ERISA §502(a)(1)(B); the plan challenges the categorical rule supporting the appellate award.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

GINSBURG, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, and BREYER, JJ., joined. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed a concurring opinion.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA does not require a disability-plan administrator to give special weight to a treating physician's opinion or bear a distinct heightened burden of explanation merely because it credits reliable conflicting medical evidence. Section 503's specific denial reasons and full-and-fair-review duties remain: an administrator may not arbitrarily refuse to credit reliable claimant evidence, including a treating physician's opinion. Judicial review applies the actual plan terms and Firestone's standard—de novo absent the relevant discretionary grant, and abuse-of-discretion review with an actual conflict considered when lawful discretion exists.

**Authority:** All nine Justices join the Court's no-preference rule, ordinary statutory duties, review standard and remedy. Stone-Zsela's additional general material-conflict explanation rule appears in his separate concurrence.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source of a medical opinion does not create an evidentiary hierarchy absent a legal basis in ERISA or its applicable claims rules. Neither prescribes the Social Security treating-physician rule. That program's regulatory choice rests on a different benefits system; its rule cannot be transplanted merely because both systems decide disability. Calling the preference procedural rather than substantive does not supply the missing statutory source. The Secretary of Labor's position supports application of ERISA's actual structure.

Nord's strongest argument is practical: continuing treatment can reveal chronic pain and functional limits, while a paid consultant's single examination may be incomplete or affected by conflicting interests. Those considerations belong in evaluation of actual reliability and the administrator's decision. They do not establish a mandatory preference in every plan. Conversely, consultant status does not establish greater reliability. Section 503 requires specific denial reasons and a full and fair review; Firestone determines the court's scrutiny according to the plan's delegation and an actual conflict. Curtiss-Wright and Spink distinguish lawful sponsor design from compliance in administration. Rush Prudential enforces an actual saved medical-coverage condition, rather than supplying a general federal hierarchy of physician evidence.

The Ninth Circuit directed benefits under the erroneous preference. Its judgment must be vacated, and the actual denial reviewed on the plan and record under the lawful standard. That inquiry gives treating continuity, pain, examination quality, functional evidence and a supported conflict their actual significance. It can produce benefits relief or a lawful denial depending on the proper review.

**Precedent treatment:** Firestone's default review rule, discretionary condition and conflict factor are applied. Curtiss-Wright and Spink retain the distinction between sponsor design and actual administration. Rush Prudential's saved-coverage and compatible federal-enforcement holdings retain their scope; Kentucky Association's insurance-saving rule supplies no physician hierarchy.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch supplies de novo review absent the relevant grant of discretion, discretionary review where lawfully granted, and consideration of an actual conflict.
- Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen and Lockheed Corp. v. Spink preserve lawful sponsor design while leaving actual plan administration subject to plan and statutory duties.
- Rush Prudential HMO, Inc. v. Moran retains enforcement of its saved medical-coverage condition through ERISA benefits relief. Its actual state-law coverage condition does not become a federal treating-physician rule.
- Kentucky Association of Health Plans, Inc. v. Miller retains its insurance-saving framework, which addresses the validity of state insurance regulation rather than medical-source weight.

#### Law After Decision

ERISA disability decisions must be reviewed without an automatic treating-physician preference or a special explanation burden triggered only by rejecting a treating source. Specific denial reasons, full and fair review, reliable evidence, actual plan terms and Firestone's discretion-and-conflict framework govern. The claimant's benefits entitlement remains ordinary adjudication under those settled rules, rather than an inference from the physician's affiliation.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins rejection of the treating-source preference, the source-triggered heightened burden and the remand for ordinary full-and-fair review. He would make explicit a further general duty: when an administrator resolves a material medical conflict against a claimant, it must provide an intelligible, record-based explanation responsive to that conflict, regardless of the physicians' affiliations. Sections 503 and 502 make notice, review and enforcement meaningful only if the decision's reasons can be evaluated. The claimant would retain the burden of establishing entitlement under the plan and applicable judicial standard; the administrator's operational duty to explain would not become a heightened burden to disprove every treating opinion.

On this record, the explanation should connect the consultant's conclusion that medication permits sedentary work to the evidence of persistent pain and the actual job and plan requirements. Treatment continuity, methodology, functional findings and material contradictions matter more than counting physicians. If required reasoned evaluation were absent, a genuine corrective decision would be necessary, with an intelligible written explanation under the applicable timetable or a consistent reasonable court-set schedule. A repeated unexplained conclusion would require enforcement of the review duty and determination of the lawful benefits consequence. A developed record warranting benefits could support the benefits relief authorized by §502(a)(1)(B).

He retains Firestone's review framework and lawful plan definitions. The remand he joins permits ordinary review for arbitrariness and any preserved conflict claim; rejection of the preference does not establish Nord's capacity to work.

#### Source Notes

538 U.S. 822. Black & Decker Disability Plan v. Nord, No. 02-469; ERISA §§502(a)(1)(B) and 503, 29 U.S.C. §§1132(a)(1)(B) and 1133; Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101; Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen, No. 93-1935, decided March 6, 1995; Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882; Rush Prudential HMO, Inc. v. Moran, No. 00-1021; Kentucky Association of Health Plans, Inc. v. Miller, No. 00-1471, decided April 2, 2003.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The appellate benefits judgment is vacated. The district court's earlier judgment for the plan is not automatically reinstated; the actual denial must receive review under the proper standard.

The Ninth Circuit must reconsider the denial under the plan's disability definition, the actual delegation of discretion, the administrative record and the claims rules governing this determination. It must evaluate the reasons given, reliable treating and consulting evidence, functional limits, pain and any supported conflict under the appropriate standard. Further administration or benefits relief follows the requirements of ordinary lawful review and the supported record. A later claims regulation may not be substituted for the rule governing this determination.

**End of entry: Black & Decker Disability Plan v. Nord, Merits decision, May 27, 2003.**

### Bunkley v. Florida, No. 02-8636

Reasoned summary disposition, May 27, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Following the separate grant of certiorari this date, the Court reviews the Florida Supreme Court's postconviction judgment rejecting the contention that the knife supporting Bunkley's armed-burglary conviction fell within Florida's common-pocketknife exception. The issue is the temporal state-law premise necessary to decide that missing-element due-process claim. Bunkley committed the burglary in 1986, and his conviction became final in 1989. Florida later characterized L. B. v. State, decided in 1997, as an evolutionary refinement unavailable retroactively under its collateral doctrine. The matter is resolved on summary review without oral argument.

#### Judgment

**Vacated and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.
**Opposing:** Kennedy, Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

The Court issued a per curiam opinion, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, SCALIA, SOUTER, GINSBURG, and BREYER, JJ., joined. KENNEDY, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** When an authoritative later state construction creates a concrete ambiguity whether an exclusion from an offense already covered the defendant when the conviction became final, a court may not reject the missing-element due-process claim solely as a request for retroactivity without identifying the law governing that conviction. A clear state-law determination that the relevant scope arose later ordinarily answers that antecedent inquiry; a general account of gradual evolution that leaves the material exclusion's timing unresolved does not.

**Authority:** The seven-Justice per curiam opinion controls the clarification rule, application and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Fiore distinguishes an authoritative clarification of existing offense elements from a genuinely new interpretation and provides a method of obtaining the necessary state-law answer. Winship requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element, and Jackson tests sufficiency against those actual substantive elements. Proof that Bunkley committed ordinary burglary therefore cannot substitute for a weapon element his offense required.

Florida's longstanding statute excludes a common pocketknife. Bunkley's folded blade measured about two and one-half to three inches; no use or threat with it is shown. L. B.'s treatment of a longer pocketknife, supported by earlier interpretive material, makes the scope of the exclusion in 1989 a substantial question. Neither blade length nor unchanged wording answers that question by itself.

The dissent reads Florida's evolutionary-refinement account as an authoritative finding that the relevant scope arose after finality. That account describes development over time but does not identify when the exclusion material to this knife became law. Obtaining that specific answer respects Florida's power to define its statute. Florida also adjudicated the federal contention; an unarticulated independent procedural bar cannot be assumed to dispose of it. The remedy is a bounded state-law inquiry followed by the corresponding federal disposition, rather than a federal definition of the knife exception.

**Question left open:** If the relevant state-law scope genuinely arose later, any necessary and properly presented retroactivity question remains for the resulting proceedings because its state-law predicate has yet to be determined.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Fiore v. White (November 30, 1999; January 9, 2001) is applied: authoritative clarification must establish whether the missing element was already law before the existing-element due-process rule can be enforced. Its genuinely-new-construction boundary remains.
- In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, is applied to proof beyond a reasonable doubt of each actual offense element.
- Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, is applied to the protection against a conviction unsupported under the governing substantive elements.

#### Law After Decision

Courts addressing a concrete missing-element claim must distinguish clarification of the earlier offense from a genuinely later change before disposing of that claim as retroactivity. State courts retain authority to define their criminal law; the federal proof requirement operates on the elements that authoritative account establishes. The holding governs this identified temporal ambiguity and does not require reopening every final conviction after a favorable statutory construction.

#### Separate Writings

**Kennedy, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** They accept Fiore's rule against sustaining a conviction without proof of an existing element, but regard Florida as having already answered the antecedent question: L. B. introduced the relevant favorable interpretation after Bunkley's conviction became final. A substantial argument for a different reading of earlier Florida law does not authorize this Court to disregard the State Supreme Court's authoritative answer. They therefore view a second clarification demand as an expansion of Fiore that intrudes on state-law authority and collateral finality, especially without plenary briefing and argument. They would leave the postconviction judgment in force.

#### Source Notes

Bunkley v. Florida, 538 U.S. 835; No. 02-8636; L. B. v. State (1997); Florida's burglary and weapon statutes; Fla. R. Crim. P. 3.850. The folded blade was approximately two and one-half to three inches long. The judicial record identifies no use or threat with the knife during the burglary.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Florida must determine, with its legal basis, whether the common-pocketknife exception as it operated when Bunkley's conviction became final in 1989 encompassed this knife, then decide the federal claim on that authoritative premise. If the exception already applied and no adequate independent procedural ground precludes relief, the state court must set aside the unsupported armed conviction and dependent life sentence and determine the lawful lesser-offense disposition. If the relevant interpretation genuinely arose later, the court must resolve the properly presented retroactivity and procedural questions. A repeated evolutionary-refinement label that still omits the material timing does not perform the required inquiry. The conviction and attributable sentence remain subject to that work; immediate release is not ordered.

The case returns to the Florida Supreme Court for the temporal determination and resulting conviction-and-sentence proceedings required by the mandate.

**End of entry: Bunkley v. Florida, Reasoned summary disposition, May 27, 2003.**

### Chavez v. Martinez, No. 01-1444

Merits decision, May 27, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 4, 2002; decided May 27, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. The court below affirmed rejection of Chavez's qualified-immunity defense to a civil damages action concerning hospital questioning on November 28, 1997. The questions concern the precedential force of Verdugo-Urquidez's Fifth Amendment discussion, actual coercive questioning without criminal use, and the independent constitutional-offensiveness and immunity inquiry.

Chavez arrived after other officers shot Martinez. He questioned Martinez during severe pain and fear of death for roughly ten minutes over forty-five minutes, with pauses for continuing medical procedures. Martinez repeatedly expressed distress and reluctance to answer and received no Miranda warnings. He was never charged and his statements were never used in a criminal case. The legal immunity appeal proceeds on supported plaintiff-favorable assumptions and does not determine disputed historical facts.

#### Judgment

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Self-Incrimination Clause damages theory | Reversed, 5–4 | O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg | The judgment sustaining this theory is reversed; this theory cannot support further damages proceedings. |
| Independent substantive due process | Vacated and remanded, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer | O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas | Independent merits and objective-immunity consideration under the Court's remand instruction. |

Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg support the due-process remand while retaining their stronger position against the asserted legal defense. Stone-Zsela would also remand the Fifth Amendment theory. O'Connor opposes the due-process remand on immunity alone; Scalia and Thomas additionally reject that claim on the present merits.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., announced the judgment of the Court and delivered the opinion of the Court as to Parts I and III and an opinion as to Part II. THOMAS, KENNEDY, STEVENS, and GINSBURG, JJ., and STONE-ZSELA, C. J., each filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. The joins and their scope are set out below.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lead opinion, Parts I-III | Souter | Breyer throughout | Announces the split judgment | Breyer joins the entire lead opinion, including Part II's limited additional-protection rationale. |
| Lead Part I | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer | Unanimous Court holding | Verdugo-Urquidez's actual precedential scope |
| Lead Part II | Souter | Breyer | Supports Fifth Amendment reversal without a controlling majority rationale | Confined need-for-additional-protection ground |
| Lead Part III | Souter | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Court's due-process remand | Independent analysis under existing objective immunity |
| Separate Part A | Thomas | O'Connor, Scalia | Supports Fifth Amendment reversal | Criminal-case condition |
| Separate Part B1 | Thomas | Scalia | Would reject the due-process claim on its present merits | Constitutional merits; O'Connor does not join |

| Separate Parts I-III | Kennedy | Stevens throughout; Ginsburg as to Parts II and III | Opposes Fifth Amendment reversal; joins due-process remand with a stronger application | I: warning omission; II: actual compulsion without criminal use; III: rejection of the legal defense on the supported intentional-exploitation account |

Part II has no controlling majority rationale. The Part III coalition does not adopt the separate writings' ultimate constitutional or immunity conclusions.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The precedent question

**Holding and operative rule:** United States v. Verdugo-Urquidez's description of the Self-Incrimination Clause as a trial right did not decide whether domestic coercive interrogation can support the civil claim presented here. That comparison is not a binding holding on this question; its persuasive force must be assessed with the authorities that actually address the privilege.

**Authority:** Part I of the lead opinion is joined directly by all nine Justices and is the opinion of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a decision's explanation must be read against the question it resolved. Verdugo-Urquidez concerned the Fourth Amendment's application to a foreign search of a nonresident alien's property. Its comparison with the Fifth Amendment did not adjudicate this domestic interrogation claim, its cause of action or its remedy. Treating that comparison as a holding would substitute an answer to a different case for examination of the criminal-case language, prospective privilege and actual-compulsion authorities. Its status does not decide the present claim in Martinez's favor: the Justices disagree about that claim on the separate grounds identified below.

**Precedent treatment:** Verdugo-Urquidez retains its foreign-search holding; its Fifth Amendment comparison has persuasive rather than controlling force on this damages question.

##### II. The Self-Incrimination Clause claim

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The judgment sustaining this Self-Incrimination Clause damages theory is reversed. O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas and Breyer support that disposition; Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg oppose it. No common rationale commands five votes.

**Authority and explanation:** Part II of the lead opinion, adopted by Souter and Breyer, concludes that Martinez has not shown a need to add this freestanding damages protection to the existing privilege, immunity, penalty and exclusion protections. Thomas's separate Part A, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, instead concludes that the absence of a criminal case or use defeats the asserted completed violation under the Clause's text. The first rationale turns on the justification for additional protection and could recognize such protection on a different showing; the second turns on an absent constitutional event. Neither is a logical subset establishing the same outcomes whenever it applies as the other. Marks therefore supplies no unified controlling rationale. The reversal binds this claim.

**Precedent treatment:** Dickerson's constitutional case-in-chief safeguards and McKune's protection against substantial automatic penalties for refusing incriminating disclosures remain in force. Neither earlier holding decided this civil-damages claim. Marks preserves the distinction between a majority judgment and a common governing rationale.

##### III. The independent due-process claim and objective immunity

**Holding and operative rule:** Criminal nonuse does not dispose of a separately supported claim that an officer's own interrogation methods constituted conscience-shocking executive abuse. The availability, scope and merits of that claim require a context-sensitive inquiry into the officer's conduct, knowledge, opportunity to deliberate, legitimate investigative needs and causal responsibility, together with separate consideration of the asserted objective defense under law existing when the officer acted.

**Authority:** Part III is the opinion of the Court, joined directly by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These six adopt the independent inquiry and exact remand instruction. Stevens, Kennedy and Ginsburg additionally state stronger application conclusions in their separate writings.

**Controlling explanation:** Lewis makes the required culpability responsive to the executive setting. Its purpose-to-harm rule for a rapidly evolving pursuit does not govern every hospital interrogation. Reasonable urgent questioning and voluntary disclosure differ from deliberate exploitation of extreme suffering to overcome resistance. Conversely, Mincey's exclusion of an involuntary hospital statement does not by itself establish this civil constitutional wrong. The lower court must analyze the independent claim rather than equate coercion for exclusion with damages liability.

Harlow and Anderson preserve particularized objective immunity. Elder requires all relevant conduct-date authority, while Hope rejects an indispensable factual twin. The November 28, 1997 inquiry must examine Rochin's brutal-investigation boundary, Mincey's actual hospital-coercion rule, Cooper v. Dupnik's actual circuit recognition of coercion-based civil claims, and other relevant then-existing authority. Cooper's planned and prolonged interrogation and its distinction between coercion and warnings must be considered at their actual scope. Lewis, Dickerson and Hope organize current analysis but cannot supply later notice. The legitimate purpose of obtaining an injured witness's account does not alone answer whether the methods employed were constitutionally abusive. Continuing treatment likewise does not alone resolve that question. Johnson and Behrens preserve the boundary between legal immunity review on supported assumptions and resolution of disputed historical facts.

**Precedent treatment:** Lewis is applied in its contextual rather than universal-intent scope. Rochin and Mincey retain their investigative-abuse and exclusion functions. Harlow, Anderson, Denius, Elder, Saucier and Hope retain objective immunity and their distinct procedural limits; the remand applies that existing defense. Johnson and Behrens preserve the interlocutory fact boundary.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Verdugo-Urquidez retains its Fourth Amendment foreign-search holding; its Fifth Amendment comparison does not settle this civil claim.
- Dickerson retains Miranda's constitutional case-in-chief protection, and McKune retains its prospective protection against substantial automatic penalties. The Fifth Amendment judgment supplies no common new general rationale replacing those holdings.
- Lewis supplies the setting-sensitive executive-abuse inquiry; its rapid-pursuit purpose-to-harm rule remains tied to that setting.
- Rochin and Mincey supply the relevant investigative-abuse and hospital-coercion authorities, with their actual remedies respected. Cooper v. Dupnik is relevant preconduct circuit authority at its actual scope rather than binding Supreme Court merits law.
- Harlow and Anderson retain particularized objective immunity. Denius retains its express rejection of an actual-reliance prerequisite. Elder requires all relevant preconduct law; Saucier retains the separate defense and interlocutory limits; Hope rejects an indispensable factual twin. These current principles do not backdate notice.
- Johnson and Behrens retain the distinction between assumed-fact legal review and factual-sufficiency disputes.
- Marks supplies no unified Fifth Amendment rationale for the two different judgment-supporting grounds.

#### Law After Decision

The Fifth Amendment damages theory in this case fails under a binding majority judgment without a common rationale. Verdugo-Urquidez supplies no binding answer to the distinct interrogation question. Independently supported executive-abuse claims require their own contextual merits and objective conduct-date immunity analysis; absence of prosecution does not dispose of them. The Court's due-process instruction preserves both lawful immunity and continuation of a supported claim, depending on that analysis.

#### Separate Writings

**Souter and Breyer, lead Part II.** The existing system protects the privilege through prospective assertion, adequate use and derivative-use immunity, protection against penalties for refusing unprotected disclosures, and exclusion. An additional extraction-based damages protection requires a demonstrated need and a limiting principle sufficient to justify its costs. Martinez has not established that need here. They reject a categorical claim that the privilege operates only at trial. They expressly leave open whether absence of Miranda warnings can support a section 1983 action in any circumstances; this actual-compulsion case does not present that warning-only question.

**Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** O'Connor and Scalia join Part A: the criminal-case condition has not occurred, so the asserted completed Self-Incrimination Clause violation fails. The privilege may still be asserted prospectively, compelled testimony requires sufficient use protection, and government may not demand surrender of immunity as a condition of public employment or contracting. McKune's automatic-penalty holding remains applicable within its scope. The precise commencement of a criminal case need not be fixed where none occurred. In Part B1, joined by Scalia, Thomas would reject the substantive-due-process claim on the attributed interrogation conduct: personal culpability and a justified constitutional ground are required, and evidentiary coercion alone cannot establish the extension sought. In Part B2, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, he independently would grant conduct-date immunity. Rochin and Mincey establish serious investigative limits, but their exclusion settings and Cooper's materially different planned interrogation did not sufficiently settle this hospital-questioning damages application in November 1997. O'Connor's agreement rests only on that personal defense and does not declare the conduct constitutional. Neither due-process ground demands additional physical injury in every case.

**Kennedy, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Stevens joins all three parts; Ginsburg joins Parts II and III. Part I distinguishes mere failure to give warnings, for which Kennedy and Stevens accept ordinary exclusion as the protection, from actual coercion. Part II treats severe official compulsion of incriminating testimony as an immediate invasion of the privilege; Kastigar and the penalty cases protect the choice before prosecution, and Verdugo-Urquidez decided a different issue. Part III permits legitimate questioning of a person in pain, including urgent efforts and voluntary accounts, but finds intentional exploitation of suffering to extract testimony constitutionally different. On the supported plaintiff-favorable exploitation account, the preconduct investigative and coercion authorities defeat the asserted legal defense. He joins the Court's remand while retaining that stronger application. He makes no new finding that Chavez threatened to delay treatment.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** The supported brutal interrogation immediately invaded protected liberty. Miranda's constitutional status, the immunity and penalty cases, and Mincey's treatment of abusive hospital questioning support a distinction between actual compulsion and a warning omission. Rochin, Mincey and Cooper supply the pertinent preconduct warning; his agreement with the remand preserves his conclusion that the asserted legal defense should fail on that account.

**Ginsburg, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** Helplessness, extreme distress, repeated pleas and persistent questioning together support actual unconstitutional compulsion and due-process abuse. Her conclusion does not depend on a finding that Martinez believed treatment would be withheld or that Chavez intended that impression. She joins Kennedy's actual-compulsion and due-process parts, but not his warning-only part; she adopts no categorical warning-remedy bar. She would reject the asserted legal defense on that totality while joining the independent remand.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He would recognize a completed Self-Incrimination Clause violation when an officer deliberately overbears a person's will to obtain self-incriminating testimonial answers in a criminal investigation, whether or not prosecution follows. Martinez would prove the coercive act and testimonial and incriminating character by a preponderance. Unwanted questioning, warning omission, or vulnerability unexploited by the officer would not suffice. Dickerson's constitutional safeguards and McKune's before-use protection support that rule; Mincey's exclusion remedy remains distinct. Compensatory loss requires separate causal proof; otherwise available nominal relief remains possible after a proved violation and subject to the personal defense and preserved claim.

He also would replace Harlow and Anderson's objective-uncertainty defense with protection for proved genuine and objectively reasonable reliance on identified authoritative law directly authorizing the conduct. The officer would bear the preponderance burden on disputed reliance facts, while the court determines the authority's legal content; silence, general investigative power and another official's assurance would not suffice. That proposal would overrule Denius's contrary immunity rule and displace Saucier, Elder and Hope only to the extent they retain that defense, preserving their distinct substantive and procedural rules. His proposed remand would afford Chavez an opportunity to prove actual reliance for the earlier conduct. Under the existing objective defense, he joins the Court's due-process remand and its November 1997 notice inquiry. He would also remand the Fifth Amendment theory for the actual coercion, personal causation, defense and injury determinations.

### Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.

Plenary decision, May 27, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On plenary review of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit's en banc judgment, 312 F.3d 677. The question is whether CERCLA §113(f)(1) permits contribution when the claimant has not been sued under §106 or §107(a).

The en banc court reversed the district court's dismissal of the federal contribution claim and remanded. The district court had dismissed that claim without prejudice and declined supplemental jurisdiction over the state claims. Cooper sold contaminated aircraft-engine maintenance properties to Aviall; both parties concede responsible-party status and contributions to contamination. Aviall cleaned up under Texas regulatory pressure, but no §106 or §107(a) civil action was brought against it. A qualifying governmental settlement is not established as the basis of this claim. The parties dispute the contribution gateway; independent cost recovery, statutory cost predicates and allocation remain distinct.

#### Judgment

**Reversed in the reviewed §113(f)(1) ruling and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

The reversal is confined to the statutory contribution theory decided below. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment while retaining the distinct construction explained in his separate writing.

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** CERCLA §113(f)(1) authorizes contribution during or following a civil action under §106 or §107(a); a claimant that has not been subject to either action cannot maintain this statutory contribution claim. The subsection's final sentence preserves independently existing contribution rights but does not create an unrestricted action under its opening sentence. Equitable allocation follows an available claim and cannot replace the statutory condition.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court controls the statutory construction and confined reversal. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on the basis stated separately.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that permission to sue during or following identified litigation gives effect to both the permissive verb and the qualifying circumstance. Reading the permission to authorize the same action before either event would deprive the condition of its work. The last sentence also has work to do: it prevents this subsection from diminishing contribution rights arising independently. Its reference to §107 is distinct from the opening sentence's reference to §107(a). Section 113(f)(3)(B)'s separate route for a person who has resolved liability to the United States or a State in a qualifying administrative or judicially approved settlement, and §113(g)(3)'s judgment and settlement limitation triggers, reinforce attention to each enacted route.

Aviall's cleanup under Texas regulatory pressure does not supply the missing §106 or §107(a) civil action. The competing reading invokes the savings sentence and incentives to clean up promptly. Preserving independent rights answers the former without erasing the express grant's condition; policy cannot supply an action the provision does not authorize. Texas Industries and Northwest Airlines require an actual source for contribution, although their different statutes do not decide CERCLA's meaning. Key Tronic preserves private recovery for proved qualifying response work and distinguishes fees and liability-position negotiations; it did not decide this contribution gateway. Bestfoods addresses direct and derivative responsibility, which likewise does not establish the statutory right to allocate costs. These parties' conceded responsibility cannot substitute for the missing predicate.

The Court resolves the presented §113(f)(1) question. The independent §107 theory's preservation and legal reach require their own adjudication; that separate statutory entitlement is outside the granted gateway question and the necessary preservation determination was not made below.

**Precedent treatment:** Texas Industries and Northwest Airlines are applied for the need to identify enacted contribution authority, with their statutory settings distinguished. Key Tronic's response-cost and fee holdings and Bestfoods's responsibility holdings retain their force without extension into an unrestricted §113 action.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Texas Industries, Inc. v. Radcliff Materials, Inc. and Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. Transport Workers Union supply the requirement of a legal source for contribution; CERCLA's own text determines this distinct statutory claim.
- Key Tronic Corp. v. United States retains its task-specific response-cost recovery, private litigation-fee exclusion and liability-negotiation distinction. Those holdings do not resolve every responsible-party claim under §107 or the contribution gateway decided here.
- United States v. Bestfoods retains its separation of direct pollution-related operation from derivative corporate responsibility. Responsibility and an available contribution action remain different statutory questions.

#### Law After Decision

Section 113(f)(1) contribution requires a prior or pending §106 or §107(a) civil action. The preservation sentence leaves independently existing rights intact, and the separate qualifying-settlement provision retains its own predicates. Voluntary cleanup and state regulatory pressure do not substitute for the civil action this subsection requires. Actual independent response-cost and state-law claims must be evaluated under their own preserved grounds and requirements.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment.** He would read §113(f)(1)'s permission together with its preservation sentence to allow contribution for otherwise qualifying cleanup costs before federal enforcement. On that reading, the named actions are assured occasions for contribution rather than exclusive prerequisites. He recognizes the contrary construction's substantial textual force but finds the whole provision's structure decisive; prompt cleanup confirms rather than replaces the textual argument. Texas Industries and Northwest Airlines refused judicially created contribution under different statutes and do not answer this express contribution-and-preservation language.

His proposed rule would still require Aviall to prove recoverable response costs and applicable statutory predicates by a preponderance before equitable allocation. Conceded responsibility establishes neither necessary expenditure nor National Contingency Plan compliance. Key Tronic requires task-specific treatment: ordinary private litigation fees and work protecting the claimant's own settlement position are excluded, while independently necessary identification of responsible polluters may qualify on proof of its response function, necessity, plan consistency and amount, with mixed tasks separated and duplicate recovery avoided. Bestfoods preserves the distinct responsibility rules. Notice, public participation, costs, allocation and preserved contractual or state claims would require their actual adjudication.

Under the Court's exclusive opening-sentence construction, he agrees to reverse only the §113(f)(1) theory and remand for determination of any preserved independent §107 claim and proper treatment of state claims. That agreement does not adopt the Court's statutory rationale or deem all cleanup expenditures unrecoverable.

### Duncan v. General Motors Corp.

Plenary merits decision, May 27, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

The Court reviews the Eighth Circuit's August 22, 2002 judgment requiring Rule 50 judgment for GM on hostile environment and constructive discharge and vacating dependent fees. The questions are whether the whole record supports the hostile-environment verdict and whether the actual corrective opportunities defeated a finding of compelled resignation. Duncan worked as a College-placed technical training clerk at GM from August 1994 until May 1997. The jury awarded $700,000 in harassment emotional-distress damages, $300,000 in discharge emotional-distress damages and $4,600 backpay on a mixed Title VII and Missouri action. The court below assumed GM's statutory-employer status and bypassed remaining grounds after resolving sufficiency.

#### Judgment

| Hostile-environment sufficiency | Reversed and remanded, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | The supported jury findings survive the sufficiency challenge decided here. |
| Constructive-discharge sufficiency | Affirmed, 6–3 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Breyer | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Ginsburg | The judgment for GM on this component remains effective. |

#### Opinion Topology

KENNEDY, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. SCALIA, J., and STONE-ZSELA, C. J., each filed an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part. The joins differ by part as set out below.

| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | O'Connor, Souter, Breyer in full; Stone-Zsela, Stevens and Ginsburg as to Parts I and III; Scalia and Thomas as to Part II | Reverses harassment sufficiency, affirms discharge sufficiency and remands remaining matters | Part I: harassment; Part II: compelled resignation; Part III: independent grounds and relief |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The whole record supports the hostile-environment verdict

**Holding and operative rule:** A hostile-environment verdict may not be set aside under Rule 50 when the supported record as a whole permits a reasonable jury to find, by a preponderance, objectively and subjectively severe or pervasive abuse because of sex. The reviewing court must draw reasonable inferences for the verdict winner, without choosing credibility or weighing competing evidence; it must consider the entire record, disregarding favorable-to-movant evidence the jury could reject while crediting uncontradicted and unimpeached evidence the jury was required to believe.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins support the rule, application and reversal of this sufficiency judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Harris requires a contextual assessment of all circumstances and imposes no psychological-injury, physical-threat or lost-productivity prerequisite. Oncale preserves the separate requirement that the abusive conditions occur because of sex. Reeves protects a reasonable whole-record inference while retaining judicial authority to reject legally insufficient verdicts.

The rejected proposition, unnecessary touches, sexualized work assignments and objects, public sex-directed humiliation and final sexist typing demand can reasonably be understood as a cumulative course affecting Duncan's working conditions. The assignments and training relationship connect the conduct to employment; the jury could find more than unrelated offensive remarks. Some incidents were separated in time, the original proposition ceased, and ordinary criticism also affected men. Those circumstances support GM's reading but do not compel it. The personally directed and sexualized tasks permit the causal and severity inference despite the contrary evidence.

DeAngelis's materially different newsletter record supplies neither a minimum catalogue of acts nor a rule that intermittent conduct cannot become an abusive employment relationship. The contrary view accepts the whole course but finds it merely offensive. The reasonable cumulative inference from these work-connected acts belongs to the jury under Reeves; appellate preference for GM's evaluation cannot replace it.

##### II. The actual corrective alternatives defeat objectively compelled resignation here

**Holding and operative rule:** Constructive discharge requires conditions objectively so intolerable that a reasonable employee would feel compelled to resign; realistic corrective opportunities bear on whether resignation was compelled, rather than operating as a categorical exhaustion prerequisite. An employee need not remain in danger, endure a futile procedure or wait indefinitely, and the announcement of an investigation alone is not a safe harbor; on this record the concrete response, unfinished agreed account and offered alternative location together preclude a reasonable finding of compelled resignation.

**Authority:** Part II of the Court's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Breyer; six direct joins support the objective-compulsion rule, its actual-alternatives application and affirmance of this sufficiency judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** Title VII's protection against an abusive environment and a claim that the employer compelled resignation require distinct showings. Harris and Oncale supply the environment and causation rules; Faragher and Ellerth preserve incurred harassment injury while keeping attribution and particular remedies distinct. The objective-compulsion component of the constructive-discharge claim requires attention to what practical choices were actually available when Duncan resigned.

After her April complaint reached responsible GM management, the personnel director removed the planter and began an EEO inquiry. Duncan agreed to provide a written account but resigned before doing so, and the College offered another location. Taken together, those supported opportunities distinguish this case from a paper policy, danger, futile proceedings or indefinite inaction. The earlier complaints and renewed May demand remain relevant. The dissent would allow the jury to infer from that course that the available steps could not make continued employment tolerable. The Court concludes that the concrete response and location alternative, with the agreed account still outstanding, prevent that inference from reasonably establishing compulsion at resignation.

##### III. The surviving harassment verdict returns for independent grounds and claim-specific relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Reversal of the hostile-environment sufficiency judgment preserves the jury's supported findings against renewed rejection on the same ground, while permitting adjudication of genuinely preserved independent coverage, attribution, trial-error and remedial issues. Under the actual-authority route, Duncan must prove by a preponderance that delegated supervisory power materially aided the harassment; title or a training role alone is insufficient, and preserved negligence theories retain their distinct requirements.

**Authority:** Part III of the Court's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; seven direct joins govern the remand of the harassment component. The discharge affirmance rests on the distinct six-Justice coalition stated in Part II.

**Controlling explanation:** Reeves protects a verdict supported under the correct sufficiency standard while allowing genuinely independent appellate grounds. Faragher and Ellerth require an actual connection between delegated authority and the hostile environment and reject a policy's existence or nonreporting alone as a complete defense. Those rules govern the attribution issue that the circuit bypassed. GM's statutory-employer status likewise was assumed, rather than decided.

The separate emotional-distress allocations help identify the two verdict components, but the mixed federal and Missouri judgment still requires lawful coverage, causation, trial and remedy analysis. A sustainable harassment recovery must reflect harassment injury, while the affirmed discharge ruling prevents restoration of resignation damages under a different label. The preserved independent grounds must be decided consistently with the sufficiency conclusion now settled. The full mixed award Duncan seeks is therefore not automatically restored.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Harris v. Forklift Systems (November 9, 1993) is applied for the objective and subjective severe-or-pervasive totality inquiry, without a diagnosis, threat or productivity-loss prerequisite.
- Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services (March 19, 1997) is applied for the distinct because-of-sex requirement and workplace context.
- Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products (June 12, 2000) is applied for whole-record Rule 50 review, protection of reasonable jury inferences and preservation of genuine independent remand grounds.
- DeAngelis v. El Paso Municipal Police Officers' Association (January 6, 1997) is distinguished on its newsletter record; its authority for lawful sufficiency review remains.
- Faragher v. City of Boca Raton (September 8, 1997) is applied for actual delegated authority materially aiding harassment and for the distinction between incurred injury and subsequent response.
- Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth (June 26, 1998) is applied for the plaintiff-proved actual-authority connection and the insufficiency of policy knowledge or nonreporting alone as a complete defense.

#### Law After Decision

The existing harassment totality and whole-record verdict standards protect a reasonable inference from the cumulative work-connected course shown here. The Court separately establishes that actual corrective opportunities bear on objectively compelled resignation, with danger, futility and indefinite delay distinguished from the concrete alternatives in this case. Employer coverage, attribution, independent trial grounds and lawful remedies retain their separate requirements; the harassment and discharge components have different controlling dispositions.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas.** They join Part II and would affirm the hostile-environment sufficiency judgment as well. Harris and DeAngelis preserve an objective boundary between offense and altered employment conditions, and Reeves permits judgment when the credited whole record falls short. They consider the sexualized work tasks and humiliation with all the other evidence, but regard the ceased proposition, limited touches and intermittent material across this employment period as insufficiently severe or pervasive. Their conclusion adds no physical-threat or productivity-loss element. The absence of sufficient hostility also defeats the greater claimed compulsion to resign in their view; the actual response and transfer opportunity provide the independent ground they join in Part II.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and dissenting in part; Stevens and Ginsburg join Part A only.** In Part A, they join Parts I and III of the Court's opinion but would also reverse the discharge sufficiency ruling. They accept that constructive discharge requires objectively compelled resignation and that realistic corrective opportunities matter. Repeated complaints, temporary improvement, renewed work-directed abuse and the final sexist demand permit a reasonable jury to doubt whether the proposed inquiry and another location supplied a practical cure. The actual GM response, Duncan's unfinished promised account and the offered placement favor GM, but their adequacy in the continuing employment relationship remains contestable. Faragher and Ellerth prevent later correction from erasing incurred conditions; Reeves leaves this reasonable competing inference to the jury. They would preserve the discharge findings subject to the same genuine independent grounds.

In Part B, Stone-Zsela would adopt a complete rule requiring proof by a preponderance that employer-connected conditions were objectively intolerable enough to compel a reasonable employee to resign and that resignation was intended or reasonably foreseeable. Realistic corrective opportunities inform that inquiry without becoming an exhaustion requirement; neither an announced investigation nor failure to finish it conclusively decides the claim. Attribution retains the actual-authority/material-aid requirements. A supported constructive-discharge recovery would require distinct causal proof of resignation injury.

### Lerman v. Board of Elections in the City of New York

Plenary merits decision, May 27, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

The Court reviews the Second Circuit's November 9, 2000 reversal of the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and its direction to enter judgment consistent with facial invalidation of New York's district-residence condition for petition witnesses. Lerman, an otherwise qualified in-state witness residing in the neighboring district, witnessed fifty-eight signatures that were rejected solely for that residence; the candidate needed thirty-eight. The question concerns the First and Fourteenth Amendment validity of that condition. The September 1999 primary has passed, but the accepted pleadings establish a reasonable expectation of repeat exposure to the continuing condition within election cycles too short for full review.

#### Judgment

**Affirmed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

STONE-ZSELA, C. J., delivered the opinion of the Court and filed a concurring opinion. THOMAS, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stone-Zsela | Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports affirmance and remand | Entire opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Thomas | No other Justice | Same invalidation and remedy | Traditional strict-scrutiny ground; no join to the Court's generalized methodology |
| Concurrence | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins the Court's opinion and judgment | Additional effective-cure remedial presumption and implementation position |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** New York violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments by excluding otherwise qualified in-state petition witnesses solely because they reside outside the candidate's electoral district. Under Anderson–Burdick, plaintiffs must establish the character and magnitude of the burden; this direct exclusion severely burdens protected petition circulation, and the State must show narrow tailoring to a compelling interest, which the asserted accountability, fraud-prevention, local-support and administrative interests do not establish for this condition.

**Authority:** The eight-Justice opinion of the Court controls the method, application and condition-specific facial relief. Thomas supports the same judgment on his separate traditional strict-scrutiny ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Meyer protects petition circulation as interactive political expression. Buckley applies that protection to the exclusion of otherwise qualified speakers and tests traceability against retained enforcement tools. Anderson and Burdick provide the governing inquiry: examine the actual burden in the complete system and the precise interests said to require it. That inquiry preserves fitted election administration while demanding strict scrutiny here, where a district boundary directly prevents a willing speaker from performing the witnessing activity.

Lerman's fifty-eight signatures were rejected solely for her address, although the candidate needed thirty-eight. The availability of other speech and professional witnesses does not restore her excluded petition activity. Qualified district signers establish local electoral support; the witness need not live among them to attest their signatures. Statewide subpoenas and supplied addresses already make this in-state witness accountable. The notary and commissioner exception further undercuts the claimed necessity of district residence, although an exception alone would not decide constitutionality. The candidate context does not turn this expressive activity into mere ballot mechanics. Cartwright's fitted support requirements concern a different function.

These defects operate across the challenged condition, supporting relief against that separable exclusion rather than only these fifty-eight signatures. The Court extends Buckley's circulation reasoning to this geographic exclusion.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, is applied for the actual-burden, precise-interest and necessity inquiry.
- Burdick v. Takushi (June 8, 1992) is applied for graduated scrutiny; severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests.
- Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, is applied to petition circulation as protected interactive political speech.
- Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation (January 12, 1999) is extended from its reviewed speaker exclusions to this unsupported district-residence condition.
- Cartwright v. Barnes (February 25, 2003) is distinguished: local-support mechanics concern signers' demonstrated electoral support, not exclusion of an otherwise qualified witnessing speaker.

#### Law After Decision

Otherwise qualified in-state witnesses may participate across electoral-district boundaries. The invalid condition may not be enforced directly or replaced by a nominally different rule producing the same unsupported district exclusion.

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** A direct restriction on political petition speech receives traditional strict scrutiny. His Buckley concurrence supplies that method; candidate petitioning remains protected communication even though the State may regulate genuine support mechanics. Witness residence does not measure local electoral support, which qualified signers provide, and statewide process and witness addresses secure accountability. The professional-witness alternative does not restore the excluded speakers' participation. He therefore agrees with facial invalidation and the same remedy, while withholding assent to a general method that calibrates scrutiny through an administrative burden balance.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the Court's constitutional holding and facial remedy. He would additionally presume relief against an established common eligibility barrier for the affected group unless the election defendants prove that a narrower order fully cures the violation. The presumption depends on a live claim and the proved barrier and reaches only the actual defendants and injuries within the court's jurisdiction; it does not assume class certification. The decree should identify the forbidden condition and require conforming instructions and review criteria before it is used again. Rejections resting only on district residence must be withdrawn under the decree, while genuine signature defects receive separate adjudication.

#### Source Notes

Lerman v. Board of Elections, 232 F.3d 135; N.Y. Election Law §§6-132(2)–(3), 6-136 and 3-218(1), in the versions governing the challenged action. The pleaded facts control review of the dismissal. Notaries public and commissioners of deeds were exempt from the challenged district-residence condition.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The decree must bar responsible election officials from rejecting otherwise valid petition witnessing solely because an otherwise qualified in-state witness lives outside the electoral district. The lower court must specify that condition and require conforming petition instructions and review practices before its next application. Rejections resting solely on the invalid condition must be withdrawn under the decree; genuine independent signature defects must be separately identified and adjudicated. Prospective relief remains tied to the established continuing claim and the officials subject to the court's jurisdiction.

The case returns for entry and implementation of the conforming declaratory and prospective decree.

| Dissent | Breyer | O'Connor | Would affirm | Finds standing and ripeness and independently construes the CDA to sustain the challenged classification. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** A facial challenge to a nonbinding agency view is unripe when concrete contractual application will materially clarify the dispute, the view presently changes no legal obligation or review right, and effective later review remains available. The Park Service's statement that concession contracts fall outside the Contract Disputes Act has those characteristics; bidding uncertainty alone does not establish sufficient present hardship on this record. The reviewed merits judgment must be vacated and this challenge dismissed without prejudice.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion of the Court controls the complete ripeness rule, its application and its limited disposition. Stevens supplies an additional judgment vote on standing alone.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court applies Abbott Laboratories' fitness-and-hardship inquiry. The Park Service lacks delegated authority to administer the CDA or determine its tribunals' statutory jurisdiction. Its challenged statement is a general policy statement on that question, not an exercise of legislative CDA power. Concessioners remain able to invoke the Act's procedures when an actual contract dispute arises. The statement therefore creates no presently compulsory change in primary conduct or loss of the independent review route.

The association correctly identifies a final agency action and a predominantly legal issue. Neither alone makes the dispute ripe. Particular contractual obligations can illuminate whether an agreement procures covered property, services, construction, alteration, repair or maintenance, or involves a covered disposal. The strongest contrary argument is that dispute-resolution uncertainty affects bids before a breach occurs. Here the nonbinding agency position does not alter the available legal remedies, while concrete review can examine genuine procurement features. That uncertainty differs from Abbott Laboratories' immediate compliance and sanction consequences. Toilet Goods distinguishes completed agency action from present readiness for review, and Ohio Forestry preserves later implementation review when effective. Lee v. Harcleroad allows review of a presently burdensome civil duty without a criminal penalty; the missing premise here is a comparable operative burden, not a criminal sanction. MCI and Smiley require actual delegated authority, which the Service concedes it lacks over the CDA.

The threshold failure prevents resolution of ultimate CDA coverage in this facial case.

**Precedent treatment:** Abbott Laboratories and Toilet Goods supply fitness, hardship and the actual-effect distinction. Ohio Forestry is applied to effective later review; Lee preserves review of supported current civil burdens. MCI and Smiley preserve delegation limits without supplying a merits construction of concession-contract coverage.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner supplies the fitness-and-hardship inquiry and the comparison to immediate practical legal consequences.
- Toilet Goods Association, Inc. v. Gardner distinguishes final agency action from a dispute improved by concrete application.
- Ohio Forestry Association, Inc. v. Sierra Club is applied where effective later review remains and adequate present hardship is absent, preserving review of genuinely binding implementation.
- Lee v. Harcleroad retains review of a supported presently burdensome civil obligation; a criminal penalty is not essential to injury or hardship.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T Co. and Smiley v. Citibank preserve the requirement of actual delegated authority. The Service's concession-management powers do not confer authority to bind CDA adjudicators.

#### Law After Decision

The challenged nonbinding CDA statement cannot sustain this facial merits adjudication before concrete contractual application. Concessioners retain the statutory review route in actual disputes, subject to its ordinary conditions. The Court leaves ultimate concession-contract coverage open because this case fails the antecedent ripeness requirement. The dismissal carries no merits bar to a later ripe CDA claim.

#### Separate Writings

**Stevens, concurring in the judgment.** Article III requires a concrete injury to the association or a member, causally connected to the challenged action and redressable by the requested relief. Allen v. Wright, Sierra Club v. Morton and Whitmore supply that requirement. The association has not identified a particular injured member, specific changed bid or comparable loss. The importance of knowing the applicable procedures does not itself establish that injury. He does not reject bidding injuries categorically. He disagrees with the Court on fitness: the Service's definite categorical interpretation presents an ordinary statutory question ready for judicial construction, and marginal applications do not necessarily make it premature. If sufficient injury were established, he would treat the issue as ripe. His dismissal ground is standing alone.

**Breyer, dissenting, joined by O'Connor.** The present and threatened monetary consequences for current and prospective concessioners establish standing and sufficient hardship. Contract procedures affect implementation costs and bids; a later ruling may not repair prices fixed under present uncertainty. Hunt permits the association to proceed for members with qualifying injury. A formal, definite interpretation is fit for statutory construction, even though lack of CDA authority reduces the weight of the Service's view. Ohio Forestry concerned more contingent implementation. Congress's recognition of prospective-bidder review under the Tucker Act confirms the reality of such procurement injury; this analogy does not enlarge the grant to the excluded prospectus claims.

They would affirm on independent statutory construction. The CDA's enumerated procurement categories do not cover every agreement with the Government. A concession authorizing a private enterprise to provide services to park visitors is not government procurement merely because the Government benefits or receives franchise compensation. The 1998 concession statute's visitor-service structure supports that distinction. An approach assuming coverage absent an express exclusion, or equating every governmental benefit with procurement, exceeds the statutory categories. Their merits position does not rest on controlling deference to the Service's CDA view.

### Beneficial National Bank v. Anderson, No. 02-306

Merits decision, June 2, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 30, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, reviewing its certified interlocutory reversal of the Middle District of Alabama's refusal to remand. The question is whether the pleaded national-bank usury claim arises under federal law because §§85 and 86 supply the exclusive substantive rule and cause of action.

Twenty-six taxpayers obtained short-term loans from Beneficial National Bank secured by anticipated tax refunds. They sued the bank and two loan arrangers in Alabama state court, seeking compensatory and punitive damages under several state-law theories, including usury; the complaint cited no federal law. Defendants removed, and the district court sustained removal on complete-preemption grounds. The bank's national charter and the usury character of the presented overcharge claim are undisputed. The bank and arrangers seek to retain the federal forum; the borrowers seek remand.

#### Judgment

**Reversed and remanded, 7–2.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer.
**Opposing:** Scalia and Thomas.

#### Opinion Topology

SOUTER, J., delivered the opinion of the Court. STONE-ZSELA, C. J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment. SCALIA, J., filed a dissenting opinion.

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer | Supports reversal and remand | Entire exclusive-cause jurisdictional rule, application and remand |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Supports reversal and remand under the Court's governing rule | Preserves a distinct proposed forum-design requirement; does not join the lead's inference from exclusivity |
| Dissent | Scalia | Thomas | Would affirm the forum judgment | Rejects conversion of the state-pleaded usury claim into a federal claim for removal |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

**Holding and operative rule:** When a claim seeks relief for usurious interest charged by a national bank, §§85 and 86 supply the exclusive federal substantive usury rule and cause of action; state-law labels do not change that claim's federal character. The exclusive federal cause supplies original jurisdiction under §1331 and permits removal under §1441, without a separate enactment specifically directing removal of this cause, subject to the governing removal requirements and any applicable express statutory exception.

**Authority:** The six-Justice opinion of the Court controls the rule, its application and the remand. Stone-Zsela agrees with the judgment on his separate stated basis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the pleaded usury injury has a federal cause of action, rather than merely a federal defense. Dearing, Barnet, Haseltine and Evans establish that the National Bank Act supplies the exclusive usury action and remedy against a national bank. Section 85 defines the allowed interest within its enacted conditions. Section 86 addresses knowingly taking, receiving, reserving or charging interest above that allowance, forfeiture of the entire interest the debt carries or the parties agreed to pay, and, where excessive interest has been paid, recovery by the payer or legal representative of twice the paid interest in an action begun within two years of the usurious transaction.

Rivet distinguishes a federal defense from Congress's replacement of a state claim with an exclusive federal cause. Avco and Metropolitan Life recognize that such a replacement gives the action its federal character. Franchise Tax Board preserves the complaint rule for claims that remain state causes; it does not require treating an exclusively federal action as state law because the pleading calls it that. The dissent would confine complete preemption to the established labor and ERISA settings, and the concurrence would require an additional demonstration of forum-converting design. But the legally exclusive cause supplies the original-jurisdiction anchor; §1441 then supplies the removal authorization. State-court competence to adjudicate federal rights is consistent with that option.

Syngenta requires an actual original-jurisdiction source, which is present here. Breuer separates that source from an express exception to removal. Neither national banking's importance nor the anticipated success of a defense substitutes for the enacted cause. The claims against this undisputed national bank seek the overcharge injury governed by §86, so the contrary forum ruling must be reversed. The interest's legality, statutory recovery and independent claims lie outside this interlocutory jurisdictional question.

**Precedent treatment:** Rivet's exclusive-cause distinction is applied to the National Bank Act; the established Avco and Metropolitan Life category extends to this exclusive action. Dearing and its related banking decisions supply remedial exclusivity; Smiley's substantive classification and compatible-law limits retain their force.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Farmers' and Mechanics' National Bank v. Dearing, 91 U.S. 29: applies the exclusivity of the federal national-bank usury remedy.
- Barnet v. National Bank, 98 U.S. 555: applies the federal remedy's exclusive operation for the usury injury.
- Haseltine v. Central Bank of Springfield, 183 U.S. 132: applies the separate federal remedy for interest paid and its statutory conditions.
- Evans v. National Bank of Savannah, 251 U.S. 108: applies the exclusive federal usury scheme, whose substantive and remedial conditions remain operative.
- Avco Corp. v. Machinists, 390 U.S. 557: extends its exclusive-federal-cause jurisdictional principle to the national-bank usury action.
- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Taylor, 481 U.S. 58: applies the distinction between complete replacement by a federal cause and ordinary defensive preemption.
- Franchise Tax Board v. Construction Laborers Vacation Trust, 463 U.S. 1: preserves the ordinary well-pleaded-complaint rule while recognizing the exclusive-federal-cause setting.
- Rivet v. Regions Bank of Louisiana, February 24, 1998: applies its distinction between statutory replacement and a federal preclusion defense; the latter remains insufficient for removal.
- Syngenta Crop Protection, Inc. v. Henson, November 5, 2002: applies the independent-original-jurisdiction requirement, satisfied here by the exclusive statutory cause.
- Breuer v. Jim's Concrete of Brevard, Inc., May 19, 2003: preserves separate examination of original jurisdiction and an express removal exception.
- Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota), N.A., June 3, 1996: preserves the federal interest classification, §85's actual rate conditions, §86's separate remedy predicates and compatible state duties.

#### Law After Decision

A claim seeking relief for usury charged by a national bank has the federal character of §86's exclusive cause and is removable under the general federal-question removal provisions. The distinction between that replacement action and a merely defeating federal defense remains controlling. Compatible formation, disclosure, contract and consumer-protection duties retain their own force; the national charter does not convert every loan dispute or every arranger claim into a federal banking cause.

#### Separate Writings

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in the judgment.** He would require Congress's statutory design to establish forum conversion in addition to an exclusive substantive remedy. Rivet's distinction between a defense and an exclusive replacement cause favors the bank, but in his view does not eliminate that further jurisdictional inquiry. Avco and Metropolitan Life rest on their demonstrated jurisdictional foundations; Dearing and Smiley establish federal substantive supremacy without alone deciding the forum. Syngenta requires an actual jurisdictional source rather than the importance of federal enforcement. State courts can give the federal usury restrictions their controlling effect, and uniformity cannot itself supply jurisdiction.

The Court holds that exclusive §86 replacement is sufficient. Applying that governing rule, he agrees that the actual usury claim may be removed and that the remaining jurisdictional consequences require separate adjudication. His agreement neither adopts the inference he rejects nor confers merits immunity or automatic dismissal of compatible state claims.

**Scalia, dissenting, joined by Thomas.** The well-pleaded-complaint rule ordinarily follows the claim the plaintiff advances. A federal defense can defeat a state cause without turning it into a pleaded federal action. The dissent retains Avco and Metropolitan Life under stare decisis, treating ERISA's close statutory parallel as adoption of the established labor-law approach, but finds no equivalent congressional instruction in the National Bank Act. Rivet's replacement language does not answer that additional step. Neither fear of state-court error nor a preference for uniform banking adjudication creates jurisdiction. The dissent would affirm the forum judgment and leave the state court to apply the governing federal defense, including the federal remedy's exclusivity.

#### Source Notes

No. 02-306; 539 U.S. 1; Anderson v. H & R Block, Inc., 287 F.3d 1038 (11th Cir. 2002); 12 U.S.C. §§85–86; 28 U.S.C. §§1292(b), 1331 and 1441. The jurisdictional issue arises before a merits adjudication; national-bank status and the usury character of the presented overcharge claim are undisputed.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The actual national-bank usury claim supplies federal-question jurisdiction and supports removal. The interlocutory direction requiring return to state court on the rejected jurisdictional ground is set aside.

The action returns through the Eleventh Circuit for proceedings in federal district court. That court must address any remaining jurisdictional issue, determine the status of independent claims and the nonbank arrangers under the actual original- and supplemental-jurisdiction rules, and adjudicate the surviving claims under their governing law. For the federal usury claim, the actual charges, allowed interest, knowing excess, payment, limitations and requested remedy must be determined under §§85 and 86. State-law pleading labels alone do not require a merits dismissal; any necessary clarification proceeds under ordinary pleading rules.

**End of entry: Beneficial National Bank v. Anderson, Merits decision, June 2, 2003.**

### Dastar Corp. v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp., No. 02-428

Merits decision, June 2, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 2, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit. The questions concern liability under Lanham Act §43(a) for uncredited copying of public-domain expression without the requisite source confusion, and whether §1117(a) permits doubling a profits award for deterrence if federal liability exists. The underlying book-copyright claim is outside the grant.

Fox arranged production of the television series Crusade in Europe based on Eisenhower's book. The series' copyright expired in 1977. After respondents began distributing restored videos, Dastar acquired copies of the original series, edited and shortened them, added material and packaging, and manufactured its own World War II Campaigns in Europe sets in 1995. Its credits identified its own production operation and omitted the earlier series. Dastar did not relabel respondents' finished tapes.

The Ninth Circuit affirmed §43(a)(1)(A) liability and twice Dastar's profits on the theory that substantial bodily appropriation without attribution established reverse passing off. It separately reversed summary judgment on the book-copyright claim and remanded it. The district court's state unfair-competition ruling depended on the federal ruling; the appellate court did not separately decide that claim. Dastar seeks reversal of the federal liability and award, while respondents seek to retain both.

Breyer took no part in the consideration or decision; no reason is publicly stated.

|---|---|---|---|
| §43(a)(1)(A) omitted-authorship liability | Reversed, 8–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | The bodily-appropriation theory cannot sustain liability for origin of these goods. |
| Profits based on that federal liability, including doubling | Vacated, 8–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Both the base profits and the enhancement lose their legal basis. |
| Dependent state-law treatment and remaining proceedings | Remanded, 8–0 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg | Reconsider the state-law treatment to the extent dependent on the rejected federal ground; the existing book-copyright remand remains effective. |

**Not participating:** Breyer.

#### Opinion Topology

SCALIA, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which STONE-ZSELA, C. J., and STEVENS, O'CONNOR, KENNEDY, SOUTER, THOMAS, and GINSBURG, JJ., joined.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Origin of goods identifies the tangible producer

**Holding and operative rule:** For an origin-of-goods claim under §43(a)(1)(A), including a claim concerning communicative goods, origin means the producer of the tangible goods offered for sale, not the creators of expression incorporated in them. A claimant must establish false designation and the statute's required likelihood of confusion about a legally protected source, affiliation, connection, association, sponsorship or approval; uncredited copying alone does not supply those elements.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the eight participating Justices controls both holdings, their applications and the remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Dastar's manufacture of its own sets identifies it as their producer. Origin does not become a different statutory concept because a purchaser values a product for the expression it contains. Wal-Mart, Two Pesos and Qualitex protect qualifying source-identifying interests under their own distinct requirements. They do not establish ownership of every feature or expressive contribution. Sears, Compco and Bonito Boats prevent use of adjacent law to recreate exclusivity that the intellectual-property scheme withholds, while preserving legitimate protection against source deception.

Respondents argue that attribution would permit copying while preventing a copier from claiming the creative source as its own. But that reading turns the origin provision into an authorship-credit obligation with no comparable term and no clear stopping point among the authors and source materials contributing to a work. The Act's source function cannot carry that obligation. Dastar's identification of itself as producer was not false as to the tangible products it manufactured. Its copying of the public-domain series therefore does not establish the adjudicated origin claim.

Actual relabeling of another producer's finished goods and likely confusion concerning sponsorship or approval remain governed by their statutory elements. Section 43(a)(1)(B) separately addresses misrepresentations in commercial advertising or promotion about the nature, characteristics, qualities or geographic origin of goods, services or commercial activities. Such a claim requires its own pleading, proof and procedural basis; it is not a substitute adjudicated ground for this award. The book-copyright claim, including its work-for-hire and renewal issues, is outside the grant. The series' public-domain status does not resolve it.

**Precedent treatment:** Wal-Mart, Two Pesos and Qualitex supply the source-protection distinction. Sears, Compco and Bonito Boats' anti-recreation principle informs the rejection of a perpetual authorship duty without displacing genuine source protection or independent copyright rights.

##### II. The profits award falls with its sole federal basis

**Holding and operative rule:** Both base profits and any enhancement resting solely on the rejected origin theory must be removed. Recovery on another surviving claim requires adjudication of that claim's own elements and remedial authority.

**Authority:** The same unanimous opinion of the eight participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Wal-Mart requires relief dependent on a rejected Lanham Act theory to be separated from independently sustainable claims. The federal profits award here rests on the failed attribution ground. Disapproval of Dastar's copying and a desire to deter repetition cannot provide the missing cause of action. The question whether deterrence permits doubling an otherwise valid §1117(a) award lacks its necessary liability predicate and is left undecided for that reason. Section 1117(a)'s adjustment and compensation-versus-penalty requirements remain applicable when a valid claim supplies the predicate. The underlying book dispute and the dependent state-law treatment retain their distinct procedural positions.

**Precedent treatment:** Wal-Mart's separation of dependent relief and independent causes is applied to the full profits award, rather than merely its doubling increment.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc., March 22, 2000: applies the distinction between copying and the required statutory source interest, and its separation of dependent Lanham Act relief from independent copyright and state-law grounds. Its product-design distinctiveness rule remains at that scope.
- Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc., June 26, 1992: preserves qualifying nonfunctional restaurant trade dress under the separate distinctiveness and confusion requirements; it supplies no ownership of incorporated expression.
- Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co., March 28, 1995: applies its source-identification function and separate eligibility limits without extending protection to authorship as such.
- Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co., 376 U.S. 225: applies its distinction between protecting against source deception and recreating exclusive control over available subject matter.
- Compco Corp. v. Day-Brite Lighting, Inc., 376 U.S. 234: applies the same distinction to the proposed attribution obligation.
- Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc., 489 U.S. 141: extends its anti-recreation reasoning to the proposed perpetual authorship duty under the origin provision, preserving independent source and copyright claims.

#### Law After Decision

The goods-origin rule applies to communicative products as well as other tangible goods: a manufacturer's failure to credit creators of copied expression does not itself misidentify the producer of its goods. Genuine product-source and sponsorship deception remain subject to §43(a)(1)(A)'s elements; §43(a)(1)(B) retains its separate advertising predicates. Liability and recovery must rest on the actual statutory cause. The distinct valid-book-copyright inquiry remains governed by copyright law.

No. 02-428; 539 U.S. 23; 15 U.S.C. §§1125(a)(1)(A)–(B), 1117(a). The television series' copyright expired in 1977; the underlying book's work-for-hire and renewal questions remain in the separate copyright proceedings. The award reviewed rests on the omitted-authorship origin theory.

The case returns through the Ninth Circuit. Before entering a revised judgment, the lower court must identify each surviving legal basis, remove every award component supported only by the rejected origin theory, and show that separation in the judgment and calculation. It must reconsider the state unfair-competition treatment to the extent dependent on the federal ground and continue the existing remand of the book-copyright claim. Any independently sustainable recovery must follow adjudication under that claim's own liability and remedy rules; an unresolved claim cannot carry the old profits award by assumption.

### Knussman v. Maryland, Fourth Circuit No. 99-2349

Plenary merits decision, June 2, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

On review of the Fourth Circuit's post-trial judgment, 272 F.3d 625, No. 99-2349. The questions concern equal-protection liability and qualified immunity for a personnel official's 1994 administration of child-nurturing leave through categorical maternal-role assumptions, and the propriety of the appellate damages-retrial direction, including preserved alternative grounds necessary to resolve the award.

Maryland permitted a primary caregiver to use up to thirty days of accrued sick leave to care for a new child and a secondary caregiver to use ten days. This nurturing benefit was distinct from medical-disability leave. Knussman's daughter was born December 9, 1994; his wife had serious pregnancy and postpartum health difficulties. The verdict-supported evidence showed that he performed the principal infant-care work while she recovered, but personnel official Jill Mullineaux treated mothers as primary caregivers and fathers as ineligible except in extreme circumstances involving the mother's incapacity. She denied his primary-caregiver request. Her participation ended when the grievance proceeded to other officials.

The jury found discrimination and awarded $375,000 for emotional injury, supported by medical testimony concerning depression, anxiety and physical symptoms. Some testimony connected distress to the later grievance and litigation as well as the original denial. Post-trial rulings left individual monetary liability only against Mullineaux, while vacating the FMLA verdict and granting the other officials immunity. Knussman did not appeal those rulings. The Fourth Circuit affirmed equal-protection liability and immunity denial, vacated the award as excessive and required a new damages trial. It left undecided Mullineaux's distinct objection that the general damages verdict included the vacated FMLA claim. She seeks immunity or relief from the verdict; Knussman seeks preservation of liability and the award.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. Caregiving eligibility cannot be assigned by a categorical sex role

**Holding and operative rule:** A State may not deny an otherwise qualified father a primary-caregiver benefit by presuming that nurturing belongs to mothers. The plaintiff must prove intentional sex-based treatment by a preponderance; the State must establish an important governmental objective substantially related to the actual classification, and genuine medical-recovery distinctions, proof of caregiving responsibility and lawful overlapping-benefit limits remain distinct from a categorical bar against fathers.

**Authority:** Part I of the opinion of the Court, directly joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the benefit's actual function determines the equality inquiry. Craig requires an important objective and substantial relationship; Hogan rejects assigning an opportunity through an unsupported sex role; Wiesenfeld rejects parental-benefit distinctions resting on assumed caregiving responsibilities. Maryland's thirty-day and ten-day benefits concerned care of the child, not the biological condition of childbirth. Treating the wife's medical-recovery leave as proof that she performed the primary infant care confuses those distinct functions.

Reeves requires respect for reasonable verdict-supported findings without choosing the official's competing factual account. The supported evidence established Knussman's principal infant care and Mullineaux's categorical maternal-role premise. Neither the newness of the benefit nor informal advice supplies an important objective substantially served by replacing actual responsibility with sex. The current Virginia rule reinforces this genuine-justification analysis. The Court applies the existing equality standard to this administration and sustains the personal liability proved against Mullineaux.

**Precedent treatment:** Craig, Hogan and Wiesenfeld are applied to the distribution of caregiving benefits. Virginia reinforces the current scrutiny standard. Reeves preserves the verdict-favorable treatment of supported trial facts.

##### II. The prohibited eligibility rule was clearly established in 1994

**Holding and operative rule:** Harlow and Anderson's objective defense does not protect the verdict-supported denial of an otherwise qualified father's caregiving benefit through a categorical maternal-role assumption: pre-December 1994 benefits and sex-classification law made that conduct's unlawfulness apparent to a reasonable official. Novelty in the leave statute and informal administrative advice do not make that established constitutional distinction reasonably uncertain.

**Authority:** Part II of the opinion of the Court, directly joined by all nine Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court assesses the actual constitutional vice rather than an abstract assertion of equality or every uncertainty in calculating leave. Wiesenfeld, Craig and Hogan predated the conduct and supplied a concrete warning against distributing family benefits and opportunities through unsupported sex roles. The permissible treatment of pregnancy-related incapacity does not justify the found categorical caregiving exclusion. Mullineaux's own denial, rather than later officials' acts, supplies the challenged conduct.

Harlow and Anderson protect objectively reasonable legal mistakes. Elder requires examination of relevant law at the conduct date, and Hope explains that an obvious application of an established rule can supply warning without a factual twin. Those principles do not demand an earlier decision construing this exact state leave enactment. Conversely, a later decision cannot create earlier notice: Virginia and Hope explain current law or method, while the warning here comes from the identified preconduct cases. Informal guidance about a new benefit cannot erase that warning. Immunity therefore fails on the supported trial facts under the existing objective rule.

**Precedent treatment:** Harlow and Anderson's particularized objective inquiry is applied; Elder supplies conduct-date legal review; Hope's rejection of a replica requirement informs current method without supplying retrospective notice. Wiesenfeld, Craig and Hogan furnish the preconduct warning.

##### III. Actual emotional injury and bounded appellate review govern the award

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1983 compensation may include emotional harm proved by a preponderance to have been caused or aggravated by the defendant's constitutional violation, but excludes the abstract value of equality, unrelated officials' acts and independent litigation stress. A court of appeals reviews the district judge's Rule 59 determination for abuse of discretion, independently correcting legal error while deferring to a supported case-specific appraisal; competing causal events alone do not establish that the jury compensated a forbidden category.

**Authority:** Part III of the opinion of the Court, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These six adopt the actual-injury rule, the existing Gasperini allocation and its application; Scalia and Thomas support vacatur through their separate reexamination ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Carey and Stachura require actual causal injury, rather than a price assigned to a constitutional right. The record contains corroborated emotional and physical symptoms, testimony connecting the condition to the discriminatory denial, cross-examination concerning litigation distress and instructions limiting recovery to injury caused by the constitutional wrong. The trial judge found sufficient evidence of significant emotional injury and declined a new trial.

Gasperini permits meaningful review of that discretion but forbids first-instance appellate valuation of jury-tried compensation. The Fourth Circuit accepted causal sufficiency and framed its objection as one of amount. Its inference that a substantial proportion belonged to later proceedings did not establish that the jury included an excluded item or that the district judge's assessment exceeded permissible discretion. Medical proof of genuine injury does not alone allocate responsibility, but neither does testimony that later proceedings aggravated the condition establish an unlawful allocation. A grievance can independently cause stress and can aggravate injury from the original discrimination; the evidence must distinguish those causal paths.

The dissent correctly insists that legal compensation boundaries are reviewable. Its proposed mandatory trial nevertheless depends here on treating the disputed allocation inference as an established category error. The preserved independent verdict objection presents that different inquiry and remains for Part IV's remand. The existing bounded review rule also permits genuine legal correction while protecting the trial assessment, answering the concurrence's objection to factual reexamination. The stated excessiveness ground cannot sustain the mandatory retrial.

**Precedent treatment:** Carey and Stachura's causal actual-injury limits are applied. Gasperini's federal trial/appellate allocation governs this review; its distinct New York compensation measure is not the measure of this federal claim.

##### IV. The independent general-verdict objection requires a limited remand

**Holding and operative rule:** After rejection of the appellate excessiveness ground, the preserved contention that the general award includes a legally unavailable FMLA component must be decided under the actual verdict, instructions and governing error rules. A lawful $375,000 award must stand if no independent reversible error remains; an inseparable legally unavailable component requires a new trial limited to compensation for the equal-protection injury.

**Authority:** Part IV of the opinion of the Court, directly joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer.

**Controlling explanation:** The court of appeals left the mixed-claim verdict objection undecided because it had already required a new trial on excessiveness. That premise has been removed. Deference to a jury and trial judge does not insulate recovery based on an unavailable cause of action; the presence of a failed claim likewise does not establish reversible error without the governing verdict and error analysis. The charge, verdict structure, actual objections and motions must establish whether an independent defect requires correction. The controlling actual-injury and review standards are settled; this is remaining procedural and remedial work under those standards. The unappealed FMLA disposition and other defendants' favorable judgments retain their force.

**Precedent treatment:** Carey and Stachura continue to define the compensable injury. Gasperini preserves the distinction between correction of legal error and substitution of an appellate compensation amount.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Craig v. Boren, 429 U.S. 190: applies important-objective and substantial-relationship scrutiny to the actual sex classification.
- Mississippi University for Women v. Hogan, 458 U.S. 718: applies the prohibition on unsupported sex-role allocation and the government's genuine-justification burden.
- Weinberger v. Wiesenfeld, 420 U.S. 636: applies its rejection of stereotyped parental-benefit distinctions to the caregiving opportunity and supplies preconduct warning.
- United States v. Virginia, June 26, 1996: reinforces the current demanding intermediate-scrutiny standard; its later date supplies no December 1994 notice.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800: applies the existing objective personal-immunity defense.
- Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635: applies particularized clarity to the actual categorical caregiver exclusion rather than abstract equality alone.
- Elder v. Holloway, February 23, 1994: applies independent consideration of all relevant preconduct law on the preserved immunity question.
- Hope v. Pelzer, June 27, 2002: applies its present-method rejection of a factual-twin prerequisite and its personal-responsibility limits; warning remains anchored in earlier law.
- Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., June 12, 2000: applies respect for reasonable verdict-supported findings without weighing credibility anew.
- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247: applies actual-injury and causation requirements to proved emotional harm.
- Memphis Community School District v. Stachura, 477 U.S. 299: preserves the exclusion of abstract constitutional-value damages while allowing compensation for actual proved injury.
- Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc., June 24, 1996: applies bounded appellate abuse-of-discretion review and the trial court's compensation function; its New York substantive measure remains confined to its distinct setting.

#### Law After Decision

Equal protection requires administration of an existing caregiving benefit by lawful individual eligibility rather than a conclusive maternal-role assumption. The found categorical denial was clearly forbidden in 1994 under ordinary objective immunity. For compensatory §1983 awards, actual causal emotional injury remains recoverable, and appellate review must distinguish genuine legal error from a replacement appraisal of a supported award. The general-verdict issue is ordinary remand work under those rules, with the existing liability judgment fixed.

#### Separate Writings

**Scalia, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment, joined by Thomas.** They join the liability, immunity and limited-remand holdings but reach damages vacatur through the stricter Seventh Amendment ground retained in their Gasperini dissent. Appellate correction of genuine legal error differs from reassessing compensatory facts or evidentiary weight. Here the circuit accepted causal sufficiency and then inferred what proportion of genuine emotional injury should be attributed to other events. Calling that inquiry abuse-of-discretion review does not cure the prohibited reexamination. Traditional trial-court Rule 59 power remains, as does review of an actually erroneous legal category or instruction. The independent FMLA general-verdict objection may therefore be resolved on remand as a legal and procedural question. They withhold Part III's broader account of permissible appellate excessiveness review.

**Stevens, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** He joins liability and immunity but would affirm the new damages trial. Carey and Stachura confine recovery to harm attributable to Mullineaux's violation. In his view, the end of her involvement and the medical testimony linking substantial continuing distress to the later grievance and litigation show that the award crosses that legal causal boundary. The appellate court may correct such a defect even when reasonable factual inferences are incidental to the correction, as his Gasperini position explains. Corroborated injury and trial discretion are significant, but they do not authorize compensation for materially independent causes. Because the lawful amount cannot reliably be separated, he would require a jury trial confined to damages caused by Mullineaux; he selects no substitute figure. That already-required trial would make a separate resolution of the FMLA verdict objection unnecessary.

#### Source Notes

Knussman v. Maryland, 272 F.3d 625 (4th Cir. 2001), No. 99-2349, decided November 7, 2001; Md. Code Ann., State Pers. & Pens. §§7-502, 7-508 (1994); 42 U.S.C. §1983; Fed. R. Civ. P. 50, 59. The court of appeals' opinion treats excessiveness as an amount question rather than total failure of causation, and separately leaves the FMLA general-verdict objection undecided.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case returns through the Fourth Circuit. That court must inspect the actual instructions, verdict, objections and motions, apply preservation and harmless-error rules to the FMLA general-verdict objection and other genuinely preserved grounds, and enter a mandate either preserving the lawful $375,000 award or identifying an independent reversible defect requiring a damages-only retrial. If retrial is required, the jury must compensate proved harm caused or aggravated by Mullineaux's discrimination, exclude independent litigation unpleasantness and unrelated official conduct, and refrain from assigning an abstract value to equality. The affirmed liability is not retried absent a separately established lawful ground; the unappealed statutory and other-defendant judgments remain effective.

### Krislov v. Rednour, Seventh Circuit No. 99-3801

Plenary merits decision after full merits submission, June 2, 2003

#### Chronology and Posture

The Court reviews the Seventh Circuit's September 5, 2000 affirmance of summary judgment and an injunction against the challenged portions of 10 ILCS 5/7-10. The question is whether Illinois may require candidate-petition circulators to be registered voters residing within the electorate represented by the candidate, consistently with the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

Krislov sought a United States Senate nomination and Sullivan a House nomination in the seventh congressional district in the 1996 Democratic primary. Both secured enough signatures, but the challenged circulator conditions excluded willing advocates and caused additional campaign expense. Krislov withdrew before the primary. The certified candidate class remains exposed to challenges under the provision. The Senate claim concerns circulation by persons outside Illinois; the House claim concerns persons outside the seventh district, including residents of other States.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### I. The candidates' injuries and continuing class claim permit decision

**Holding and operative rule:** A candidate's loss of willing petition advocates and diversion of campaign resources are concrete injuries even when enough signatures are ultimately collected. Here the continuing certified class of candidates exposed to the challenged provision supplies the live prospective controversy after the named candidates' election ended.

**Authority:** Part I of the Court's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, controls the threshold determination.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that eventual ballot qualification does not restore the political association and resources lost through the challenged exclusions. Buckley recognizes the protected conversation conducted by petition advocates; Lerman applies that protection to candidate petitions. Those injuries belong to the candidates themselves because the qualifications restrict their chosen means of communicating and obtaining support. Prospective relief requires a continuing controversy as well as past injury. The certified class covers candidates whose nominations have been or will be challenged under section 7-10 and remains exposed to the common conditions. That class controversy supplies the present basis for decision. Lerman's supported individual repeat exposure and this case's class exposure are distinct grounds for reaching a continuing election restriction.

##### II. The State and district boundaries cannot exclude these advocates

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments by excluding otherwise qualified, accountable candidate-petition advocates because they reside outside Illinois for a Senate nomination or outside the seventh congressional district for the House nomination, including residents of other States. Under Anderson–Burdick, courts weigh the character and magnitude of the actual injury against the State's precise asserted interests and the extent to which those interests require the restriction: severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests, while reasonable, nondiscriminatory burdens ordinarily may be justified by important regulatory interests; these direct geographic exclusions impose severe burdens that the asserted grounds fail to justify.

**Authority:** Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer; eight direct joins control both applications and the graduated method. Thomas supports both judgments under traditional strict scrutiny.

**Controlling explanation:** Meyer protects the political conversation involved in collecting signatures. Buckley requires examination of the speakers excluded and the actual verification need. Anderson and Burdick preserve reasonable, nondiscriminatory administration while requiring correspondingly stronger justification for severe burdens. Lerman establishes that candidate witnessing retains this expressive character and invalidates an unsupported in-state district screen. Requiring an advocate to relocate imposes the same severe exclusion here.

Qualified signers demonstrate local electoral support, and qualified voters select nominees. Circulators ask for that support and communicate the candidates' views. State or district residence does not establish boundary knowledge or the ability to obtain valid signatures. Circulator certification and the signature-challenge process address those tasks directly. The candidate's eventual success and access to other speakers do not restore the excluded association. Cartwright's valid support requirement measures the electorate's support, a different function.

The interstate application also requires its own justification. Lerman's statewide process does not establish Illinois authority over every nonresident. But the Board's actual local-support, valid-signature, integrity and outside-influence grounds supply no necessary connection between residence and this advocacy. The Board did not advance the process-consent or demonstrated nonresident-fraud grounds identified by the court below. Enforcement difficulty cannot be presumed from a boundary alone. Reserving voting to Illinois electors does not justify restricting candidates' association with outside advocates; a desire to exclude their political influence targets the protected association itself. These reasons defeat both geographic applications.

##### III. Otherwise eligible residents may circulate without voter enrollment

**Holding and operative rule:** Illinois may not exclude otherwise qualified, accountable candidate-petition advocates merely because they are unregistered voters, including persons already resident in and eligible to register within the represented electorate. Under the graduated standard stated in Part II, the substantial restriction on the candidates' chosen advocates is not justified by the asserted nomination, local-support and signature-verification interests; the injunction therefore protects eligible unregistered Illinois residents in the Senate application and eligible unregistered seventh-district residents in the House application.

**Authority:** Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Ginsburg; six direct joins control this rule and its application. Thomas supplies a seventh judgment vote under traditional strict scrutiny. O'Connor and Breyer dissent from this component.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ability to register does not answer the loss of an otherwise qualified political advocate. Buckley rejects voter status as the necessary qualification for the petition conversation where retained mechanisms serve the actual verification task more directly. Lerman establishes that the candidate setting preserves that protected function. The candidates here lost willing advocates and incurred additional expense; the burden is a restriction on their chosen speakers, not simply a step in counting electoral support.

Accessible enrollment does distinguish a resident from a person who would have to move. It does not establish why the resident's voter status is needed to solicit or attest qualified signatures. Qualified signers show support; certification, challenges and fraud enforcement address reliability. The formal nomination setting supplies no sufficient functional distinction from Buckley's protected-speaker reasoning. This is a substantial restriction on circulation that those asserted interests do not justify.

The partial dissent would preserve enrollment only for eligible residents while leaving outsiders free of it, limiting relief to the applications it considers unconstitutional. The Court rejects the remaining resident restriction on its own merits: the same protected task and verification means leave that exclusion unjustified. Relief against the whole challenged condition follows because its resident application also violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments, rather than from a need to make every remedial line uniform.

##### IV. The existing injunction protects the class against both common barriers

**Holding and operative rule:** The injunction against both challenged circulator qualifications remains operative for the certified candidate class and the responsible officials within the court's jurisdiction. Its enforcement must give effect to the invalidation of nonregistration and residence outside the represented electorate as grounds for rejecting otherwise valid circulation, while preserving independent lawful signature requirements.

**Authority:** Part IV is joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas and Ginsburg; seven direct joins control full affirmance and implementation. O'Connor and Breyer join its geographic application only.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the existing class injunction matches the common conditions adjudicated here. A candidate-specific exemption would leave those same conditions operating against the exposed class. Buckley and Lerman distinguish invalid speaker exclusions from the retained means of verifying qualified electoral support. That distinction gives the affirmed order its operative scope: officials must implement the invalidations through the procedures specified in the judgment, and lawful independent defects remain adjudicable. The existing injunction supplies the vehicle for that enforcement.

#### Precedent Treatment

- Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, is applied to candidate-petition circulation as interactive political expression; its protection concerns the advocates' conversation and available speaker pool.
- Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, is applied for assessment of the actual burden, precise asserted interests and necessity of the restriction.
- Burdick v. Takushi (June 8, 1992) is applied for graduated scrutiny: severe burdens require narrow tailoring to compelling interests, while reasonable, nondiscriminatory regulation retains room for important administrative interests.
- Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation, Inc. (January 12, 1999) is extended from its reviewed initiative-circulator exclusion to these candidate-petition enrollment conditions; accessible registration does not supply the missing functional justification for excluding an otherwise qualified advocate.
- Lerman v. Board of Elections in the City of New York (May 27, 2003) is applied to the protected candidate-circulation function and the in-state district exclusion. Its reasoning is extended to the Senate-wide and interstate applications through an independent assessment of Illinois's asserted grounds, rather than an assumption that statewide process answers interstate accountability.
- Cartwright v. Barnes (February 25, 2003) is distinguished: a genuine signature requirement measures electoral support and does not establish a justification for excluding the messenger who seeks it.

#### Law After Decision

The protection of otherwise qualified petition advocates applies to the challenged candidate-nomination system. Illinois's Senate-wide and House-district geographic exclusions, including their operation against residents of other States, and its voter-enrollment exclusion against eligible residents are unenforceable under the affirmed class injunction.

Courts applying Anderson–Burdick must distinguish a signer's electoral qualification from an advocate's expressive task and assess the actual restriction and its justification. The State's power to define its electorate does not itself establish power to exclude the candidate's chosen political advocates.

#### Separate Writings

**Thomas, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** He joins the threshold determination and the injunction's full affirmance and implementation. Direct exclusion of political petition speakers receives traditional strict scrutiny. Meyer protects the conversation; his Buckley concurrence protects the candidates' available advocates, and his Lerman concurrence applies that protection to candidate nominations. The formal nomination setting does not turn the messenger into a voter or an administrative officer. Qualified electors demonstrate support and select nominees; the advocates communicate with them. Neither Illinois residence for the Senate petition, seventh-district residence for the House petition, nor local voter enrollment establishes fitness to perform that task. The Board's actual interests fail narrow tailoring in light of retained verification, and excluding outside influence cannot justify the lost association. He therefore agrees with every invalidation and the same injunction, while withholding assent to the generalized method that calibrates scrutiny by administrative burden.

**O'Connor, joined by Breyer, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join the threshold and both geographic holdings, including the exclusion of residents of other States, and the geographic protection of the decree. Their Burdick and Buckley approach distinguishes an accessible enrollment step from a requirement to relocate. Lerman establishes the severe exclusion imposed by a district boundary; Illinois's stated interests likewise fail to justify the State-wide barrier. Local signers supply electoral support, and the candidate's advocate need not be an elector.

They would sustain the enrollment condition only for otherwise qualified persons already resident in and eligible to register within the represented electorate: Illinois residents for the Senate petition and seventh-district residents for the House petition. They acknowledge the excluded voices but regard readily available enrollment within a formal nomination system as a reasonable administrative burden supported by the asserted interests. Their proposed partial reversal would narrow the existing injunction only to permit that application. It would leave otherwise qualified outsiders free of a voter-enrollment obligation while retaining one for eligible residents. They defend that difference as a limit on constitutional relief to the applications they consider invalid; they would not extend the invalidation merely to make its coverage uniform.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins the entire opinion and the affirmed class protection. He further treats the enrollment exclusion, as well as the geographic exclusions, as a severe restriction on the candidates' choice of willing advocates that requires narrow tailoring to a compelling interest. Registration and residence do not themselves establish honesty or accountability; actual enforcement difficulty requires proof rather than a presumption from those statuses.

He would additionally presume relief covering an established common eligibility barrier and the persons it governs unless the election defendants prove that a narrower order fully cures the violation. The presumption depends on a live plaintiff or certified class and the actual common injury, and operates within the court's jurisdiction. Here the certified class and shared exclusions supply that basis; a candidate-by-candidate exemption would leave the barrier controlling the class's advocacy.

#### Source Notes

Krislov v. Rednour, 226 F.3d 851 (7th Cir. 2000), No. 99-3801, judgment September 5, 2000; 10 ILCS 5/7-10; 42 U.S.C. §1983. The judgment reviewed followed summary judgment and a Rule 54(b) judgment on the challenged circulator requirements. The candidates and certified candidate class assert their own injuries; prospective circulators were not separately joined plaintiffs.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The affirmed injunction protects the certified candidate class against the responsible election officials within the court's jurisdiction. The lower court must retain an order identifying nonregistration and residence outside the represented electorate as forbidden grounds for rejecting otherwise valid circulated signatures. Officials must conform their instructions and signature-review criteria before the next application of the petition-review rules. Rejections resting solely on either forbidden ground must be corrected under the order; independent lawful signature defects remain separately adjudicable. Compliance is measured by the operative procedures and treatment of challenged signatures.

The mandate affirms the Seventh Circuit judgment. The existing injunction remains in force under the lower court's continuing jurisdiction for the implementation specified in the judgment.

**End of entry: Krislov v. Rednour, Plenary merits decision after full merits submission, June 2, 2003.**
