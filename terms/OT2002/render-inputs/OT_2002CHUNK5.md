## Event

Henderson v. Stalder, No. 02-523; review of 287 F.3d 374 (Fifth Circuit No. 00-31171). Merits decision, November 18, 2002. The Court reviews the Fifth Circuit judgment vacating a preliminary injunction against Louisiana’s Choose Life specialty-plate program and directing dismissal for lack of standing. The question is whether a claimant personally excluded from plate or grant participation must show that relief guarantees the desired plate or grant, and how that inquiry affects the distinct claims and requested interim relief.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed in part, vacated in part, and the case is remanded.

## Opinion Topology

Thomas delivers the signed Opinion of the Court. Complete-opinion coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. Every member joins all holdings and all judgment components.

## Holdings

### Personal equal participation and effective redress

**Holding and operative rule.** A claimant ready and able to participate who challenges a governmental barrier denying equal consideration need not prove that lawful relief will confer the ultimate benefit: ending the preference can redress the injury when it ends that claimant’s own unequal treatment. The claimant must establish personal actual or imminent exclusion, traceability and likely redress with support appropriate to the litigation stage, and the requested order must address the same opportunity and barrier alleged.

**Authority.** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by the complete nine-Justice coalition identified in Opinion Topology, controls this holding and the following holding directly.

**Controlling explanation.** Northeastern Florida identifies denial of equal opportunity, rather than failure to win a benefit, as the injury; Adarand preserves that distinction. Heckler v. Mathews recognizes that withdrawing a discriminatory preference can provide equality. Orr and Arkansas Writers’ Project likewise permit adjudication although more than one lawful cure remains available. Louisiana therefore cannot defeat redressability solely because striking the challenged preference would give Keeler no opposing plate and Planned Parenthood no grant.

The State’s stronger objection is that withdrawing this particular plate or grant preference may not cure the personal exclusion actually pleaded. That objection requires examination, not a guaranteed-benefit requirement. Lujan requires each claimant’s own concrete stake and stage-appropriate support, and Reform Party requires a remedy connected to that stake. Keeler alleges a desire to buy an opposing plate; Planned Parenthood alleges exclusion from grant eligibility. The district court must identify the actual opportunity, challenged barrier, proper defendant and lawful relief for each claim, addressing readiness, eligibility, traceability and redress. It must separately determine the operative pleading and lawful entry of Planned Parenthood and LaMothe after the interlocutory appeal began; an addition below does not enlarge that appeal retroactively. Rules 15 and 54(c) govern permissible pleading changes and relief for the actual controversy. These are claimant-specific applications of the rule announced today.

### Distinct jurisdictional claims and preliminary relief

**Holding and operative rule.** Standing must be established separately for the claimant and claim for which relief is sought, and jurisdiction must precede adjudication of the contested constitutional merits. Correcting an erroneous standing rule does not restore a preliminary injunction without the jurisdictional, merits and equitable predicates for that relief.

**Controlling explanation.** Valley Forge requires a concrete personal injury rather than disagreement with asserted governmental religion, and Flast supplies a bounded taxpayer route. Here the $3.50 handling charge offsets a portion of administrative costs and the statute requires uncompensated advisory service. Those provisions and these allegations do not establish the asserted general-tax injury; participation of named religious organizations does not establish concrete injurious religious administration. The distinct claims of parties properly within the appeal therefore remain dismissed.

Steel Co. prevents the courts from assuming disputed jurisdiction to decide the program’s contested merits. Government-speech or private-forum classification, the grant conditions’ validity and severability remain for proper proceedings after jurisdiction because the standing judgment did not decide them. Any properly presented Tax Injunction Act objection is an independent threshold issue. The preliminary injunction remains vacated: a corrected legal rule supplies neither the evidence necessary for interim jurisdiction nor the required likelihood and equitable findings. The district court must decide a renewed request under the ordinary requirements after identifying the claimant’s own injury and the order capable of repairing it.

## Precedent Treatment

- Northeastern Florida and Adarand: applied to distinguish equal opportunity from guaranteed receipt of a benefit; personal participation and stage-appropriate support remain required.
- Heckler v. Mathews: its withdrawal-of-preference remedy principle extends to the actual expressive and grant-participation exclusions alleged here, subject to an effective personal remedial connection.
- Orr and Arkansas Writers’ Project: applied to preserve adjudication where several lawful legislative cures remain possible.
- Lujan: applied for personal injury, traceability, likely redress and litigation-stage support.
- Reform Party v. Democratic National Committee: applied to require the challenged barrier and requested relief to correspond to the claimant’s own exclusion.
- Steel Co.: applied to require jurisdiction before the contested constitutional merits.
- Valley Forge and Flast: applied to the separate religious and taxpayer claims at their existing limits.

## Law After Decision

Personal exclusion from a governmental participation opportunity may be redressed by ending the challenged unequal treatment even when the claimant will receive neither the favored message nor the grant. Courts must apply that rule claimant by claimant to the pleaded opportunity, causal barrier and lawful relief. The threshold inquiry preserves separate taxpayer, religious and statutory jurisdictional requirements; renewed interim protection requires its own supporting findings.

## Separate Writings

None.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns through the Court of Appeals for the district court to conduct the claimant-specific standing, pleading and jurisdictional inquiries stated above and, where jurisdiction exists, further proceedings on the actual claims. The preliminary injunction remains vacated. Any renewed request for interim relief must be resolved with the ordinary supporting findings and an order directed to the identified barrier.

## Event

Adland v. Russ; review of 307 F.3d 471 (Sixth Circuit). Merits decision, March 4, 2003. The Court reviews the permanent injunction against Section 8 of Kentucky’s 2000 joint resolution directing relocation of a Ten Commandments monument to the Capitol grounds near the Floral Clock, with the resolution included in the display. The question is whether this proposed official presentation explains history or identifies civil authority with religious profession.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment is affirmed.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

The permanent injunction against implementation of the identified Section 8 proposal remains in force. Its reach is the placement and accompanying official presentation directed by that provision. A materially different historical presentation may be considered through the ordinary process governing compliance with or modification of the injunction.

## Opinion Topology

Breyer delivers the Opinion of the Court. Complete-opinion coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Kennedy files a dissenting opinion, joined by Scalia and Thomas. The dissent agrees that the concrete proposal is fit for review but rejects the Court’s application of the governing Establishment Clause rule.

## Holdings

### Review of the authorized proposal

**Holding and operative rule.** People facing a supported, recurring personal encounter with a sufficiently definite, officially directed display may challenge that display before its installation. Where the monument, site, accompanying governmental text and proposed relationship to the surrounding material are identified, the constitutional controversy is fit for adjudication despite remaining implementation details.

**Authority.** Breyer’s Opinion of the Court, joined by the six-Justice complete-opinion coalition in Opinion Topology, directly controls this holding and the following holding.

**Controlling explanation.** Robinson requires personal contact rather than abstract disagreement, and Lujan requires a supported threatened injury. The individual plaintiffs regularly use the Capitol for political advocacy and would encounter the directed placement there. Texas and Ohio Forestry distinguish indefinite future action from an operative, sufficiently defined command. Section 8 identifies this monument, its location near the Floral Clock and inclusion of the resolution; Kentucky identifies seven surrounding memorials. The challenged presentation is therefore concrete. As Santa Fe illustrates, a court may evaluate an adopted governmental mechanism before its first completed religious performance. Possible later additions do not change the proposal presently directed and defended. The personal prospective encounter and defined official action permit resolution now.

### Historical explanation and official religious identification

**Holding and operative rule.** Government may preserve and explain religious artifacts, but it may not use its own official presentation to proclaim religious teachings as a foundation of present civil authority or commend their acceptance through governmental authority. The challenger must establish that prohibited function from the complete attributable presentation; Kentucky’s directed Section 8 arrangement does so and may be enjoined.

**Controlling explanation.** Agostini retains Lemon as one inquiry: specific prohibitions or distinct doctrines govern where applicable; established permissible relationships govern their own settings; otherwise attribution and function are assessed under ordinary procedure. Purpose, effects and institutional relationships supply evidence, not separate prongs. Robinson distinguishes official adoption from private origin and prohibits identifying present civil authority with faith. Santa Fe likewise examines the government’s actual undertaking.

Kentucky selects a prescriptive Decalogue monument with religious symbols and requires the accompanying resolution. The resolution praises Scripture and Christianity through attributed statements, describes the Commandments as the Commonwealth’s foundational legal code and frames the placement as recalling biblical foundations. The full combination uses present governmental authority to proclaim a religious foundation, bringing the proposal within Agostini’s prohibition on official religious adoption. Long storage of the 1971 donation also defeats the asserted premise of nearly three decades of continuous exhibition.

The State and dissent fairly invoke historical acknowledgment. The attributed quotations, seven civic memorials and much larger Floral Clock must all be considered. But the unrelated memorials do not turn the resolution’s legal-foundation proclamation and the prescriptive monument into an explanatory account of legal development. That conclusion rests on the complete directed arrangement, not one quotation, ownership, size, religious motive or a count of secular objects. A constitutional historical exhibit needs no prescribed curator’s format or exhaustive catalogue; its actual function must be explanation rather than adoption. Good News Club’s protection for independently chosen private expression concerns a different relationship. The injunction reaches this official proposal; the unchallenged school provision and a materially different historical exhibit lie outside the question presented.

## Precedent Treatment

- Robinson v. City of Edmond: applied for recurring-contact standing, official attribution and the distinction between historical references and identification of civil authority with faith; its specific civic-seal holding informs this contextual application without becoming a categorical monument rule.
- Lujan: applied for supported personal prospective injury.
- Texas v. United States and Ohio Forestry Association v. Sierra Club: applied to distinguish the concrete placement command from indefinite future implementation.
- Lemon, as modified by Agostini: applied as one governmental-authority-and-function inquiry; the former independent prongs remain superseded.
- Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe: applied to the adopted official operation and its availability for review before completed implementation.
- Good News Club v. Milford Central School: distinguished because private access differs from the State’s own selected presentation.

## Law After Decision

A government’s historical presentation of religious material is evaluated by its actual attribution and function under Agostini. An official display that proclaims religious teaching as the foundation of present civil authority crosses the constitutional line even when labeled historical and surrounded by secular memorials. The permanent restraint here governs Kentucky’s identified Section 8 arrangement; a different historical proposal is subject to the same functional inquiry.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, agrees that the plaintiffs’ prospective encounter and the sufficiently defined proposal permit review. He also applies Agostini’s distinction between historical explanation and official religious adoption. The disagreement concerns the function of this arrangement. In the dissent’s view, the inherited artifact, attributed statements of historical figures and civic memorial setting commemorate the asserted contribution of religion to law rather than adopt the monument’s theology as present governmental truth. Public attribution settles whose presentation it is, but does not settle what it does. Robinson’s continuing civic-seal prohibition does not turn every official acknowledgment of religious influence into a profession of faith; neither that decision nor Agostini demands an academically complete legal-history exhibit. The religious language requires consideration, but the dissent reads the resolution as a historical account rather than an invitation to accept religious commands through current civil authority. Kennedy would dissolve the injunction against the described proposal. This reasoning does not depend on coercion as the exclusive Establishment Clause rule.

## Procedure After Action

The permanent injunction remains enforceable when the mandate issues. Officials must refrain from implementing the enjoined Section 8 placement and framing. Any compliance or modification proceeding must assess the actual governmental presentation, including its location and explanatory text, under the controlling functional rule.

## Source Notes

Adland v. Russ, 307 F.3d 471; Kentucky Senate Joint Resolution No. 57 (2000), particularly Sections 7 and 8. The proposal directs the monument’s relocation and inclusion of the resolution; the reviewed controversy concerns that proposed presentation.

## Event

Boeing Co. v. United States, Nos. 01-1209 and 01-1382, 537 U.S. 437. Merits decision, March 4, 2003. Argued December 9, 2002. On writs of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 258 F.3d 958.

The principal question is whether Treasury's research-expense allocation rule validly governs combined taxable income under the DISC provisions for 1979–1984 and the FSC provisions for 1985–1987, despite Boeing's use of airplane product-line accounting. The conditional cross-petition seeks corresponding reversal of Boeing Sales Corporation's favorable judgment only if Boeing prevails on that allocation question.

Boeing allocated approximately $3.6 billion in company-sponsored research by airplane program, including programs with no current sales. The IRS applied the broader transportation-equipment category. The Ninth Circuit reversed the district court's refund judgment for Boeing. Boeing seeks restoration of refunds under its product-line theory; the United States defends the regulation and the judgments resulting from its application.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the judgments in both consolidated dockets.

| No. 01-1382: conditional companion consequence | Affirmed, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Scalia, Thomas | Boeing Sales Corporation's favorable judgment stands because the condition for changing it does not occur. |

## Holdings

### Delegated research-expense allocation

**Holding and operative rule:** For the DISC and FSC computations governing Boeing's 1979–1987 tax years, Treasury may allocate currently deducted research expenses across the relevant two-digit transportation-equipment category under Treasury Regulation §1.861-8(e)(3). The court first identifies actual statutory authority and enforces its limits; within that authority, the regulation reasonably treats research as related to the broader technological category it can support, including when an individual program produces no current receipts.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court, supported in full by the seven-member complete-opinion coalition stated in Opinion Topology. Each member adopts the authority inquiry, category rule, application, and judgment.

**Controlling explanation:** The Code's allocation structure, including §§861 and 863, and Treasury's implementing authority under §7805(a) authorize rules for allocating the deductions used in combined taxable income. Sections 994(a)(2) and 925(a)(2) tie that income to export receipts, but do not require every research deduction to follow only the airplane program to which the company assigned it. Haggar requires an actual delegation and respect for statutory boundaries before a formal regulation receives controlling treatment. Mead confirms that agency expertise or official status alone does not supply that authority; MCI requires enforcement of an enacted limit before deference. Those requirements are satisfied here by the allocation scheme and the regulation issued to implement it.

Research is speculative. Work on a program that yields no current sales can produce knowledge, methods, and experience useful to other transportation products. Treasury therefore reasonably identifies the broader product category as the class of income to which these research deductions relate. Boeing's project accounting does not establish a statutory requirement of exclusive project tracing. The specific marginal-costing grants in §§994(b)(2) and 925(b)(2) concern establishing or maintaining export markets; the Court's authority analysis rests on the general allocation and implementing scheme, with those specific grants confined to their terms.

### Export grouping and the incorporated expense rule

**Holding and operative rule:** A qualifying choice to group export transactions by product or product line for the DISC or FSC pricing methods does not displace the research-expense allocation rule incorporated into the calculation of combined taxable income. Boeing's claimed refunds based on exclusive airplane-program allocation therefore fail, and the condition for changing the companion judgment is absent.

**Authority:** The same complete-opinion coalition adopts this holding in full as a necessary part of the Court's allocation ruling.

**Controlling explanation:** The DISC rule, §1.994-1(c)(6)(iii), requires allocation consistent with §1.861-8; the FSC counterpart, §1.925(a)-1T(c)(6)(iii)(D), likewise incorporates expense allocation. The grouping provisions do a separate job: they identify transactions to which an administrative pricing method will be applied. The DISC provision making an accepted grouping controlling and the distinct FSC grouping provisions retain that operation while the incorporated research rule determines the expenses entering the computation.

The dissent reads the specific grouping choice as controlling the allocation of costs as well as receipts. That reading would deprive the express research-allocation cross-reference of its assigned work. A taxpayer's accepted pricing group remains meaningful without becoming an election to treat potentially shared research as legally unrelated to the rest of its product category. Section 174 permits current deduction of research costs, but does not prescribe the income class that bears them for this calculation. United Dominion requires the computation supplied by the actual governing regulations. Here that computation includes a promulgated research rule expressly incorporated into the export system. The validity of that rule, rather than a new finding of abusive accounting, defeats the refund theory.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Haggar Apparel Co.: applies its requirement of actual delegated authority and statutory boundaries before deference to a reasonable formal regulation.
- United States v. Mead Corp., 533 U.S. 218: applies its distinction between delegated interpretive lawmaking and administrative expertise or official status alone.
- MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. AT&T, 512 U.S. 218: preserves statutory limits on administrative power; the allocation rule operates within the authority identified here.
- Christensen v. Harris County, 529 U.S. 576: distinguishes its nonbinding advisory interpretation from this operative, incorporated regulation.
- United Dominion Industries, Inc. v. United States, 532 U.S. 822: applies its insistence on the computation supplied by the governing scheme; the expense rule here forms part of that scheme.

## Law After Decision

The governing DISC and FSC provisions permit Treasury's research-specific allocation across the applicable transportation-equipment category for these tax years. The taxpayer's accepted transaction grouping determines how the pricing alternatives are applied, while the incorporated research rule supplies the expense allocation. The delegation-first method remains the governing review rule; this decision adds the answer to the particular grouping and allocation conflict.

## Separate Writings

Scalia, joined by Thomas, dissents. The export-specific regulations make an accepted product grouping controlling, and the dissent reads that choice as governing cost attribution as well as the pricing method. Applying the broad research category first strips the more specific grouping rule of the effect it grants the taxpayer. Possible incidental benefits from research cannot replace the relationship required by that rule.

The dissent also rejects the suggestion that research costs allocated to a program without current receipts necessarily disclose an impermissible omission. Section 174 allows immediate deduction of speculative research, and expense-allocation systems can place costs in classes that presently produce no income. The legal question is which class the operative rules select. On that source-based ground, the dissent would reverse both judgments and require consistent tax and refund calculations. It confines its objection to the export regime's specific rules.

## Procedure After Action

The Ninth Circuit's judgments stand. Boeing's demand for restoration of its refund on the rejected product-line allocation theory is denied, and Boeing Sales Corporation's favorable companion judgment remains intact.

The two-digit classification governs the years at issue.

## Event

Clay v. United States, No. 01-1500, 537 U.S. 522. Merits decision, March 4, 2003. Argued January 13, 2003. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 30 Fed. Appx. 607.

The question is whether, after affirmance of a federal conviction and no petition for certiorari, §2255 finality occurs when the appellate mandate issues or when the certiorari period expires. The Seventh Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Clay's motion as untimely using the mandate date. Clay seeks consideration of his collateral motion; the United States agrees with the certiorari-expiration rule, and appointed amicus defends the judgment.

Clay's arson and drug convictions were affirmed on November 23, 1998. The mandate issued December 15, 1998. His time to seek certiorari expired February 22, 1999, and he filed his initial §2255 motion on February 22, 2000. The limitations ruling prevented adjudication of his collateral claims.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Seventh Circuit and remands for further proceedings on Clay’s motion.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

Clay's motion is timely under the ordinary final-judgment trigger. The mandate-based limitations dismissal is reversed.

## Opinion Topology

Ginsburg delivers the signed Opinion of the Court. Complete-opinion coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer. All join the complete finality rule, its application, and the judgment.

## Holdings

### Finality includes the available certiorari period

**Holding and operative rule:** When a federal conviction is affirmed on direct appeal and the defendant does not petition for certiorari, the judgment becomes final for §2255's ordinary one-year limitation when the time to seek certiorari expires. The appellate mandate does not start that clock while ordinary Supreme Court review remains available. Clay's February 22, 2000 motion was timely because his certiorari period expired on February 22, 1999.

**Authority:** The unanimous opinion of the Court; every member of the complete-opinion coalition in Opinion Topology adopts the rule, statutory explanation, application, and reversal with remand.

**Controlling explanation:** Finality in the ordinary collateral-review setting includes the end of direct review and the expiration of the time available to seek it. Griffith, Barefoot, and Johnson supply that established usage. The mandate performs a different function by returning authority to the lower court. Supreme Court Rule 13 measures the ordinary certiorari period from the judgment, not the mandate, so that administrative return cannot extinguish the remaining opportunity for direct review.

The appointed amicus relies on the contrast with §2244(d)(1)(A), which expressly mentions completion of direct review or expiration of the time to seek it. Section 2255 instead uses the unqualified term final. That difference does not select the mandate as an earlier event. Unlike a provision that specifies one event while omitting another, §2255 leaves the familiar legal concept intact. The fuller state-prisoner formulation also supplies a uniform federal measure across differing state usages. Russello's recognition that textual differences may matter does not make this difference decisive without its statutory context. Lindh likewise teaches attention to the particular comparison, not an automatic rule for every omission.

The Court therefore gives §2255 the ordinary direct-review meaning of finality. The rule follows from the statute and review framework independently of the Government's agreement. The Seventh Circuit used the wrong starting event; the district court must proceed with Clay's motion under the corrected calculation and the remaining applicable requirements.

## Precedent Treatment

- Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314: applies its ordinary account of finality after direct review ends or the certiorari opportunity expires.
- Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, and United States v. Johnson, 457 U.S. 537: apply their established collateral-review usage including the expiration of available direct review.
- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16: preserves attention to meaningful statutory differences while rejecting the inference that §2255's unqualified finality term selects the mandate.
- Lindh v. Murphy: applies contextual comparison and distinguishes its separate temporal question; its construction of AEDPA's application to identified pre-enactment cases remains intact.

## Law After Decision

For federal convictions affirmed on direct appeal without a certiorari petition, the ordinary §2255 one-year period runs from expiration of the time to seek certiorari. Courts must use that lawful expiration date in the statutory calculation. The rule resolves the finality trigger that produced the dismissal here and governs comparable pending disputes under that trigger.

## Separate Writings

None.

## Procedure After Action

The district court must proceed to Clay's unresolved collateral grounds under the now-settled timeliness of his motion.

## Event

North Jersey Media Group, Inc. v. Ashcroft; review of 308 F.3d 198 (Third Circuit). Merits decision, March 4, 2003. The Court reviews the reversal of a preliminary injunction against categorical closure of deportation hearings designated by the Attorney General as special-interest matters. The questions concern qualified public and press access, supported security closure, and the preliminary remedy. The directive followed September 11, 2001; the Court of Appeals decided the case on October 8, 2002.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment is vacated and the case is remanded.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Vacated and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas.

The appellate judgment denying the constitutional access right is vacated. The lower courts must resolve the remaining preliminary-relief predicates, protected closure applications and the necessary reach of relief under the qualified-access rule. The existing nationwide preliminary decree is not automatically restored.

## Opinion Topology

Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court. Complete-opinion coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer. Stone-Zsela also files a concurring opinion. O’Connor files a dissenting opinion, joined by Scalia and Thomas. The six majority Justices join every controlling proposition; Stone-Zsela’s concurrence adds a remedial proposal without withdrawing any join.

## Holdings

### Qualified access to adversarial removal hearings

**Holding and operative rule.** The public and press have a qualified First Amendment right to attend adversarial removal hearings adjudicating deportation of persons already within the country. The history-and-function inquiry examines that kind of adjudication; an Executive designation of selected hearings as special-interest matters does not create a separate category whose asserted secrecy defeats the right.

**Authority.** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined by the six-Justice complete-opinion coalition identified in Opinion Topology, directly controls all three holdings.

**Controlling explanation.** Richmond Newspapers connects public access to informed oversight; Press-Enterprise and El Vocero direct attention to the history and function of the adjudicative proceeding. Deportation hearings have an established regulatory presumption of openness, adversarial safeguards, presentation of evidence and an adjudicator deciding serious consequences for individuals. Public observation promotes accuracy, fairness and accountability. The history is shorter and less uniform than that of criminal trials, but its convergence with this developed adjudicative function establishes qualified protection.

The government and dissent emphasize the administrative setting and historical exceptions. A regulation alone would not create a constitutional right, and useful oversight alone would not establish one. Here actual practice and the trial-like proceeding support each other. Exceptions for sensitive information fit a qualified right. The experience inquiry concerns this structural adjudication, not a recently designated security subset. Both general risks and benefits inform the function inquiry; particular investigative dangers must also receive their full weight when closure is sought. Woodford confirms that history and function may extend protection beyond the criminal courtroom, while its prison-specific application of Turner concerns a different setting. This holding governs access to the hearings, with the protected closure process stated next.

### Protected security review rather than designation alone

**Holding and operative rule.** Closure requires supported findings that an overriding interest is likely to be prejudiced, that the restriction is narrowly fitted to protect it, and that reasonable alternatives have been considered. Officials may establish that even a whole hearing requires closure through protected submissions and findings; a special-interest designation alone is insufficient.

**Controlling explanation.** El Vocero requires a particularized justification for restricting adjudicative access. National security can provide an overriding interest. The government’s account of cumulative intelligence risks deserves substantial evidentiary weight: innocent fragments may expose investigative sources, methods, entry routes, compromised networks or gaps in official knowledge.

The strongest objection is that explaining why one hearing should close can reveal its identity or its place in a larger investigation. The rule permits an initial sealed application, protected findings and justified temporary restriction while prompt review occurs. Common investigative evidence may support multiple applications; the adjudicator must assess its connection to the hearing or portions withheld. An individual determination therefore can consider the investigation as a whole. Protection of identities, partial closure, redaction and delayed disclosure must be considered where reasonable, and may be rejected on supported grounds that they would be ineffective. Adequate findings must permit meaningful review while protecting sensitive information. Expertise receives substantial weight but cannot replace that determination. The directive’s categorical designation and generalized explanation fail because they substitute a label for supported application of necessity and fit, rather than establish which protected proceeding requires what restriction.

### Preliminary protection and the scope of remand

## Event

United States v. Navajo Nation, No. 01-1375, 537 U.S. 488. Merits decision, March 4, 2003. Argued December 2, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, 263 F.3d 1325.

The question is whether the Indian Mineral Leasing Act and the regulations governing this coal lease impose the particular fiduciary duty necessary for the Nation's monetary claim concerning the Secretary's approval and handling of the royalty amendment. The Federal Circuit reversed dismissal by the Court of Federal Claims. The United States seeks rejection of the asserted compensable duty; the Nation seeks adjudication of the economic loss allegedly caused by the Secretary's intervention.

The 1964 Peabody lease initially provided a royalty of 37.5 cents per ton, approximately two percent of gross proceeds. A Bureau of Indian Affairs official ordered a twenty-percent royalty in 1984. During administrative review, Peabody met privately with Secretary Hodel, who directed delay and encouraged negotiations. The Nation alleges that, without knowing of that intervention, it negotiated the 12.5-percent arrangement approved in 1987 and lost the opportunity for a better economic result. The allegations, the preliminary administrative rate, and the later bargain are distinct from proof of breach or loss.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the Federal Circuit and remands for proceedings consistent with rejection of the asserted IMLA damages theory.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Reversed and remanded, 5–4.**

**Supporting:** Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter.

The Federal Circuit's determination that the identified IMLA approval regime supports the asserted monetary duty is reversed.

## Opinion Topology

Ginsburg delivers the signed Opinion of the Court.

## Holdings

### The asserted IMLA approval duty does not support this damages claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A tribal claim under the Indian Tucker Act requires a particular statutory or regulatory obligation that can fairly be interpreted to support compensation for breach. The conduct-date Indian Mineral Leasing Act and coal-leasing regulations governing this lease do not impose the asserted compensable fiduciary duty concerning the Secretary's approval and handling of the negotiated royalty amendment; the Nation's damages theory under those provisions therefore fails.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court, adopted in full by the five-member complete-opinion coalition identified in Opinion Topology. All five adopt the source requirement, the construction of this approval regime, and reversal on that ground.

**Controlling explanation:** Mitchell I and Mitchell II require the Court to identify the undertaking established by the governing law before drawing monetary consequences from trust principles. The Indian Tucker Act supplies a waiver and forum, not the substantive obligation. The IMLA leaves the commercial bargain to the Tribe and the lessee, subject to Secretarial approval and the applicable royalty provisions. Those terms assign a different responsibility from the beneficial resource management established in Mitchell II. They do not establish the compensable obligation asserted here.

The Nation's strongest argument concerns the Secretary's alleged secret intervention for Peabody, not simply disappointment with the negotiated price. The dissent finds a bounded loyalty duty in the legally necessary approval and review function. But identifying an official's power to affect bargaining does not by itself establish the fiduciary undertaking on which this monetary claim depends. The claim still requires a duty in the applicable enactment or regulations, and the identified approval and royalty terms do not provide it. Protective purpose and alleged misconduct cannot enlarge those terms into the asserted compensation obligation.

The Court applies that source inquiry to this particular leasing regime; comprehensive management identical to Mitchell II is not a universal prerequisite for every fiduciary claim. Rejection of this damages theory is not a determination that the alleged intervention was lawful.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535 (Mitchell I): applies its requirement to identify the particular responsibilities supplied by the governing statute rather than infer them from a general trust relationship.
- United States v. Mitchell, 463 U.S. 206 (Mitchell II): preserves monetary liability for source-defined fiduciary duties; distinguishes the beneficial management assigned there from the approval and royalty provisions governing this coal lease. The difference is the particular undertaking, not a universal demand for an identical management scheme.

## Law After Decision

The identified IMLA coal-lease approval and royalty provisions do not support the compensable fiduciary duty asserted in this claim. The Indian Tucker Act's waiver remains distinct from the substantive source; alleged interference with tribal bargaining must be tied to a legally established duty before this monetary liability can follow. That source-specific determination governs the case on remand.

## Separate Writings

Souter dissents. In Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, Stevens, and O'Connor, he reads mandatory approval under §396a and the operative review authority as a specific protective responsibility. The Tribe's power to negotiate does not eliminate the federal responsibility retained at the approval stage. The Secretary must exercise that function loyally and may not secretly use it to favor the lessee, then knowingly approve a bargain that his own intervention unfairly distorted. That duty protects the Tribe's bargaining rather than making the Government its commercial manager.

Mitchell requires an enacted source; the required approval and review function provides it at this defined scope. General trust principles explain the loyalty incident of that function. The alleged private intervention, delay, and resulting bargain require adjudication. Possible authority to reject the preliminary twenty-percent adjustment does not answer a claim of disloyal administration, because the claim does not depend on an assured right to that precise rate. The Nation must prove breach, the economic result the lawful process would have produced, and compensable loss. The dissent would affirm the availability of that claim and remand for those determinations.

In Part II, joined by Stevens and O'Connor, Souter explains a further protective constraint on approval: the Secretary should withhold approval when there is good reason to doubt that the negotiated royalty falls within the range of reasonable market rates. Tiger, Sunderland, and Anicker explain the protective function of federal approval; Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe and Kerr-McGee identify the revenue-protective purposes of mineral leasing alongside tribal autonomy. The two purposes must operate together. This safeguard would require scrutiny of a materially suspect bargain while leaving negotiation with the Tribe. A reasonable-range inquiry is not a guarantee of the highest attainable royalty; the value of the lawful overall bargain and any resulting loss still require proof.

## Procedure After Action

The case is remanded to implement the Court's source-specific rejection of the IMLA damages theory in the ordinary course of the preserved proceedings.

## Event

## Holdings

### I. The federal remedy displaces the conflicting homestead exemption

**Holding and operative rule:** When any part of real property is used or intended to be used to commit or facilitate a violation of 21 U.S.C. chapter 13, subchapter I, punishable by more than one year's imprisonment, Oklahoma's homestead exemption cannot bar the forfeiture authorized by the offense-date §881(a)(7). The statute protects an owner's interest to the extent the owner establishes that the relevant act or omission occurred without that owner's knowledge or consent; constitutional limits also govern, and state law identifies the property interest.

**Authority:** Thomas's signed Opinion of the Court has this complete nine-Justice coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. Every member supports both holdings and their remedy in full.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the asserted exemption conflicts with the operation of the federal statute. Section 881(a)(7) covers real property, including land, appurtenances, improvements and leasehold interests, subject to the stated offense condition and ownership defense. Section 903 preserves state law except where positive conflict prevents the two rules from consistently standing together. An absolute homestead immunity would prevent the United States from using its authorized remedy against the same qualifying interest.

Cipollone and Geier require attention to the operative federal rule and actual conflict, rather than a general demand for national uniformity. Sprietsma rejected an inference of federal immunity from an agency's decision not to require equipment. Here Congress affirmatively authorized forfeiture on stated conditions. The exemption would defeat that authority. A State may define the ownership interest without supplying an additional immunity against its federally authorized forfeiture. Lees's homestead argument therefore fails, while the separate constitutional limit on the amount forfeited must still be applied.

### II. The punitive forfeiture requires findings that permit a real proportionality judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** A forfeiture under §881(a)(7) violates the Excessive Fines Clause when grossly disproportionate to the gravity of the wrongdoing for which it is imposed. Lees bears the burden of establishing constitutional excessiveness; the court must make supported findings on the material relationship between the property interest and value, actual offense, owner involvement, culpability, harm, benefit and pertinent penalties, then reach a fresh constitutional conclusion, reviewed independently on appeal with supported subsidiary facts reviewed for clear error.

**Authority:** The same unanimous Opinion of the Court directly adopts the gross-disproportionality rule, review standards and limited findings remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the present conclusory ruling cannot sustain constitutional review. Austin subjects this statutory category to the Excessive Fines Clause and separates statutory facilitation from actual excessiveness. Bajakajian supplies gross disproportionality, substantial respect for legislative punishment judgments, and the distinction between independent legal review and clear-error review of facts. Mackby applies that distinction to a developed comparison of the actual exaction and supported wrongdoing, with liability intact while the constitutional amount is determined.

The jury's rejection of Lees's statutory defense establishes neither the home's constitutional valuation nor the degree of her involvement that supports the amount. The reported $136,000 appraisal is evidence, not a completed finding. Drug distribution and knowing facilitation are serious, and the property's use may be important; a sufficient nexus alone does not resolve whether forfeiting the entire interest is excessive. The grandson's conduct cannot automatically become Lees's culpability, and her separate earlier Valium conviction cannot automatically supply the gravity of the present forfeiture offense.

The remand court must address each consideration that materially affects this comparison, using adequate existing evidence and additional proceedings only when necessary and procedurally permissible. The listed considerations are not an exhaustive checklist, and a maximum-fine comparison alone is not conclusive. The missing findings prevent a present determination that the whole exaction is either proportionate or excessive. The court must preserve effective relief under its existing procedural authority and complete the findings and constitutional decision before enforcing the final exaction.

## Event

United States v. White Mountain Apache Tribe, No. 01-1067, 537 U.S. 465. Merits decision, March 4, 2003. Argued December 2, 2002. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, 249 F.3d 1364.

The question is whether the 1960 statute placing Fort Apache property in trust while reserving federal use supports a monetary claim under the Indian Tucker Act for breach of a duty to preserve the property under federal use or control. The Federal Circuit reversed the Court of Federal Claims' jurisdictional dismissal. The United States seeks dismissal; the Tribe seeks further proceedings on its claim for deterioration of the occupied trust property.

Congress expressly created the trust and reserved use for administrative or school purposes. The Government used buildings within that property. The Tribe alleges that the controlled buildings deteriorated and seeks restoration costs. The challenged judgment concerns the availability of the monetary claim.

## Participation

All nine Justices participated: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, and Breyer.

## Public Action

The Court affirms the Federal Circuit and remands for further proceedings in the Court of Federal Claims.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed and remanded, 6–3.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer.

**Opposing:** Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas.

The Tribe's monetary preservation claim may proceed under the 1960 Act and the Indian Tucker Act.

## Opinion Topology

Souter delivers the signed Opinion of the Court.

| Writing | Author | Coalition | Relationship and scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Souter | Complete-opinion coalition: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer | Full joins to the statutory preservation duty, fair inference of compensation, and remand. |
| Dissent | Kennedy | Kennedy; joined by Scalia and Thomas | Would reverse and require dismissal of this monetary maintenance claim for lack of the requisite substantive undertaking. |

## Holdings

### Preservation of the controlled trust property

**Holding and operative rule:** The 1960 Fort Apache Act's express trust, coupled with the United States' retained and exercised use or control of the trust property, imposes a duty to exercise reasonable care to preserve the property subject to that use or control. The federal reservation of use operates within the trust and carries that preservation responsibility for the controlled assets.

**Authority:** The opinion of the Court, adopted in full by the six-member complete-opinion coalition in Opinion Topology. That coalition directly adopts the defined duty, its statutory basis, and its application to the pleaded claim.

**Controlling explanation:** Congress placed the identified land and improvements in trust for the Tribe while allowing the Secretary to use them for administrative or school purposes for as long as needed. The Government exercised that authority over the property at issue. A trustee using the beneficiary's corpus must take reasonable care to preserve what it controls. Here that responsibility gives effect to the statutory relationship Congress created; it is not inferred from federal influence over tribal affairs generally.

Mitchell I found no federal timber-management responsibility where the allotment scheme left use and management with the allottees. Mitchell II recognized fiduciary duties where enacted provisions assigned resource management to the United States. The same source-specific inquiry applies here. The express trust identifies the beneficiary and corpus; the retained use identifies the Government's responsibility for the assets it occupies or controls. The dissent treats that use as a limitation on the beneficial estate without a maintenance undertaking. But the property remains trust property during federal use. Reading the reservation as permission to allow the controlled corpus to deteriorate would sever the retained authority from the trust that qualifies it.

Common-law preservation principles explain the content of that established undertaking. They do not replace the statute as its source. The claims court must identify the assets and periods of federal control and apply the reasonable-preservation duty to them.

### Compensation for breach of the defined duty

**Holding and operative rule:** The identified statutory preservation duty can fairly be interpreted to require monetary compensation for loss caused by its breach. The Indian Tucker Act supplies the waiver and forum, and the 1960 Act supplies the substantive duty; together they permit the Tribe's claim to proceed to adjudication of breach, causation, and compensable loss.

**Authority:** The same complete-opinion coalition adopts the compensation holding, the source-first sequence, and the remand in full.

**Controlling explanation:** Mitchell II separates the waiver of sovereign immunity from the substantive source of a claim. Once the Indian Tucker Act supplies the waiver, the substantive enactment need not repeat an unequivocal consent to suit or an express damages clause. The inquiry is whether the duty established by that enactment fairly supports compensation for its breach. A preservation obligation concerning identified trust assets does so: a prospective command to care for property cannot compensate loss already caused by a trustee's neglect.

Testan and Sheehan preserve the requirement of a substantive compensation source; their nontrust regimes gave reasons against the monetary remedies sought there. They do not require Congress to state an express damages remedy for every established fiduciary undertaking. Nor does the dissent's point that ordinary trustees may obtain indemnification for proper expenses defeat jurisdiction over alleged breach-caused deterioration. The funding of proper upkeep and the measure of loss from breach are distinct questions.

On remand, the claims court must determine what reasonable preservation required for the identified assets, whether the Government breached that duty, what deterioration the breach caused, and the supported monetary loss. A historic-preservation restoration standard is not adopted. The significance of trustee indemnification from the corpus for proper maintenance and the funding responsibility of a rent-free federal occupant remain for adjudication because the jurisdictional record does not resolve those questions. Those limits concern the duty's application and measure, not whether this statutory relationship supports a monetary claim.

## Precedent Treatment

- United States v. Mitchell, 445 U.S. 535 (Mitchell I): preserves its requirement to examine the actual statutory undertaking; the bare-title relationship there is distinguished from the Government's use of this expressly entrusted corpus.
- United States v. Mitchell, 463 U.S. 206 (Mitchell II): applies its separation of waiver, source-defined fiduciary duty, and monetary compensation; extends that method to the preservation responsibility attached to this express trust and retained use.
- United States v. Testan, 424 U.S. 392: preserves the need for a substantive source fairly supporting compensation; its federal classification and pay regime is distinguished from this established trust duty.
- United States v. Sheehan, 456 U.S. 728: distinguishes the nontrust monetary scheme there while preserving its requirement that substantive law support the claimed payment.

## Law After Decision

An express statutory trust over identified tribal property, coupled with the Government's retained and exercised use or control, carries reasonable preservation responsibility for that controlled property and supports compensation for breach-caused loss. The waiver/source distinction remains intact. Lower courts must identify the legally entrusted assets and responsibility before applying trust principles, then determine breach and loss under the established duty.

## Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents. The 1960 Act reserves federal use but does not undertake maintenance or beneficial management comparable to the legal responsibilities in Mitchell II. The reservation can qualify the Tribe's beneficial estate without committing the United States to repair the property at public expense. Practical occupation, however extensive, cannot itself supply the missing enacted duty.

The dissent also objects to moving from a preservation obligation to personal funding responsibility without identifying the terms that support it. Ordinary trustees may be indemnified from the corpus for proper upkeep. In the dissent's view, Testan and Mitchell require a substantive undertaking before either fiduciary duties or monetary consequences can be inferred; the majority's use-based approach supplies too much through common-law analogy. The dissent would dismiss this damages theory.

The case returns through the Federal Circuit to the Court of Federal Claims for adjudication of the Tribe's monetary claim under the holdings above.

## Event

Ewing v. California, No. 01-6978, 538 U.S. 11. Argued November 5, 2002; decided March 5, 2003. Direct review of the California Court of Appeal's April 25, 2001 judgment in No. B143745 sustaining the sentence; the California Supreme Court denied review. The question is whether twenty-five years to life for theft of three golf clubs worth approximately $1,197, with Ewing's prior robbery and three residential burglaries, is grossly disproportionate.

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission or argument and at decision. No recusal or other nonparticipation is identified.

## Public Action

The Court reverses the sentence and remands for lawful resentencing.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Sentence reversed and remanded for lawful resentencing, 5–4. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The theft conviction remains intact. California must replace the twenty-five-years-to-life sentence with a lawful proportionate sentence that may substantially account for the established priors. The state remand process must provide a reasonable, enforceable resentencing schedule. A new lawful sentencing judgment establishes compliance; the invalid punishment may not be repeated under another label. The surviving conviction and any valid custody authority govern custody during implementation.

## Holdings

### The actual sentence is grossly disproportionate despite the serious prior record

**Holding and operative rule:** The Eighth Amendment's narrow gross-disproportionality principle prohibits this twenty-five-years-to-life sentence for Ewing's nonviolent theft of approximately $1,197, even after substantial weight is given to his prior robbery and three residential burglaries. In applying that principle to this recidivist sentence, the present offense, the established prior record and the actual minimum before parole eligibility must be assessed together: recidivism aggravates the gravity of the offense but cannot replace it; the challenger must establish gross disproportionality rather than a preferable sentence.

**Authority:** Stevens's Opinion of the Court has this complete coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. All five directly adopt this as-applied holding, its actual-offense and actual-severity grounds, and sentence-only remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the quarter-century minimum is constitutionally excessive on this record. Rummel permits severe recidivist punishment and gives meaningful parole availability significance. Solem preserves a proportionality limit despite prior convictions. Harmelin's narrow proportionality reasoning, adopted by only part of that Court, accords substantial legislative latitude; it does not erase the surviving limit. Henderson applies that limit to actual offense, offender and release conditions. Its first conviction, tiny completed sale and absence of ordinary parole materially differ from Ewing's case, so its outcome alone does not decide this one.

California may punish repeated serious offending more severely and use incapacitation to protect the public. Ewing's robbery and burglaries therefore carry substantial weight; he is not being sentenced as a first-time shoplifter. But the new crime remains nonviolent theft of three golf clubs, and twenty-five years is the earliest ordinary parole eligibility, not assured release. Giving the priors their full force still leaves an exceptional mismatch between the actual current offense and that minimum with a possible life term. The prior crimes do not transform this theft into a new robbery or burglary.

The dissent's incapacitation rationale explains a substantial enhancement, but does not establish a constitutionally sufficient relation for every duration. Nor does the sentencing judge's discretion to reduce the felony classification or dismiss strikes validate the sentence actually imposed. The constitutional limit requires judgment about degree; that feature does not justify abandoning the protection retained in Solem and the narrow Harmelin approach. California must impose a lawful replacement sentence that may substantially reflect the serious priors. The holding concerns this offense, offender and punishment combination.

## Precedent Treatment

- Rummel v. Estelle: applied for the legitimacy of substantial recidivist enhancement and the relevance of actual parole availability; its result does not make all enhanced terms interchangeable.
- Solem v. Helm: applied for an enforceable gross-disproportionality limit despite recidivism and attention to the actual offense and punishment.
- Harmelin v. Michigan: its narrow proportionality rationale is accurately attributed to the Justices who adopted it; its noncapital individualized-sentencing holding remains intact, and its serious drug-offense result does not settle this recidivist theft application.
- Henderson v. Norris: applied for actual-offense, actual-offender and release analysis, while distinguishing its first-offender, tiny-sale and no-ordinary-parole combination.

## Law After Decision

The narrow proportionality principle invalidates this offense, serious-prior-record and minimum-sentence combination. Recidivism receives substantial weight while the present offense and actual release minimum retain constitutional significance. California retains authority to impose a substantial lawful enhancement; the replacement term is determined through resentencing under that limit.

## Separate Writings

**Kennedy, dissenting, joined by O'Connor.** The narrow proportionality principle requires exceptional restraint in reviewing legislatively authorized prison terms. Ewing's current felony theft follows robbery and repeated residential burglary, permitting California to give substantial force to incapacitation after lesser sanctions failed. The actual serious recidivism and ordinary parole eligibility distinguish Henderson's first tiny-sale conviction and permanent imprisonment. Kennedy and O'Connor acknowledge the long minimum and nonviolent present theft, but find no initial inference of gross disproportionality requiring additional comparisons. They would affirm this sentence without validating every recidivist punishment or abandoning the constitutional limit.

**Scalia, dissenting.** The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause does not provide the asserted constitutional right to proportionality in prison duration. His textual and historical position in Harmelin remains his ground. Choosing among incapacitation, deterrence and retribution, and then balancing those purposes against a term of years, supplies no administrable constitutional measure on his account. The punishment may be challenged as sentencing policy, but the proposed proportionality inquiry does not establish the asserted right. His acceptance of monetary excessiveness review in Bajakajian does not supply a prison-term proportionality rule. He would affirm on this distinct ground and does not join the other dissent's application.

**Thomas, dissenting.** Thomas likewise rejects a constitutional prison-duration proportionality requirement. His objection is not limited to the difficulty of administering a test: even an administrable formulation would not require him to retain Solem under stare decisis. His conditional application in Henderson assumed the framework for that case and did not adopt its foundation or commit him to applying it in every later case. He would affirm because the asserted right does not exist on his reading, and joins neither the Court's proportionality holding nor Kennedy's application of that principle.

## Procedure After Action

The case returns for resentencing under the stated remand schedule and constitutional rule. Unjustified failure to resentence is subject to enforcement through available judicial process; the theft conviction remains in force.

Helm, 463 U.S. 277; Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957. The sentence permits consideration for parole only after the actual minimum; it does not assure release then.

## Event

Lockyer v. Andrade, No. 01-1127, 538 U.S. 63. Argued November 5, 2002; decided March 5, 2003. Review of the Ninth Circuit's judgment, 270 F.3d 743, granting federal habeas sentence relief after the District Court denied it. The underlying state merits affirmance occurred in 1997. The question is whether sustaining consecutive twenty-five-years-to-life terms for two low-value thefts unreasonably applied the then-established Eighth Amendment proportionality principle under §2254(d)(1).

## Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participate at submission or argument and at decision. No recusal or other nonparticipation is identified.

## Public Action

The Court affirms entitlement to conditional sentence relief and remands for its implementation.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Entitlement to conditional sentence relief affirmed; remanded for implementation, 5–4. Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. **Opposing:** O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.

The Ninth Circuit's judgment granting sentence relief is affirmed on the objective-unreasonableness and independent constitutional grounds stated here. Both theft convictions remain intact. The District Court must implement the conditional sentence-only writ prescribed in Holding III.

## Opinion Topology

Souter delivers the signed Opinion of the Court.

## Holdings

### I. Objective statutory review is separate from the constitutional merits

**Holding and operative rule:** For a claim decided on the merits in state court, §2254(d)(1) requires a decision contrary to this Court's clearly established governing holdings or an objectively unreasonable application of them; an independently incorrect result alone is insufficient. The relevant substantive holdings are those established at the state merits adjudication, a general principle may clearly establish law without a factual twin, and entitlement to a writ additionally requires the underlying constitutional violation and appropriate relief.

**Authority:** Part I of Souter's Opinion of the Court has this complete nine-Justice coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer. The statutory framework has nine direct supporters; the later application and relief do not.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the state adjudication must be assessed as a whole. Terry Williams separates the contrary-to and unreasonable-application clauses and requires objective legal judgment rather than a count of judges. Bell preserves the additional need to establish the actual constitutional violation. Early rejects a citation-based defect and a mandatory factual-twin requirement; Visciotti requires the whole supported record and actual analysis rather than isolated shorthand or nominally correct terminology.

Those current review-method decisions do not supply substantive proportionality law newly available to the state court in 1997. Rummel, Solem and Harmelin furnish the earlier substantive law used here. The state court's recidivism reasoning and the two separate offenses must receive their full weight. The Ninth Circuit's clear-error formulation is not the statutory test, but correcting that formulation does not itself decide whether the state result falls within the range the earlier law permits.

### II. Sustaining the actual fifty-year minimum unreasonably applied the established principle

**Holding and operative rule:** The state court's decision sustaining two consecutive twenty-five-years-to-life terms for these two nonviolent thefts totaling approximately $150, with the established serious prior burglaries fully considered, objectively unreasonably applied the preexisting gross-disproportionality principle. The actual fifty-year minimum must be evaluated as imposed, including the separate criminal occasions and prior record, rather than divided into terms whose combined severity escapes review.

**Authority:** Part II of Souter's Opinion of the Court has this complete coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg and Breyer. These five directly support the unreasonable-application ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this is an extreme application beyond the latitude preserved by the earlier cases. Rummel sustained severe recidivist punishment with meaningful earlier parole availability. Solem enforced a proportionality limit for a recidivist property offender and distinguished ordinary parole from executive grace. Harmelin sustained punishment for a materially different serious drug offense while preserving the narrow principle through the Justices who accepted it. These distinctions leave considerable legislative choice, but they also give the general limit practical content.

The State's strongest ground is the serious burglary history, two distinct new thefts and incapacitation of a persistent offender. The Court accepts those predicates and assesses the full state rationale, including the possibility of eventual parole. They justify substantial punishment. They do not reasonably explain a fifty-year minimum for the actual two low-value thefts. Separate occasions support separate punishment, but repeating the same incapacitation premise does not make the additional twenty-five-year block automatically reasonable: the second minor theft does not, without further justification, double the already asserted long-term danger.

The dissent regards this as an area left contestable by the earlier cases. The Court instead finds their existing limit exceeded by the actual aggregate, not merely a sentence it would prefer to reduce. The conclusion depends on neither a missing citation nor an identical earlier fact pattern. It follows from the established distinctions concerning offense gravity, recidivism and real release conditions applied to this extraordinary combination.

### III. The constitutional violation requires a conditional sentence-only writ

**Holding and operative rule:** The actual fifty-year minimum for these two low-value nonviolent thefts is grossly disproportionate even with the serious priors and separate occasions fully considered. Andrade is entitled to a conditional writ requiring California, within a reasonable period fixed by the District Court, to impose a lawful proportionate sentence or end custody attributable to the invalid punishment; both theft convictions remain intact.

**Authority:** Part III of Souter's Opinion of the Court has the same five-Justice coalition as Part II. These Justices independently find the underlying Eighth Amendment violation and adopt the conditional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that satisfying §2254(d)(1) is not itself a substitute for deciding constitutional entitlement. Solem's actual-offense proportionality, Rummel's recidivism and parole distinctions, and Harmelin's narrow constitutional limit govern the merits as well. On the supported record, the exceptional disparity between these two thefts and the actual half-century minimum persists after substantial weight is assigned to the serious priors. That same supported disparity establishes the violation as a separate legal conclusion.

California retains responsibility for selecting a lawful replacement sentence under its valid recidivist law and the constitutional limit. The District Court must hear the parties, fix a reasonable compliance period, and enter a conditional order specifying resentencing and proof of compliance. The State must file a lawful new judgment or establish that custody attributable to the invalid punishment has ended. If it fails to comply in time, the writ must operate against that custody; any independent valid custody basis must be identified separately. This corrects Andrade's aggregate punishment while preserving the convictions.

## Precedent Treatment

- Rummel v. Estelle: applied for legitimate recidivist punishment and the constitutional significance of actual parole availability; its earlier meaningful release conditions distinguish its result.
- Solem v. Helm: applied as preexisting enforceable proportionality law for a recidivist property sentence, with actual conduct and release conditions central to the comparison.
- Harmelin v. Michigan: its serious drug-offense result is distinguished and its narrow proportionality rationale is attributed to the Justices adopting it; neither the result nor the fracture makes the surviving principle unenforceable.
- Williams v. Taylor (Terry Williams): applied for the separate statutory clauses, objective unreasonableness, general governing holdings and distinct actual constitutional entitlement.
- Bell v. Cone: applied to preserve objective statutory review and the independent merits requirement.
- Early v. Packer: applied to the actual contextual adjudication; lack of citations or a factual twin does not decide statutory eligibility.
- Woodford v. Visciotti: applied to require whole-opinion and whole-record evaluation; its reasonable prejudice assessment is distinguished from the extreme proportionality application here.
- Henderson v. Norris: its later, pre-AEDPA sentence holding does not supply substantive law established at Andrade's earlier state adjudication; its separate temporal and review premises remain effective.

## Law After Decision

Section 2254(d)(1) remains an objective restriction distinct from the underlying constitutional claim. This actual fifty-year minimum for the two low-value thefts, with the serious priors and separate occasions considered, crosses both boundaries. Courts must assess the sentence as imposed and identify the earlier substantive holding before independently establishing the statutory defect, actual violation and available remedy. Present review-method cases do not move the date of the substantive law governing the state adjudication.

## Separate Writings

**O'Connor, concurring in Part I and dissenting from Parts II–III, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas.** The dissent accepts the objective §2254(d)(1) inquiry, the 1997 substantive-law baseline and the need to consider the actual fifty-year aggregate. It would reverse federal relief because Rummel, Solem and Harmelin leave this recidivist application within the range of reasonable state adjudication. The serious burglaries, two separate theft occasions and retained parole possibility allow substantial incapacitative punishment even where the present offenses are low-value and nonviolent. Solem's permanent-imprisonment result and Harmelin's different offense do not make the exact state application objectively indefensible.

The dissent does not demand a factual twin or treat the general principle as categorically incapable of supporting relief. It concludes that this particular application remains contestable within the law's permitted range, which is different from approving the punishment on independent direct review. Rejecting the Ninth Circuit's clear-error formulation is therefore only part of its analysis; the state result also survives the correct statutory standard on its assessment. Scalia and Thomas join this statutory ground without adopting the prison-duration proportionality foundation they reject. They apply the existing holdings in measuring the statutory constraint rather than removing those holdings from the inquiry.

The case returns through the Court of Appeals to the District Court for the conditional order and implementation specified in Holding III.

## Event

Tenafly Eruv Association, Inc. v. Borough of Tenafly; review of 309 F.3d 144 (Third Circuit). Merits decision, March 5, 2003. The Court reviews the reversal of the denial of preliminary relief against removal of the association’s lechis, small markers attached to utility poles to complete an eruv boundary. The question is whether selective enforcement against those religious attachments, while comparable secular and other religious attachments are tolerated, violates the Free Exercise Clause and warrants interim protection.

## Participation

Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg and Breyer participated.

## Public Action

The judgment directing preliminary protection is affirmed, and the case is remanded for its implementation and further proceedings.

## Judgment & Remedy

**Affirmed, 9–0.**

**Supporting:** Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer.

The association is entitled to preliminary protection against removal under the challenged selective enforcement practice. The case returns for entry or maintenance of fitted interim relief and ordinary merits proceedings. The protection preserves the identified attachments while permitting supported, comparably applied safety and property requirements and genuinely evenhanded prospective regulation.

## Opinion Topology

Scalia delivers the Opinion of the Court. Complete-opinion coalition, including the author: Stone-Zsela, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer. All nine Justices join the complete rule, application and interim disposition.

## Holdings

### Selective administration of an attachment ordinance

**Holding and operative rule.** An attachment ordinance is not neutral and generally applicable when its actual administration materially singles out religious exercise while tolerating substantial comparable secular or other religious conduct presenting the harms invoked to justify enforcement. Once the association substantiates that selectivity, the Borough must show a compelling interest and narrow tailoring; comparability turns on the asserted safety, property and visual risks, including size or duration insofar as they affect those risks.

**Authority.** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, joined by all nine Justices identified in Opinion Topology, directly controls this holding and the following holding.

**Controlling explanation.** Lukumi examines text, exceptions and operation and requires strict justification when comparable harms remain substantially unregulated. Its rule reaches informal exemptions and administration as well as express statutory exceptions. Smith protects genuinely neutral, generally applicable regulation; facially broad wording cannot supply those qualities to a selectively administered rule.

The Borough tolerated house numbers, notices, school ribbons, church-related signs and seasonal displays while insisting on removal of the privately maintained eruv markers. The lechis burden no asserted municipal interest merely because they have religious significance. The Borough invokes differences between temporary notices and permanent ritual markers, but permanent house numbers answer a generalized permanence defense, and the supported record supplies no harm-related distinction adequate to explain this practice. Dimensions, placement, ownership and duration remain relevant when connected to actual risks; comparability does not require identity in every physical characteristic. The tolerated comparable uses undermine both the claimed necessity of the selective burden and its tailoring.

The continuing interference with Sabbath observance supports irreparable harm. The attachments are inconspicuous, and the supplied preliminary record identifies no supported countervailing harm outweighing that interference. Those circumstances, together with likely success under Lukumi, sustain preliminary protection. The interim order must address the selective removal practice while preserving lawful neutral controls. The association’s independent expressive-speech classification lies outside the granted Free Exercise determination and is unnecessary to the requested protection.

### Equal treatment of private exercise and the Establishment Clause

**Holding and operative rule.** Applying the same actual attachment permissions to privately organized religious exercise does not make that exercise the Borough’s religious activity. The Establishment Clause therefore supplies no justification for this selective removal of the eruv markers.

**Controlling explanation.** Agostini requires identification of the governmental act and the function it performs, applying established permissible relationships on their own terms. Good News Club distinguishes administration of private access from governmental religious adoption; Pinette and Chabad likewise assess actual attribution. Here the association maintains the religious boundary, the utilities consented to the attachments, and the Borough’s relevant act is administration of its attachment rule. Tolerating comparable private markers does not give the Borough responsibility for their religious significance. The asserted risk of appearing to endorse the eruv cannot turn private religion’s presence into a sufficient reason for unequal exclusion.

Marshfield concerned continuing governmental presentation of a religious centerpiece; this privately maintained boundary presents a different function. Utility consent informs the property setting, while the Borough retains its lawful regulatory authority. Equal treatment under the actual permissions reconciles Free Exercise protection with Agostini’s governmental-function inquiry.

## Precedent Treatment

- Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah: applied to objective selective administration, tolerated comparable harms and the resulting compelling-interest and narrow-tailoring requirements.
- Employment Division v. Smith: its rule remains applicable to genuinely neutral and generally applicable regulation; this actual practice fails those predicates.
- Agostini v. Felton: applied through its governmental-attribution and function inquiry and established private-use relationships.
- Good News Club, Pinette and Chabad: applied to distinguish permitted private religious use from official religious adoption.
- Marshfield: distinguished because continuing municipal religious presentation differs from the private eruv arrangement here.

## Law After Decision

Actual administration governs religious neutrality. Municipalities must justify selective burdens on religious attachments under strict scrutiny when substantial comparable uses are tolerated, and the comparison must follow the real safety, property and visual interests invoked. Equal treatment of this privately maintained eruv is compatible with the Establishment Clause. The association receives interim protection under those rules while the merits and any permanent relief proceed.

## Separate Writings

None.

## Procedure After Action

On remand the district court must enter or maintain preliminary relief suspending the challenged removal direction and requiring the responsible Borough officials to administer it accordingly. Existing markers remain subject to supported, comparably applied safety requirements. The court may enforce its interim decree while resolving the merits and any permanent remedy; a changed policy must be assessed by its actual treatment of comparable risks.
