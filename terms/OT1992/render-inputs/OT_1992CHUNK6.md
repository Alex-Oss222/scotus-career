## United States v. Olano — 1993-04-26

### Event

United States v. Olano; No. 91-1306; October Term 1992. Argued: 1992-12-09; decided 1993-04-26. Source locator: 507 U.S. 725 (1993). Ninth Circuit, 934 F.2d 1425 (1991). Certiorari review of the identified judgment.

Question: Whether the unobjected-to presence of instructed alternate jurors during deliberations permits reversal under Rule 52(b), and how error, plainness, substantial rights and remedial discretion differ.

Two alternates remained during deliberations after a three-month joint trial; the court instructed them not to participate. One later left at his request. Counsel did not make a timely objection. The Government concedes error and plainness on the assumption that Rule 24(c) was not waivable; the Court does not decide that assumption. No participation or chilling effect was shown, and respondents did not request a hearing. The Ninth Circuit separately reversed convictions for insufficiency, then vacated the remaining convictions under a per se alternate-presence rule and did not reach other appellate issues.

### Holdings

#### Forfeiture, waiver and the limits on plain-error authority

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 52(b) permits correction of a forfeited legal error only if it is plain and affects substantial rights; valid intentional relinquishment of a known right extinguishes error, while mere failure timely to assert a right does not. Plain means clear or obvious under current law, and the defendant ordinarily must show prejudice affecting the outcome; errors first clarified on appeal, special outcome-independent categories and justified prejudice presumptions are reserved.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that failure to object changes the conditions of appellate relief without making a violated rule disappear. Johnson v. Zerbst supplies the distinction between intentional surrender and omission; whether a right can be waived and what participation or procedures waiver requires depend on the particular right. Young reads Rule 52(b) as a limited power, not two disjunctive routes based on either plainness or substantial rights. Its prejudice inquiry ordinarily examines the record, as harmless-error review does, but places persuasion on the defendant who forfeited the objection. This does not decide that every error demands a specific outcome showing. Fulminante preserves the distinction between trial errors and defects affecting the tribunal’s basic structure. Here the Government’s concessions permit the Court to assume error and plainness without resolving whether Rule 24(c) can be waived. They do not concede prejudice. The preserved constitutional error in Brecht arose under a different review posture and does not supply the burden here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458: applies intentional relinquishment; right-specific waiver requirements remain.
- United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1: applies limited plain-error authority and ordinary prejudice requirement.
- Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279: preserves structural/trial-error distinction without deciding every Rule 52(b) category.
- Simulated Brecht, April 21, 1993: distinguished; preserved Doyle habeas review does not control forfeited Rule 24(c) error.

#### Correction remains discretionary

**Holding and operative rule:** Even when plain forfeited error affects substantial rights, correction under Rule 52(b) is permitted, not automatic, and should occur only when the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Actual innocence is not a necessary condition, but satisfaction of substantial rights alone does not satisfy this additional inquiry.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the word “may” leaves a separate remedial judgment to the reviewing court. Atkinson supplies its governing measure: the effect on the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Young carries that standard into Rule 52(b). Treating every prejudicial plain error as requiring correction would erase the discretion the Rule deliberately retains. The contrary argument that relief must always depend on actual innocence also fails. Innocence can make correction compelling, but serious injury to the administration of justice can exist independently of guilt. The collateral gateway addressed in Sawyer concerns a different procedural obstacle and does not rewrite this appellate rule. These distinctions prevent either an automatic reversal rule or an innocence-only restriction. Because respondents have not satisfied the preceding substantial-rights requirement, the Court does not decide whether this particular intrusion, had it been prejudicial, would warrant the exercise of remedial discretion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157: applies the independent fairness, integrity and public-reputation standard.
- United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1: applies discretionary, circumscribed correction.
- Simulated Sawyer, June 22, 1992: distinguished; capital actual-innocence gateway is not a universal limit on appellate correction.

#### Instructed alternates do not establish prejudice by presence alone

**Holding and operative rule:** For these alternates, instructed not to participate, mere presence during deliberations neither establishes prejudice nor warrants a presumption of prejudice under Rule 52(b). Without a specific showing of participation or a chilling effect, respondents have not shown an effect on substantial rights; the per se new-trial ruling must be reversed.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deliberative privacy protects against improper influence, and the inquiry must attend to that purpose. Parker and Remmer examine intrusions for prejudice; Smith v. Phillips rejects a new trial merely because a juror encountered a potentially compromising situation. Those authorities do not make this Rule 24(c) violation harmless by definition, but they defeat treating every intrusion as automatically reversible. The alternates heard the same evidence, took the ordinary oath, and received an express direction not to participate. Richardson supports the ordinary assumption that jurors follow instructions. Respondents showed neither actual participation, including nonverbal influence, nor restraint of the regular jurors’ discussion, and the circumstances do not justify presuming either. The Court does not decide the Government’s burden had an objection been preserved. It also reserves what Rule 606(b) would permit at a hearing and whether an appellate court may order such a hearing on plain-error review; none was requested here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Parker v. Gladden, 385 U.S. 363, and Remmer v. United States, 347 U.S. 227: apply the focus on prejudicial influence, preserving appropriate presumptions in other circumstances.
- Smith v. Phillips, 455 U.S. 209: applies rejection of automatic retrial for every compromising contact.
- Richardson v. Marsh, 481 U.S. 200: applies the ordinary instruction-following presumption, not an irrebuttable cure.
- Simulated Zafiro, January 25, 1993: consistent record-specific instruction analysis; no blanket rule that instructions cure intrusions.

### Separate Writings

Kennedy joins the Court. He emphasizes that the defendant’s burden is the usual framework, not necessarily the formulation for an error noticed by the court on its own initiative. Rule 24(c) remains independently obligatory; trial judges should not put counsel in the position of resisting a proposed departure from the prescribed jury structure. A preserved objection would place the burden of showing harmlessness on the Government, which could face substantial difficulty. None of those observations permits withholding an objection and demanding automatic reversal later.

Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, would affirm. Drawing on Vasquez v. Hillery and Gray v. Mississippi, he regards the privacy of deliberations as protecting the tribunal’s integrity independently of measurable verdict prejudice. He nonetheless distinguishes satisfaction of substantial rights from discretionary relief. A reasonable judge could decline reversal after considering the nature of the violation, counsel’s conduct and likely effect; his conclusion is that the Ninth Circuit did not abuse its broad supervisory discretion by granting relief here.

## United States v. California — 1993-04-26

### Event

United States v. California; No. 91-2003; October Term 1992. Argued: 1993-02-23; decided 1993-04-26. Source locator: 507 U.S. 746 (1993). Ninth Circuit, 932 F.2d 1346 (1991). Certiorari review of the identified judgment.

Question: Whether reimbursement of a contractor’s California taxes creates a federal common-law refund action for state-law assessment errors, or permits subrogation after the contractor’s refund claim has lapsed.

California assessed about $14 million against Williams Brothers Engineering Company for 1975–1981. The federal cost-plus contract reimbursed taxes and used advance funding. At federal direction WBEC contested the assessments administratively and in timely state actions, then stipulated to a $3 million refund and dismissal without prejudice in January 1988. The United States sued in May 1988 for the remaining $11 million plus interest and declaratory relief. WBEC’s remaining claims were then barred. Legal incidence fell on WBEC; the alleged error was state-law classification, not a direct unconstitutional tax on the United States.

### Holdings

#### Reimbursement does not create the asserted federal refund right

**Holding and operative rule:** Federal reimbursement or advance funding of an independent contractor’s state taxes does not alone create a federal common-law action for money had and received to recover alleged state-law assessment errors. The contractor’s legal tax liability does not become a direct tax on the United States merely because the Treasury bears its economic cost.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source of the money does not establish the source of the asserted right. New Mexico separates a tax on the Government itself from a tax on an independent contractor whose expenses the Government pays. The same distinction applies to the attempt to create a refund obligation here. Brady likewise rejects using a private indemnity arrangement to change a third party’s legal rights. California demanded a debt from WBEC; WBEC neither stole the federal money nor stood in a relationship making California responsible for a wrongful diversion. Bayne and Gaines therefore do not supply an implied obligation to the United States: those cases involved unlawful taking and legally responsible recipients. The Government’s financial interest is substantial, but it cannot alone transform a state-law tax dispute into this federal restitution action. The Court leaves direct federal taxation and any remedy Congress creates outside the holding and does not decide whether California correctly classified the purchases.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. New Mexico, 455 U.S. 720: applies legal incidence/economic burden distinction.
- Brady v. Roosevelt S.S. Co., 317 U.S. 575: applies limits on altering third-party rights by indemnity.
- Bayne v. United States, 93 U.S. 642, and Gaines v. Miller, 111 U.S. 395: distinguished; unlawful diversion and recipient responsibility are absent.

#### Later subrogation does not revive the expired claim

**Holding and operative rule:** Although indemnification gave the Government a right to seek subrogation, applicable California law required active assertion before substitution occurred; by the May 1988 federal suit, WBEC’s remaining refund claim had lapsed. The United States takes that derivative claim subject to its pre-existing infirmity; the Court does not decide generally whether state or federal limitations govern state-law claims brought by the United States.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that acquiring the opportunity to seek substitution is not the same as acquiring an enforceable claim free of existing defenses. Munsey Trust states the ordinary rule that a subrogee stands in the place of the party whose obligation it paid. Under Offer, payment furnished a right to seek subrogation, but active assertion was required to perfect that substitution. WBEC’s timely actions ended when it dismissed them without prejudice, and it could not commence a new refund action by the time the United States invoked subrogation. Guaranty Trust resolves the resulting problem: federal protection from state limitation periods does not revive a right already impaired when acquired. Summerlin protects a live federal claim from subsequent state restrictions and remains intact. The Government’s argument therefore fails on the condition of this derivative claim, not on a general rule subjecting sovereign claims to state deadlines. The completed partial refund remains untouched.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Munsey Trust Co., 332 U.S. 234: applies derivative nature of subrogation.
- Offer v. Superior Court, 194 Cal.114: applies the state-law active-assertion requirement.
- Guaranty Trust Co. v. United States, 304 U.S. 126: applies pre-acquisition infirmity rule.
- United States v. Summerlin, 310 U.S. 414: distinguished and preserved for live federal claims against subsequent state limits.

## Edenfield v. Fane — 1993-04-26

### Event

Edenfield v. Fane; No. 91-1594; October Term 1992. Argued: 1992-12-07; decided 1993-04-26. Source locator: 507 U.S. 761 (1993). Eleventh Circuit, 945 F.2d 1514 (1991). Certiorari review of the identified judgment.

Question: Whether Florida may categorically prohibit direct personal solicitation of business clients by certified public accountants on the justifications and evidence presented.

Fane proposed contacting business executives to offer CPA services. The Board invoked prevention of fraud, protection of privacy and professional independence. It supplied neither studies nor anecdotal evidence showing the alleged harms from CPA solicitation; the Dooner affidavit made generalized assertions. Contrary professional evidence and the controlled business setting distinguished lawyer solicitation of vulnerable accident victims. The injunction reaches the challenged business-solicitation prohibition, not fraud or coercion.

### Holdings

#### Real advancement must be established in the relevant professional setting

**Holding and operative rule:** When truthful, nonmisleading solicitation concerns lawful CPA services, the State must demonstrate that the harms supporting its restriction are real and that the restriction alleviates them to a material degree. Florida’s unsupported predictions about direct solicitation of business clients do not satisfy that burden; the ruling neither requires empirical studies in every case nor immunizes deceptive or coercive solicitation.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State must connect its legitimate concerns to the speech it suppresses. Central Hudson requires more than a substantial interest stated in the abstract; Fox preserves a reasonable fit rather than demanding the least restrictive imaginable rule. Florida’s interests in preventing fraud, protecting privacy and maintaining independence are substantial, but the record does not establish the asserted threat from CPA business solicitation. The Board’s generalized affidavit does not show that such contacts undermine professional judgment, and the professional evidence points the other way. Ohralik permitted prophylaxis against personal solicitation by lawyers in circumstances involving vulnerable clients and pressure for immediate commitment. Business executives considering accounting services can control the time and setting and need not decide immediately. That difference defeats the proposed analogy, not every professional prophylactic rule. Nor can labeling the prohibition a time, place and manner restriction cure its failure materially to advance the asserted interests, even assuming that alternative framework applies. Fraud, coercion and materially different settings remain open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S.557: applies the real-advancement burden.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S.469: retains reasonable fit without least restrictive means.
- Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Assn., 436 U.S.447: distinguished by professional setting, vulnerability and pressure.
- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S.781: alternative time/place/manner characterization cannot cure deficient advancement.
- Cincinnati v. Discovery Network, March 24, 1993, simulated: consistent application; no expansion to strict scrutiny.

#### Consistent interpretive commentary is authoritative

**Holding and operative rule:** Guidelines commentary interpreting or explaining a guideline is authoritative unless it violates the Constitution or a federal statute, or is inconsistent with, or a plainly erroneous reading of, that guideline. Seminole Rock provides the appropriate analogy because the Commission explains a rule it promulgated. The absence of congressional review for commentary amendments does not make every explanation optional, but it does preserve the boundary between interpretation and amendment. A contrary statute, constitutional command or guideline controls, and a plainly erroneous reading receives no authority. The Eleventh Circuit’s earlier construction is therefore not a sufficient reason to disregard a later consistent explanation. ## Moreau v. Klevenhagen — 1993-05-03

### Holdings

#### The earlier-hire component: the transition rule supplies the agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** For employees described in § 207(o)(2)(A)(ii) who were hired before April 15, 1986, the regular compensatory-time practice in effect on that date constitutes the agreement or understanding the statute otherwise requires before the work is performed, operating independently of any further individual or collective agreement for that cohort.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 207(o)(2) does not leave employees hired before the FLSA's public-sector amendments without a route to compensatory time merely because no contemporaneous individual agreement was separately documented. Congress's transition text supplies the missing agreement for that already-employed group by reference to the practice actually in effect when the amendments took hold. This does not excuse an employer from the ordinary individual-agreement route for anyone hired afterward, and does not convert a unilateral employer practice into an agreement outside the transition cohort the statute defines.

**Precedent treatment:** No prior Supreme Court holding addressed this transition provision; the Court applies its text without displacing any earlier authority.

#### The later-hire component: individual agreements where no representative could bind

**Holding and operative rule:** Where employees have selected a representative organization that state law does not authorize to enter a binding § 207(o)(2)(A)(i) agreement, the employer may rely on individual § 207(o)(2)(A)(ii) agreements with employees hired on or after April 15, 1986, rather than lose the compensatory-time option entirely for want of a legally capable collective representative.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory reference to an agreement with "a representative" does not compel recognition of an entity state law does not permit to make the relevant binding commitment. Congress created a limited compensatory-time option grounded in actual consent, not a general public-sector collective-bargaining entitlement; state law determines whether a selected organization can make the collective agreement, while federal law determines the resulting overtime consequences. Individual consent remains necessary for this cohort.

**Precedent treatment:** No Supreme Court precedent is overruled, limited, or extended beyond its own terms.

No general federal collective-bargaining right, no exemption from overtime law, and no presumption of individual consent is created.

## Cardinal Chemical Co. v. Morton International, Inc. — 1993-05-17

### Holdings

#### An intermediate noninfringement ruling does not terminate counterclaim jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** The Federal Circuit’s affirmance of noninfringement does not deprive it of jurisdiction over a live, independent invalidity counterclaim merely because its own ruling would resolve the infringement claim. That intermediate ruling remains subject to this Court’s review and does not justify treating validity review as automatically advisory.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an intermediate appellate court cannot treat its own noninfringement ruling as the final, unreviewable end of the litigation. Its judgment remains subject to this Court’s review. A reversal of noninfringement could restore the immediate importance of the independently adjudicated validity issue, so the appellate court’s own ruling cannot by itself remove authority to review that issue. Altvater confirms that a counterclaim seeking a declaration of invalidity presents more than a defense to the particular infringement claim. Electrical Fittings does not support the automatic rule: that case involved an unnecessary validity adjudication in rejecting infringement, not an independent counterclaim judgment requiring appellate treatment. Judicial caution about unnecessary questions cannot convert the Federal Circuit’s merits decision into a jurisdictional event beyond further review. This is an independently sufficient reason to reject the automatic mootness premise. The Court does not disturb the noninfringement affirmance here, decide technical validity or foreclose actual mootness arising from a different event.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Altvater v. Freeman, 319 U.S.359: applies independent-counterclaim distinction.
- Electrical Fittings Corp. v. Thomas & Betts Co., 307 U.S.241: distinguished; unnecessary affirmative-defense adjudication is different.

#### An established invalidity controversy does not disappear without a change

**Holding and operative rule:** Once an independent invalidity counterclaim presents an actual controversy, the party asserting later mootness must demonstrate a change that eliminates it. Affirmance of noninfringement alone does not establish that change or authorize automatic vacatur of the invalidity judgment.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the distinct validity controversy continues unless something actually ends it. Aetna places the initial burden of establishing a real controversy on the party seeking declaratory relief. That threshold was met here. W.T.Grant assigns the burden differently when a party contends that an established controversy has later become moot. The automatic practice identifies no new fact ending the dispute about the patents’ validity. Altvater explains why an invalidity counterclaim remains distinct from a defense to infringement: success on the particular infringement allegation need not resolve the parties’ continuing patent controversy. Munsingwear addresses vacatur after genuine intervening mootness; it does not supply the missing intervening event here. Both parties ask for validity review but disagree about the result, so their agreement on the need for review does not eliminate adversity, as Chadha’s treatment of agreement on a legal issue confirms. The Court leaves genuine later mootness available and does not decide validity itself.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Altvater v. Freeman, 319 U.S.359: applies independent-counterclaim distinction.
- Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S.227, and United States v. W.T.Grant Co., 345 U.S.629: distinguishes initial controversy burden from later mootness burden.
- United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S.36: actual intervening mootness rule preserved; automatic application rejected.
- INS v. Chadha, 462 U.S.919: agreement on one legal issue does not eliminate underlying adversity.

#### The automatic practice cannot be saved as discretion on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** The Federal Circuit may not routinely vacate a live invalidity judgment merely because it affirms noninfringement, nor save that same automatic practice by calling it discretionary. On this record the validity appeal should be considered; unusual case-specific grounds for declining review or actual mootness remain available, but no universal duty to decide validity in every patent appeal is imposed.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that jurisdiction cannot be acknowledged while the same automatic refusal is preserved under another name. The validity counterclaim has consequences beyond whether the particular accused products infringe. Blonder-Tongue explains why an adjudication of invalidity can protect others from repeated enforcement of a patent that should not restrict the public. Routine vacatur also prevents review of the district court’s work and can force the prevailing accused infringer to confront the same validity controversy again. The patent holder has sought review as well, so this record presents no reason to preserve a categorical refusal. Judicial restraint is important, but it does not justify a standing practice that disregards a live independent judgment whenever a separate ground resolves infringement. The Federal Circuit should consider the validity appeal on remand. The Court does not eliminate lawful case-specific discretion, dictate the technical validity result or require unnecessary constitutional adjudication. An unusual case presenting a genuine reason to withhold review must be assessed on its own circumstances.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Blonder-Tongue Laboratories, Inc. v. University of Illinois Foundation, 402 U.S.313: applies distinct public and preclusion significance of invalidity.
- Altvater, 319 U.S.359: independent judgment cannot be discarded solely because infringement fails.
