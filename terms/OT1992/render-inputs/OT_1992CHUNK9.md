## Northeastern Florida Chapter, AGC v. Jacksonville

### 1. Event

**Northeastern Florida Chapter, AGC v. Jacksonville**, No.91-1721; October Term 1992; merits disposition effective 1993-06-14; argued 1993-02-22. On writ of certiorari to Eleventh Circuit, 951 F.2d 1217: district judgment vacated and dismissal without prejudice directed for lack of standing.

**Question:** Whether ready and able competitors must prove a winning bid to challenge unequal bidding conditions, and whether replacement moots this controversy.

**Material record:** Jacksonville’s original ordinance reserved ten percent of city contracting expenditures for designated minority business enterprises. The association alleged that members regularly bid for city work and would have bid for the reserved contracts but for the restrictions. Those allegations were not challenged in the parties’ summary-judgment submissions. In October 1992 the city replaced the ordinance with a program containing several methods, including a sheltered market reserving contracts for certified Black- and female-owned firms. The new program changed coverage and percentages but retained a mechanism excluding other firms from competing for particular awards.

**Render form: compact.** Basis: Seven-to-two judgment; no material change from the comparator.

### 6. Holdings

#### Equal opportunity to compete is a concrete injury

**Holding and operative rule:** An association may challenge a governmental barrier preventing members who are able and ready to bid from competing on equal terms, without proving that a member would have won a contract, when a member has individual standing, the interest is germane to the association’s purpose, and neither the claim nor the requested relief requires individual-member participation. The unchallenged allegations accepted here establish the required connection; genuinely disputed elements remain subject to stage-appropriate proof.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, Equal opportunity to compete is a concrete injury portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the injury is denial of equal treatment in competition, not eventual loss of a contract. Turner protects eligibility for consideration, and Bakke likewise distinguishes a chance to compete from a guaranteed benefit. Members regularly engaged in city contracting allege that they would bid on reserved work if allowed. The ordinance itself prevents that participation, and relief removing the barrier would redress the injury. Warth does not require proof of a successful bid in a competition the government closed to the bidder; it requires a concrete connection between the plaintiff and the challenged conduct. Hunt still governs the association’s representation of its members. Lujan’s evidentiary rules remain: the parties did not contest these allegations in their summary-judgment submissions, and the Court does not excuse missing proof when an element is genuinely disputed. Nothing in this threshold decision determines whether the preference is constitutionally justified.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Turner v. Fouche, 396 U.S.346 — equal eligibility applied.
- Bakke, 438 U.S.265 — opportunity injury distinguished from guaranteed selection.
- Warth, 422 U.S.490 — concrete causal connection retained.
- Hunt, 432 U.S.333 — association requirements retained.
- Lujan, June 12, 1992 — concrete injury and stage-specific proof preserved.

#### The replacement continues the controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** Repeal does not moot this dispute because the successor’s sheltered-market method continues to exclude the association’s members from particular competitions on the challenged basis. Changes in groups, percentages and methods do not remove that injury; a replacement actually ending it could produce a different result.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, The replacement continues the controversy portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the operative program must be examined for the injury actually alleged. City of Mesquite rejects automatic mootness from voluntary repeal when the challenged conduct continues or can recur. Jacksonville’s new program changes details but retains a sheltered market in which specified firms alone may compete. The association continues to confront the unequal opportunity that gives it standing. The city’s argument that the old ordinance no longer exists identifies a change in legal instrument, not disappearance of the injury. Diffenderfer involved a change removing the operative statutory basis for the challenged treatment; it does not make every legislative revision conclusive. The conclusion is confined to the replacement mechanism shown here. On remand the courts must address the current program and relief capable of redressing the injury, rather than automatically restoring every provision of an injunction against the old ordinance. The constitutional merits of either preference remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- City of Mesquite, 455 U.S.283 — voluntary cessation applied.
- Diffenderfer, 404 U.S.412 — distinguished because the challenged injury continues.

## South Dakota v. Bourland

### 1. Event

**South Dakota v. Bourland**, No.91-2051; October Term 1992; merits disposition effective 1993-06-14; argued 1993-03-02. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit, 949 F.2d 984: treaty authority sustained on 104,420 former trust acres; separate inquiry on approximately 18,000 formerly non-Indian fee acres remanded.

**Question:** Whether the acquisition and public-recreation statutes abrogated treaty-derived regulation of non-Indian hunting and fishing, and what residual authority remains.

**Material record:** The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty reserved lands for Sioux use and occupation. The Flood Control Act of 1944 authorized the water project and public recreation. The Cheyenne River Act of 1954 transferred specified tribal interests for the project, provided compensation, and reserved stated access, hunting, and fishing rights for the Tribe or its members. The Government also acquired former non-Indian fee lands. The Tribe enforced hunting and fishing rules on the acquired area; South Dakota challenged its authority over non-Indians. The dispute distinguishes ownership, access, treaty-based regulation, and the different histories of the two land categories.

**Render form: full.** Basis: Stone opposes the abrogation disposition; judgment and proposition coalitions materially differ.

### 2. Participation

### 6. Holdings

#### Acquisition displaces exclusion-derived regulation

**Holding and operative rule:** The Flood Control Act and Cheyenne River Act displaced the treaty right of exclusive occupation on the taken former trust lands and the non-Indian hunting-and-fishing regulation derived from that exclusion right. Retained tribal access and personal hunting-and-fishing privileges do not themselves preserve authority to license or regulate non-Indian public users.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, Acquisition displaces exclusion-derived regulation portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress’s treatment of these lands supplies the clear indication required by Dion. The Fort Laramie Treaty originally protected exclusive use and occupation, including control of non-Indian access. Congress acquired the project lands, authorized public recreation under federal management, settled the rights taken, and retained shoreline access and hunting and fishing subject to the regulations governing corresponding public uses without retaining exclusion of the public. Montana explains the relationship between exclusive occupation and the regulatory authority claimed from it; Brendale confirms the importance of the land’s legal status. Tribal use rights remain meaningful without carrying power to condition the public access Congress provided. Menominee does not compel the opposite result, because this conclusion rests on the particular acquisition and access enactments together, not an assumption that every federal project extinguishes treaties. The Court requires no particular verbal formula for abrogation. It does not decide reservation boundaries, tribal criminal jurisdiction or every possible regulation on acquired acreage.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dion, 476 U.S.734 — clear congressional choice required, without an invariable express-words requirement.
- Montana, 450 U.S.544 — exclusion-derived authority and effects of alienation applied.
- Brendale, 492 U.S.408 — land-specific setting considered without inventing a universal rule from its fracture.
- Menominee, 391 U.S.404 — protection against casual implied abrogation preserved; particular enactments distinguished.

#### Residual authority remains a distinct inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** Loss of treaty-based exclusion does not decide whether a Montana exception or properly established federal authorization permits particular regulation. Remand preserves the exceptions for consensual commercial dealings, contracts, leases or other arrangements with the Tribe or its members, and for conduct threatening or directly affecting tribal political integrity, economic security, health or welfare, within Montana’s limits. General inherent sovereignty alone does not restore the abrogated regulatory power.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, Residual authority remains a distinct inquiry portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source rejected today must not be confused with every possible source of tribal civil authority. Montana generally limits regulation of nonmembers on non-Indian land but identifies two exceptions requiring attention to actual relationships and conduct. The court of appeals relied on treaty-based control for the former trust acreage while preserving a separate inquiry for formerly non-Indian fee land. The district court found neither exception satisfied on either land category, but the court of appeals did not review those findings for the former trust lands; that review remains. The Corps-regulation theory was not raised below, was omitted from the Government’s brief and conflicted with record evidence of the Corps’ view. The Court declines deference to that undeveloped litigating position. The State therefore receives no declaration that every non-Indian activity throughout the reservation is exclusively subject to state law. Any claimed authorization and concrete conflict with superior federal rules must be assessed on their own terms. Remand permits that inquiry without reviving the displaced exclusion right or collapsing the two land categories into a single historical ownership premise. No result on those undeveloped questions is directed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Montana, 450 U.S.544 — both consensual-relationship and serious tribal-interest exceptions expressly retained.
- United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S.313 — retained internal tribal powers do not themselves establish nonmember regulation after the abrogation found here.

### 9. Separate Writings

Blackmun, joined by Souter, dissents. Dion requires evidence that Congress considered the disputed treaty right and chose abrogation. Building a dam and assuring recreation can coexist with conservation regulation; loss of absolute exclusion does not prove loss of every incident of territorial sovereignty. ## Local 144 Nursing Home Pension Fund v. Demisay

### 1. Event

**Local 144 Nursing Home Pension Fund v. Demisay**, No.91-610; October Term 1992; merits disposition effective 1993-06-14; argued 1993-01-11. On writ of certiorari to Second Circuit, 935 F.2d 528: summary judgment for existing funds reversed and a reserve-transfer remedy directed under LMRA §302.

**Question:** Whether §302(e) authorizes supervision and asset transfer from otherwise qualifying funds to successor funds.

**Material record:** The employers left a bargaining association in 1981 and established successor funds under 1984 agreements. They promised their employees continued recognition of credited service and no loss of benefits. The existing funds were not parties to those agreements and remained responsible for vested benefits. The requested transfer would help finance the successor funds’ additional obligations. Both ERISA and LMRA theories were pleaded, but the present review concerns the LMRA theory.

**Render form: compact.** Basis: Unanimous judgment; six-Justice opinion and three-Justice concurrence preserve comparator substance.

### 6. Holdings

#### Section 302 restrains prohibited transactions

**Holding and operative rule:** Section302(e) authorizes restraint of violations of the payment, demand, receipt and related prohibitions in §302(a) and (b); §302(c)(5) identifies qualifying trusts rather than a general supervisory remedy for later administration. Retaining these reserves supplies no transfer remedy without an actual prohibited transaction; payment to a nonqualifying trust remains subject to the statute.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, Section 302 restrains prohibited transactions portion; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedial grant must be tied to conduct Congress prohibited. Subsection(a) addresses employer payments, and subsection(b) their request and receipt. The exception describes a trust established for designated employee benefits and money held for that purpose; subsequent misconduct may violate fiduciary or contractual duties without becoming a prohibited receipt. Arroyo’s actual distinction between qualifying receipt and later diversion supports that reading. Its broader supervisory language, like Amax’s related discussion, did not decide this question and cannot enlarge subsection(e). Robinson rejected an additional reasonableness requirement and reserved supervisory scope. The employers’ promise that successor funds would recognize prior service neither bound the existing funds to a new agreement nor transferred their assets. The contrary structural-defect theory substitutes a desired allocation for an operative statutory violation. The Court leaves the separately pleaded ERISA transfer and fiduciary claims for the court of appeals and grants no immunity from independently established duties.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Arroyo, 359 U.S.419 — lawful receipt distinguished from later diversion; contrary supervisory language is dictum.
- Amax, 453 U.S.322 — fiduciary independence retained; supervisory language does not establish jurisdiction.
- Robinson, 455 U.S.562 — reasonableness-review rejection retained; reserved supervisory scope resolved.
- Mertens, June 1, 1993 — unchanged; its remedy limit does not resolve these unreviewed ERISA claims.

Retention thus creates no structural violation even if courts can enforce continuing compliance with the express statutory standards. They regard the Court’s broader supervision holding as unnecessary.

## Concrete Pipe & Products of California, Inc. v. Construction Laborers Pension Trust for Southern California

### 1. Event

**Concrete Pipe & Products of California, Inc. v. Construction Laborers Pension Trust for Southern California**, No.91-904; October Term 1992; merits disposition effective 1993-06-14; argued 1992-12-01. On writ of certiorari to Ninth Circuit, unpublished affirmance reported at 936 F.2d 576; district court confirmed the arbitrator’s withdrawal-liability award.

**Question:** Whether the assessment and arbitration scheme, statutory presumptions and this employer’s withdrawal liability violate procedural due process, substantive due process or the Takings Clause.

**Material record:** After withdrawing from a multiemployer pension plan, the employer was assessed a share of unfunded vested benefits. The plan initially calculated liability; an arbitrator resolved the contested assessment. The employer challenges both the plan’s financial interest and the statutory presumptions, including actuarial assumptions used to value liabilities. The liability was substantial relative to its net worth, but the record does not establish a physical appropriation of identified property.

### 6. Holdings

#### The preliminary assessment is not an adjudication

**Holding and operative rule:** The interested plan sponsor’s withdrawal-liability assessment and demand are enforcement acts, not an adjudication. Due process permits that preliminary demand because the dispute first receives adjudication before a neutral arbitrator, under the lawful arbitral burdens stated separately below.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, The preliminary assessment is not an adjudication portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional requirement of impartial adjudication attaches to the process that decides the dispute, not every preliminary act asserting a claim. Tumey and Ward require an impartial decisionmaker and reject the notion that ordinary appellate review necessarily cures an earlier biased adjudication. Those principles do not make the plan sponsor an adjudicator merely because it calculates an amount, demands payment and performs preliminary review. Withrow distinguishes preliminary functions from the eventual decision of a contested claim. The sponsor acts as a claimant whose assertion can be disputed in neutral arbitration. That distinction answers the employer’s objection to the sponsor’s institutional interest without treating the interest as irrelevant or permitting it to control the arbitrator. Whether the statutory presumptions compromise the neutral proceeding remains a separate question. The Court sustains the preliminary structure; it does not authorize a plan to replace independent adjudication with review that merely accepts its own determination.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Tumey, 273 U.S.510 — interested adjudication forbidden; the sponsor is not the adjudicator here.
- Ward, 409 U.S.57 — appellate review does not automatically cure initial biased adjudication; that premise is absent here.
- Withrow, 421 U.S.35 — preliminary and adjudicative functions distinguished.

#### The factual presumption assigns ordinary persuasion

**Holding and operative rule:** In neutral arbitration, §1401(a)(3)(A) places on the employer the burden to disprove the challenged factual determination by a preponderance. It does not add appellate deference to the sponsor’s factual determinations, a clear-and-convincing requirement or a conclusive presumption.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, The factual presumption assigns ordinary persuasion portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (8 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the factual presumption must be read in the context of independent arbitration. The statute combines preponderance language with unreasonable or clearly erroneous terminology. Those expressions ordinarily serve different functions, so the text presents a genuine construction problem rather than an unmistakable command to defer to the sponsor. Crowell supports resolving that difficulty through a permissible construction avoiding a serious constitutional question. An ordinary burden of persuasion gives the statutory presumption effect while preserving the arbitrator’s independent resolution of contested facts. The employer may show that the sponsor’s factual determination is wrong by the ordinary preponderance standard; it need not demonstrate the additional defect that an appellate tribunal would demand from a reviewed adjudication. Section1401(c)’s separate presumption in judicial review of the arbitrator’s findings remains distinct and is not imported into arbitration. The arbitrator’s treatment of the withdrawal-date dispute complied with this construction, so the Court requires no second arbitration on this record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S.22 — constitutionally sound construction used within the interpretive choice the text permits.
- Section1401(c) — judicial review presumes arbitral findings correct unless rebutted by a clear preponderance; no transfer of that standard into arbitration.

#### Actuarial rebuttal retains both statutory routes

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §1401(a)(3)(B), a contesting employer may rebut the actuarial calculation by a preponderance showing either that assumptions and methods were unreasonable in the aggregate, considering plan experience and reasonable expectations, or that the actuary made a significant error in applying them. The professional and statutory constraints make this presumption constitutionally permissible here; fraud need not be proved.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, Actuarial rebuttal retains both statutory routes portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actuarial estimation differs from a sponsor’s assertion of historical fact. Under the applicable §1393(a)(1) route, the assumptions and methods must be reasonable in the aggregate, account for plan experience and reasonable expectations, and together offer the actuary’s best estimate of anticipated plan experience. Professional responsibilities and the need for coherent valuation limit arbitrary selection against a withdrawing employer. A range of reasonable estimates is possible, so demonstrating another permissible estimate does not establish aggregate unreasonableness. But the employer also retains the distinct statutory route based on a significant application error; that route does not disappear because the assumptions themselves are reasonable. Nor does either route require proof of corruption or fraud. The Court therefore rejects the claim that the calculation is effectively unreviewable. On this record the neutral arbitrator applied the actuarial presumption and the employer failed to establish the required defect. This conclusion does not authorize sponsor control inconsistent with the statute, ratify every actuarial choice, or replace the independent factual inquiry with professional deference on matters outside actuarial judgment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Section1393 — statutory constraints on actuarial assumptions and methods applied.
- Section1401(a)(3)(B) — aggregate unreasonableness OR significant application error preserved as separate routes.
- Connolly, 475 U.S.211 — pooled-plan structure provides context, not an irrebuttable actuarial presumption.

#### This withdrawal allocation is rational

**Holding and operative rule:** Allocating a proportionate share of unfunded vested-benefit costs to this withdrawing employer is rational in light of its participation in the pooled pension undertaking. Neither private contribution limits nor the absence of a one-to-one match between its contributions and benefits to its own employees defeats this substantive-due-process application.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, This withdrawal allocation is rational portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress could connect withdrawal liability to participation in a common pension arrangement. Usery permits rational allocation of costs associated with an enterprise’s past activity, and R. A. Gray sustains the legislative response to withdrawal incentives threatening pension security. Concrete Pipe received the advantages of a pooled arrangement in which contributions and service credits were not segregated employer by employer. Its share can therefore reflect pooled obligations rather than only benefits earned while employees worked for it. Private contractual limits do not determine the reach of otherwise constitutional federal economic legislation. The employer’s concern about retrospective burdens warrants examination, but it does not establish irrationality in the allocation actually imposed. Connolly confirms the relevance of participation and the system’s protective purpose without foreclosing every as-applied challenge. The Court does not decide a preserved challenge to classification as a defined-benefit plan, because that classification was not presented for review, or establish liability based on an employer’s wealth alone.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Usery v. Turner Elkhorn, 428 U.S.1 — rational retrospective economic allocation applied.
- PBGC v. R. A. Gray, 467 U.S.717 — pension-protection allocation sustained within actual statutory setting.
- Connolly, 475 U.S.211 — pooling and contractual limits treated consistently with its constitutional holding.

#### The asserted impact does not establish a taking

**Holding and operative rule:** This withdrawal liability is not a taking when its economic impact, reasonable investment-backed expectations and the character of the governmental action are considered together. The substantial monetary burden alone does not establish appropriation or a constitutional percentage ceiling; broader reliance on the former 30%-of-net-worth limit is not independently decided.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, The asserted impact does not establish a taking portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

#### An applied federal rule governs still-open direct review

**Holding and operative rule:** When this Court applies a federal rule to the parties before it, that rule governs all still-open direct-review cases concerning events within its substantive scope, whether those events predate or postdate announcement. Reliance and fiscal disruption are substantial practical concerns, but they cannot make the substantive federal rule vary among contemporaneous open cases. The state refund interpretation here depends on the rejected prospectivity premise and is not an independent state ground insulating that error. The Court distinguishes finality and remedy from choice of governing law. It does not reopen closed judgments, calculate refunds, or decide a hypothetical case in which the Court expressly reserves application to the parties before it.

## Brooke Group v. Brown & Williamson

### 1. Event

**Brooke Group v. Brown & Williamson**, No.92-466; October Term 1992; merits disposition effective 1993-06-21; argued 1993-03-29. ### 6. Holdings

#### Below-cost pricing must carry a prospect of recoupment

**Holding and operative rule:** A primary-line predatory-pricing claim requires pricing below an appropriate measure of the defendant’s costs and a reasonable prospect of recovering the predatory investment through anticompetitive returns. Tacit oligopolistic coordination is a legally possible mechanism; actual success, an express conspiracy or a single-firm monopoly is not indispensable, but intent, concentration and injury to a rival alone are insufficient.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, Below-cost pricing must carry a prospect of recoupment portion; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute protects competition rather than insulating a competitor from low prices. Brown Shoe supplies that distinction, and Matsushita and Cargill explain why below-cost losses injure competition only if a plausible later return can make the predatory strategy worthwhile. Robinson-Patman’s primary-line standard requires a reasonable prospect of that recovery, not proof that recoupment already occurred or automatic importation of the Sherman Act’s dangerous-probability formulation. Utah Pie permits supported circumstantial proof of competitive injury; it does not establish liability merely from discriminatory prices and harm to a smaller rival. Oligopolistic coordination is not categorically excluded, because durable supracompetitive returns may in principle arise without one firm obtaining a monopoly. But the evidence must connect the strategy to recovery of the defendant’s own losses, including the consequences of sharing subsequent gains with rivals. The Court need not select an exclusive accounting measure of cost because the separate recoupment requirement resolves this appeal.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brown Shoe, 370 U.S.294 — competition/competitor distinction applied.
- Matsushita, 475 U.S.574 — recoupment logic applied without requiring an express conspiracy here.
- Cargill, 479 U.S.104 — low prices distinguished from actionable predation.
- Utah Pie, 386 U.S.685 — read consistently with competitive injury; not treated as rival-protection liability.
- Spectrum Sports, January 25, 1993 — intent cannot replace competitive danger; Sherman Act elements not transplanted wholesale.

#### This record lacks the necessary recoupment showing

**Holding and operative rule:** Even crediting below-cost pricing and predatory purpose and drawing reasonable inferences for Liggett, the evidence does not permit a reasonable jury to find the required prospect of Brown & Williamson recouping its losses through the alleged coordinated pricing mechanism. The failure of that necessary element sustains judgment as a matter of law without a categorical oligopoly exemption.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, This record lacks the necessary recoupment showing portion; O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the evidence must establish the links in the proposed market mechanism, not merely a hostile plan. Liggett points to parallel profitable pricing, internal discipline documents, later list-price increases and expert testimony about costs and promotions. Those materials support intent and a proposed strategy, but the required inference also concerns durable anticompetitive returns sufficient to repay Brown & Williamson’s own investment. Expanding generic output, excess capacity, lower-price entry, discounts separating list from net prices and Reynolds’ contrary incentives undermine that connection. The predator would bear its own losses while sharing any coordinated gains. Matsushita permits scrutiny of whether the proposed mechanism has evidentiary support; it does not authorize choosing between witnesses merely because judges prefer one. On the whole record, the missing recovery showing cannot be supplied by intent or concentration. The Court therefore affirms on competitive injury and does not decide the district court’s independent antitrust-injury and causation grounds or declare that oligopoly recoupment can never be proved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Matsushita, 475 U.S.574 — economically plausible mechanism must have evidentiary support.
- Cargill, 479 U.S.104 — rival losses do not alone establish competitive harm.
- Utah Pie, 386 U.S.685 — circumstantial proof permitted, but necessary recovery inference absent here.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, Direct review permits the necessary sentence correction portion; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy must correct the defective sentencing mechanism without adjudicating guilt anew. Teague governs collateral use of new procedural rules, not a case still on direct review. Moreover, Graham held that the relevant effective-mitigation duty was established before its own finality date; that settled duty supplies the governing rule here. The defect here concerns what the jury was legally able to do with supported mitigation, not a judicial determination that youth necessarily outweighs aggravation. A lawful instruction or special issue can permit the relevant judgment without requiring jurors to deny deliberateness or predicted danger they actually find. The State retains authority to structure its sentencing process consistently with that duty. The Court neither prescribes exact future wording nor automatically selects life imprisonment regardless of state law. It reverses only the judgment sustaining death and remands for a constitutionally adequate capital proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence.
