## FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.

### Event

FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.; No. 92-603; October Term 1992. Argued 1993-03-29; decided 1993-06-01. Review of the District of Columbia Circuit, 965 F.2d 1103 (1992).

Question: Whether the Cable Act’s exemption for facilities serving multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, without using a public right-of-way, survives the reviewed equal-protection challenge.

Satellite master antenna television operators challenged the distinction between systems serving commonly controlled buildings and systems serving separately owned buildings. After an earlier remand for an explanation, the court of appeals found the distinction unsupported and required extension of the exemption. The 1992 Cable Act renumbered the relevant definition from §522(6) to §522(7); that citation change did not enact the court of appeals’ constitutional conclusion. The reviewed exemption applies only to facilities serving subscribers in one or more multiple-unit dwellings under common ownership, control or management, unless the facilities use a public right-of-way. Separate First Amendment scrutiny was not adjudicated below on the new statutory footing.

Render form: full. Basis: The judgment is unanimous, but the Chief joins the bargaining and regulatory-cost rationale without joining the separate monopoly rationale; that changes the supporting coalition for an independently sufficient proposition.

### Participation

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-03-29) and decision (1993-06-01). No recusal or other nonparticipation is established. Nine participants; quorum six; five votes required for a judgment or directly controlling rationale. White remains seated. Standing fallback used for the claim that Congress’s 1992 reenactment or silence rendered the controversy moot; Stone joins the majority disposition on that component without adding a ground.

### Public Action

Reversed and remanded, 9–0 in the judgment.

### Holdings

#### Reenactment does not moot the live constitutional controversy

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress’s reenactment of the cable definition without changing the challenged distinction does not adopt a lower court’s constitutional invalidation or eliminate the live controversy defended by the FCC. The Court therefore reaches the reviewed equal-protection question.

**Authority:** Thomas’s threshold opinion; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the parties continue to dispute the validity of an operative statutory distinction. Congress’s failure to alter that distinction while revising other portions of the cable legislation does not turn the court of appeals’ judgment into an act of Congress. A legislative adoption inference sometimes used to understand statutory language does not establish that Congress enacted a judicial conclusion about constitutional validity. The FCC continues to defend the distinction, and reversal would alter the parties’ rights under the exemption. The amendment therefore leaves a concrete controversy rather than an advisory question. This conclusion decides only the proposed ground for mootness. It does not treat congressional silence as approval of every agency interpretation, decide the new legislation’s constitutionality, or resolve the separate speech objections. Those objections must be addressed through the questions and record actually presented for their consideration.

**Precedent treatment:**

- The lower court’s constitutional judgment is reviewed, not treated as legislation adopted by congressional silence; no earlier controlling decision is overruled.

#### Common-owner bargaining and regulatory costs sustain the economic line

**Holding and operative rule:** For an economic classification involving neither a suspect class nor a fundamental right under the question reviewed, a reasonably conceivable legitimate basis suffices; the challenger must negate such a basis, and formal legislative findings are unnecessary. Congress could rationally distinguish these commonly owned, controlled or managed dwellings from separately owned properties because bargaining arrangements and the relationship between regulatory cost and consumer protection differ; Moreno and Cleburne continue to forbid irrational or impermissibly motivated classifications.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Parts I and II-A; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress could make this ownership distinction without proving that it was the only satisfactory line. Fritz permits economic classifications supported by a conceivable legitimate relationship, and the entered Nordlinger decision confirms that imperfect fit does not itself defeat rationality. A common owner can bargain for service on behalf of the property and can choose among suppliers before committing the premises. Congress could also conclude that regulating small systems imposes costs disproportionate to the consumer protection obtained. Separate ownership changes those bargaining conditions and permits expansion without the same common control. The absence of a fixed subscriber cap and the existence of atypically large exempt facilities do not negate the general relationship. Neither legislative silence about an actual motive nor the absence of empirical findings shifts the challenger’s burden. Moreno and Cleburne still exclude a forbidden purpose or irrational prejudice; no such defect or settled contrary fact defeats this relationship here. Separate speech scrutiny remains undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States Railroad Retirement Board v. Fritz, 449 U.S.166: conceivable legitimate grounds and imperfect economic fit applied.
- Sullivan v. Stroop, 496 U.S.478: ordinary review of social and economic classifications applied.
- Nordlinger v. Hahn, June 18, 1992: the entered rational-basis holding applied without converting its particular tax grounds into universal immunity from review.
- Department of Agriculture v. Moreno, 413 U.S.528: illegitimate-purpose limit preserved.
- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: irrational-prejudice limit preserved.

#### Competitive entry independently supports the ownership distinction

**Holding and operative rule:** Congress could independently distinguish separately owned buildings because an incumbent provider can add nearby buildings at a lower marginal cost than a new entrant can incur to install its own reception facilities, creating a plausible first-mover advantage. This conceivable competition concern is sufficient under ordinary rational-basis review without proof that every regulated system possesses monopoly power.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Part II-B; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the ownership line has a sufficient competition rationale independent of the regulatory-cost explanation. A provider already operating a reception facility may connect additional separately owned buildings more cheaply than a new competitor can construct the facilities necessary to serve them. Congress could regard that advantage as a reason to retain franchising oversight. In a commonly owned development, by contrast, a single owner can negotiate for the whole property before selecting a provider. Fritz permits Congress to address such differences through an administrable category even when particular systems do not display the predicted advantage. The argument that all systems avoid public rights-of-way identifies a common characteristic but does not eliminate this different competitive characteristic. The holding does not find actual monopoly power, decide an antitrust claim or require a regulator to prove market dominance in every application. Nor does it decide whether a different standard applies to a separately presented burden on protected speech.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fritz, 449 U.S.166: a second conceivable legitimate economic basis independently sustains the line.
- Nordlinger, June 18, 1992: multiple sufficient rational grounds and tolerance for imperfect fit applied within ordinary review.

## Nobelman v. American Savings Bank

### Event

Nobelman v. American Savings Bank; No. 92-641; October Term 1992. Argued 1993-04-19; decided 1993-06-01. Review of the Fifth Circuit, 968 F.2d 483 (1992), which affirmed rejection of the proposed Chapter 13 treatment.

Question: Whether §506(a) permits the debtors to modify the unsecured portion of a partially secured mortgage claim protected by §1322(b)(2).

The bank’s claim of approximately $71,335 was secured by a residence valued without objection at $23,500. The plan would pay the value-supported portion under the note but treat the balance as unsecured, with no payment on that balance. The bankruptcy court rejected the plan, and the district court and Fifth Circuit affirmed. The reviewed question proceeds on the statutory principal-residence premise; the deed’s ancillary language concerning condominium common areas, escrow, insurance proceeds and rents is not adjudicated as additional collateral.

### Holdings

#### Valuation does not remove the protected rights of this mortgage holder

**Holding and operative rule:** When a creditor retains a secured portion of an allowed claim secured only by a security interest in real property that is the debtor’s principal residence, §1322(b)(2) protects the holder’s rights in the mortgage claim as a whole, notwithstanding §506(a) valuation and bifurcation. The debtor may not modify the deficiency portion in a way that alters those protected repayment and enforcement rights; independent statutory restrictions and the express cure authority remain effective.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that valuation and protection of the mortgage holder’s rights answer different questions. Section 506(a) permits valuation of the bank’s collateral and confirms that the claim retains a secured portion. Butner supplies the state-law rights attached to the mortgage: repayment on the agreed terms, retention of the lien, acceleration, foreclosure and recovery of a permissible deficiency. Section 1322(b)(2) protects the holder of the described claim, rather than merely the dollars assigned secured status by valuation. Reducing the unsecured balance would change the repayment rights in the same note and thus modify the protected rights. The debtors’ last-antecedent reading is grammatically possible, but the full phrase and the focus on the holder’s rights supply the better reading. Ron Pair requires attention to that enacted text, not a transfer of Dewsnup’s different Chapter 7 rule. Section 362’s stay and §1322(b)(5)’s cure authority operate independently. Wholly unsecured liens and disputed additional collateral are not decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Butner v. United States, 440 U.S.48: state law identifies the property and contract rights to which federal protection attaches.
- United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S.235: the text of each Bankruptcy Code provision receives independent effect.
- Dewsnup v. Timm, January 15, 1992: Chapter 7 §506(d) rule distinguished; §506(a) valuation is not displaced.

## Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District

### Event

Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District; No. 91-2024; October Term 1992. Argued 1993-02-24; decided 1993-06-07. Review of the Second Circuit, 959 F.2d 381 (1992), affirming summary judgment for the school district.

Question: Whether the district could exclude a religious perspective on family and child-rearing from an otherwise admitted after-hours community subject, and whether the Establishment Clause required that exclusion.

The church sought after-hours access to school facilities for a film series addressing family life and child-rearing from a Christian perspective. The district permitted social, civic and recreational uses but twice rejected this application under its religious-use prohibition. The requested meetings were open to the public and lacked school sponsorship. An earlier denial of Sunday worship services is not challenged here. The Court does not treat every kind of worship as within the forum or decide the broadest possible forum classification.

Render form: full. Basis: The Court adds an express implementing remand to the verified reversal-only historical decree. Distinct Establishment Clause joins also require clear proposition-level reporting.

### Holdings

#### A religious perspective cannot be excluded from an admitted subject

**Holding and operative rule:** When a public school makes an after-hours forum available for private discussion of family life and child-rearing, it may not reject that otherwise eligible discussion solely because the viewpoint is religious. This prohibition applies even assuming a nonpublic forum; it does not require an unlimited public forum or invalidate neutral restrictions on subjects and activities outside the actual forum.

**Authority:** White’s speech portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the district’s stated reason for denial is viewpoint discrimination. Perry and Cornelius permit a government to define a limited forum but require neutrality among perspectives on a subject it admits. Family life and child-rearing were eligible topics, and the films addressed those subjects from a religious perspective. Describing religion itself as an excluded subject cannot change that relationship. Nor does exclusion of every religious denomination cure discrimination between religious and nonreligious views. The record does not establish that the application was denied for anticipated disorder or some independent neutral eligibility rule. The district’s later alternatives therefore cannot sustain the stated denial. The Court need not decide whether all of the property is a public forum or whether every religious activity must be permitted there. The earlier Sunday-service application is outside this case. On remand, genuinely neutral scheduling, safety and subject-matter rules remain available, but the same viewpoint distinction does not.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association, 460 U.S.37: viewpoint neutrality within a nonpublic forum applied.
- Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, 473 U.S.788: permissible forum limits distinguished from forbidden viewpoint exclusion.
- Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S.263: religious perspective does not by itself remove otherwise eligible private speech from equal-access protection.

#### Equal private access does not establish religion here

**Holding and operative rule:** Allowing this voluntary, publicly accessible, after-hours film presentation without school sponsorship does not violate the Establishment Clause. Equal access serves a secular forum purpose and neither primarily advances religion as governmental activity nor creates excessive entanglement; this holding authorizes access to private speech, not an official devotional program.

**Authority:** White’s establishment portion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter (6 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the district cannot justify viewpoint discrimination by invoking an establishment problem that equal access does not create. Widmar explains why a government’s neutral provision of space does not make every private speaker’s message its own. The proposed films would be shown after school, attendance would be voluntary, and the meetings would be open to the public rather than presented as school instruction. Under Lemon, the forum serves a secular community-use purpose; allowing a religious perspective on an admitted subject does not produce governmental advancement of religion or excessive entanglement. The entered Lee decision addresses official graduation worship and religious pressure within that school event, conditions absent here. The contrary argument confuses public ownership of the room with school sponsorship of the message. Nothing in this decision orders religious instruction, public financing of devotion or compulsory participation. It also leaves outside review the separate denial of Sunday worship services and other activities outside the forum’s lawful scope.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Widmar, 454 U.S.263: neutral access and private attribution applied.
- Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S.602: purpose, primary effect and entanglement applied, not replaced.
- Lee v. Weisman, June 24, 1992: the entered official-worship and pressure holdings distinguished and preserved.

## Minnesota v. Dickerson

#### Authorized touch can supply probable cause without an additional search

**Holding and operative rule:** An officer lawfully conducting a protective patdown may seize contraband when the authorized touching itself supplies probable cause of its incriminating character and the officer has lawful access to it. The officer may not manipulate or otherwise explore a nonthreatening object beyond the frisk to obtain that probable cause; immediate recognition requires probable cause, not certainty.

**Authority:** White’s Part II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Constitution does not make sight categorically superior to touch when the original contact is lawful. Horton allows seizure of an immediately incriminating object encountered with lawful access, and Long permits seizure of contraband actually discovered within a legitimate protective search. A tactile perception satisfying the same conditions creates no additional search merely because it comes through a different sense. Terry nevertheless limits the patdown to its protective purpose. Hicks prevents an officer from undertaking a further search to establish the probable cause needed to justify the seizure. The State therefore cannot rely on an evidentiary manipulation to create its own authority. The contrary categorical rule overlooks cases in which the authorized contact alone supplies probable cause. Conversely, an officer’s bare assertion of immediate recognition does not erase the need for a supported account of the authorized contact. The holding does not license exploratory reaching into clothing or continuing a completed frisk on unparticularized suspicion.

## Gilmore v. Taylor

### Event

Gilmore v. Taylor; No. 91-1738; October Term 1992. Argued 1993-03-02; decided 1993-06-07. Review of the Seventh Circuit, 954 F.2d 441 (1992), reversing denial of federal habeas relief and directing a conditional writ.

Question: Whether Teague permits collateral relief under Falconer’s rule for the interaction of Illinois murder and voluntary-manslaughter instructions.

Taylor received a murder sentence of thirty-five years after a trial at which he relied on provocation and related mitigation. The charge permitted a murder verdict upon the stated elements without requiring jurors first to resolve the separate manslaughter instructions. Illinois discretionary review ended June 4, 1987, and Taylor did not seek certiorari. #### Falconer announces a new rule for this collateral proceeding

**Holding and operative rule:** Falconer’s specific requirement governing the interaction of these murder and voluntary-manslaughter instructions was not dictated by precedent at Taylor’s finality and is therefore a new rule under Teague. **Holding and operative rule:** The Falconer rule regulates trial procedure rather than removing conduct or a class of persons from the State’s substantive power to punish, and it is not a watershed procedural rule essential to the fundamental accuracy and fairness of the criminal process. #### Corrective adjustments operate within the authorized cost-method system

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1395x(v)(1)(A)(ii) requires suitable retroactive corrective adjustments when, for a provider and fiscal period, aggregate reimbursement produced by the methods proves inadequate or excessive; the Secretary permissibly construes that command as reconciliation of interim reimbursement to the amount payable under lawful reasonable-cost methods and limits. **Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the corrective-adjustment clause must operate within the reimbursement system Congress authorized. The statute permits cost methods and limits based on generalizations, while other provisions contemplate interim payments later reconciled to the amount lawfully due. Reading clause (ii) as an unconditional command to reimburse every provider’s actual excess costs would largely undo those limits and the specific exceptions accompanying them. The competing reading has textual force because the clause refers to methods and aggregate reimbursement rather than merely interim payments. That force establishes a real interpretive question; it does not make the hospitals’ construction compulsory. Chevron permits the Secretary’s reasonable resolution of that ambiguity, supported by the statutory arrangement and contemporaneous regulations. Georgetown’s rejection of unauthorized retroactive rulemaking does not independently decide this construction. The hospitals concede that no regulatory exemption or exception applies, and their challenges to the methods themselves are outside this review. Nothing here validates an independently unlawful limit or abolishes existing exceptions.

#### A changed agency interpretation is not automatically disqualified

**Holding and operative rule:** An agency’s change of statutory interpretation may reduce the weight accorded its view, but change alone does not make a considered, otherwise permissible construction unlawful. **Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

Cardoza-Fonseca confirms that statutory meaning and the limits of the delegation remain judicial questions. Here the Secretary’s history includes changes in explanation, and the Court considers those changes rather than pretending the position has been uniform. The current interpretation nevertheless accords with the authorized cost-method system and has support in the contemporaneous regulatory arrangement. Georgetown denies controlling weight to unsupported litigation rationalizations and forbids unauthorized retroactive rules; neither proposition requires rejection merely because the agency has changed its view. The hospitals’ contrary argument would convert reduced persuasive weight into automatic invalidity. The Court rejects that conversion, not the requirement of reasoned agency action. ## Antoine v. Byers & Anderson, Inc.

#### The court may decide a necessary antecedent question of governing law

**Holding and operative rule:** In this live dispute, the court of appeals could consider whether the statute authorizing the challenged activity remained in force even though the parties initially assumed it did, after giving them a fair opportunity to brief the issue. The city therefore cannot prevail merely by avoiding an express denominational name. Ordinance 87-72 reaches more secular conduct, but operates with the same scheme; its independent validity in another setting is reserved. The words ritual and sacrifice can have secular uses, and unequal impact alone is insufficient. The demonstrated definitions and operation supply the targeting conclusion without making subjective legislative motive an indispensable condition.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Fowler v. Rhode Island, 345 U.S.67: discrimination in the treatment of religious exercise prohibited.
- Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S.872: targeting falls outside the neutral-law rule; that rule is not reconsidered here.

#### Substantial exemptions for comparable secular harm defeat general applicability

**Holding and operative rule:** A law is not generally applicable where it substantially leaves unregulated comparable secular conduct threatening the interests invoked to burden religious exercise. Each challenged ordinance fails that minimum requirement here, including 87-72’s express state-law exemption for small-number hog or cattle operations; the Court does not define every boundary of general applicability.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Part II-B; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a city cannot pursue these asserted harms only when they arise from religious conduct. Smith’s general-applicability boundary and Fowler’s equality principle require examination of the comparable activities left outside the prohibitions. Food slaughter, hunting, fishing, pest eradication and permitted euthanasia undermine the claim that the first three ordinances comprehensively prevent unnecessary animal killing. The public-health distinction is equally deficient: disposal of carcasses and consumption of uninspected meat create the same risks regardless of religious motivation, yet the scheme leaves comparable secular conduct alone. Ordinance 87-72 has its own defect, an express exception for small-number hog and cattle operations qualifying under state law. The city does not explain why those operations escape the asserted health and cruelty concerns. This is substantial underinclusion, not an inconsequential imperfection in a comprehensive law. The Court need not define a universal general-applicability test or hold that every exemption invalidates every regulation. The unequal pursuit of the identified harms resolves these ordinances.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Smith, 494 U.S.872: general-applicability requirement applied, without reopening its neutral-law holding.
- Fowler, 345 U.S.67: unequal treatment of religious exercise applied.
- Hobbie v. Unemployment Appeals Commission, 480 U.S.136: protection against unequal treatment of religious observers preserved.

#### Narrow tailoring independently fails for every ordinance

**Holding and operative rule:** A restriction of religious practice that is nonneutral or not generally applicable must satisfy compelling-interest scrutiny and narrow tailoring. These ordinances fail narrow tailoring independently of whether the asserted ends could be compelling because comparable harms are left unregulated and neutral, narrower controls on disposal, care, killing methods and location could address the city’s concerns.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Part III, narrow-tailoring ground; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the city’s chosen means are not narrowly drawn to its asserted ends. Yoder and McDaniel require scrutiny of the actual burden, not acceptance of an abstractly important objective. General disposal rules could address carcasses left in public places; care requirements could address the conditions in which animals are held; and rules about killing methods could address cruelty without classifying the religious reason as unnecessary. The district court’s view that secrecy makes narrower rules unenforceable does not explain why a ban on private sacrifice would work better than rules against publicly observable disposal. A slaughterhouse label likewise supplies no justification by itself, and the first three ordinances apply regardless of location. The exemption in 87-72 further defeats the fit asserted for its own location rule. Under Arkansas Writers’ Project, failure of narrow tailoring suffices to invalidate the challenged restriction. The Court does not decide entitlement to an exemption from a genuinely evenhanded anti-cruelty law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Wisconsin v. Yoder, 406 U.S.205: rigorous justification of restrictions on religious exercise applied.
- McDaniel, 435 U.S.618: demanding scrutiny of religious disabilities applied.
- Arkansas Writers’ Project, Inc. v. Ragland, 481 U.S.221: lack of narrow tailoring independently suffices under strict scrutiny.
- Schneider v. State, 308 U.S.147: collateral public harms cannot justify unnecessary suppression when direct regulation is available.

#### The selectively pursued interests are not compelling in this application

**Holding and operative rule:** Where the government burdens protected religious conduct but leaves appreciable comparable harm unregulated despite feasible controls, it has not established that its asserted interest justifies the selective restriction at the highest constitutional level. Hialeah’s substantial underinclusion independently defeats its compelling-interest showing for this scheme; the decision does not declare animal welfare or public health inherently unimportant.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Part III, compelling-interest ground; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court also holds that the city has not established a compelling justification for the discrimination actually imposed. Strict scrutiny examines the interest in applying the particular restriction, not merely the importance of public health or humane treatment in general. The city permits many secular killings and fails to address comparable disposal and inspection risks while prohibiting the religious practice. Those choices leave appreciable harm of the same asserted kind outside the scheme. Florida Star illustrates why an interest cannot sustain an exceptional speech restriction when substantial equivalent harm remains unaddressed, and the same defect is decisive here. The city’s assertion that secular food use or pest control is obviously necessary does not explain why religious reasons alone must be discounted. This ground is independent of the narrower-means failure. It does not resolve the constitutionality of a comprehensive anti-cruelty law or deny the seriousness of the interests identified. It holds only that the selective scheme does not establish the required justification.

- Yoder, 406 U.S.205: the asserted interest must justify the particular restriction at the required level.

## Wisconsin v. Mitchell

### Event

Wisconsin v. Mitchell; No. 92-515; October Term 1992. Argued 1993-04-21; decided 1993-06-11. Review of the Wisconsin Supreme Court, 169 Wis.2d153, 485 N.W.2d807 (1992), which held the penalty enhancement unconstitutional.

Question: Whether the First Amendment forbids enhancement of the penalty for an independently criminal battery because the defendant intentionally selected the victim on account of race, and whether evidentiary use of speech makes the provision substantially overbroad.

A jury convicted Mitchell of aggravated battery after he directed a group attack on a white youth. The jury also found intentional racial selection under the applicable Wisconsin statute. The ordinary two-year maximum became seven years, and Mitchell received four years. Section939.645 applies when a person commits a covered crime and intentionally selects the victim, or property because of the owner’s or occupant’s status, on account of race, religion, color, disability, sexual orientation, national origin or ancestry. It requires a special verdict on those predicates and does not apply where proof of such status is already required for conviction of the underlying crime. The felony maximum may increase by up to five years and the fine by up to $5,000; the separate misdemeanor tiers are not the sentence at issue. Amendments enacted in 1992 are outside this case. The state court characterized the practical effect as punishment of thought; it did not supply a narrowing definition that eliminates the intentional-selection predicate.

Render form: full. Basis: The entered R.A.V. holding is substantial overbreadth, not the historical content-selection rule, and Stone takes the separate overbreadth disposition without an additional ground under the standing fallback. The reasoning and supporting coalition require full reporting.

### Holdings

#### Federal constitutional effect is not settled by a state court’s characterization

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court accepts the state court’s authoritative construction of statutory terms, but independently determines the federal constitutional effect of the law so construed. Wisconsin’s characterization of the intentional-selection enhancement as punishment of thought therefore does not resolve the First Amendment question or remove the statutory requirement of an independently committed crime.

**Authority:** White’s Parts I and II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respect for state-law construction does not surrender the federal constitutional inquiry. Ferber and Terminiello require acceptance of authoritative state definitions that determine what a law prohibits. The Wisconsin court here did not redefine a statutory term to eliminate the underlying crime or intentional victim selection. It instead characterized the practical effect of considering discriminatory motive as punishment of thought. Whether that effect violates the First Amendment is the question this Court must decide. The State’s reply that the statute concerns conduct is not, by itself, a complete answer either: the enhanced penalty does depend on a particular motive. The Court must therefore examine whether that motive is constitutionally relevant to the criminal act rather than presume validity from a label. This method preserves binding state-law meanings while allowing independent federal review. It creates no license to rewrite the selection requirement or decide unrelated state-law sentencing questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- New York v. Ferber, 458 U.S.747: authoritative state construction accepted.
- Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S.1: binding construction of operative state-law terms distinguished from the federal characterization at issue here.

#### Intentional racial selection can aggravate an independently criminal battery

**Holding and operative rule:** The First Amendment permits an enhanced penalty for an independently criminal battery when the defendant intentionally selects the victim because of race under the applicable statutory predicate. The State may prove a relevant motive through lawful evidence, but may not punish abstract racist belief, expression or association without its legally relevant connection to the offense or another legitimate issue.

**Authority:** White’s Parts I and II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the selection requirement identifies a feature of the criminal attack, not an independent offense of offensive belief. Barclay permits consideration of racial motivation connected to the murder there; the entered Dawson decision preserves that distinction while prohibiting irrelevant proof of abstract belief. A legislature may account for the distinct individual and community harms it reasonably attributes to deliberately discriminatory attacks. Hishon and Roberts likewise show that considering discriminatory purpose does not automatically convert regulation of conduct into punishment of protected expression. The entered R.A.V. decision invalidated substantial reach into protected speech and reserved the broader question of distinctions within unprotected speech categories. This statute instead requires an independently criminal act and intentional selection of its target. Nothing in that reserved question requires invalidation here. The State must prove the actual statutory nexus, and speech evidence remains subject to relevance and other evidentiary limits. No punishment for belief alone or automatic admission of offensive statements is authorized.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dawson v. Delaware, March 9, 1992: entered connection-to-legitimate-issue limit applied; abstract belief remains protected.
- Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S.939: racial motivation connected to the criminal act distinguished from abstract association evidence.
- R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, June 22, 1992: entered substantial-overbreadth holding distinguished; noncontrolling content-selection reasoning is not imported.
- Hishon v. King & Spalding, 467 U.S.69, and Roberts v. United States Jaycees, 468 U.S.609: regulation of discriminatory conduct distinguished from suppression of a protected message.
- Runyon v. McCrary, 427 U.S.160: protection for belief does not confer a right to engage in unlawful discrimination.

#### Speculative future evidentiary use does not establish substantial overbreadth

**Holding and operative rule:** The possibility that a person might suppress protected views for fear that those views could later help prove intentional selection in a future crime does not establish substantial overbreadth of this enhancement. The First Amendment permits relevant statements to prove criminal intent, subject to evidentiary safeguards and Dawson’s prohibition on punishment for abstract belief.

**Authority:** White’s Part III; White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the proposed chilling chain is too speculative to invalidate the statute on its face. It assumes that a person will withhold protected expression because the person might later commit a covered crime and the expression might then prove the required discriminatory selection. The statute does not prohibit the present expression itself. Haupt establishes that earlier statements may be relevant evidence of criminal intent even though the statements could lawfully have been made. Dawson supplies the necessary boundary: irrelevant belief or association is not a legitimate basis for punishment. Ordinary rules of relevance, reliability and admissibility continue to operate, so the decision does not admit every offensive remark a prosecutor offers. The contrary argument would turn legitimate evidentiary use into a general constitutional obstacle to proving motive. That attenuated concern is unlike a law directly reaching a substantial range of protected speech. It therefore does not sustain this facial challenge to the intentional-selection enhancement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Haupt v. United States, 330 U.S.631: relevant earlier statements may establish criminal intent subject to careful evidentiary scrutiny.
- Dawson, March 9, 1992: the entered limit on irrelevant abstract-belief evidence preserved.
- R.A.V., June 22, 1992: direct substantial reach into protected expression distinguished from speculative evidentiary chilling.

## United States Department of Treasury v. Fabe

### Event

United States Department of Treasury v. Fabe; No. 91-1513; October Term 1992. Argued 1992-12-08; decided 1993-06-11. Review of the Sixth Circuit, 939 F.2d341 (1991), reversing summary judgment for the United States on the competing priority statutes.

Question: To what extent §1012(b)’s first clause preserves Ohio insurance-liquidation priorities against the federal priority commanded by §3713.

Ohio ordered American Druggists’ Insurance Company liquidated in 1986. The United States asserted more than $10.7 million in claims as obligee on surety bonds and claimed federal priority. Ohio’s scheme placed administration first, specified employee wages second, policy-related and certain other enumerated claims third, general creditors fourth and government claims fifth. The wage preference covers up to $1,000 for services within the year before the liquidation complaint and excludes officers and directors. The federal statute requires first payment on the established insolvency/act-of-bankruptcy predicate; §3713(a)(2) excludes cases under Title11, but this state insurer liquidation is the setting at issue. The parties agree that §3713 conflicts with the state scheme and does not specifically relate to the business of insurance. The district court applied the Pireno factors and upheld federal priority throughout; the Sixth Circuit protected the state scheme.

### Holdings

#### The first clause protects the policy-performance priority

**Holding and operative rule:** Where a conflicting federal statute does not specifically relate to insurance, §1012(b)’s first clause preserves a state liquidation priority to the extent it regulates fulfillment of policyholder obligations. Ohio’s policyholder protection therefore prevails over §3713 in that scope; neither the insurance label nor inclusion in a broadly named class protects every other claim.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor (5 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that performance of the insurance promise remains part of insurance regulation when the insurer becomes insolvent. National Securities identifies the insurer-policyholder relationship as the relevant focus and permits a statute with different functions to be evaluated by the function actually at issue. Ohio’s priority for policy obligations is directed to payment of those promises, not merely to the existence of an insurance company. Royal Drug and Pireno do not confine insurance to writing the contract while making its performance irrelevant; their antitrust settings also differ from the first clause’s laws enacted for the purpose of regulation. The government’s argument that every claimant has become an ordinary creditor overlooks the continuing policy obligation. The first clause supplies Congress’s own allocation between qualifying state law and a conflicting federal law not specifically addressing insurance. The Court protects that operation, not the entire liquidation code. It does not decide every antitrust use of the phrase or confer priority on every claim grouped in Ohio’s third class.

**Precedent treatment:**

- SEC v. National Securities, Inc., 393 U.S.453: policyholder relationship and function-specific treatment of a state statute applied.
- Group Life & Health Insurance Co. v. Royal Drug Co., 440 U.S.205: antitrust setting distinguished; insurance companies’ every business decision is not insurance regulation.
- Union Labor Life Insurance Co. v. Pireno, 458 U.S.119: risk-transfer and policy-relationship criteria considered without excluding actual policy performance.
- Prudential Insurance Co. v. Benjamin, 328 U.S.408: Congress’s support for state insurance regulation confirms the enacted allocation.
- United States v. Knott, 298 U.S.544: pre-Act priority setting distinguished; it does not nullify §1012(b)’s later operative text.

#### Necessary administration shares the protected function

**Holding and operative rule:** A liquidation-administration priority is preserved against §3713 to the extent the administration is reasonably necessary to implement the protected payment of policyholder obligations. Ohio’s administration preference serves that function here, but the ruling does not transform ordinary wage debts or any expense merely labeled administrative into a protected claim.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor (5 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that protecting policyholder payment would be ineffective if the process necessary to collect and distribute the insurer’s assets could not begin. The Ohio liquidator must preserve and recover assets, evaluate claims and administer the distribution. Administration reasonably necessary to carry out that work is connected directly to fulfillment of the policy obligations protected by National Securities. This is more than a general assertion that every creditor preference may improve an insurer’s reliability. Royal Drug rejects that unlimited indirect-benefit reasoning, and the Court does not revive it. The government’s all-creditors approach would deny protection to the very process that makes the specifically protected policyholder priority possible. Conversely, calling a debt an administration expense cannot settle whether it has the required function. On remand, disputed actual expenses must receive their proper legal classification. The separate ordinary wage preference does not become necessary administration merely because employees helped the insurer before liquidation, and the Court fixes no amount of allowable expense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- National Securities, 393 U.S.453: the protected policyholder relationship includes administration necessary to implement the policy-payment function.
- Royal Drug, 440 U.S.205: indirect benefit to insurer reliability alone remains insufficient.

#### Ordinary creditor preferences remain subject to federal priority

**Holding and operative rule:** Section1012(b)’s first clause does not preserve Ohio’s ordinary employee-wage and general-creditor preferences against §3713 merely because those priorities appear in an insurance-liquidation statute. Their indirect relationship to policyholder welfare is too tenuous; the United States retains federal priority over these unprotected preferences, subject to the actual statutory trigger.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court; the four dissenters agree in this disposition on their broader ground; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor (5 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a limited protection for insurance regulation cannot become an exception for every debt of an insurance company. National Securities directs attention to the protected relationship, and Royal Drug rejects the premise that every measure improving a company’s reliability necessarily regulates the business of insurance. Ordinary employees and commercial creditors claim in those capacities, not by enforcing the insurance promise. Their possible contribution to the insurer’s general financial health does not connect their priority sufficiently to payment of policyholder obligations. The broader Sixth Circuit approach would erase the statutory boundary between regulation of insurance and regulation of all an insurer’s affairs. Section 3713 therefore controls the competing priorities for these classes. The four dissenters would reach that same result through federal priority throughout, but their agreement in result does not enlarge the majority’s rationale. The effect on remaining state priorities and severability belongs on remand under state law; the Court does not supply an equitable reordering of the whole statute.

### Law After Decision

Before: §3713 ordinarily controlled conflicting creditor priorities, subject to McCarran-Ferguson’s actual insurance exception. After: five preserve policyholder priorities and necessary administration under the first clause, while the wage and general-creditor preferences yield. Nine agree on that latter disposition but only five join the specific partial-protection rationale. The second clause’s antitrust exemption and the separate §1013(b) boycott provision remain unaltered; no Marks combination is needed.

### Separate Writings

Kennedy, joined by Scalia, Souter and Thomas, dissents from protecting any of the competing Ohio priorities against the United States. National Securities, Royal Drug and Pireno, in their view, concern the contractual transfer and spreading of risk between insurer and insured. Distribution after the company fails regulates relations among creditors, including policyholders as creditors, rather than that insurance transaction. The repeated phrase business of insurance should retain a consistent meaning across §1012(b), even though the two clauses address laws and activities differently. They object that the majority’s partial protection selects an appealing distribution without establishing why this priority statute regulates the insurance relationship in one class but not another. They accept that one broader enactment can have genuinely different regulatory functions, as National Securities illustrates, but deny that the competing priorities here supply that distinction. States remain free to provide liquidation procedures because insurers are excluded from the ordinary federal bankruptcy regime; those procedures must nevertheless honor federal priority. Their proposed full reversal is noncontrolling as to policyholders and administration, while reaching the same result as the Court for ordinary wages and general creditors.
