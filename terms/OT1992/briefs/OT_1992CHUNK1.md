**Question Presented / Granted Scope:** Whether forcible removal of a mobile home with alleged police participation implicates the Fourth Amendment even without a separate invasion of privacy or liberty.  
**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Seventh Circuit, en banc, 942 F.2d 1073 (1991), affirming summary judgment for the defendants on the federal claims.  
**Relief Requested:** The Soldals seek reversal and further proceedings on their § 1983 unreasonable-seizure claim. Respondents seek affirmance, including on the alternative ground that the evidence does not establish state action.

**Material Record:** Private park employees disconnected and towed away the family’s mobile home while eviction litigation remained pending. The district court found insufficient proof of conspiracy, but the court of appeals treated joint participation as sufficiently supported for summary-judgment purposes and rejected Fourth Amendment coverage. That procedural assumption is not a final finding against every defendant.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** The alternative state-action ground can support affirmance without a cross-petition because it would not enlarge the judgment. ##### Issue 1: Seizure and attribution

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** The Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments and 42 U.S.C. § 1983 govern. United States v. Jacobsen, 466 U.S. 109 (1984), distinguishes infringement of privacy from meaningful interference with possessory interests. United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696 (1983), and Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), separately scrutinize seizures. Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144 (1970), supplies joint-action principles. Monell, 436 U.S. 658 (1978), still limits municipal liability.

**Open Point:** Whether the civil setting or absence of a privacy intrusion removes this alleged taking of a home from Fourth Amendment coverage; whether the summary-judgment record permits attribution.

##### Issue 2: Separate merits and remedial inquiries

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Fourth Amendment coverage does not establish unreasonableness. Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989), directs use of an applicable specific constitutional protection rather than displacement by generalized substantive due process. Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527 (1981), concerns a distinct procedural-due-process inquiry.

**Open Point:** The lower court did not adjudicate the seizure’s reasonableness under the proper Fourth Amendment framework.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Below:** The en banc court treated a deprivation of property unaccompanied by privacy or liberty injury as outside the Fourth Amendment, particularly in private eviction litigation.

**Family:** The text protects houses and effects against seizures independently of searches; police participation supplies the governmental connection.

**Defendants:** A contrary rule could federalize routine repossessions; the park, not the deputies, physically removed the home, and state procedures already protected possession.

**Limits and alternatives:** A private repossession alone is not state action. Coverage leaves reasonableness, causation, municipal policy, and immunities to their own rules. The state-action argument remains an alternative ground rather than an additional appeal.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirmance is possible on a supported absence of state action, or on the proposed restricted reading of seizure coverage. Reversal on coverage returns the case for reasonableness and the remaining § 1983 requirements; it does not direct damages. A court accepting the summary-judgment inference of joint action need not finally determine that inference. The separate due-process theory need not be reached if the specific Fourth Amendment route suffices.

##### Issue 1: Prevailing-party eligibility

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Section 1988 permits a reasonable attorney’s fee to a prevailing party in covered civil-rights litigation. Carey v. **Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), makes degree of success central to fee reasonableness and requires an explained exercise of discretion. **Hobby:** The action sought substantial compensation and obtained essentially none; a large fee would reward near-total failure.

**Necessary distinction:** Eligibility does not compel the full lodestar or any particular positive fee. Conversely, the absence of compensatory damages does not itself establish the absence of a legally operative judgment. The amount issue was not the basis of the decision below.

Any decision on amount must distinguish limited monetary success, the actual claim vindicated, and demonstrated nonmonetary significance rather than presume public benefit.
