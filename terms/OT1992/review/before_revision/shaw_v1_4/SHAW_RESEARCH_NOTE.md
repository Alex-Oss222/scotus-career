# Shaw: what follows from the selected baseline

**Simulated date: June 28, 1993. Current revision: v1.4.** This note compares the legal consequences of the user's selected Stone position with other available theories. It does not recommend a districting policy. The revised position is in `SHAW_REVISED_POSITION.md` and the Shaw Section II of chunk 10.

## Dismissal and map validity are different questions

Shaw was an appeal from dismissal. The reported complaint alleged deliberate racial districting but did not assert vote dilution. The historical decision allowed the asserted equal-protection theory to proceed; it did not finally invalidate the map. That procedural account supplies the record, while the same-case merits reasoning is not treated as precedent entering Stone's simulation. [Shaw, 509 U.S. 630, 636–642, 658 (1993)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/509/630/).

| Legal premise | Consequence at this posture | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| The user's baseline: racial territorial design alone does not establish a deprivation | Affirm dismissal of that equal-protection theory | The map receives no general certificate of validity. |
| Assignment by race itself is a cognizable personal injury | A legally sufficient complaint can proceed to justification and factual adjudication | This changes the selected baseline; allegations alone do not resolve every justification and remedy question. |
| An independently cognizable deprivation is properly alleged | Apply that claim's own pleading rules and elements | No such deprivation can be invented from an omitted record; a proven access violation does not automatically require redrawing the map. |

Thus, invalidating the map **solely for racial design** would contradict the supplied premise. Invalidation for an independently established wrong remains logically consistent with it. The present text selects neither an imagined violation nor a hypothetical remedial finding.

## Clarifications incorporated

**Precedent is support, not mechanical compulsion.** *Wright* sustained a factual finding that the alleged racial purpose was unproved; it did not resolve the consequence of every proved racial design. *UJO* sustained the particular plan through fractured reasoning. Together they leave an interpretive dispute rather than an already adopted version of Stone's full rule. [Wright, 376 U.S. 52, 53–58 (1964)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/376/52/); [UJO, 430 U.S. 144 (1977)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/430/144/).

**The classification analogy requires an affirmative answer.** *Croson* subjects racial allocation of competitive opportunities to rigorous scrutiny. The v1.4 revision removes the suggestion that identifying racial assignment itself as an injury is a circular argument. Stone instead defends a distinction based on the operation of the challenged institution, acknowledging that shared formal access is insufficient to establish equal treatment. That remains his contested interpretation, not a holding already supplied by *Croson*. [488 U.S. 469, 493–505 (1989)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/488/469/).

**No heightened pleading is introduced.** The court reads factual allegations and favorable inferences under then-applicable notice pleading. Plaintiffs need not attach experts or a replacement map, and a valid claim cannot be rejected because counsel used an imperfect label. Amendment requests retain ordinary Rule 15 treatment. [Conley, 355 U.S. 41, 45–48 (1957)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/355/41/); [Foman, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/371/178/).

**Civic-status injury has an identifiable subject.** The inquiry asks what government imposed on the claimant under the doctrine protecting the interest involved. A discriminatory classification or separation may itself supply an injury without a second governmental act, financial loss, or a changed election result. Civic status creates no freestanding cause of action. *Allen* distinguishes personal unequal treatment from generalized stigmatic objection, but its standing analysis does not decide Shaw's substantive question. [468 U.S. 737, 755–757 (1984)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/468/737/).

**Territory is no immunity.** The *Gomillion* majority addressed loss of the municipal franchise. Whittaker's concurrence presents a broader counterargument: racial transfer between political divisions could violate equal protection even with equivalent voting privileges in the destination. The revision identifies that disagreement expressly. *White v. Regester* separately protects against impaired participation despite access to ballots. [Gomillion, 364 U.S. 339, 341–349 (1960)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/364/339/); [White, 412 U.S. 755, 765–770 (1973)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/412/755/).

**Statutory permission, compulsion, and compliance remain separate.** The relevance of racial voting evidence under §2 does not establish that this map was required; §5's nonretrogression principle does not itself demand the maximum number of majority-minority districts. Rejecting the design-only injury therefore does not depend on a finding that federal officials compelled these lines. [Gingles, 478 U.S. 30, 43–51 (1986)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/478/30/); [Beer, 425 U.S. 130, 140–142 (1976)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/425/130/).

## Practical limit

The selected rule channels litigation toward an identified constitutional or statutory wrong. It also leaves some deliberate racial designs outside this particular constitutional theory. That consequence cannot be erased by promising that another claim will always succeed. Courts would still need to distinguish an imposed civic inequality from objections to composition or anticipated representative behavior, using the actual allegations, applicable elements, and relief supported by proof.

## Researched revisions incorporated in v1.4

**Individual rights govern the inquiry.** Issue 2 now grounds protection of white plaintiffs in the personal character of equal protection and applies the same inquiry to every claimant. Statewide racial political strength cannot answer a personal deprivation. Brennan's *UJO* discussion reinforces the concern but remains a concurrence. Its correct pinpoint is **430 U.S. 171 n.1**; the original reporter places the footnote there, although an HTML transcription displays it after page 179. [Shelley, 334 U.S. 21–22](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/334/1/); [UJO, original U.S. Reports, 430 U.S. 171 n.1](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep430/usrep430144/usrep430144.pdf#page=28).

**Formal access is insufficient by itself.** Issue 4 adds *Anderson*'s official racial ballot labels and *McLaurin*'s segregated treatment within common facilities. They preserve constitutional protection beyond denied eligibility, lost votes, and financial harm. Stone's distinction between their governmental acts and demographic district design is expressly his interpretation; neither case already decides the districting question. [Anderson, 375 U.S. 399, 402–404 (1964)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/375/399/); [McLaurin, 339 U.S. 637, 640–642 (1950)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/339/637/).

**The civic-status boundary uses existing legal elements.** An official designation may itself impose unequal treatment; no second act or downstream measurable injury is universally required. Illustrations involving constituent services or participation processes remain source-specific applications, not a new cause of action. *Knight* supplies the limit on a guaranteed policymaking audience. *Heckler* illustrates why standing based on personal equality can coexist with a judgment sustaining the classification. The revised lower-court sequence retains these distinctions. [Knight, 465 U.S. 271, 283–287 (1984)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/465/271/); [Heckler, 465 U.S. 728, 737–741, 744–751 (1984)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/465/728/).

**The disposition and earlier reservations remain.** Stone still proposes affirmance of dismissal of the asserted racial-design-only equal-protection theory. The revision neither finds the map lawful overall nor replaces the governing classification standards. The statutory, congressional-prevention, and broader §9 discussions added in v1.3 remain intact. If Stone instead recognized assignment itself as an actionable injury, that would change the selected premise and ordinarily require further adjudication after reversal of dismissal, rather than immediate invalidation on the allegations alone.

## Additions incorporated in v1.3 and their scope

The user-directed revision incorporates the supplied combined text while retaining the existing distinction between Stone's proposed disposition, independently eligible law, and the historical record. The additional statutory and congressional-power explanation does not change his proposed affirmance of the racial-design-only claim's dismissal, supply findings validating North Carolina's map, or enter a simulated holding.

**Coalition and crossover claims remain conditional.** The revision preserves assessment under the statutory rule that actually governs; it does not decide that §2 necessarily permits combining distinct protected groups or requires districts where a minority elects its preferred candidate with crossover support. *Gingles* reserved submajority influence claims and their governing standards. *Growe* assumed aggregation without deciding its permissibility, requiring evidence of cohesion rather than demographic addition; *Voinovich* likewise assumed crossover/influence cognizability without deciding it. Cohesion alone establishes neither liability nor an exemption from other applicable requirements. [Gingles, 478 U.S. 46–47 n.12](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/478/30/); [Growe, 507 U.S. 25, 40–42 & n.5 (1993)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/507/25/); [Voinovich, 507 U.S. 146, 154, 158 (1993)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/507/146/). The latter two historical opinions predate June 28, 1993, but are same-term comparators, not automatically controlling simulated holdings: any use as governing law depends on the corresponding rulings actually becoming effective in the simulation.

**Supported congressional prevention has an existing legal foundation.** Congress may enforce the Reconstruction Amendments through valid categories that reach conduct not separately established as unconstitutional in each application. *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* sustained rational coverage using evidence of discrimination and supported inference of danger in other covered jurisdictions. *Morgan* applied the McCulloch standard with substantial deference to Congress's judgment. *City of Rome* sustained prevention of discriminatory effects notwithstanding the absence of discriminatory purpose in the challenged changes. These authorities support the added explanation; they do not establish North Carolina's compliance with a particular statutory duty. [South Carolina v. Katzenbach, 383 U.S. 301, 324, 328–331 (1966)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/383/301/); [Morgan, 384 U.S. 641, 650–656 (1966)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/641/); [City of Rome, 446 U.S. 156, 173–178 (1980)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/446/156/).

**Support and continuing fit are not new procedural hurdles.** The language about demonstrated patterns, serious risks, and reasonable fit describes a supported route within Congress's enforcement authority and Stone's jurisprudence. It adds no mandatory study, prescribed findings, local constitutional judgment, or independent reauthorization cycle. *City of Rome* upheld renewal despite progress, based on Congress's assessment of remaining conditions and risks. Coverage, bailout, and a properly presented constitutional challenge retain their enacted conditions, lawful decisional channels, and applicable burdens. Implementers may rely on valid legislative determinations but must satisfy the authority and application-specific obligations governing their own acts. [Morgan, 384 U.S. 653–656](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/641/); [City of Rome, 446 U.S. 180–182](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/446/156/).

**The broader §9 justification position remains reserved.** The revision identifies Stone's existing supported-repair position, including its rejection of an additional scrutiny tier after the defined permission is satisfied. This dismissal does not adopt that position as governing law for another field, replace a controlling classification standard, or decide the justification of an independently established deprivation. Any such departure requires the governing precedent and the Master's §16 discipline to be addressed in a case presenting it. Congress's enforcement authority itself supplies no power to diminish independent constitutional guarantees. [Morgan, 384 U.S. 651 n.10](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/641/).
