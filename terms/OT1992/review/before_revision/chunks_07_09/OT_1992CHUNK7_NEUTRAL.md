##### Issue 1: Mandatory sequence before institution of an FTCA action

##### Issue 1: The preserved make-whole remedy in ERISA’s trust-law setting

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** ERISA § 502(a)(3), 29 U.S.C. § 1132(a)(3), expressly permits participants, beneficiaries, and fiduciaries to seek an injunction or other appropriate equitable relief to redress covered violations or enforce the statute and plan. Section 409 and § 502(a)(2) separately address liability and redress against fiduciaries. Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S. 134 (1985), requires respect for the enacted enforcement scheme; Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101 (1989), permits trust-law guidance compatible with that scheme. Clews v. Jamieson, 182 U.S. 461, 479–481 (1901), and the historically relevant law of trusts support the equitable character of monetary restoration in breach-of-trust litigation. Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189 (1974), and Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local No. 391 v. Terry, 494 U.S. 558 (1990), distinguish legal damages from equitable monetary relief. Those authorities do not themselves resolve this ERISA provision’s application to the requested nonfiduciary compensation. [Record and authority sources](#sources-52).

**Open Point:** Whether monetary restoration for a knowing participant in a fiduciary breach falls within the particular equitable relief Congress authorized, notwithstanding the ordinary legal character of compensatory damages.

##### Issue 2: Statutory authorization, the disclaimed alternative ground, and limits of remand

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Section 502(a)(3) authorizes redress of violations of the relevant ERISA subchapter or plan terms; it is not authority to award equitable relief at large. A fiduciary’s breach of § 404, 29 U.S.C. § 1104, supplies an identified statutory violation. Whether the express remedial provision permits redress against a knowing nonfiduciary participant is distinct from whether that participant independently owes all of the fiduciary’s statutory duties. Section 502(l), enacted in 1989, addresses the Secretary’s penalties measured by recoveries involving fiduciary breaches or other persons’ knowing participation; it is contextual evidence, not a separate private cause of action. The traditional third-person participation rule is reflected in Restatement (Second) of Trusts § 326 (1959). Hewitt’s express disclaimer matters to the scope and necessity of resolving an alternative statutory argument. [Record and authority sources](#sources-52).

**Open Point:** How far the Court must resolve nonfiduciary statutory coverage to permit the preserved monetary claim to proceed when the respondent has disclaimed that independent ground for affirmance.

Participants and the United States as amicus invoke equity’s traditional jurisdiction over breach-of-trust claims, including monetary restoration for knowing assistance by a third person. They argue that § 502(a)(3) expressly authorizes equitable redress of the fiduciary’s statutory violation and that § 502(l), added in 1989, contemplates monetary recoveries involving other knowing participants. Hewitt relies on Congress’s distinctions among equitable, legal, and remedial relief and on the contrast between § 409’s express fiduciary liability and the narrower language in § 502(a)(3). Its argument is not that injunctions and restitution cease to be equitable when money is involved, but that these requested compensatory damages remain legal.
