# Shaw: what follows from the selected baseline

**Simulated date: June 28, 1993.** This note compares the legal consequences of the user's selected Stone position with other available theories. It does not recommend a districting policy. The revised position is in `SHAW_REVISED_POSITION.md` and the Shaw Section II of chunk 10.

## Dismissal and map validity are different questions

Shaw was an appeal from dismissal. The reported complaint alleged deliberate racial districting but did not assert vote dilution. The historical decision allowed the asserted equal-protection theory to proceed; it did not finally invalidate the map. That procedural account supplies the record, while the same-case merits reasoning is not treated as precedent entering Stone's simulation. [Shaw, 509 U.S. 630, 636–642, 658 (1993)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/509/630/).

| Legal premise | Consequence at this posture | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| The user's baseline: racial territorial design alone does not establish a deprivation | Affirm dismissal of that equal-protection theory | The map receives no general certificate of validity. |
| Assignment by race itself is a cognizable personal injury | A legally sufficient complaint can proceed to justification and factual adjudication | This changes the selected baseline; allegations alone do not resolve every justification and remedy question. |
| An independently cognizable deprivation is properly alleged | Apply that claim's own pleading rules and elements | No such deprivation can be invented from an omitted record; a proven access violation does not automatically require redrawing the map. |

Thus, invalidating the map **solely for racial design** would contradict the supplied premise. Invalidation for an independently established wrong remains logically consistent with it. The present text selects neither an imagined violation nor a hypothetical remedial finding.

## Clarifications incorporated

**Precedent is support, not mechanical compulsion.** *Wright* sustained a factual finding that the alleged racial purpose was unproved; it did not resolve the consequence of every proved racial design. *UJO* sustained the particular plan through fractured reasoning. Together they leave an interpretive dispute rather than an already adopted version of Stone's full rule. [Wright, 376 U.S. 52, 53–58 (1964)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/376/52/); [UJO, 430 U.S. 144 (1977)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/430/144/).

**The classification analogy is addressed directly.** *Croson* describes personal injury from a racial barrier to competing for contracts. The revised Stone text explains why, under the selected premise, territorial membership does not alone impose the same differential opportunity. That is the fictional justice's proposed distinction, not a claim that the contracting precedent is irrelevant or overruled. [Croson, 488 U.S. 469, 493–494 (1989)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/488/469/).

**No heightened pleading is introduced.** The court reads factual allegations and favorable inferences under then-applicable notice pleading. Plaintiffs need not attach experts or a replacement map, and a valid claim cannot be rejected because counsel used an imperfect label. Amendment requests retain ordinary Rule 15 treatment. [Conley, 355 U.S. 41, 45–48 (1957)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/355/41/); [Foman, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/371/178/).

**Civic-status injury has an identifiable subject.** The proposed inquiry asks what government actually imposed on the claimant—for example, exclusion from institutional membership or race-based withholding of ordinary access. It does not require money or a changed election result. *Allen* distinguishes personal unequal treatment from a generalized stigmatic objection, although its standing analysis does not decide Shaw's substantive question. [Allen, 468 U.S. 737, 755–756 (1984)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/468/737/).

**Territory is no immunity.** *Gomillion* involved boundary manipulation depriving excluded voters of their municipal franchise. *White v. Regester* concerned impairment of effective political participation despite access to ballots. These examples prevent Stone's distinction from becoming a requirement of total disenfranchisement or an exemption for geographic discrimination. [Gomillion, 364 U.S. 339, 341–348 (1960)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/364/339/); [White, 412 U.S. 755, 765–770 (1973)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/412/755/).

**Statutory permission, compulsion, and compliance remain separate.** The relevance of racial voting evidence under §2 does not establish that this map was required; §5's nonretrogression principle does not itself demand the maximum number of majority-minority districts. Rejecting the design-only injury therefore does not depend on a finding that federal officials compelled these lines. [Gingles, 478 U.S. 30, 43–51 (1986)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/478/30/); [Beer, 425 U.S. 130, 140–142 (1976)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/425/130/).

## Practical limit

The selected rule channels litigation toward an identified constitutional or statutory wrong. It also leaves some deliberate racial designs outside this particular constitutional theory. That consequence cannot be erased by promising that another claim will always succeed. Courts would still need to distinguish an imposed civic inequality from objections to composition or anticipated representative behavior, using the actual allegations, applicable elements, and relief supported by proof.
