## Brecht v. Abrahamson

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name:** Brecht v. Abrahamson  
**Citation or Docket:** 507 U.S. 619; No. 91-7358.  
**October Term:** 1992.  
**Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, pre-decision preparation; certiorari. The listed historical merits proceeding is retained as the reboot's working procedural premise, not as a newly rendered simulated grant.  
**Simulated Event Date:** 1993-04-21, the historical decision date used as the simulation calendar date.  
**Question Presented / Granted Scope:** Whether Chapman’s harmless-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard or Kotteakos’s substantial-and-injurious-effect standard governs federal habeas review of the conceded Doyle trial error.  
**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Seventh Circuit, 944 F.2d 1363 (1991), reversing the district court’s habeas grant and applying Kotteakos.  
**Relief Requested:** Brecht seeks reversal and habeas relief; Wisconsin seeks affirmance under the less demanding collateral-review standard or the record’s asserted harmlessness.

**Material Record:** Brecht admitted shooting the victim but testified that it was accidental. The prosecutor referred both to silence before Miranda warnings and to silence after them. The latter references violated Doyle; the former were treated as permissible impeachment under the relevant pre-warning cases. Wisconsin’s intermediate court reversed, its supreme court found the error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, and the federal district court disagreed under Chapman because credibility was central. The Seventh Circuit used Kotteakos and found no substantial effect. The record includes substantial inculpatory evidence and permissible impeachment, but their significance relative to the unconstitutional references is contested.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** This is pre-AEDPA § 2254 review of a preserved constitutional trial error, not a jurisdictional bar, a default case, or a structural-error claim. Characterizing the violation as merely prophylactic would evade Doyle’s due-process basis. The historical use of Chapman in habeas cases and the absence of an express holding deciding this precise standard must both be accurately stated.

**Relevant Dates:** State supreme court judgment: 1988. Federal district court ruling: 1991. Argument: December 1, 1992.  
**Companion or Related Matters:** Withrow shares the calendar date but concerns cognizability of Miranda claims, not the same error. Sullivan is a later direct-review structural-error matter.

##### Issue 1: Violation and error classification

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Doyle v. Ohio, 426 U.S. 610 (1976), and Wainwright v. Greenfield, 474 U.S. 284 (1986), prohibit use of post-warning silence inconsistent with the assurances accompanying Miranda. Jenkins v. Anderson, 447 U.S. 231 (1980), and Fletcher v. Weir, 455 U.S. 603 (1982), distinguish the pre-warning settings at issue. Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279 (1991), separates trial errors amenable to prejudice analysis from defects in the framework of trial.

**Open Point:** The violation is conceded; its effect is disputed. No structural-error classification is compelled by the mere existence of a constitutional violation.

##### Issue 2: Collateral harmless-error standard

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 2254 and 2111; Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), places on the beneficiary of constitutional trial error the burden to establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), supplies the substantial-and-injurious-effect standard in its statutory setting. Prior habeas decisions used Chapman without squarely deciding the proposed distinction.

**Open Point:** Whether the nature of collateral review justifies replacing Chapman’s standard and allocation in this setting, and how the chosen rule applies to the whole trial record.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Wisconsin, the United States as amicus, and below:** Collateral review serves a narrower role after state adjudication. Finality and federalism justify relief only for an error with a substantial effect. The improper references were limited and cumulative of extensive permissible impeachment.

**Brecht:** The State should not retain a constitutionally tainted conviction merely because it reaches federal court later. Chapman already permits harmless-error treatment; no statute replaces its standard. Credibility was the central defense issue, so a few references can be consequential.

**Limits:** Neither transcript-page counting nor the existence of considerable guilt evidence alone answers whether this error contributed to the verdict. Conversely, constitutional error does not require automatic reversal.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirm using Kotteakos after expressly adopting that collateral-review distinction; retain Chapman and either apply it or remand for its proper application; or affirm on Chapman only if the whole record establishes harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. A changed standard, a changed burden, and the ultimate remedy must be separately stated.

## Withrow v. Williams

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name:** Withrow v. Williams  
**Citation or Docket:** 507 U.S. 680; No. 91-1030.  
**October Term:** 1992.  
**Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, pre-decision preparation; certiorari. The listed historical merits proceeding is retained as the reboot's working procedural premise, not as a newly rendered simulated grant.  
**Simulated Event Date:** 1993-04-21, the historical decision date used as the simulation calendar date.  
**Question Presented / Granted Scope:** Whether Stone v. Powell bars federal habeas review of Miranda claims after a full and fair state opportunity, and whether the district court could grant relief on an unpleaded post-warning involuntariness theory without notice or an opportunity to respond.  
**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Sixth Circuit, 944 F.2d 284 (1991), affirming habeas relief on both the Miranda ground and the district court’s additional involuntariness ground.  
**Relief Requested:** The warden seeks reversal or restriction of habeas review and relief; Williams seeks affirmance.

**Material Record:** During questioning about a double murder, a sergeant threatened to lock Williams up. Williams made inculpatory statements, later received Miranda warnings, waived rights, and made additional statements. The state trial court found warnings timely and admitted the statements. The federal petition principally alleged Miranda error. The district court found the pre-warning statements after the custody-triggering threat inadmissible and also, without an evidentiary hearing or argument, declared the post-warning statements involuntary. The petition did not independently plead that due-process theory, and the warden did not consent to trying it.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Distinguish the cognizability of a Miranda claim from its merits, and both from an independent voluntariness claim. Stone v. Powell’s limitation is not absence of Article III or statutory jurisdiction. Rule 15(b) consent cannot be presumed from litigation of a different claim. The State was not given an opportunity to supply evidence on the additional theory.

##### Issue 1: Habeas cognizability

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 2254; Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966); Stone v. Powell, 428 U.S. 465 (1976); Kimmelman v. Morrison, 477 U.S. 365 (1986). Powell’s Fourth Amendment exclusionary-rule limitation has a particular rationale and does not by its terms dispose of every constitutional trial claim.

**Open Point:** Whether that rationale should be extended to Miranda, with its relationship to self-incrimination, reliability, and ordinary voluntariness review.

##### Issue 2: A fair opportunity to litigate the additional claim

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** The Due Process Clause’s voluntariness inquiry is distinct from the warnings safeguard. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(b), as applicable through habeas procedure, and ordinary notice and opportunity principles govern issues not pleaded.

**Open Point:** Whether an unpleaded and unconsented voluntariness theory may sustain the writ without factual or adversarial development.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Warden and United States as amicus:** The State already offered a full and fair opportunity; the costs of collateral relitigation justify extending Powell to Miranda. Independently, the district court lacked a properly litigated basis for the post-warning voluntariness ruling.

**Williams:** Miranda safeguards a trial privilege and helps prevent unreliable admissions. Eliminating review would often relocate disputes into less structured voluntariness litigation rather than save work. The pre-warning custody error remains a proper habeas claim.

**Limits:** Cognizability does not establish prejudice or a right to relief on every statement. A pro se petition is read fairly, but that does not authorize a court to decide a materially different contested theory without giving the opposing party a chance to answer.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirm Miranda cognizability while reversing the unpleaded voluntariness ruling and remanding the remedy. Alternatively extend Powell and dismiss or deny that component under the newly adopted limitation, expressly distinguishing the separate notice error. No lawful path simply affirms the post-warning factual conclusion as if it had been tried.

## CSX Transportation, Inc. v. Easterwood

**Case Name:** CSX Transportation, Inc. v. Easterwood  
**Citation or Docket:** 507 U.S. 658; Nos. 91-790 and 91-1206.

**Simulated Event Date:** 1993-04-21, the historical decision date used as the simulation calendar date.  
**Question Presented / Granted Scope:** Whether the Federal Railroad Safety Act and implementing regulations preempt Georgia negligence claims based on inadequate crossing warnings and excessive train speed.  
**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Eleventh Circuit, 933 F.2d 1548 (1991), affirming preemption of the speed claim but reversing summary judgment on warning-device preemption.  
**Relief Requested:** CSX seeks preemption of both claims; Easterwood seeks to preserve the warning claim and revive the speed claim through the cross-petition.

**Material Record:** A CSX train struck Thomas Easterwood’s truck at the Cook Street crossing in Cartersville, Georgia, on February 24, 1988. His widow alleged negligent warnings and excessive speed. Federal regulations set maximum speeds by track classification. A plan for federally funded warning improvements at this crossing was shelved and allocated funds diverted; the record does not establish installation under the specific federal warning-device requirements asserted. General federal involvement in crossing safety and a traffic-control manual are not the same as a project-specific operative requirement.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Both cross-petition dockets are part of this one listed matter. The issue is preemption, not proof of negligence or causation. The FRSA’s express covering language and local-hazard saving provision must be analyzed duty by duty. The ordinary excessive-speed claim is not automatically an essentially local safety hazard merely because the accident occurred at a particular place. The duty to slow or stop for a specific individual hazard is reserved if not presented.

##### Issue 1: Express preemption and warning-device coverage

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Then-operative 45 U.S.C. § 434 preserves state requirements until federal regulations cover the subject, with an essentially-local-hazard saving provision. 23 C.F.R. Preserving the speed claim requires a legal basis outside the covered subject or a valid saving provision, not generalized resistance to preemption. The surviving warning claim returns for ordinary merits proceedings.

## United States v. Olano

### SECTION I — NEUTRAL MODELING PACKET

#### A. CASE, RECORD, AND POSTURE

**Case Name:** United States v. Olano  
**Citation or Docket:** 507 U.S. 725; No. 91-1306.  
**October Term:** 1992.  
**Entry Stage and Event:** MERITS, pre-decision preparation; certiorari. The listed historical merits proceeding is retained as the reboot's working procedural premise, not as a newly rendered simulated grant.  
**Simulated Event Date:** 1993-04-26, the historical decision date used as the simulation calendar date.  
**Question Presented / Granted Scope:** Whether unobjected-to presence of alternate jurors during deliberations is reversible plain error, and how Rule 52(b) distinguishes error, plainness, substantial rights, remedial discretion, waiver, and forfeiture.  
**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit, 934 F.2d 1425 (1991), vacating convictions because alternate-juror presence was treated as inherently prejudicial and reversible per se.  
**Relief Requested:** The United States seeks reversal; Olano and Gray seek to preserve the new-trial ruling.

**Material Record:** Two alternates remained with the deliberating jury under an instruction not to participate. No timely defense objection preserved the Rule 24(c) violation. The Government concedes error and plainness for this review. The record does not establish that the alternates participated, influenced the verdict, or chilled discussion. The lower court presumed prejudice from their presence rather than requiring a case-specific showing or a justified category of presumed prejudice.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** Treat the case on the Government’s concession and the forfeiture premise; do not decide that counsel’s conduct necessarily constituted a valid intentional waiver of every relevant right. Rule 52(b) governs unpreserved federal trial error, not collateral habeas review. The then-effective Rule 24(c), not a later amendment permitting retention under different procedures, supplies the violation.

##### Issue 1: Plain error and intentional waiver

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure 24(c) and 52(b); United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157 (1936); United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1 (1985); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938). Rule 52(b) permits, rather than commands, correction of plain error affecting substantial rights; intentional relinquishment and failure to object have different consequences.

**Open Point:** The precise sequence, ordinary prejudice allocation, and relationship between substantial rights and discretionary correction.

##### Issue 2: Jury intrusion and prejudice

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Parker v. Gladden, 385 U.S. 363 (1966), and the received jury-influence cases recognize the importance of deliberative privacy. Richardson v. Marsh, 481 U.S. 200 (1987), ordinarily permits reliance on lawful instructions, without making all jury intrusions harmless.

**Open Point:** Whether these nonparticipating alternates require a categorical presumption of prejudice or whether the record must establish an effect.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Respondents and below:** Deliberative privacy is fundamental, and silent outsiders may alter discussion in ways a later record cannot capture. A presumption avoids making proof depend on inaccessible jury dynamics.

**Government:** The rule violation is conceded but the alternates were instructed not to participate. Presence alone does not establish influence or warrant a per se reversal rule. Forfeiture ordinarily places the prejudice showing on the defendant, and correction remains discretionary.

**Limits:** Plain-error relief is not confined to actual innocence; serious damage to fairness or institutional integrity can matter. A valid waiver may extinguish error, but mere silence does not establish that waiver.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Reverse the per se rule and remand because no necessary prejudice is shown; affirm only upon a justified presumed-prejudice category or record-supported substantial-rights finding and the further discretionary inquiry. Reserve cases where law becomes clear only after trial and categories of structural or presumptively prejudicial error not presented.

**Case Name:** United States v. California

**Question Presented / Granted Scope:** Whether federal reimbursement of a contractor’s state taxes creates a federal common-law refund action for alleged state-law assessment errors, and whether subrogation permits recovery after the contractor’s state claim has become time-barred.  
**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Ninth Circuit, 932 F.2d 1346 (1991), affirming summary judgment for California.  
**Relief Requested:** The United States seeks an approximately $11 million refund plus interest and declaratory relief; California seeks affirmance.

**Material Record:** California assessed sales and use tax deficiencies against Williams Brothers Engineering Company for 1975–1981. The federal contract reimbursed costs including taxes. At federal direction the contractor pursued administrative and timely state judicial remedies, then stipulated in January 1988 to a partial refund and dismissal without prejudice. ##### Issue 1: Federal interest and federal cause of action

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** United States v. New Mexico, 455 U.S. 720 (1982), separates legal incidence on a contractor from federal economic reimbursement. Federal restitution precedents including Bayne v. United States, 93 U.S. 642 (1876), and Gaines v. Miller, 111 U.S. 395 (1884), involve different relationships to wrongfully held federal money.

**Open Point:** Whether indemnification alone supplies an independent federal common-law right to recover a state tax alleged erroneous only under state law.

##### Issue 2: Derivative subrogation and pre-existing defenses

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Traditional subrogation gives the subrogee no greater claim than the holder through whom it proceeds. Guaranty Trust Co. v. United States, 304 U.S. 126 (1938), distinguishes federal immunity from limitations from the acquisition of a claim already subject to an infirmity.

**Open Point:** Whether the Government may avoid the contractor’s matured state-law bar through its later subrogation action.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**United States:** The money ultimately came from the federal treasury, supporting recovery for money had and received; alternatively, payment entitles it to subrogation and federal protection against state limitations.

**California and below:** Voluntary reimbursement does not make the contractor’s tax a direct federal tax or establish an implied obligation running from the State to the United States. A derivative claim carries the existing state-law bar.

**Limits:** A direct tax on the United States, a congressionally created remedy, or recovery of unlawfully diverted federal funds would raise different sources. None can be presumed merely from financial incidence.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirm if there is no independent federal claim and subrogation inherits the matured bar. Reverse only upon identifying a valid independent source or a subrogation rule that actually avoids the existing defense. No need to decide whether California correctly assessed the tax on the state-law merits.
