**Question Presented / Granted Scope:** Whether Chapman’s harmless-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard or Kotteakos’s substantial-and-injurious-effect standard governs federal habeas review of the conceded Doyle trial error.  
**Court and Judgment Under Review:** Seventh Circuit, 944 F.2d 1363 (1991), reversing the district court’s habeas grant and applying Kotteakos.  
**Relief Requested:** Brecht seeks reversal and habeas relief; Wisconsin seeks affirmance under the less demanding collateral-review standard or the record’s asserted harmlessness.

**Material Record:** Brecht admitted shooting the victim but testified that it was accidental. The prosecutor referred both to silence before Miranda warnings and to silence after them. The latter references violated Doyle; the former were treated as permissible impeachment under the relevant pre-warning cases. Wisconsin’s intermediate court reversed, its supreme court found the error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, and the federal district court disagreed under Chapman because credibility was central. The Seventh Circuit used Kotteakos and found no substantial effect. The record includes substantial inculpatory evidence and permissible impeachment, but their significance relative to the unconstitutional references is contested.

**Threshold, Preservation, and Vehicle Matters:** This is pre-AEDPA § 2254 review of a preserved constitutional trial error, not a jurisdictional bar, a default case, or a structural-error claim. Characterizing the violation as merely prophylactic would evade Doyle’s due-process basis. ##### Issue 1: Violation and error classification

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** Doyle v. Ohio, 426 U.S. 610 (1976), and Wainwright v. Greenfield, 474 U.S. 284 (1986), prohibit use of post-warning silence inconsistent with the assurances accompanying Miranda. Jenkins v. Anderson, 447 U.S. 231 (1980), and Fletcher v. Weir, 455 U.S. 603 (1982), distinguish the pre-warning settings at issue. Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279 (1991), separates trial errors amenable to prejudice analysis from defects in the framework of trial.

**Open Point:** The violation is conceded; its effect is disputed. No structural-error classification is compelled by the mere existence of a constitutional violation.

##### Issue 2: Collateral harmless-error standard

**Other Governing Law and Material Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 2254 and 2111; Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), places on the beneficiary of constitutional trial error the burden to establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), supplies the substantial-and-injurious-effect standard in its statutory setting. Prior habeas decisions used Chapman without squarely deciding the proposed distinction.

**Open Point:** Whether the nature of collateral review justifies replacing Chapman’s standard and allocation in this setting, and how the chosen rule applies to the whole trial record.

#### C. DECISION-MATERIAL LITIGATION POSITIONS

**Wisconsin, the United States as amicus, and below:** Collateral review serves a narrower role after state adjudication. Finality and federalism justify relief only for an error with a substantial effect. The improper references were limited and cumulative of extensive permissible impeachment.

**Brecht:** The State should not retain a constitutionally tainted conviction merely because it reaches federal court later. Chapman already permits harmless-error treatment; no statute replaces its standard. Credibility was the central defense issue, so a few references can be consequential.

**Limits:** Neither transcript-page counting nor the existence of considerable guilt evidence alone answers whether this error contributed to the verdict. Conversely, constitutional error does not require automatic reversal.

#### D. LAWFUL PATHS AND UNCERTAINTY

Affirm using Kotteakos after expressly adopting that collateral-review distinction; retain Chapman and either apply it or remand for its proper application; or affirm on Chapman only if the whole record establishes harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. A changed standard, a changed burden, and the ultimate remedy must be separately stated.
