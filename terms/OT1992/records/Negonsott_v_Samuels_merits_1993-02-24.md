The Kansas Act, Act of June 8, 1940, ch.276, 54 Stat.249, now 18 U.S.C. §3243, provides: “Jurisdiction is conferred on the State of Kansas over offenses committed by or against Indians on Indian reservations, including trust or restricted allotments, within the State of Kansas, to the same extent as its courts have jurisdiction over offenses committed elsewhere within the State in accordance with the laws of the State.” Its second sentence provides: “This section shall not deprive the courts of the United States of jurisdiction over offenses defined by the laws of the United States committed by or against Indians on Indian reservations.” These public-domain statutory quotations were checked against the complete official opinion text and lower appendix.

Section 1153 ordinarily assigns the listed major crimes committed by an Indian in Indian country to federal law and penalties; §3242 supplies the federal trial framework. The state aggravated-battery conduct is treated here as corresponding to a federal major assault. The question is the Kansas Act's coexistence with that allocation, not whether the State may prosecute a federal charge. The lower federal court regarded the Act as ambiguous but resolved it in favor of concurrent authority using legislative history. This Court affirms because the enacted text is unambiguous. Federal criminal jurisdiction is saved, not repealed; no implied repeal or general state-jurisdiction rule is necessary.

## Public action, judgment and remedy

The judgment of the Tenth Circuit is affirmed. The Kansas Act authorizes this state-law prosecution.

Unanimous, 9–0: Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas affirm denial of the jurisdiction-based habeas claim. Negonsott, a Kickapoo Indian living on the Kickapoo Reservation in Brown County, Kansas, was convicted of aggravated battery after shooting another Indian on that reservation in March 1985. The Kansas Supreme Court had reinstated the conviction in State v. Nioce, 239 Kan. 127, 716 P.2d 585 (1986), after the trial court initially set it aside for lack of jurisdiction. The later sentence was three to ten years. The Court orders no new trial or jurisdictional dismissal and leaves unrelated conviction claims unadjudicated.

## Controlling holdings

### Kansas-specific concurrent authority over state offenses

**Holding and operative rule.** Under 18 U.S.C. §3243, Kansas may prosecute offenses committed by or against Indians on Indian reservations, including trust or restricted allotments, within Kansas to the same extent its courts may prosecute offenses elsewhere in the State under state law, even when the conduct also violates the Indian Major Crimes Act. Federal courts retain their jurisdiction over federally defined offenses; this concurrent authority over conduct does not authorize Kansas courts to adjudicate federal criminal charges.

**Authority.** White's unanimous opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls the grant, the saving clause and the disposition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Kansas Act gives effect to both state and federal authority. Its first sentence grants Kansas jurisdiction over offenses involving Indians on reservations without excluding the major offenses. Its second sentence preserves the jurisdiction of federal courts over offenses defined by federal law. A single act may violate both bodies of law without allowing a state court to try a federal charge. Reading the second sentence to preserve exclusive federal authority over the conduct would withdraw a substantial part of the first sentence's unqualified grant. The otherwise exclusive operation of the Indian Major Crimes Act therefore does not defeat this specific congressional authorization. County of Yakima illustrates the need to identify the precise state power Congress authorized; it supplies no general state power over Indian country. Here Congress supplied the necessary authorization expressly. Because the text resolves the allocation, the canon favoring Indians in cases of statutory ambiguity does not change the result. Kansas could prosecute Negonsott for aggravated battery. The Court decides neither the independent scope of tribal adjudicatory authority nor any question of successive prosecutions.
