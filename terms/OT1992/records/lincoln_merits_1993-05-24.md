Question: Whether termination of an Indian Health Service program funded from an unearmarked lump sum is reviewable as an allocation decision under the APA, and whether this organizational and policy change required notice and comment.

The Service ended the regional Indian Children’s Program and reallocated resources toward a nationwide program. Congress funded the Service through lump-sum appropriations; no statute or regulation earmarked continued funding for this particular program. The district court ordered reinstatement, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed on reviewability and notice-and-comment grounds. The constitutional due-process claim was not resolved; the appellate court also did not decide the separate publication theory or the propriety of the reinstatement remedy.

## Controlling holdings

#### Allocation of this unearmarked lump sum is committed to agency discretion

**Holding and operative rule:** When Congress supplies a lump-sum appropriation without a binding program earmark or other meaningful allocation standard, an agency’s distribution among authorized statutory purposes is committed to its discretion under §701(a)(2). Legislative-history references and the general federal trust relationship do not require continued funding of this particular program; enforceable statutory boundaries and the separately presented constitutional claim remain distinct.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appropriations leave this choice to the Service. Chaney explains why a decision requiring the balancing of resources, priorities and expertise may lack a judicially manageable standard, and Locomotive Engineers confirms that the APA preserves established categories of discretionary administration. The relevant enactments authorize health services broadly but do not require continuation of this regional program. References in committee materials describe expectations without becoming an enacted funding restriction. Nor does the general trust relationship with Indian beneficiaries choose one eligible group’s program over services for others. Overton Park remains different because an actual statutory restriction can supply law for a court to enforce. The Service must therefore remain within Congress’s authorized purposes, but courts may not replace its allocation judgment on the grounds advanced here. Under Webster, the independent due-process claim requires separate consideration; discretion under the APA does not itself answer that constitutional question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S.821: discretionary resource-allocation rationale applied beyond the enforcement setting within the statutory limits stated.
- ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S.270: recognized discretionary administrative categories applied.
- Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe, 401 U.S.402: distinguished; an actual statutory standard remains enforceable.
- Webster v. Doe, 486 U.S.592: separate constitutional review preserved.

#### This organizational and policy change does not require notice and comment

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming the program’s termination is a rule, this reallocation and organizational policy change falls within §553(b)(A)’s exceptions for agency organization and general policy statements and does not require notice and comment. The ruling concerns a change in service availability, not a new binding eligibility rule, and leaves the separate §552 publication question unresolved.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the procedural requirement depends on what the agency action does. The Service redistributed resources and reorganized its delivery policy; it did not promulgate a new binding eligibility standard disqualifying persons who otherwise qualified for benefits. Section553(b)(A) excludes the organizational and general policy choices at issue from notice and comment. Ruiz does not establish that every reduction in a program serving Indians requires that procedure. It addresses an agency’s obligation to follow its own applicable requirements and cannot supply a universal rulemaking command absent from the statute. The Court does not rely on the separate benefits exception in §553(a)(2), because the Department has undertaken to follow notice-and-comment requirements despite that exception. That undertaking does not eliminate the relevant §553(b)(A) exclusions. Whether another publication duty under §552 applies was not decided below and remains outside this holding, as does the separate constitutional claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morton v. Ruiz, 415 U.S.199: distinguished; compliance with applicable agency procedures does not create a universal notice-and-comment duty.
