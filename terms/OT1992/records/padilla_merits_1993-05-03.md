## Controlling holdings

#### Suppression depends on the defendant’s own protected interest

**Holding and operative rule:** A defendant’s participation in a conspiracy neither creates nor defeats a personal Fourth Amendment claim: the defendant must show interference with his or her own protected property interest in a seizure or reasonable expectation of privacy in a search. The conspiracy exception is rejected, and the lower court must evaluate each reviewed defendant’s actual interests without reopening the unchallenged illegality of the stop.

**Authority:** Per curiam Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority agreement at this level of generality; no Marks inference.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that association in criminal activity cannot substitute for a personal constitutional injury. Alderman expressly rejects extending suppression rights merely because defendants are coconspirators. Rakas, Salvucci and Rawlings likewise require a defendant to identify the protected interest that the challenged official conduct invaded. The Ninth Circuit’s exception departed from that rule by treating a conspiratorial relationship as an independent source of standing. Actual ownership, control or a privacy interest may matter, but the conspiracy label itself does not. Jacobsen distinguishes interference with possession from invasion of privacy, and Soldal preserves that distinction in the home-seizure setting; an individualized inquiry must therefore consider the type of official conduct at issue. The Court does not decide that every respondent lacks such an interest, or that the evidence must be admitted against all of them. The Government has not challenged the unlawful-stop determination. Strubbe’s unpetitioned loss also remains untouched. Remand is required to assess the remaining defendants under the ordinary personal-rights rule.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Alderman v. United States, 394 U.S.165: applies express rejection of vicarious coconspirator rights.
- Rakas v. Illinois, 439 U.S.128; United States v. Salvucci, 448 U.S.83; Rawlings v. Kentucky, 448 U.S.98: applies individualized protected-interest inquiry.
- United States v. Jacobsen, 466 U.S.109: preserves distinct search and seizure interests.
- Soldal v. Cook County, December8 1992, simulated: consistent possessory/privacy distinction; home holding not enlarged into categorical vehicle standing.
