## Event and chronology

**Godinez v. Moran**, No.92-725; October Term 1992. Merits disposition effective 1993-06-24; argued 1993-04-21. On writ of certiorari to Ninth Circuit, 972 F.2d 263 (1992).

**Question:** Whether the constitutional competence standard for pleading guilty or waiving counsel is higher than the standard for standing trial, and how competence relates to a knowing and voluntary waiver.

**Material record:** Two psychiatrists found Moran competent to stand trial. About two and a half months later he sought to discharge counsel and plead guilty, explaining that he wanted to prevent presentation of mitigation. The trial court conducted a colloquy and expressly found competence and knowing, intelligent, voluntary waivers. Moran referred to prescribed medication; the court did not inquire further into it. A later state postconviction hearing rejected incompetence. The Ninth Circuit relied on the suicide attempt, medication, answers, and desire to waive mitigation in finding an inadequate inquiry under its higher standard. Direct review had already reduced the death sentence for the ex-wife's murder to life without parole; the other two death sentences remained.

**Relief sought:** Nevada’s warden seeks reversal of the conditional-writ judgment. Moran seeks preservation of relief allowing withdrawal of the pleas and proceedings with counsel.

## Controlling holdings

#### A common capacity standard and a separate actual waiver

**Holding and operative rule:** Dusky supplies the constitutional mental-capacity standard for standing trial, pleading guilty and choosing to waive counsel: sufficient present ability to consult with counsel with rational understanding, and rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings. A competent defendant may relinquish the rights only through a knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver; competence does not establish that the particular choice was informed and uncoerced.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that mental capacity and the validity of a choice are separate questions. Dusky requires rational understanding, not a bare awareness of the courtroom or the charges. Faretta protects a competent defendant’s informed choice of self-representation without requiring the legal skill of a lawyer. That distinction defeats the claim that the decision to waive counsel necessarily requires professional ability to conduct a defense. It does not relieve the judge of examining the choice actually made. Boykin requires an informed guilty plea, and Zerbst requires a valid relinquishment of counsel. Westbrook’s additional inquiry is preserved through those waiver requirements rather than a second, higher constitutional capacity threshold. The Ninth Circuit therefore cannot sustain its conditional writ on the premise that ordinary trial competence is constitutionally insufficient as a capacity standard. States remain free to provide further safeguards. The Court decides neither competence for execution nor a distinct rule for the later conduct of a trial without counsel.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S.402: the functional competence standard governs each presented choice.
- Faretta v. California, 422 U.S.806: legal skill is distinct from competence to choose representation.
- Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S.238, and Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S.458: actual informed and voluntary waiver remains separately required.
- Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S.150: additional waiver inquiry preserved; a higher constitutional capacity quantum is not required.

#### Existing findings do not eliminate the distinct inquiry on remand

**Holding and operative rule:** A further competence determination is required when circumstances give the court reason to doubt present competence; no automatic hearing is required for every plea or waiver. Reversal of the higher-standard ruling leaves the existing claims about adequate inquiry and informed, voluntary relinquishment for consideration under the correct standards and the applicable pre-AEDPA treatment of state findings.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that rejecting the Ninth Circuit’s legal standard does not adjudicate every factual and remedial issue in Nevada’s favor. The trial court expressly found competence and valid waivers, and a later state hearing considered the challenge. Those findings must be addressed, not described as nonexistent. Pate and Drope nevertheless require attention to circumstances suggesting present incapacity, including relevant changes after an earlier examination. The medication and other circumstances therefore bear on the questions the proper doctrines ask; they are neither automatic proof of incompetence nor matters conclusively answered by older reports. Medina leaves that inquiry duty intact, and the entered Riggins decision does not equate medication with incapacity. The reviewing court must also consider whether any necessary retrospective determination can reliably be made and whether each relinquishment was informed and voluntary. The unraised Teague defense is not introduced here. The conditional-writ direction resting on the higher standard falls; no already-reduced death sentence is restored.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S.375, and Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S.162: reasonable doubt concerning present competence triggers inquiry.
- Medina, June 22, 1992, and Riggins, May 18, 1992: their entered limited rules are preserved, not enlarged into conclusive findings here.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S.37: the Court may decline to introduce an unraised Teague defense.
