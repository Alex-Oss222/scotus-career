## Controlling holdings

#### The sentencing remand does not prevent review of this completed conviction ruling

**Holding and operative rule:** Under §1257 and Cox’s surviving-federal-question category, the separate sentencing remand does not prevent review of the finally resolved federal challenge to Sullivan’s conviction, which will remain whatever lawful sentence follows. Review does not reach the correctness of the penalty-phase relief or create a general exception for interlocutory criminal rulings.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the unresolved sentencing proceedings do not remove the federal conviction question from review. Cox permits review when further proceedings cannot eliminate the already determined federal issue that survives them. Louisiana conclusively sustained guilt against this constitutional challenge, while granting relief for a distinct failure of counsel in the penalty phase. A new sentencing hearing cannot supply the missing constitutionally adequate jury determination of guilt and cannot reconsider the federal ruling sustaining the conviction. The two components must therefore remain separate both in assessing finality and in defining the Court’s mandate. This application does not turn every intermediate criminal ruling into a final judgment: the conviction question here is concluded below and remains irrespective of the remaining sentence proceedings. The Court accordingly reaches the harmless-error issue while leaving the defendant’s independently obtained sentencing relief intact.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 U.S.469, 480–481: surviving-federal-question finality applied to the distinct conviction and sentencing components.

#### A charge vitiating the reasonable-doubt standard leaves no valid verdict for harmless review

**Holding and operative rule:** When an unconstitutional reasonable-doubt charge vitiates the jury’s governing standard for all factual findings, the defendant has received no constitutionally valid jury verdict of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. That structural defect requires reversal without harmless-error assessment; an appellate court’s hypothetical verdict cannot substitute for the required jury determination, and this rule does not classify every instructional mistake as structural.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the required finding belongs to a properly instructed jury. Winship supplies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard, and Duncan assigns the criminal verdict to the jury. Chapman asks whether a constitutional error contributed to the verdict actually rendered; it does not authorize a reviewing court to make the missing verdict itself. Here the conceded Cage defect reaches the standard governing all findings, so no valid jury determination remains against which to measure the error. Rose involved a separable erroneous presumption and therefore does not establish harmlessness where the entire adjudicative foundation is missing. Fulminante identifies defects of that structure as outside ordinary harmless-error analysis. The strength of the evidence cannot transfer the jury’s constitutional responsibility to appellate judges. The Court reserves different instructional omissions and inaccuracies, accepts the conceded defect rather than reopening it under Estelle, and leaves Brecht’s separate preserved-Doyle habeas rule unchanged.

**Precedent treatment:**

- In re Winship, 397 U.S.358: proof-beyond-reasonable-doubt requirement applied.
- Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S.145: jury’s constitutional role applied.
- Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S.39: conceded defect taken as the predicate, not relitigated.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S.18: actual-verdict inquiry preserved; no substitute hypothetical verdict permitted.
- Rose v. Clark, 478 U.S.570: separable presumption error distinguished.
- Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S.279: structural-defect category applied.
- Estelle v. McGuire, December4,1991: contested-charge framework preserved; no reopening of the conceded defect.
- Brecht v. Abrahamson, April21,1993: preserved-Doyle habeas rule unchanged; not a Kotteakos constitutional-habeas rule.
