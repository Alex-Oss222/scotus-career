## Controlling holdings

#### Corrective adjustments operate within the authorized cost-method system

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1395x(v)(1)(A)(ii) requires suitable retroactive corrective adjustments when, for a provider and fiscal period, aggregate reimbursement produced by the methods proves inadequate or excessive; the Secretary permissibly construes that command as reconciliation of interim reimbursement to the amount payable under lawful reasonable-cost methods and limits. The clause does not require an individualized exception whenever actual costs exceed a ceiling, and authorized exceptions and independent challenges to unlawful methods remain intact.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the corrective-adjustment clause must operate within the reimbursement system Congress authorized. The statute permits cost methods and limits based on generalizations, while other provisions contemplate interim payments later reconciled to the amount lawfully due. Reading clause (ii) as an unconditional command to reimburse every provider’s actual excess costs would largely undo those limits and the specific exceptions accompanying them. The competing reading has textual force because the clause refers to methods and aggregate reimbursement rather than merely interim payments. That force establishes a real interpretive question; it does not make the hospitals’ construction compulsory. Chevron permits the Secretary’s reasonable resolution of that ambiguity, supported by the statutory arrangement and contemporaneous regulations. Georgetown’s rejection of unauthorized retroactive rulemaking does not independently decide this construction. The hospitals concede that no regulatory exemption or exception applies, and their challenges to the methods themselves are outside this review. Nothing here validates an independently unlawful limit or abolishes existing exceptions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S.837: traditional tools followed by permissible construction of remaining ambiguity applied.
- Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S.204: unauthorized retroactive rulemaking distinguished; its holding does not decide this clause’s meaning.
- Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, March 9, 1992: entered text/coexistence principle preserved; the dispute concerns what these provisions require, not implied repeal.

#### A changed agency interpretation is not automatically disqualified

**Holding and operative rule:** An agency’s change of statutory interpretation may reduce the weight accorded its view, but change alone does not make a considered, otherwise permissible construction unlawful. The Court must examine the text, delegated setting and reasons; an unsupported litigation position cannot substitute for the legally required agency explanation or obtain automatic controlling weight.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that consistency matters without making an agency’s first view irrevocable. FEC v. Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee recognizes that a reasonable agency construction may receive deference within its delegated field. Cardoza-Fonseca confirms that statutory meaning and the limits of the delegation remain judicial questions. Here the Secretary’s history includes changes in explanation, and the Court considers those changes rather than pretending the position has been uniform. The current interpretation nevertheless accords with the authorized cost-method system and has support in the contemporaneous regulatory arrangement. Georgetown denies controlling weight to unsupported litigation rationalizations and forbids unauthorized retroactive rules; neither proposition requires rejection merely because the agency has changed its view. The hospitals’ contrary argument would convert reduced persuasive weight into automatic invalidity. The Court rejects that conversion, not the requirement of reasoned agency action. No general deference regime replaces Chevron, and neither a defective explanation nor an independently unlawful method is insulated by this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- FEC v. Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, 454 U.S.27: reasonable agency interpretation within the statutory delegation applied.
- INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S.421: judicial interpretation and the significance of inconsistent agency positions preserved.
- Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S.204: unsupported litigation rationalizations receive no automatic controlling weight; change itself is a different issue.

## Continuity and published noncontrolling positions

Souter, joined by Stevens and Scalia, dissents on the statutory text. Clause (ii) speaks of methods that produce inadequate or excessive aggregate reimbursement for a provider and period, not merely estimates pending final settlement. Section 1395g already authorizes adjustment of interim payments, so assigning that same work to clause (ii) deprives its distinct language of effect. Clause (i)’s no-cost-shifting requirement reinforces a final reimbursement obligation. Aggregate treatment prevents a right to reimbursement for every isolated cost item, but it still requires correction of methods producing an inadequate total. On that reading the statute is unambiguous and Chevron cannot authorize the Secretary’s contrary construction. The dissent does not rest on a categorical refusal to defer to agencies that change position. It would reverse the Eighth Circuit’s denial of the asserted adjustment right; that proposed result is noncontrolling.

Supreme Court review ends by affirmance. No reimbursement amount or individualized hearing is ordered, and abandoned method challenges are not revived. The decision changes no Court membership, allotment or pending original-jurisdiction stage.
