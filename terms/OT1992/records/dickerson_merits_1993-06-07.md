## Controlling holdings

#### The legally consequential diversion record keeps the case live

**Holding and operative rule:** Completion and dismissal under Minnesota’s §152.18 diversion procedure do not moot this review where reversal could restore a retained record legally usable in later proceedings. The conclusion rests on those identified statutory consequences, not on an invented conviction or a universal presumption about every dismissed charge.

**Authority:** White’s Part I; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that completion of probation does not eliminate the legal stake in this suppression judgment. Minnesota’s procedure withholds a judgment of conviction and dismisses the charge after successful diversion, so the Court does not rely on the ordinary consequences of a conviction that never occurred. The statute nevertheless retains a nonpublic record. Goodrich identifies its potential use in Minnesota proceedings, and Frank identifies its relevance to federal criminal-history treatment. Reversal could therefore alter a legally operative record rather than merely declare that a past search was proper. Sibron’s concern with continuing legal consequences supports adjudication on this concrete basis. Dickerson’s contrary argument correctly emphasizes dismissal, but overlooks the consequences the statute preserves despite dismissal. The Court does not decide that every expired diversion arrangement or dismissed prosecution necessarily remains live. The identified uses of this retained record, and the effect the requested judgment could have on them, are sufficient here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S.40: continuing legal consequences support a live controversy; the actual diversion scheme supplies those consequences.
- State v. Goodrich, 256 N.W.2d 506 (Minn.1977): retained diversion record’s state-law consequences applied.
- United States v. Frank, 932 F.2d 700 (8th Cir.1991): identified federal criminal-history use of the retained disposition considered.

#### Authorized touch can supply probable cause without an additional search

**Holding and operative rule:** An officer lawfully conducting a protective patdown may seize contraband when the authorized touching itself supplies probable cause of its incriminating character and the officer has lawful access to it. The officer may not manipulate or otherwise explore a nonthreatening object beyond the frisk to obtain that probable cause; immediate recognition requires probable cause, not certainty.

**Authority:** White’s Part II; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Constitution does not make sight categorically superior to touch when the original contact is lawful. Horton allows seizure of an immediately incriminating object encountered with lawful access, and Long permits seizure of contraband actually discovered within a legitimate protective search. A tactile perception satisfying the same conditions creates no additional search merely because it comes through a different sense. Terry nevertheless limits the patdown to its protective purpose. Hicks prevents an officer from undertaking a further search to establish the probable cause needed to justify the seizure. The State therefore cannot rely on an evidentiary manipulation to create its own authority. The contrary categorical rule overlooks cases in which the authorized contact alone supplies probable cause. Conversely, an officer’s bare assertion of immediate recognition does not erase the need for a supported account of the authorized contact. The holding does not license exploratory reaching into clothing or continuing a completed frisk on unparticularized suspicion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S.1: the protective purpose limits the scope of the frisk.
- Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S.1032: seizure of contraband found within an authorized protective search applied.
- Horton v. California, 496 U.S.128: lawful access and immediately apparent incriminating character extended to lawful touch.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S.321: probable cause may not be manufactured through a separate unauthorized search.
- Soldal v. Cook County, December 8, 1992: independent possessory protection preserved; no enlargement of a frisk follows.

#### Manipulation after excluding a weapon requires suppression here

**Holding and operative rule:** When the accepted findings establish that the officer identified contraband only by squeezing and manipulating an object after determining that it was not a weapon, the examination exceeds Terry and cannot justify the seizure. That independent ground sustains suppression here despite the state court’s erroneous categorical rejection of tactile recognition.

**Authority:** White’s Parts III and IV; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (7 Justices). Direct majority support at this level of generality; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the state court’s supported account resolves the legality of this seizure. The officer’s protective examination had established that the lump was not a weapon before further manipulation disclosed its identity as cocaine. Terry did not authorize that further investigation, and Hicks does not allow the information gained through it to justify the intrusion retrospectively. Rejecting the state court’s categorical view of touch therefore does not require reversal of its judgment. The remand position points to imprecision in the trial findings and asks whether the state court’s legal error influenced its reconstruction. The majority nevertheless accepts the state court’s supported finding of the additional examination, which independently answers the correct constitutional question. It does not demand a reconstruction of every passing subjective thought or reject the officer’s sensory competence in general. On this record the authorized search ended before the necessary identification occurred, and the cocaine remains suppressed without another evidentiary opportunity for the State.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Terry, 392 U.S.1: protective authority ended when the object was known not to be a weapon.
- Hicks, 480 U.S.321: information obtained by the additional exploration cannot validate it retrospectively.
