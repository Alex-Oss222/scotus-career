The commercial-handbill definition covers printed material advertising sale of merchandise or business for gain and specified paid events; the distribution provision selectively prohibits commercial handbills in public places. The case concerns its newsrack application, not adjudication of every handbill application. The asserted safety and aesthetic interests are substantial, and the rack categories cause the same physical harms. The district court's separate procedural-due-process rejection was not cross-appealed. The Sixth Circuit's possible broader view of truthful commercial speech is unnecessary and not adopted.

## Controlling holdings

### 1. The selected commercial category lacks the required fit

**Holding and operative rule.** When truthful, nonmisleading commercial publications concerning lawful activity and newspapers cause the same asserted physical harms through their newsracks, the City must justify why its selected commercial category reasonably fits its safety and aesthetic interests. Cincinnati has not done so: removing respondents' sixty-two racks while retaining approximately 1,500–2,000 physically similar newspaper racks violates the First Amendment; a small reduction is not invalid merely for being small, and no least-restrictive-means or all-sources-at-once requirement follows.

**Authority.** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven, throughout.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the content distinction must do constitutional work connected to the City's asserted objectives. Central Hudson requires a substantial interest, direct advancement and an appropriately limited restriction of protected commercial speech. Board of Trustees v. Fox makes that fit reasonable and proportional, not necessarily perfect or the least restrictive available. Here safety and appearance are substantial concerns, but respondents' racks do not create different physical harms from the many newspaper racks the City leaves untouched. The lower value assigned to commercial speech does not explain the choice of this category to address those harms. Removing sixty-two racks produces some reduction; its size alone is not decisive. The defect is the unexplained selection among equivalent sources of the stated problem. Neutral size, location, number and maintenance rules illustrate alternatives relevant to fit without becoming mandatory alternatives. The Court does not abolish commercial-speech distinctions or adopt the view that all truthful commercial speech receives full strict scrutiny.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S.557 (1980): applies the existing commercial-speech test to the accepted classification.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S.469 (1989): applies reasonable proportional fit and government burden; preserves rejection of least restrictive means.
- Metromedia, Inc. v. San Diego, 453 U.S.490 (1981): distinguishes the billboard treatment; does not permit an unrelated content distinction without the required fit.

### 2. The selective prohibition is not content neutral

**Holding and operative rule.** A newsrack prohibition determined by whether its publication is a commercial handbill is content based when the asserted safety and aesthetic harms do not justify that distinction without reference to the publication's content. Cincinnati cannot sustain this selective ban as a content-neutral time, place and manner restriction merely because its objective is benign; otherwise lawful neutral regulation of newsracks remains available.

**Authority.** Stevens's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven. This rejects the City's independent time, place and manner defense.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the City cannot convert this selective prohibition into a neutral regulation by describing its objective as safety or appearance. Ward v. Rock Against Racism and Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence permit appropriate regulation of the time, place and manner of speech when it is justified without reference to content. The operative distinction here requires looking at the publication and excludes commercial handbills while leaving newspapers in the same physical setting. The City's interests explain concern about racks generally but do not explain that selection. Renton v. Playtime Theatres permits attention to distinct secondary effects; the record identifies no comparable difference between the physical effects of these categories of racks. Benign motive therefore does not establish content neutrality. The Court does not hold that every regulation touching commercial expression is invalid or that the City lacks authority over sidewalks. A properly drawn neutral regulation remains subject to the established standards and may address the demonstrated physical problems.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S.781 (1989): applies justification without reference to content; a benign objective alone does not validate the selective line.
- Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S.288 (1984): preserves valid content-neutral time, place and manner regulation.
- Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 475 U.S.41 (1986): distinguishes different secondary effects; no such difference justifies this rack classification.

## Precedent treatment and law after decision

The holding-specific lines supply the operative treatment. Central Hudson and Fox remain the governing commercial-speech framework; Metromedia is distinguished, not overruled. Ward, Clark and Renton retain their existing operation. The decision creates no general commercial/noncommercial equality rule. Actual Casey's bounded medical-information holdings and the other actual speech decisions remain in their distinct settings.

The particular selective ban fails reasonable fit and cannot be upheld as content-neutral time, place and manner regulation. Incremental regulation remains possible, and alternatives matter without imposing least restrictive means. A city must explain a content distinction's relation to the harms it invokes; physical rack rules remain available if otherwise lawful. No claim about all newsrack restrictions, misleading advertising, unlawful transactions or every commercial classification is resolved.

## Continuity and published noncontrolling positions

Blackmun joins the Court but would afford truthful, noncoercive commercial speech about lawful activity the full protection ordinarily afforded speech. Virginia Pharmacy protects the public's interest in receiving useful commercial information; commercial motivation alone does not justify reduced protection. Deceptive or coercive practices, offers of unlawful transactions and appropriate time, place and manner regulation remain regulable. His broader rule is not needed to invalidate this selective rack prohibition and does not replace the Court's Central Hudson/Fox analysis.

White, joined by Thomas, would uphold the ordinance's application. The dissent sees each removed rack as a real reduction of obstruction and visual clutter and allows the City to proceed incrementally. Metromedia permits differing treatment of commercial advertising, and Fox rejects a demand that government use the least restrictive means. In the dissent's view, the accepted distinction between commercial publications and newspapers permits the City to preserve the latter while addressing a portion of the physical problem. The dissent warns that the Court's use of the small affected fraction and alternative regulations undervalues local discretion. The Court answers that neither small scale nor incremental action alone is the defect; the unexplained content selection is.

Blackmun: full protection for truthful, noncoercive lawful commercial speech, while deception/coercion, unlawful transactions and valid time/place/manner rules remain regulable. White/Thomas: accepted commercial distinction permits incremental rack removal; Fox and Metromedia leave municipalities more discretion. Neither separate view replaces the seven-Justice fit rule.
