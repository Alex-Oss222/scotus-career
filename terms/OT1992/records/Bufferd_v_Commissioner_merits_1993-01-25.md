## Controlling holdings

**Holding and operative rule.** For an assessment of an S corporation shareholder’s individual income tax, the return that starts the ordinary § 6501(a) limitations period is the shareholder’s return, including applicable extensions and statutory exceptions. Expiration of the period associated with the corporation’s § 6037 information return does not bar correcting pass-through items in a timely shareholder assessment; the corporate return remains operative for corporate-level taxes to which it applies.

**Authority.** White’s opinion for the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. All nine Justices adopt this rule and its explanation; it controls by a direct majority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the assessment period follows the taxpayer whose liability is being assessed. Section 6501(a) links the return to that liability, and § 6501(c)(4) permits the taxpayer and Commissioner to extend the period by agreement. Compo’s erroneous loss and credit flowed to Bufferd; correcting those items did not assess a corporate tax. Automobile Club of Michigan explains why a filing lacking the information needed to compute the relevant tax does not start the assessment period. Germantown Trust likewise concerns an adequate return for the taxpayer assessed, not a substitute return for someone else. Section 6037 does not require a different answer: its limitations language retains work when the corporation owes tax, including when its S election is invalid. The burden of retaining corporate records cannot change the taxpayer Congress identified. Bufferd’s individual extension therefore defeats this corporate-period defense. The Court does not decide the unpressed wording objection to that extension or the operation of special statutory audit regimes.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Automobile Club of Michigan v. Commissioner, 353 U.S. 180, 188 (1957), applied by analogy: a filing insufficient to compute the relevant taxpayer’s liability does not trigger that taxpayer’s assessment period; the holding is not a rule that informational completeness alone transforms one person’s return into another’s.
- Germantown Trust Co. v. Commissioner, 309 U.S. 304 (1940), distinguished in scope: a sufficient return for the same taxpayer may trigger limitations despite its form; it supplies no corporate-return substitute for a shareholder’s own return.
- Holywell Corp. v. Smith, simulated February 25, 1992, left undisturbed: distinct fiduciary and trust filing duties do not decide the limitations question here.
No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The contrary corporate-period rule applied in Kelley v. Commissioner, 877 F.2d 756 (9th Cir. 1989), cannot govern this shareholder-assessment question. No broader C corporation rule is decided.
