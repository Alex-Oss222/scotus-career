## Event, chronology and neutral record

October Term 1992. Argued 1993-03-02; event effective 1993-06-14. The official reporter supplies these dates. The supplied merits-calendar premise provides the certiorari review; no new petition poll or grant is invented.

**Court and judgment under review:** Eighth Circuit, 949 F.2d 984: treaty authority sustained on 104,420 former trust acres; separate inquiry on approximately 18,000 formerly non-Indian fee acres remanded.

**Questions and relief:** Whether the acquisition and public-recreation statutes abrogated treaty-derived regulation of non-Indian hunting and fishing, and what residual authority remains. South Dakota seeks reinstatement of the injunction against tribal enforcement. The tribal officials seek affirmance of the former-trust-land holding and retention of the distinct remand concerning former fee land.

**Material facts and record limits:** The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty reserved lands for Sioux use and occupation. The Flood Control Act of 1944 authorized the water project and public recreation. The Cheyenne River Act of 1954 transferred specified tribal interests for the project, provided compensation, and reserved stated access, hunting, and fishing rights for the Tribe or its members. The Government also acquired former non-Indian fee lands. The Tribe enforced hunting and fishing rules on the acquired area; South Dakota challenged its authority over non-Indians. The dispute distinguishes ownership, access, treaty-based regulation, and the different histories of the two land categories.

**Threshold and preservation:** The State’s suit seeks prospective relief against tribal officials. This is not a damages claim against the Tribe or a general determination of reservation boundaries. The two categories of acquired land cannot be treated as if they shared the same treaty and ownership history. The scope of particular regulations that directly conflict with lawful federal project rules is not established merely by the jurisdictional dispute.

#### B. JUDGMENT AND REMEDY

**Stone's Proposed Judgment or Disposition:** AFFIRM the Eighth Circuit’s judgment sustaining treaty-based authority on the former trust lands and preserving the separate remand concerning former non-Indian fee lands.

**Stone's Remedy and Remand Position:** Reject the injunction insofar as it rests on categorical extinguishment of compatible treaty-based hunting and fishing regulation on the 104,420 acres. Retain the fee-land inquiry and permit adjudication of concrete conflicts with superior federal law. No exclusive tribal control of all public access is decreed.

#### C. ISSUE POSITIONS

##### Issue 1: Acquisition does not do every jurisdictional job

**Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone reaches the abrogation question separately for the former trust lands and leaves the fact-dependent former-fee-land inquiry on remand.

**Stone's Legal Position:** Apply Dion and Menominee differently from the State’s proposed reading; distinguish Montana’s land-transfer setting without overruling its general rule or exceptions.

**Stone's Proposed Holding:** Congress’s acquisition of these lands and provision for public recreation displaced exclusive possession to the extent required by the project, but did not clearly extinguish otherwise compatible treaty-based regulation of non-Indian hunting and fishing.

**Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** Stone traces the claimed regulation to the 1868 treaty and retained sovereignty, then reads the 1944 and 1954 enactments for the congressional choice Dion requires. The State's strongest argument is that public acquisition removed the exclusion-based authority from which regulation followed. The statutes do remove exclusive possession incompatible with the project and public recreation, but compatible conservation regulation need not defeat that access. Compensation, reserved personal hunting rights, and Corps regulations do not themselves establish the disputed governmental power or a new delegation. They must be assessed with the treaty and acquisition enactments rather than made substitutes for those sources. This construction preserves only authority not clearly displaced; the distinct former-fee-land history and Montana inquiry remain on remand.

**Boundary or Reserved Question:** Stone does not replace Dion with an absolute express-words requirement. Regulation preventing the access Congress commanded yields to federal law; particular fees or restrictions can be tested on that basis. Montana remains controlling for the distinct former-fee-land question unless a recognized exception is established.

## Controlling holdings

#### Acquisition displaces exclusion-derived regulation

**Holding and operative rule:** The Flood Control Act and Cheyenne River Act displaced the treaty right of exclusive occupation on the taken former trust lands and the non-Indian hunting-and-fishing regulation derived from that exclusion right. Retained tribal access and personal hunting-and-fishing privileges do not themselves preserve authority to license or regulate non-Indian public users.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, Acquisition displaces exclusion-derived regulation portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress’s treatment of these lands supplies the clear indication required by Dion. The Fort Laramie Treaty originally protected exclusive use and occupation, including control of non-Indian access. Congress acquired the project lands, authorized public recreation under federal management, settled the rights taken, and retained shoreline access and hunting and fishing subject to the regulations governing corresponding public uses without retaining exclusion of the public. Montana explains the relationship between exclusive occupation and the regulatory authority claimed from it; Brendale confirms the importance of the land’s legal status. Tribal use rights remain meaningful without carrying power to condition the public access Congress provided. Menominee does not compel the opposite result, because this conclusion rests on the particular acquisition and access enactments together, not an assumption that every federal project extinguishes treaties. The Court requires no particular verbal formula for abrogation. It does not decide reservation boundaries, tribal criminal jurisdiction or every possible regulation on acquired acreage.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dion, 476 U.S.734 — clear congressional choice required, without an invariable express-words requirement.
- Montana, 450 U.S.544 — exclusion-derived authority and effects of alienation applied.
- Brendale, 492 U.S.408 — land-specific setting considered without inventing a universal rule from its fracture.
- Menominee, 391 U.S.404 — protection against casual implied abrogation preserved; particular enactments distinguished.

#### Residual authority remains a distinct inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** Loss of treaty-based exclusion does not decide whether a Montana exception or properly established federal authorization permits particular regulation. Remand preserves the exceptions for consensual commercial dealings, contracts, leases or other arrangements with the Tribe or its members, and for conduct threatening or directly affecting tribal political integrity, economic security, health or welfare, within Montana’s limits. General inherent sovereignty alone does not restore the abrogated regulatory power.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, Residual authority remains a distinct inquiry portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the source rejected today must not be confused with every possible source of tribal civil authority. Montana generally limits regulation of nonmembers on non-Indian land but identifies two exceptions requiring attention to actual relationships and conduct. The court of appeals relied on treaty-based control for the former trust acreage while preserving a separate inquiry for formerly non-Indian fee land. The district court found neither exception satisfied on either land category, but the court of appeals did not review those findings for the former trust lands; that review remains. The Corps-regulation theory was not raised below, was omitted from the Government’s brief and conflicted with record evidence of the Corps’ view. The Court declines deference to that undeveloped litigating position. The State therefore receives no declaration that every non-Indian activity throughout the reservation is exclusively subject to state law. Any claimed authorization and concrete conflict with superior federal rules must be assessed on their own terms. Remand permits that inquiry without reviving the displaced exclusion right or collapsing the two land categories into a single historical ownership premise. No result on those undeveloped questions is directed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Montana, 450 U.S.544 — both consensual-relationship and serious tribal-interest exceptions expressly retained.
- United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S.313 — retained internal tribal powers do not themselves establish nonmember regulation after the abrogation found here.

## Published separate writings and continuity

The compensation settlement does not identify every surrendered governmental power. Superior federal project law remains controlling.
