## Controlling holdings

#### Sentence-four judgment ends the action and confers prevailing status

**Holding and operative rule:** A sentence-four judgment reversing the Secretary’s denial of benefits with a remand ends the §405(g) civil action and makes the successful claimant a prevailing party; the court must enter judgment rather than retain the merits action pending an administrative award. Sentence-six remands instead retain jurisdiction for the statutorily specified proceedings and later return to court.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute supplies distinct remand routes with different procedural consequences. Sentence four authorizes a judgment affirming, modifying or reversing the Secretary’s decision, with or without remand. Finkelstein and Melkonyan give effect to that judgment and distinguish it from sentence six, which allows a pre-answer remand on the Secretary’s good-cause motion or a remand for new material evidence with good cause for its earlier omission. A claimant who obtains reversal through sentence four has secured judicial relief, not merely a favorable statement during ongoing litigation. Garland’s prevailing-party rule, consistent with the entered Farrar eligibility holding, therefore applies without waiting for the agency to award benefits. That status does not establish every EAJA requirement or the reasonable amount. Administrative success cannot replace the court judgment as the statutory filing event. The Court preserves the different sentence-six sequence, including return to court and a final judgment after the further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sullivan v. Finkelstein, 496 U.S.617, and Melkonyan v. Sullivan, 501 U.S.89: the two statutory remand routes applied.
- Texas State Teachers Assn. v. Garland Independent School District, 489 U.S.782, and entered Farrar, December 14, 1992: enforceable judicial relief establishes prevailing status, not automatic fee amount.

#### The missing separate judgment prevents the fee deadline from expiring

**Holding and operative rule:** Under the then-effective rules, EAJA’s thirty-day application period follows a properly entered judgment and expiration of the applicable sixty-day appeal period; the absence of the required Rule 58 separate judgment prevents that deadline from expiring. Schaefer’s application is timely although the sentence-four remand was itself a final merits disposition.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantive finality and the formal entry starting appellate time must not be collapsed. Section 2412(d) measures its thirty days from a judgment that is final and no longer appealable. Rule 4(a) gives sixty days to appeal in this action involving a federal officer and requires entry in compliance with Rule 58. The district court’s April 4, 1989 remand resolved the judicial merits but lacked the separate judgment document. Indrelunas gives that formality effect. Mallis permits an appeal without insisting on the document in appropriate circumstances; it does not deem the unstarted appeal period expired against a party seeking fees. The Secretary therefore cannot establish untimeliness from the remand date or the later administrative decision. Proper entry remains necessary where still absent. This conclusion sustains the award reviewed here without importing a later deemed-entry deadline, enlarging recoverable services or reopening EAJA issues outside the appeal.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Indrelunas, 411 U.S.216: separate-document requirement applied as then in force.
- Bankers Trust Co. v. Mallis, 435 U.S.381: ability to take an appeal without the document distinguished from expiration of appellate time.
- Melkonyan, 501 U.S.89: final court judgment, not agency decision, governs the EAJA clock.

#### Hudson does not preserve administrative-fee coverage after an ordinary terminated sentence-four action

**Holding and operative rule:** Hudson’s integrated administrative-proceeding fee rationale does not apply to a sentence-four remand properly entered without retained jurisdiction, or to retention of jurisdiction that is challenged. It remains applicable to qualifying sentence-six proceedings in which the court retains the action, subject to EAJA’s other requirements.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Hudson must be read with the statutory structure clarified in Finkelstein and Melkonyan. Hudson involved a remand treated, without objection, as part of a continuing judicial action. Its fee rationale depended on that continuing jurisdiction and the administrative work’s relationship to the judicial case. Sentence four, however, requires a terminating judgment; a court cannot preserve the action simply to postpone the fee clock until benefits are awarded. The argument that effective representation on remand favors broader coverage identifies a policy concern but does not supply authority to retain an action the statute ends. The qualifying sentence-six route remains different because the Secretary returns to the court after further proceedings. The holding neither eliminates Hudson in that setting nor awards new administrative fees in this appeal. Prevailing status after a sentence-four reversal and entitlement to compensation for later administrative work remain separate questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sullivan v. Hudson, 490 U.S.877: retained-jurisdiction rationale limited in light of the statutory distinction; sentence-six coverage preserved.
- Finkelstein, 496 U.S.617, and Melkonyan, 501 U.S.89: sentence-four termination controls; statutory retention is reserved for sentence six.

## Continuity and published noncontrolling positions
