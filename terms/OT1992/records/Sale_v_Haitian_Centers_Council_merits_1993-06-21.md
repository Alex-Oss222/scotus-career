##### Issue 1: Statutory protection follows this American return operation

**Threshold and Merits Reach:** Stone accepts review under § 1292(a)(1) of the actual refusal of a preliminary injunction, and the interim-relief controversy against administering officers. He agrees that the earlier litigation does not preclude the distinct new-policy claim before the Court, without reopening the separate screened-out claims already subject to preclusion. **Stone's Legal Position:** Resolve the open geographic statutory question through text, amendment, context, and the coordinated operation before the Court. Rebut the inherited extraterritoriality presumption with a specific statutory indication rather than treating geography or the Attorney General limitation as irrelevant.

**Stone's Proposed Holding:** The amended § 243(h) constrains this coordinated American immigration operation when officers forcibly return an alien intercepted at sea to threatened persecution. The Executive cannot avoid the prohibition simply by arranging that the return occurs before the person reaches the territorial sea.

**Essential Reasoning and Record Application:** The Government's contrary reading is substantial: section 243(h) names the Attorney General within a domestic immigration scheme, and removing within the United States could protect persons in admission proceedings without reaching a Coast Guard operation offshore. Stone finds the better reading in the combination of that deletion, the work done by return in addition to deportation, and the coordinated American immigration return at issue. Those features supply the indication Aramco requires; general humanitarian purpose alone would not. The Attorney General reference locates a statutory immigration responsibility that the Executive cannot defeat by assigning performance of this return to another component. General entry-suspension authority does not erase the more specific prohibition so construed. Stevic and Cardoza-Fonseca distinguish mandatory withholding from asylum; treaty consistency supports the statutory reading without deciding independent treaty self-execution. Youngstown requires compliance with a valid statutory limit but does not itself establish the statute's geographic meaning.

**Boundary or Reserved Question:** The holding concerns persons actually subjected to the specified American return operation. It creates neither a worldwide duty to rescue nor a right to admission, and does not decide independent treaty self-execution, unrelated military custody, or the entitlement of any individual whose protection claim has not been found.

## Controlling holdings

#### Section 243(h) does not govern this offshore return operation

**Holding and operative rule:** Section243(h)’s restriction on deporting or returning an alien to threatened persecution does not govern the challenged interdiction and repatriation beyond the territorial sea. The 1980 removal of the words within the United States does not extend this domestic immigration provision to the coordinated offshore operation.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, Section 243(h) does not govern this offshore return operation portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the provision’s scope must be determined from its statutory setting as well as its protective language. Aramco supplies the presumption against extraterritorial application absent a sufficient indication of congressional intent. Section243(h) addresses the Attorney General’s authority in the domestic immigration system, including exclusion and deportation. Removing the earlier territorial phrase extends protection within that procedural setting without clearly regulating the Coast Guard’s high-seas conduct. The word return has work to do in that setting and does not alone overcome the contextual limitation. Stevic and Cardoza-Fonseca distinguish mandatory withholding from discretionary asylum but do not establish offshore reach. The contrary textual argument is substantial, particularly given the amendment, yet it does not show the required geographic extension. This is a construction of the enacted protection, not an executive emergency exception to an applicable statute. The Court does not determine whether any individual qualifies for refugee protection or whether another source limits a different operation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- EEOC v. Arabian American Oil Co., 499 U.S.244 — geographic presumption applied.
- INS v. Stevic, 467 U.S.407 — withholding standard distinguished from territorial reach.
- INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S.421 — asylum/withholding distinction preserved.
- Youngstown, 343 U.S.579 — no power to disregard an applicable statute inferred; coverage is the issue decided.

#### Article 33 does not independently limit the high-seas operation

**Holding and operative rule:** Article33 of the Refugee Convention, as undertaken through the 1967 Protocol, does not extend its return prohibition to this high-seas repatriation operation. That territorial construction resolves the asserted treaty restraint without deciding whether the Convention otherwise supplies an independently enforceable cause of action. Article33(2) withholds its benefit where reasonable grounds identify a danger to the security of the country of presence, or a refugee convicted by final judgment of a particularly serious crime constitutes a danger to that country’s community.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, Article 33 does not independently limit the high-seas operation portion; White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct proposition-level majority; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the treaty text must be read as a whole in its setting. The prohibition on expelling or returning a refugee to threatened territory is central to Article33, but its language and the context of the territorial refuge system do not establish a rule governing every offshore encounter. The security exception’s reference to the country in which the refugee is present and the negotiating materials support the territorial understanding of the obligation undertaken. The contrary reading gives broad force to return and to the humanitarian object, but purpose alone cannot substitute for the geographic scope of the actual undertaking. The Court does not treat the treaty as meaningless: it preserves its prohibition in the setting it covers while rejecting its application to this operation. Because the asserted substantive restriction does not reach the conduct, the Court need not decide independent self-execution or construct a new remedial action. Nor does this holding authorize return from domestic custody contrary to applicable law.

**Precedent treatment:**

- 1951 Refugee Convention Article33 and 1967 Protocol — construed geographically for this operation, without a self-execution holding.
- Stevic, 467 U.S.407 — treaty/statutory relationship considered without importing a general offshore duty.
