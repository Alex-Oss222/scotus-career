## Event and chronology

Voinovich v. Quilter, No. 91-1618; historical reporter locator 507 U.S. 146; October Term 1992. Argued December 8, 1992; decided March 2, 1993. Direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. 1253 from the three-judge Northern District of Ohio. The lower court invalidated Ohio's legislative apportionment on Section 2, Fifteenth Amendment purpose and Fourteenth Amendment population grounds. Ohio officials seek reversal; challengers seek to retain invalidation and corrective districting. The questions are whether the State must demonstrate remedial necessity before creating majority-minority districts, whether the asserted influence-dilution and purpose claims succeed on this record, and whether departures above ten percent foreclose a county-boundary justification. The 1991 plan was revised in 1992. ## Controlling holdings

#### H1. Section 2 does not make a State prove remedial necessity before choosing majority-minority districts

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2 does not prohibit a State from choosing majority-minority districts merely because no federal violation compels their creation; challengers bear at least the initial burden of showing, under the totality of circumstances, unequal electoral opportunity caused by the challenged arrangement. A federal court may order such districts only when their creation is necessary to remedy an established federal-law violation, but that limit does not become a categorical restriction on state redistricting authority; independently applicable federal constitutional and statutory requirements remain enforceable.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part II.B, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt the complete proposition; it directly controls the rejection of the lower court's statutory prohibition and burden allocation.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 protects equal electoral opportunity rather than a preferred distribution of minority voters among districts. Gingles requires challengers to demonstrate unequal access under the totality of circumstances; it does not declare a particular district form inherently unlawful. Concentrating minority voters can improve their ability to elect preferred candidates, diminish that ability, or leave it unchanged. The consequences require proof. The District Court instead required Ohio to demonstrate a prior statutory violation before creating majority-minority districts. That reverses the statute's initial allocation of the burden and mistakes an absence of federal compulsion for a federal prohibition. Growe recognizes the State's primary responsibility for apportionment and limits federal judicial intervention to an established federal defect. A State derives its districting authority independently of a federal court's remedial power. Its choice remains subject to federal requirements, but Section 2 supplies no categorical ban here. The Court does not decide the distinct constitutional challenge to conscious racial classification, which was neither presented below nor raised in this appeal.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): followed for the challengers' burden and the totality-of-circumstances inquiry; no district form is automatically unlawful under Section 2.
- Growe v. Emison, simulated decision of February 23, 1993: applied to distinguish the State's primary districting authority from federal judicial remedial authority; its actual majority holdings remain controlling.

#### H2. The assumed influence-dilution claim fails for lack of the required majority bloc voting

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming that Section 2 reaches the asserted loss of minority influence districts, the claim fails because challengers did not establish sufficiently cohesive white majority voting usually to defeat the minority's preferred candidates. The Gingles prerequisites apply with attention to the claim's nature: an influence claim would require modification or elimination of the first prerequisite that the minority could constitute a district majority, but the Court need not select that adaptation or decide whether influence claims are cognizable because the independently necessary bloc-voting showing is absent.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Parts II.A and II.C, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt this independently dispositive rejection of the presented Section 2 claim and each stated reservation.

**Controlling explanation:** Packing can impair electoral opportunity, just as dispersing a cohesive minority can. Gingles recognizes both possible mechanisms, and Growe applies its threshold framework to challenges involving single-member districts. Here, however, challengers seek additional districts in which black voters would be an influential minority relying on crossover votes, rather than districts in which they could themselves form a majority. The Court assumes this theory is cognizable and does not mechanically demand satisfaction of a majority-size prerequisite inconsistent with that assumption. Whether that first prerequisite should be modified or eliminated can remain open. The third prerequisite independently defeats the claim: the District Court found an absence of racially polarized voting, and challengers accept that finding. Without sufficiently strong white bloc voting to frustrate the minority's preferred candidates, this record does not establish the unequal opportunity asserted. The lower court cannot replace that missing causal proof with the bare observation that creating majority-minority districts removes black voters from other districts. Neither all influence claims nor all packing claims are rejected.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): applied to the necessity of majority bloc voting and the causal mechanism of dilution; its reservations about influence claims remain unresolved.
- Growe v. Emison, simulated decision of February 23, 1993: followed for application of the threshold framework to single-member district challenges, with its influence-claim reservation preserved rather than silently decided.

#### H3. The particular finding of intentional discrimination is clearly erroneous

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted vote-dilution claim, the finding that Ohio intentionally injured black voters cannot stand on this record: attention to perceived federal-law requirements and possession of an opponent's speculative documents, without evidence of reliance, do not establish the prohibited purpose the District Court inferred. The Court reverses that finding as clearly erroneous without deciding the Amendment's coverage of dilution or its relationship to race-conscious districting generally.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt this record-specific constitutional disposition; none adopts a general immunity for political objectives or racial awareness.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court assumes that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted dilution claim. Beer v. United States leaves that coverage question unresolved, and this case does not require an answer. The asserted violation depends on the intentional injury to black voters that the District Court found. Neither cited piece of evidence supports that inference. Tilling's decision to honor federal requirements when he perceived a conflict with state law demonstrates attention to the Supremacy Clause, not a purpose to abridge minority voting strength. The documents he possessed were an opponent's speculation about possible partisan strategies; they were not his adopted instructions, and no evidence shows that he relied on them. The affirmative record instead shows participation by minority organizations and incorporation of substantial features of the Ohio NAACP's proposal. Possible political objectives do not eliminate the need for evidence connecting those objectives to prohibited racial injury. On this record the finding is clearly erroneous. The Court expresses no broader view on race-conscious districting or the unpresented racial-classification claim.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Beer v. United States, 425 U.S. 130 (1976): followed in leaving unresolved whether the Fifteenth Amendment reaches vote-dilution claims; the assumption made here creates no new coverage holding.
- Mobile v. Bolden, 446 U.S. 55 (1980): its plurality and separate writings are background to the alleged intentional-discrimination claim, not treated as a single majority rationale or a new categorical rule governing every race-conscious map.

#### H4. Population deviations above ten percent require a genuine justification inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** A state legislative plan whose maximum total population deviation exceeds ten percent establishes a prima facie case requiring the State to justify the disparity; crossing that threshold does not make preservation of county boundaries legally unavailable as a justification. The District Court must determine whether the actual plan reasonably advances that rational state policy, including its substantiated connection to the deviations and consistency of implementation, and whether the resulting disparities nevertheless exceed constitutional limits; the Court does not decide that Ohio has met its burden.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt this standard and the population-only remand; neither the State's success nor a particular remedial map receives a controlling vote.

**Controlling explanation:** Equal protection requires substantially equal population in state legislative districts, but it does not demand mathematical identity in every district. Brown v. Thomson explains that deviations below ten percent are generally minor, while larger disparities establish a prima facie case requiring state justification. Ohio's plan crosses that threshold, so its officials must substantiate their explanation. The District Court nevertheless erred by treating county preservation as legally incapable of justifying deviations above ten percent. Mahan v. Howell permits a rational policy preserving political subdivisions to justify some disparities; Brown preserves both the need for a reasonable connection between plan and policy and an independent constitutional limit on the resulting inequality. Invoking county lines by name does not establish that connection. The inquiry concerns the actual plan, the consistency of the policy's application, and the magnitude of the departures it produces. The record has not received that analysis under the proper standard. The Court therefore remands this issue alone, without approving the deviations or ordering any particular racial arrangement.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Brown v. Thomson, 462 U.S. 835 (1983): followed for the prima facie threshold, the required connection to a rational state policy, and the separate constitutional limit; departures below ten percent remain generally minor, not categorically immune.
- Mahan v. Howell, 410 U.S. 315 (1973): followed in allowing a substantiated political-subdivision policy to justify departures above ten percent; it does not automatically validate Ohio's particular departures.
