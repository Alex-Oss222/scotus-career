## Controlling holdings

### Trial cannot begin under the voluntary-absence exception without initial presence

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule43 does not permit a criminal trial to begin in a defendant’s absence merely because that absence is voluntary; its voluntary-absence continuation exception requires that the defendant was initially present when trial began. Crosby’s convictions from such a trial must be set aside and the case remanded for proceedings complying with the rule; no constitutional minimum or change to another enumerated exception is decided.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine participating Justices adopt the rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the rule’s express boundary governs even when a defendant deliberately flees. Rule43 begins with a requirement of presence and limits exceptions to those the rule itself supplies. The continuation provision applies to a defendant initially present who voluntarily absents himself after trial begins. Diaz permits completion of an ongoing trial following such a departure; Taylor applies that same rule without requiring an additional warning that the trial will continue. Neither decision authorizes beginning the trial of an absent defendant. The Government’s burden of rescheduling witnesses and proceedings does not create an additional exception. Initial presence also gives a workable assurance that a later departure abandons a proceeding the defendant knows is under way. The district court’s finding of knowing flight therefore does not save this trial. Reversal requires a lawful proceeding; it does not excuse flight or decide what separate sanctions may be available. Because the rule resolves the case, the Court leaves the constitutional question open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Diaz v. United States, 223 U.S.442 — applied at its actual scope: voluntary departure permits continuation after initial presence, not commencement in absence.
- Taylor v. United States, 414 U.S.17 — applied: no special warning is required for voluntary midtrial absence; its initial-presence premise remains essential.
- United States v. Crosby, 917 F.2d362 — rejected on Rule43: practical burdens and knowing pretrial flight cannot replace the rule’s express commencement condition.
