## Event and chronology

On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 939 F.2d 854. Argued October 6, 1992; decided January 12, 1993. The question is whether an association is categorically excluded from the benefits of then-effective 28 U.S.C. §1915 despite the Dictionary Act’s inclusion of associations within person. The Council, elected by inmates to communicate with prison administration, sued correctional officers over discontinuation of free tobacco and sought permission to proceed without prepaying fees. The district court found the indigence showing inadequate and offered to consider a supplemented application; it did not expressly rule that associations were categorically ineligible. The Ninth Circuit recognized associations’ eligibility and ordered IFP status granted because the Council could not hold assets or a bank account. The officers seek a natural-person-only construction; the Council seeks statutory fee relief. The merits of its prison-policy claims are outside this decision. This is an uncoordinated January 12 decision.

## Law of the decision

**Holding 1 — associations may apply.**

**Holding and operative rule.** An association is not categorically excluded from then-effective §1915: the Dictionary Act’s inclusion of associations within person applies because §1915’s affidavit, financial-inability and counsel provisions can operate through ordinary agency and representation rules. The context exception remains operative, but these features do not establish an incompatible statutory setting or authorize a natural-person-only restriction.

**Authority.** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five votes for this construction and its operative limits. Kennedy’s separate account of permissible workability analysis adds no condition to the Court’s holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Congress’s express definition includes the Council within the class allowed to seek fee relief. United States v. A & P Trucking Co. recognizes that an artificial entity can act through people; Wilson v. Omaha Indian Tribe gives effect to the Dictionary Act while testing the actual statutory context. Here an association can litigate through lawful counsel, establish facts through an authorized agent and lack resources to pay costs. The personal language of an affidavit and poverty does not make those ordinary legal acts impossible. Nor does the permission to request counsel imply that every eligible applicant must be able to appear without counsel. Mallard preserves that permission as discretionary, while Osborn preserves the entity’s representation requirement. The officers’ administrative objections deserve consideration when the court examines a particular application; they do not establish categorical exclusion. Neitzke cautions that efficient docket management cannot substitute for the enacted screening standard. The Court preserves the contextual exception and decides association eligibility, not an entitlement to fee relief or a comprehensive insolvency test for every entity.

**Precedent treatment.**
- United States v. A & P Trucking Co., 358 U.S. 121 (1958): applied for giving statutory entity language effective operation through ordinary legal mechanisms; criminal-liability rules are not imported.
- Wilson v. Omaha Indian Tribe, 442 U.S. 653 (1979): applied for the Dictionary Act’s inclusive default subject to actual context; no requirement of an express contrary definition is created.
- Neitzke v. Williams, 490 U.S. 319 (1989): applied to preserve statutory construction against a policy substitution based on administrative convenience.
- Mallard v. United States District Court, 490 U.S. 296 (1989): preserved; §1915(d) permits a request for counsel and does not compel service.
- Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738 (1824): preserved; an entity’s lawful counsel requirement is separate from financial eligibility.

**Holding 2 — eligibility does not establish the showing.**

**Holding and operative rule.** An eligible association must submit a properly authorized affidavit establishing its actual inability to pay costs or give security and its asserted entitlement to redress; the court may examine legally available resources and sham concealment without automatically attributing every member’s assets to it. Inability to hold an entity bank account or a balance-sheet deficit alone does not compel fee relief, so the Council’s application returns for the actual financial and representation determinations; §1915 neither licenses lay entity advocacy nor guarantees appointed counsel.

**Authority.** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies this remand rule and limitation. The dissenters would require denial because of entity status and do not join the Court’s application process.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Ninth Circuit moved too quickly from eligibility to an unconditional grant. Section 1915(a) makes the affidavit and inability-to-pay showing necessary and gives the court permission to grant relief once the statutory requirements are satisfied. Adkins explains the need to examine financial ability in the applicant’s actual circumstances; its personal necessities-of-life language does not itself define an association’s resources. The Council’s prohibition on maintaining funds may be relevant, but it does not alone resolve whether resources can lawfully be made available for the litigation, including through direct payment. The court must verify the affiant’s authority and assess the showing without simply aggregating every member’s property. Osborn keeps entity representation separate, and Mallard prevents converting a discretionary counsel request into compulsory appointment. Neitzke and Denton preserve lawful screening for frivolous or malicious proceedings and clearly baseless facts. The Court finds neither the Council indigent nor its underlying claim meritorious. It remands those application questions without announcing a universal organizational insolvency formula.

**Precedent treatment.**
- Adkins v. E. I. DuPont de Nemours & Co., 335 U.S. 331 (1948): applied to actual financial inability; its individual-applicant facts do not categorically exclude associations or prescribe a corporate insolvency test.
- Osborn and Mallard: preserved as distinct counsel and representation limits rather than grounds for automatic financial disqualification.
- Neitzke and Denton v. Hernandez (May 4, 1992): preserved for their respective legal and factual screening rules, with Denton’s abuse-of-discretion review and independent correction of legal error unchanged.

## Precedent treatment and law after decision

No earlier Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The Dictionary Act and §1915 are construed together. The Court rejects categorical association ineligibility, but does not adopt the Ninth Circuit’s conclusive bank-account inference. The screening, counsel and representation precedents retain the distinct operation stated in Holdings. The conflicting Fifth Circuit FDM Manufacturing exclusion cannot govern associations under this construction.

Effective January 12, 1993, an association may seek §1915 relief through an authorized affidavit and lawful representation. Courts must decide actual financial inability and the remaining statutory conditions; eligibility does not compel a grant, aggregate every member’s assets, excuse concealment, authorize lay advocacy or compel counsel. The Court adds this eligibility/remand rule to the opening law without changing Neitzke/Denton screening. It does not establish that every insolvent corporation qualifies, adopt a universal organizational means test, resolve the Council’s tobacco-policy claims, or decide a constitutional right to subsidized organizational litigation. The statutory resolution makes the asserted First Amendment avoidance theory unnecessary to decide.

### Published separate writings

Kennedy joins the Court. He considers it permissible to ask whether the Dictionary Act’s broad meaning can work within the particular statute; that contextual inquiry need not be illegitimate policymaking. But authorized affidavits, an actual resources inquiry and separate counsel rules supply a workable application here. He therefore agrees that practical difficulties do not overcome the inclusive definition or justify categorical exclusion. His concurrence does not guarantee the Council relief on remand.

Souter, joined by White, O’Connor and Scalia, dissents. He regards four features together as sufficient contextual indication that §1915 addresses natural persons: the possibility of proceeding without requested counsel, language of personal poverty, the applicant’s own sworn assertion and its perjury deterrent, and the absence of an organizational means standard. A permissive counsel provision fits individuals able to appear on their own, while entities require licensed counsel. Ordinary personal-indigency measures do not resolve which corporate or member resources count, and agency affidavits do not remove all authorization and deterrence concerns. He would reverse the Ninth Circuit and require denial of this entity’s motion. His natural-person restriction does not control.
