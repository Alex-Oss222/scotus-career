The Hills computed percentage depletion for their oil and gas interests and added unrecovered costs of machinery, tools, pipes and other separately depreciable tangible improvements to the mineral interests’ adjusted bases. The Commissioner excluded those costs, assessed additional minimum tax, and denied refunds after payment. The Claims Court granted the Hills summary judgment on undisputed facts, and the Federal Circuit affirmed over Chief Judge Nies’s definition-based dissent. The underlying statutory calculation subtracts adjusted basis at year end, determined without the current year’s depletion deduction, from the depletion deduction for each § 614 property. The question concerns basis classification, not whether the business acquired useful improvements or may recover their cost through depreciation.

The issue-specific snapshot is former §§ 56(a), 57(a)(8), 614(a), 1016 and the coordinated depreciation/depletion provisions applicable to 1981–1982; Treasury Regulations §§ 1.57-1(h)(3), 1.611-1(d)(3)–(4), 1.611-2(b), 1.612-1 and 1.612-4. ## Controlling holdings

**Holding and operative rule.** For tax years governed by former § 57(a)(8), the adjusted basis offset against percentage depletion is the adjusted basis of the taxpayer’s § 614 interest in the mineral deposit, determined without that year’s depletion deduction; it excludes separately depreciable tangible drilling and development improvements identified in Treasury Regulation § 1.612-4(c)(1). Lawfully capitalized intangible costs recoverable through depletion remain in depletable basis; this classification does not erase distinct statutory provisions governing other costs or later taxable years.

**Authority.** Souter’s opinion for the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas. All nine Justices adopt this rule and its explanation; it controls by a direct majority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the basis of a mineral deposit does not include the basis of its separately depreciable equipment. Section 57(a)(8) expressly incorporates § 614’s definition of property as an interest in a mineral deposit. The established regulations distinguish minerals in place from the larger enterprise that also includes improvements. Section 1016 adjusts the basis of the property identified; it does not combine separate depreciation and depletion accounts, even when the assets are sold together. The taxpayers’ commercial-integration argument therefore addresses a different unit from the one Congress selected. Nor does the heading of the cost-depletion regulation change that statutory definition. Permitting the same equipment balance repeatedly to shelter percentage depletion would further disconnect the preference from the mineral investment it measures. The regulations’ treatment of capitalized intangible costs supplies no contrary rule: costs represented by physical improvements are recovered through depreciation, while qualifying other capital costs enter depletion. This decision preserves those distinctions and does not apply the later prospective depletion amendment to the years before the Court.

The controlling classification follows the coordinated statutory and regulatory text. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled or made into a general rule against multiple tax benefits. ## Law after decision

Effective January 25, 1993, the § 614 definition governs former § 57(a)(8)’s adjusted-basis offset, with tangible depreciable improvements excluded and properly depletable capital costs preserved. Before this decision the separate statutory and regulatory categories existed; this decision resolves their use in the minimum-tax calculation for the years presented. It does not convert commercial integration into a common basis account or forbid a tax benefit Congress actually authorized. Under the separately admitted Energy Policy Act source, amended § 57(a)(1) does not apply to § 613A(c) depletion deductions for taxable years beginning after December 31, 1992; § 56(g)(4)(F) carries its own coordinated exception. That provision states, in clause (i), that the allowance for depletion for property placed in service in a taxable year beginning after December 31, 1989, is cost depletion under § 611, and then, in clause (ii) ("Exception for independent oil and gas producers and royalty owners"), that "[i]n the case of any taxable year beginning after December 31, 1992, clause (i) (and subparagraph (C)(i)) shall not apply to any deduction for depletion computed in accordance with section 613A(c)." The 1981–1982 holding remains operative for liabilities governed by the former provision. No general question about every mineral-development expense is decided. A direct nine-Justice holding leaves no Marks question.
