## Event and chronology

On writ of certiorari to the D.C. Circuit, 948 F.2d 1317 (1991), which reversed dismissal of the employer’s preemption suit. The question is whether a workers’ compensation continuation obligation measured by an existing ERISA-covered health plan is preempted even if separately administered through an exempt arrangement. The District seeks dismissal; the Board seeks protection against enforcement.

The challenged D.C. Code §36-307(a-1) applies when an employer already provides health insurance and the employee receives or is eligible to receive workers’ compensation. Eligibility means either absence from work because of a job-related injury for which the employee has filed a compensation claim, or the employer’s knowledge of a job-related injury causing the employee’s absence and pursuant to which compensation may become due under §36-315. Coverage must equal the existing insurance, at the benefit level when receipt or eligibility began, for no more than 52 weeks; the employer must pay the entire cost, including the contribution the employee otherwise would have made. The duty lasts only while receipt or eligibility continues, within that cap. Paragraph (5) directs reimbursement to the employer from the special fund established by Act §41 (D.C. Code §36-340); when the employer fails to provide coverage and the employee procures it and is reimbursed by the employer under subsection (d), fund reimbursement is limited to what the employer would have paid had it supplied coverage. Under subsection (d), the employee’s insurance-reimbursement remedy requires a request and employer refusal or neglect. Its distinct alternative concerning urgently needed treatment or service does not dispense with that insurance request. The Mayor must order suspension of compensation, medical payments and health coverage during an employee’s unreasonable refusal of medical or surgical treatment, an examination by the employer’s chosen physician, or vocational rehabilitation, unless circumstances justify the refusal. Neither the fund mechanism nor these duration and enforcement qualifications removes the binding use of the covered plan’s benefit content.

### Airline rates, routes and services under the express preemption clause

**Governing question:** State enforcement under 49 U.S.C. App. §1305(a)(1), including generally applicable laws and the general aviation remedies savings clause.

**Current rule:** Section 1305(a)(1) preempts state enforcement having a connection with or reference to covered air-carrier rates, routes or services. General applicability does not avoid that express relation, and §1506's general remedies clause preserves remedies outside the specific prohibition without undoing it. Fare-marketing requirements that directly regulate fare descriptions, restrictions, availability, surcharges and round-trip pricing fall within that construction. Independently, the identified package of compelled disclosures and advertising restrictions is preempted because it substantially constrains the marketing of restricted fares and significantly affects fares themselves. Directly covered requirements need not conflict with an existing federal rule; consistency alone does not defeat the express prohibition.

## Law of the decision

#### Covered-plan content as a mandatory legal measure

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §514(a) preempts this continuation obligation insofar as it incorporates an ERISA-covered health plan’s benefit content as the legal measure of mandatory replacement coverage, even when an employer may administer the replacement benefits separately through an exempt workers’ compensation arrangement. No §514(b) savings claim is decided, and this ruling does not preempt requirements measured solely by an ERISA-exempt governmental or church plan.

**Authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; eight Justices adopt the same operative rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that separate administration does not remove the obligation’s legal dependence on a covered plan. Shaw supplies the connection-or-reference construction of §514(a), while preserving separately administered plans maintained solely to meet workers’ compensation, unemployment-compensation or disability-insurance requirements. Here the District’s command fixes replacement coverage by the employee’s existing covered benefits, for the statutory continuation period. Mackey and Ingersoll-Rand establish that such operative reference can require preemption even when a law addresses another subject. Alessi likewise prevents a workers’ compensation label from insulating interference with a covered plan. Section 4(b)(3) excludes the separately maintained arrangement from ERISA coverage; it does not transform the referenced covered plan into an exempt one. Fort Halifax’s one-time-payment rule does not apply to this conceded health plan. The Court leaves unpresented §514(b) savings questions and applications measured solely by exempt plans outside this holding. It decides no general rule that incidental economic influence or every mention of benefits suffices.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85 — applied; its separate-unit permission concerns independently fixed exempt benefits and does not eliminate operative reference to a different covered plan.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825 — applied; specific legal reference is distinguished from ordinary general garnishment.
- Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133 — applied; dependence on a covered plan can trigger preemption despite the nominal subject of the state rule.
- Alessi v. Raybestos-Manhattan, Inc., 451 U.S. 504 — applied; regulation through workers’ compensation does not avoid ERISA’s covered-plan restriction.
- Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 — distinguished; a one-time payment without ongoing administration differs from this health plan.
- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724 — preserved; its insurance-saving holding supplies no additional §4(b) escape from a covered-plan reference.
- Morales v. Trans World Airlines, June 1, 1992 — consistent similar-language analogy only; its aviation holding does not replace ERISA’s own coverage and saving provisions.

Neither requires the same administrative plan. District conceded relation to existing covered plan; applicability solely by reference to exempt governmental/church plans is distinct. Amended law effective March 6, 1991.

Statutory components: ERISA §514(a) preempts state laws insofar as they relate to an employee plan described in §4(a) and not exempt under §4(b), subject to §514(b)'s independent exceptions. Section 4(b)(3) excludes plans maintained solely to comply with workers' compensation/unemployment/disability laws; it does not rewrite §514(a)'s treatment of a separate covered plan. No insurance/banking/securities or criminal-law saving is asserted.
