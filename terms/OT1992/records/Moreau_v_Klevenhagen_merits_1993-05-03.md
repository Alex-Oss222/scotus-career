## Participation and entering law

All nine Justices participate: Chief Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. No case-specific nonparticipation is established. Nine qualify for quorum; five of nine control the judgment.

29 U.S.C. § 207(o)(2)(A) permits public-employer compensatory time either (i) under an agreement with a representative authorized to bargain, or (ii), for employees not covered by that route, under an agreement or understanding reached between the employer and the individual employee before performance of the work. Section 207(o)(2)'s transition provision, for employees described in subclause (A)(ii) hired before April 15, 1986, treats the regular compensatory-time practice in effect on that date as the required agreement or understanding for that cohort. Neither route creates a general federal collective-bargaining entitlement; state law governs whether a selected organization may make the binding statutory agreement.

## Law of the decision

### The earlier-hire component: the transition rule supplies the agreement

**Holding and operative rule:** For employees described in § 207(o)(2)(A)(ii) who were hired before April 15, 1986, the regular compensatory-time practice in effect on that date constitutes the agreement or understanding the statute otherwise requires before the work is performed. This transition rule operates independently of, and does not require, a further individual or collective agreement for that cohort.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 207(o)(2) does not leave employees hired before the FLSA's public-sector amendments without a route to compensatory time merely because no contemporaneous individual agreement was separately documented at a time when the statute did not yet require one of them. Congress's own transition text supplies the missing agreement for that already-employed group by reference to the practice actually in effect when the amendments took hold, recognizing that employees hired under the pre-existing practice had no occasion to execute the individual agreement the amendments would later require of new hires. This reading respects both the statute's general insistence on an actual agreement and its express accommodation for employees whose employment predated the amendment, avoiding the anomalous result of stripping compensatory time from an entire existing workforce for want of paperwork the law did not yet demand when they were hired. It does not excuse an employer from the ordinary individual-agreement route for anyone hired afterward, and it does not convert a unilateral employer practice into an agreement outside the transition cohort the statute defines; the practice supplies the agreement only for the discrete group Congress identified.

**Precedent treatment:** No prior Supreme Court holding addressed this transition provision; the Court applies its text without displacing any earlier authority.

### The later-hire component: individual agreements where no representative could bind

**Holding and operative rule:** Where employees have selected a representative organization that state law does not authorize to enter a binding § 207(o)(2)(A)(i) agreement, the employer may rely on individual § 207(o)(2)(A)(ii) agreements or understandings with the affected employees hired on or after April 15, 1986, rather than lose the compensatory-time option entirely for want of a legally capable collective representative.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by all nine participating Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The statutory reference to an agreement with "a representative" does not compel recognition of an entity Texas law does not permit to make the relevant binding commitment; reading the statute otherwise would force an employer to treat a legally incapable organization as if it held bargaining authority state law withholds. Congress created a limited compensatory-time option grounded in actual consent, not a general public-sector collective-bargaining entitlement; state law determines whether a selected organization can make the collective agreement, while federal law determines the resulting overtime consequences that follow from whichever route is actually available. Where the collective route is unavailable for that reason, falling back to individual agreements or understandings preserves the compensatory-time option Congress intended rather than eliminating it for an entire workforce based on a state-law limitation the employer did not create. Individual consent remains necessary for this cohort, and this holding does not presume it from selection of a representative alone; the employer must still obtain the affected employee's own agreement or understanding before compensatory time may be substituted for cash overtime.

**Precedent treatment:** No Supreme Court precedent is overruled, limited, or extended beyond its own terms; the Court decides only the two routes actually presented on this record.

### Limits and questions not reached

The Court decides no general federal right to public-sector collective bargaining, no exemption from ordinary overtime law, and no presumption of individual consent outside the two components actually presented. It does not address a compensatory-time practice adopted after April 15, 1986, or any employee for whom a legally authorized representative could have made the § 207(o)(2)(A)(i) agreement.
