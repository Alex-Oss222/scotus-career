## Controlling holdings

#### Appropriate equitable relief in ERISA’s trust setting is identified by trust-law remedies

**Holding and operative rule:** Section502(a)(3)’s appropriate equitable relief means remedies historically recognized in equity. Because ERISA establishes a statutory trust regime, those remedies are identified by reference to the law of trusts as it fits the statute’s text and structure. A remedy is not legal damages merely because it requires the payment of money. The provision does not reach punitive awards, general tort compensation, remedies that became available only after the merger of law and equity, or every remedy a court of equity could award incidentally to its jurisdiction.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute’s reference to equitable relief must be read in the setting Congress chose. ERISA codifies fiduciary duties drawn from the law of trusts, and Firestone directs courts to that body of law where it fits the enacted text. Breaches of trust lay within equity’s jurisdiction, and Clews confirms that courts of equity remedied them by ordering money restored. Curtis and Terry distinguish legal compensation from equitable categories in deciding jury-trial rights; Terry also recognizes that an action against a trustee for breach of fiduciary duty was equitable. Neither decision holds that a remedy equity gave for a breach of trust becomes legal because it is measured in money. The enacted word still does work: it excludes punitive awards, general tort compensation, remedies that arose only after law and equity merged and remedies an equity court granted merely as an incident of its jurisdiction. Russell construed §409 and expressly left this provision open. Reading §502(a)(3) through trust law preserves Congress’s distinction between legal and equitable relief while placing trust restoration on the equitable side.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S.101: trust-law guidance applied to identify the equitable remedies within the enacted text.
- Clews v. Jamieson, 182 U.S.461: equity’s power to restore trust losses by a monetary decree applied.
- Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S.189: its distinction of legal compensation preserved for the claims it addressed; it does not reclassify equitable trust remedies.
- Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers v. Terry, 494 U.S.558: its recognition that actions against trustees for breach of fiduciary duty were equitable applied; its jury-trial classification of a different remedy is not extended to this provision.
- Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S.134: its §409 enforcement-structure holding preserved; its reserved §502(a)(3) question is answered only as stated in these holdings.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February26,1992: appropriate-remedy presumption distinguished because Congress expressly limited this provision to equitable relief.
- United States v. Burke, May26,1992: its fractured judgment supplies no controlling general remedial-range test.

#### Trust-law monetary restoration, including surcharge against fiduciaries, is appropriate equitable relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Section502(a)(3) permits appropriate monetary restoration traditionally available in trust litigation, including surcharge against fiduciaries where its requirements are satisfied: actual loss caused by the breach, or the elimination of unjust enrichment the breach produced. The provision does not authorize punitive damages or general tort compensation. For a third party the inquiry is more demanding, and a defendant’s status as actuary, lawyer or other service provider does not make it a fiduciary. Where an otherwise actionable claim establishes a nonfiduciary’s knowing participation in an actionable breach of trust, monetary restoration matching the loss attributable to that participation is not categorically excluded merely because the defendant did not receive the lost assets. Hewitt’s nonfiduciary status is fixed, and the Ninth Circuit’s categorical rejection of the participants’ monetary claim cannot stand.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the participants’ claim could not be dismissed solely because it seeks money. Courts of equity charged a trustee who committed a breach of trust, by surcharge, with the resulting loss to the trust estate or the profit the breach produced; that monetary decree was the ordinary equitable remedy for a fiduciary’s breach, and it is available against ERISA fiduciaries where its requirements are met. Equity extended restoration to a third person who knowingly participated in the trustee’s breach, as Clews illustrates and the law of trusts records. That liability rested on the participant’s share in the wrong rather than on its possession of trust property, so it was equitable even when the participant received nothing. The Ninth Circuit rejected the claim without asking whether the restoration sought had that traditional basis. The remedy is bounded: it restores the loss the breach or participation caused, not every consequence of a defendant’s conduct, and supplies no punitive award or general compensation for professional injury. Professional status does not make a defendant a fiduciary, and the inquiry for a third party is more demanding than for a trustee.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Clews v. Jamieson, 182 U.S.461: equitable monetary restoration for breach of trust, including surcharge and restoration against a knowing participant, applied.
- Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S.101: trust law applied to identify surcharge against fiduciaries and the remedy against a knowing participant.
- Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S.134: its rejection of extracontractual and punitive recovery under §409 preserved; no punitive or general compensatory remedy is recognized here.

#### Restoration requires an actionable claim and its equitable predicates; the antecedent action question is reserved

**Holding and operative rule:** Trust-law monetary restoration under §502(a)(3) requires an authorized statutory action and an actionable violation; a historically recognized equitable basis for relief against the particular defendant; the knowledge or fiduciary responsibility that basis requires; actual loss or another recognized equitable measure; and causation and the traditional equitable limits. Negligent professional advice is not knowing participation. Section502(a)(3) does not create a cause of action for every trust wrong. Because Hewitt expressly disclaimed the argument, the Court reserves whether §502(a)(3) supplies an action against a nonfiduciary who knowingly participates in a fiduciary’s breach; the reservation respects the parties’ presentation and is not a jurisdictional ruling.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that recognizing the remedy’s equitable character neither turns allegations into liability nor makes ERISA a general malpractice statute. Equity charged a third person with a trust loss only when that person knew of the breach and took part in it; mistaken or careless advice, however costly, did not suffice, and a professional’s role in plan administration does not itself establish the required knowledge. The remedy must answer an actual violation of ERISA or the plan, must be measured by a loss the breach or participation caused or another measure equity recognized, and remains subject to the defenses and limits equity applied. The participants’ allegations, accepted on review of dismissal, are not findings of breach, knowledge, participation, causation or loss. Hewitt disclaimed the separate contention that §502(a)(3) supplies no action against a nonfiduciary, and the Court decides the remedial question presented without answering that antecedent question either way. On remand the preserved claim proceeds under these conditions and ordinary proof rules.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S.134: respect for ERISA’s enforcement structure applied; no cause of action is inferred for every trust wrong.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February26,1992: the distinction between an existing action’s available relief and the creation of an action preserved.

#### The knowing-participation penalty provision confirms the remedy without creating a private action

**Holding and operative rule:** Section502(l), which measures the Secretary of Labor’s civil penalty by amounts recovered from a fiduciary for a breach or from any other person for knowing participation in it, confirms that monetary recoveries against knowing participants and equitable relief coexist in ERISA’s scheme. It does not create a private cause of action, define the elements of liability or enlarge the relief §502(a)(3) authorizes. Congress’s distinction between legal and equitable relief is preserved; trust-specific monetary restoration falls on the equitable side of that distinction.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support for this exact proposition; no Marks synthesis.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §502(l) is consistent evidence, not a separate source of the claim. Enacted in 1989, it measures the Secretary’s penalty by amounts recovered, by settlement with the Secretary or under a court order in proceedings the Secretary brings under §502(a)(2) or §502(a)(5), from a fiduciary for a breach or from another person for knowing participation in it. Section502(a)(5) authorizes the Secretary to obtain appropriate equitable relief in the same terms §502(a)(3) gives participants. Congress thus assumed that a knowing participant could be ordered to pay money in an equitable proceeding, which confirms that a monetary decree and equitable relief are not mutually exclusive under ERISA. The provision addresses public penalties, however; it does not authorize private suits, state the elements of a participant’s liability or expand the remedies §502(a)(3) itself confers. The Court therefore relies on it only to confirm the trust-law reading and does not treat it as resolving whether a private action lies against a nonfiduciary participant. Congress’s choice elsewhere in ERISA to separate legal from equitable relief remains controlling for remedies equity did not traditionally provide.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S.134: ERISA’s differentiated enforcement provisions are read together; §502(l) adds confirmation, not a new private remedy.

## Continuity and published noncontrolling positions

**Concurrence — Souter; no other joins:** Souter joins the Court’s opinion and writes to stress the line it draws between trust-specific equitable restoration and tort damages. The remedies the Court recognizes restore to the trust the loss a fiduciary’s breach caused, through surcharge where its requirements are satisfied, and the loss attributable to a knowing participant’s share in that breach. That restoration is measured by the trust estate, not by the full range of personal, consequential or emotional harm a tort plaintiff might recover, and it does not turn professional negligence into a federal claim. His Burke concurrence rejected a mandatory broad-remedies test for tort-type recovery; the Court’s holding is consistent with that view, because it recognizes only the restoration equity gave in trust litigation against fiduciaries and knowing participants, and it keeps general compensatory damages outside §502(a)(3). The equitable limitation remains a real boundary, and the separate state malpractice claim remains the vehicle for ordinary professional injury. This position does not control.

**Dissent — Scalia; joined by Blackmun, Kennedy, Thomas:** Scalia, joined by Blackmun, Kennedy and Thomas, would hold that §502(a)(3)’s appropriate equitable relief encompasses traditionally equitable categories of relief, not compensatory damages merely because a court of equity could award them in trust litigation. Curtis and Terry distinguish compensation for loss, ordinarily legal relief, from those categories. In the dissent’s view, reading equitable relief to include what an equity court could award in a trust controversy deprives the enacted limitation of its work and disregards ERISA’s use of different remedial language elsewhere. Firestone directs consultation of trust law where it fits the statute; it does not, the dissent argues, authorize replacing the express limit with the remedial jurisdiction of historical equity, and Clews shows only that trust courts could award money. Section502(l) in its view assumes recoveries without defining what §502(a)(3) confers. Because the participants seek compensation for plan losses rather than a preserved restitution remedy, the dissent would affirm. It would leave the disclaimed antecedent action question, unreviewed remedies and the separate malpractice disposition untouched, and it does not bar every monetary equitable remedy. It does not control.
