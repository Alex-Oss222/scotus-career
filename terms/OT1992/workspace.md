#### Martin v. District of Columbia Court of Appeals and Martin v. McDermott, 1992-11-02

Source: [Decision Record](records/Martin_v_District_of_Columbia_Court_of_Appeals_fee_motions_and_petition_1992-11-02.md).

#### I. No. 92-5584: an arguable current petition is not defeated by filing history

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule 39.8 permits denial of leave to file without prepayment only upon a finding that the particular petition is frivolous or malicious; earlier unsuccessful filings cannot substitute for that finding. No. 92-5584's distinct general-rule and prospective statutory arguments clear the nonfrivolousness threshold on this record, so the Court grants leave without deciding that the arguments warrant certiorari or would succeed on the merits.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine participating Justices adopt this filing-specific application and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Martin's history does not dispose of the paper now before it. Zatko permits discretionary fee denial for identified frivolous submissions in an extreme pattern of abuse; it does not make previous denials conclusive. Feldman distinguishes a challenge to a general bar rule from review of an individual admission judgment. Martin invokes that distinction and asserts prospective statutory grounds. His personal grievance, extensive collateral litigation and uncertain statutory theories present substantial obstacles, but those obstacles do not establish that every distinct question is frivolous. Denton preserves the difference between an arguable claim and a clearly baseless allegation; its statutory screening rule does not mechanically govern every feature of this Court's filing practice. The Court neither credits the disputed medical allegations nor finds that the asserted general classification actually exists. It decides no question of statutory coverage, retroactivity, preservation, immunity or ultimate jurisdiction. The motion is granted; the petition remains subject to ordinary discretionary review.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Zatko v. California, 502 U.S. 16 (November 4, 1991): applied as the existing discretionary, particular-submission rule; its extreme-abuse finding does not establish the frivolousness of a different current petition.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 460 U.S. 462 (1983): its general-rule/individual-judgment distinction supplies an arguable legal question here; the Court does not decide which side of that distinction the complete complaint occupies.
- Denton v. Hernandez, 504 U.S. 25 (May 4, 1992): its distinction between arguability and clearly baseless factual allegations informs this limited assessment; its statutory review and remedial rules remain unchanged and are not imported wholesale into Rule 39.

#### II. No. 92-5618: particular frivolousness and the available paid route

**Holding and operative rule:** Where the current recusal petition identifies no concrete constitutionally disqualifying interest and develops no federal challenge to the independent state jurisdictional ground that defeats the action, those defects together establish frivolousness for this Rule 39.8 motion. In light of Martin's established extreme repetitive filing abuse, the Court exercises its discretion to deny this motion only, allowing payment of the Rule 38 fee and submission in Rule 33 form through November 23, 1992; the petition itself is not denied by this order.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six of nine control the motion, its stated ground and the compliance opportunity. Stone-Zsela, Blackmun and Stevens dissent from this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this motion must be judged from the McDermott petition itself. Zatko permits a targeted fee denial when a present frivolous submission accompanies established extreme abuse. The Delaware judgment rests on the state rule that an appeal from the Justice of the Peace court retain the same action and parties. Martin's petition develops no federal objection to that jurisdictional ground. It instead attributes unconstitutional bias to professional ties, appearances and related allegations without identifying a concrete disqualifying interest comparable to the personal financial stake in Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Lavoie. A genuine constitutional defect in the decisionmaker cannot be dismissed merely by calling another ground jurisdictional. Here, however, the undeveloped constitutional premise and the unaddressed jurisdictional obstacle operate together. Neither filing volume, the state court's characterization, nor an affiliation alone supplies the finding. Denying this fee privilege leaves paid, compliant filing available. The Court decides no general rule insulating judicial affiliations from scrutiny and enters no merits judgment on the recusal claim.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Zatko v. California, 502 U.S. 16 (November 4, 1991): applied to this distinct submission; fee denial remains discretionary and separate from disposition of certiorari.
- Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Lavoie, 475 U.S. 813 (1986): distinguished because this petition establishes no comparable concrete personal financial stake; other constitutionally disqualifying interests are not categorically excluded.

#### III. No prospective filing restriction on this record

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court declines to impose a prospective fee-and-form condition on Martin's unidentified future civil-certiorari petitions because the established record does not show that the available treatment of particular filings is inadequate. This case-specific refusal decides neither that a prior warning is invariably required nor that prospective restrictions are categorically unlawful, and imposes no condition on future civil or criminal filings.

**Authority:** Part III of the per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the sufficiency of this narrower, record-specific ground without resolving the Court's power on a different record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy should remain confined to the filings before it. McDonald and Sindram permit measures directed at concrete abuse of extraordinary writs; Sindram also identifies the special risk created by petitions that have no filing deadline. Those decisions do not compel a restriction on all future ordinary civil petitions. Zatko denied the identified motions and preserved a paid route. This record establishes no subsequent warning, breach of that order or sequence of intervening adjudications demonstrating that its narrower response failed. Moreover, one present petition raises distinct arguments sufficient to permit filing. The established repetitive history supports close examination and, in the companion matter, fee denial, but does not demonstrate a need to prejudge unknown papers. The Court therefore gives the Clerk no prospective exclusion instruction. Its conclusion rests on this record and available remedies, without deciding a universal notice requirement, a numerical threshold, or the legality of every possible future restriction.

**Precedent treatment:**
- In re McDonald, 489 U.S. 180 (1989): preserved as authority for targeted protection against extraordinary-writ abuse; no extension to all future civil-certiorari petitions is made here.
- In re Sindram, 498 U.S. 177 (1991): its extraordinary-writ setting and preservation of other filing categories are distinguished; its existing force is unchanged.
- Zatko v. California, 502 U.S. 16 (November 4, 1991): its limited remedy is preserved; no warning, compliance default or further adjudication is inferred from that order.

#### Church of Scientology of California v. United States, 1992-11-16

Source: [Decision Record](records/church-of-scientology-v-united-states-merits-1992-11-16.md).

**Holding and operative rule.** Compliance with an IRS summons-enforcement order does not moot a timely appeal when, if enforcement proves unlawful, a court can afford meaningful partial relief by ordering return or destruction of copies the Government retains. Complete restoration of secrecy is unnecessary; this ruling establishes the availability of review, not entitlement to that remedy.

**Authority.** Stevens’s opinion for the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas: nine votes for this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an appeal remains a live controversy when judicial relief can still protect a concrete interest. Mills v. Green supplies the requirement of possible effectual relief; it does not demand that a court undo every consequence of the challenged order. The IRS has learned the contents of these recordings, but it also retains copies. An order requiring their return or destruction could address the Church’s continuing interest in possession and privacy. The Government’s argument confuses irreversible disclosure with the absence of every remedy. Reisman v. Caplin establishes that IRS enforcement orders are appealable. Sections 7402(b) and 7604(a), which authorize judicial enforcement, do not withdraw that appellate review after compliance. The Court need not determine the whole extent of a district court’s power to condition enforcement. Nor does recognizing possible relief decide whether these recordings are privileged, whether the crime-fraud exception applies, or whether the Government may later use information derived from them. Those questions do not prevent review of the order.

**Precedent treatment.** Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), applied: some effectual relief suffices. Reisman v. Caplin, 375 U.S. 440, 449 (1964), applied: summons-enforcement orders remain judicially reviewable. United States v. Zolin, 491 U.S. 554 (1989), left undisturbed: its privilege ruling and divided disposition of enforcement-condition jurisdiction are not reopened.

**Current-law effect:** From November 16, 1992, the governing mootness inquiry in this setting asks whether a court can still grant meaningful relief concerning retained materials. The availability of partial return/destruction relief suffices despite irreversible acquisition of knowledge. Existing appellate review of IRS enforcement orders remains available. The merits, scope of district-court enforcement conditions, future-use restrictions, claimed preclusion, and a proposed third-party exception are reserved. No privilege, tax-exemption, damages, or suppression doctrine changes.

**Precedent treatment:** Mills and Reisman are applied as stated in Holdings. Zolin is left undisturbed. The Ninth Circuit’s Kersting-based rule that completed production alone moots the appeal cannot stand in circumstances where retained copies allow effectual relief. United States v. Ryan, 402 U.S. 530 (1971), and Perlman v. United States, 247 U.S. 7 (1918), concern finality and immediate appeal, not an automatic mootness rule; this decision establishes no general third-party exception. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

#### Parke v. Raley, 1992-12-01

Source: [Decision Record](records/parke-v-raley-merits-1992-12-01.md).

**Holding 1 — initial production burden.**

**Holding and operative rule.** In Kentucky’s collateral challenge to a final counseled conviction used for sentence enhancement, due process permits the Commonwealth to prove the judgment and require the defendant to produce evidence of infringement or procedural irregularity. If the defendant refutes regularity, the Commonwealth must affirmatively establish constitutional validity under Kentucky’s procedure; a missing old transcript alone, without alleged official misconduct, does not establish invalidity.

**Authority.** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight votes at this level. Blackmun agrees with the result in a separate writing limited to sentencing.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the State may begin with the regularity of a proved final judgment while allowing a genuine challenge to its validity. Boykin v. Alabama requires a knowing and voluntary guilty plea and an affirmative record on direct review. It does not allocate every burden when a final judgment is later challenged in a separate sentencing proceeding. Johnson v. Zerbst recognizes that collateral constitutional claims may carry a challenger’s evidentiary burden. Kentucky allows rebuttal, provides a hearing when needed, and requires affirmative state proof once regularity is refuted. The defendant participated in the earlier proceeding, while the State may face comparable difficulty reconstructing it. A missing transcript therefore does not itself demonstrate fundamental unfairness. Burgett v. Texas preserves the prohibition against use of an invalid uncounseled conviction; it does not make every unavailable plea record proof of a constitutional defect. This decision neither transfers the burden of proving an offense nor permits courts to ignore evidence that a particular plea was involuntary.

**Precedent treatment.** Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), distinguished as direct review without weakening its valid-waiver requirement. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468–469 (1938), applied to collateral regularity. Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), preserved for constitutionally defective uncounseled convictions. Medina v. California (June 22, 1992), distinguished: its narrow competence-burden holding does not establish a controlling categorical ban on practical due-process analysis.

**Holding 2 — measure of proof.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Due process does not require the clear-and-convincing extrarecord proof demanded by the Sixth Circuit to establish validity of an old guilty plea in this collateral sentencing proceeding. Kentucky’s retention of the ultimate persuasion burden does not entail that heightened constitutional quantum; the fact of prior conviction and the validity inquiry remain distinct.

**Authority.** The same eight-Justice Court opinion; Blackmun independently agrees on his sentencing ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Sixth Circuit demanded more than the Constitution requires when it prescribed clear and convincing extrarecord evidence. Boykin did not decide the measure of proof in a collateral proceeding, and its direct-review record requirement does not supply that missing rule. Johnson v. Zerbst permits ordinary collateral proof of constitutional invalidity; it does not require every uncertainty from an aged record to be resolved by a heightened state burden. Reconstruction can be difficult for both parties. Kentucky responds by recognizing an initial presumption, allowing contrary evidence and requiring a responsive state showing when the presumption is refuted. Neither the constitutional importance of a voluntary plea nor that responsive burden establishes the particular quantum imposed below. The Court rejects that mandatory quantum without announcing one evidentiary measure for every state system. Kentucky’s separate requirement to prove the fact of prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt remains unaffected, as do the prosecution’s burdens concerning the present offense.

**Precedent treatment.** Boykin is not extended into a clear-and-convincing collateral proof requirement. Zerbst supports the distinction between constitutional validity and a universally heightened burden. The Sixth Circuit’s Dunn v. Simmons, 877 F.2d 1275 (1989), cannot govern insofar as it demands that constitutional quantum and shifts the entire production burden solely because no transcript survives.

**Holding 3 — the 1981 plea.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Under then-applicable 28 U.S.C. §2254(d), a competent state court’s factual determination, evidenced by written findings, an opinion or other reliable and adequate written indicia after a merits hearing involving the applicant and the State receives a presumption of correctness subject to the conditions and exceptions below; the ultimate federal question whether a plea was knowing and voluntary remains independently determined. On the established earlier warnings, contemporaneous 1981 charge form and jury warning, representation, education, unimpaired condition and testimony here, Raley’s 1981 plea was knowing and voluntary and the Sixth Circuit’s conditional new-hearing remedy is unwarranted.

**Statutory qualifications.** The presumption does not govern if the applicant establishes, it otherwise appears, or the State admits that (1) the state hearing did not resolve the merits of the factual dispute; (2) its factfinding procedure was inadequate to afford a full and fair hearing; (3) material facts were not adequately developed; (4) the state court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction or jurisdiction over the applicant; (5) an indigent applicant was denied appointed counsel in deprivation of his constitutional right; (6) the applicant did not receive a full, fair and adequate hearing; or (7) the applicant was otherwise denied due process in that proceeding. Under paragraph (8), the presumption also does not govern when the pertinent state record is produced and the federal court, considering that record as a whole, concludes that the factual determination lacks fair record support. At a federal evidentiary hearing, once the state factual determination is duly proved, and absent those circumstances, the applicant must establish its error by convincing evidence. That federal factual-review burden is distinct from Kentucky’s allocation and measure of proof of a prior plea’s constitutional validity. Inferences receive deference only when fairly supported by the facts; no presumption attaches to the ultimate federal legal conclusion.

**Authority.** The same eight-Justice Court opinion; Blackmun concurs in reversal without a separate contrary factual conclusion.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the record supports the Kentucky courts’ inference of continued understanding. Marshall v. Lonberger distinguishes historical findings, which receive statutory deference when fairly supported, from the ultimate federal validity of the plea. Raley’s earlier advice is established by a rights-and-waiver form and counsel’s confirmation, not by the bare existence of a conviction. In 1981 he again had counsel, understood the charge form and acknowledged a jury-trial warning. His education, lack of impairment and testimony about sentencing and bargaining support the inference that he understood the choice he made. His inability to recall additional warnings does not compel the contrary inference. The state hearing resolved the factual dispute through adequately developed evidence and fair procedures; the whole pertinent record fairly supports its findings. No jurisdictional, counsel or other due-process defect in that hearing is shown. Applying Boykin and North Carolina v. Alford independently to those facts, the plea was knowing and voluntary. The Court does not presume understanding from any prior conviction or make every experienced defendant’s plea valid. It reverses the conditional writ on this particular showing; the unchallenged 1979 determination remains intact.

**Precedent treatment.** Marshall v. Lonberger, 459 U.S. 422, 431–438 (1983), applied to supported historical inferences and independent legal review. Boykin and North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25, 31 (1970), applied to an informed voluntary choice. No later habeas deference standard enters.

**Current-law effect:** Kentucky may apply its rebuttable production allocation in this collateral challenge; it must follow its own responsive affirmative-proof rule after rebuttal. The Sixth Circuit cannot require the State to bear the entire burden by clear and convincing evidence solely because old transcripts are unavailable. Current law does not establish a universal collateral proof quantum or a categorical no-right-to-collateral-review rule. Then-§2254(d) governs historical facts subject to the hearing, factual-development, jurisdiction, counsel, due-process and whole-record-support exceptions set out in Holding 3; federal validity remains independently determined. The 1981 conditional writ is reversed on the actual evidentiary showing. Direct-review Boykin, the exclusion of invalid uncounseled convictions, criminal-element proof, and the Medina fracture remain undisturbed.

**Precedent treatment:** Boykin, Zerbst, Burgett, Lonberger, Alford, and the limited Medina rule are treated separately as described in Holdings. The Sixth Circuit’s Dunn v. Simmons allocation and compulsory proof quantum are rejected; no Supreme Court precedent is overruled. Kentucky’s Dunn v. Commonwealth, 703 S.W.2d 874 (1985), supplies the actual state procedure, not an independently created federal rule. The statutory sentencing provisions and Kentucky’s Gadd construction remain distinct from the federal due-process permission.

#### Richmond v. Lewis, 1992-12-01

Source: [Decision Record](records/richmond-v-lewis-merits-1992-12-01.md).

**Holding 1 — an actual cure is necessary.**

**Holding and operative rule.** When a death sentence in a State that weighs aggravation against mitigation rests on an unconstitutionally vague aggravating factor, remaining valid factors do not alone cure the error. The sentence may stand only after an authorized sentencer actually makes a constitutionally valid new sentencing determination, including lawful appellate reweighing, or the State establishes constitutional harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Authority.** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: eight votes. Thomas also writes separately; Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an invalid factor cannot remain uncorrected in the process choosing death. Arizona’s statute directs consideration of aggravating and mitigating circumstances and permits death when an aggravator exists and mitigation is not sufficiently substantial to call for leniency. Its courts construe that direction as weighing. The survival of two valid aggravators therefore establishes eligibility without establishing a lawful selection decision. Clemons v. Mississippi permits an authorized state appellate court to correct the error through actual reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review. The operative Stringer, Sochor and Espinosa decisions require the substance of that correction, not an assumption that the same outcome would follow. No particular formula of words is indispensable, but a general obligation of independent review is not performance of a new sentencing calculus. The federal habeas court cannot supply Arizona’s missing weighing itself. This rule leaves the State free to choose an authorized constitutional cure and does not require an automatic life sentence.

**Precedent treatment.** Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 (1990), applied: lawful state appellate cure is permitted, automatic factor-count affirmance is not. Stringer v. Black (March 9), Sochor v. Florida (June 8) and Espinosa v. Florida (June 29), all 1992, applied at their controlling levels: actual weighing error requires actual constitutional correction. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), preserves the State’s beyond-reasonable-doubt harmlessness obligation.

**Holding 2 — Arizona’s adjudication did not cure this sentence.**

**Holding and operative rule.** On the parties’ premise that a majority of the Arizona Supreme Court had to cast valid curative votes, this sentence cannot stand: even assuming the two Justices retaining the narrowed aggravator lawfully reweighed, the two other affirming Justices relied conclusively on the remaining criminal history without a new balance of mitigation. The denial of sentence relief is reversed for a conditional writ allowing a reasonable period for constitutional correction or vacation of the death sentence and imposition of a lawful lesser sentence.

**Authority.** The same eight-Justice Court opinion. No rule demanding identical reasoning from every member of a state appellate majority is adopted.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the state judgment lacks enough votes for a lawful cure on the premise the parties accept. Chief Justice Holohan and Justice Hays retained the heinous-or-depraved factor and balanced mitigation against three aggravators. The Court assumes, without deciding, that their application of the later narrowing construction and their reweighing were valid. Justices Cameron and Gordon rejected that factor, but their separate explanation treated Richmond’s murder and kidnapping history as conclusive justification for death. They did not perform a new balance against the mitigation, which included evidence of rehabilitation. Their general agreement with an independent-review obligation cannot overcome that specific explanation. Justice Feldman would have imposed life. Thus at most two valid votes supported affirmance, and one more was necessary. The Court need not decide whether Richmond drove over the victim, whether the principal opinion permissibly applied the narrowed factor, or whether separately valid rationales could otherwise combine. The conviction remains untouched; only the uncorrected death sentence requires relief.

**Precedent treatment.** Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), and Lewis v. Jeffers, 497 U.S. 764 (1990), preserved: narrowing constructions and appropriately reviewed state applications may be valid, but do not supply a curative vote absent here. State v. Gretzler, 135 Ariz.42, 659 P.2d1 (1983), remains the state narrowing source; its proper application by Holohan and Hays is not decided. Clemons and the operative weighing-error decisions require the stated remedy.

**Current-law effect:** Arizona is a weighing State for this sentencing rule. Remaining valid aggravators do not cure a vague factor’s effect on the selection process. An actual lawful state cure or constitutional harmlessness is necessary, and the federal habeas court cannot replace that cure with its own sentencing balance. On the parties’ majority-vote premise, the1983 adjudication lacks a sufficient curative affirmance. This application strengthens no general rule about fractured state judgments beyond the accepted premise. Existing weighing doctrine remains operative; Scalia’s eligibility-only theory and Thomas’s preserved retroactivity objection remain noncontrolling.

**Precedent treatment:** The weighing-error cases and Chapman are applied as stated in Holdings. Walton/Jeffers permit properly narrowed aggravators and appropriate habeas review; they do not authorize an automatic affirmance with remaining factors. The historical Ninth Circuit’s alternative treatment of Arizona as outside weighing is rejected under the statutory direction and authoritative Arizona construction. The Court assumes rather than resolves the validity of Holohan-Hays’s Gretzler application. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled, and no rule requiring a single common state appellate rationale is established.

#### Soldal v. Cook County, 1992-12-08

Source: [Decision Record](records/Soldal_v_Cook_County_merits_1992-12-08.md).

#### A seizure of a home protects possession independently of privacy

**Holding and operative rule:** Meaningful government-attributable interference with possession of a home is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment even without a search, invasion of privacy or liberty, or criminal investigation. The family’s alleged forcible dispossession meets that threshold, but coverage alone does not establish unreasonableness or every element of §1983 liability.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine Justices adopt the same rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that taking a home implicates the Fourth Amendment even when officials do not search it. Jacobsen distinguishes a search’s invasion of privacy from a seizure’s interference with possession; Place likewise examines the detention of property independently of a search. Hicks confirms that the absence of a new privacy intrusion does not remove a seizure from constitutional scrutiny. The alleged removal here deprived the family of its home while deputies prevented resistance. Adickes permits the joint-action inference accepted below at summary judgment, and respondents’ alternative ground does not require disturbing that determination. The concern that this rule federalizes every repossession confuses coverage with liability. Private conduct alone is insufficient, reasonableness remains to be adjudicated, and municipal policy, causation, immunity and relief retain their separate requirements. The Court does not decide those matters or extend the Amendment to every category of property. A home is expressly protected by its text.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Jacobsen, 466 U.S. 109 — applied; possession and privacy remain independently protected interests.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696 — applied; seizure scrutiny does not require an accompanying search.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321 — applied; plain-view seizure remains subject to the Fourth Amendment.
- Adickes v. S. H. Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144 — applied to the summary-judgment joint-action inference, without a final conspiracy finding.
- Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 — preserved; seizure coverage does not establish municipal liability.
- Wyatt v. Cole, May 18, 1992 — preserved; attribution does not settle immunity or a private merits defense.

#### A due-process characterization does not displace the specific seizure claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A court may not reject an otherwise applicable Fourth Amendment claim merely because the same conduct also concerns a deprivation of property under the Due Process Clause. Each applicable constitutional protection must be examined on its own terms; this disposition does not adjudicate the family’s separate substantive-due-process claim.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; unanimous holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a general description of the controversy cannot erase a specific constitutional protection. Graham directs courts to use the explicit constitutional source governing the challenged conduct instead of replacing it with generalized substantive due process. It does not authorize treating the dominant character of a dispute as a reason to disregard another applicable constitutional command. Here, the seizure of a home falls within the Fourth Amendment’s own terms. The landlord-tenant setting and the availability of state remedies therefore cannot end that inquiry. Parratt addresses the adequacy of process for a property deprivation; it does not decide whether a government-attributable seizure is reasonable. Hudson’s prison-specific treatment of possession does not establish a general exclusion for homes outside prison. The family may pursue the restored seizure claim without obtaining a ruling here on its separate substantive-due-process contention. The Court leaves that question, and the remaining liability and remedial issues, unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 — applied; the specific Fourth Amendment source governs its covered conduct and is not displaced by a dominant-character theory.
- Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527 — distinguished; postdeprivation procedural remedies do not answer the distinct seizure claim.
- Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517 — confined to its prison setting for the relevant coverage proposition; no rule excluding possession of a home follows.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 8, 1992, Fourth Amendment seizure coverage protects government-attributable meaningful interference with possession of a home independently of privacy, liberty, a search, or criminal investigative purpose. A competing due-process characterization does not displace the specific claim. Ultimate reasonableness and separate §1983 liability/remedy requirements remain open.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Jacobsen, Place, Hicks, Adickes and Graham are applied; Parratt and Hudson do not supply the proposed categorical bar. The general §1983 and immunity frameworks remain unchanged.

#### Farrar v. Hobby, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/Farrar_v_Hobby_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### Enforceable nominal relief establishes eligibility

**Holding and operative rule:** A civil-rights plaintiff who obtains an enforceable nominal-damages judgment against the defendant from whom fees are sought is a prevailing party under §1988. The judgment must directly benefit the plaintiff by changing the defendant’s legal obligation; its nominal amount does not defeat eligibility.

**Authority:** Part I of White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; all nine Justices support this controlling proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an enforceable dollar judgment is actual relief. Carey recognizes nominal damages for a proved denial of procedural due process when compensatory injury cannot be shown. That judgment obliges Hobby to pay money he otherwise had no duty to pay. Hewitt denied prevailing-party status where favorable judicial language produced no relief, and Rhodes did so where a declaration could benefit neither plaintiff. Those limitations do not describe this enforceable award. Garland makes a material alteration in the parties’ legal relationship the statutory threshold and assigns the extent of success to the amount inquiry. Its discussion of an insignificant technical victory does not convert an enforceable monetary judgment into no judgment at all. The estate therefore qualifies as a prevailing party. This conclusion neither revisits the jury’s liability or causation findings nor guarantees any positive fee; §1988 still requires that any amount awarded be reasonable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 — applied; nominal relief vindicates the established procedural violation without presumed compensatory injury.
- Hewitt v. Helms, 482 U.S. 755 — distinguished; favorable language without enforceable relief differs from a payment judgment.
- Rhodes v. Stewart, 488 U.S. 1 — distinguished; ineffective declaratory relief differs from a judgment directly benefiting the estate.
- Texas State Teachers Assn. v. Garland Independent School District, 489 U.S. 782 — applied and clarified; magnitude affects amount, not eligibility where nominal relief is enforceable.

#### Reasonable amount requires reconsideration on this review

**Holding and operative rule:** When the court of appeals denies fees solely because a nominal-damages plaintiff is ineligible, correction of that error does not itself determine a reasonable amount. On this record, the fee judgment is vacated and remanded for preserved reasonableness objections and an explained Hensley assessment of actual success, allowing reduction to zero without automatically reinstating the prior award or requiring proportionality to damages.

**Authority:** Part II of White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter; five Justices control the remedy and stated fee principles.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the amount should be reconsidered under the correct eligibility rule. Hensley makes the degree of success central, requires exclusion of unrelated unsuccessful work, and calls for an explanation of the fee awarded. The failed demand for $17 million plainly bears on that assessment. Hobby expressly argues for zero fees or a substantial reduction. Petitioners object that the Fifth Circuit never decided amount and seek remand of that question. The Court chooses to leave the preserved alternative to lower-court review of the discretionary award; it does not treat that argument as waived or unbriefed. Rivera forecloses a mechanical ratio between damages and fees; it does not justify compensating unsuccessful work or inventing a public benefit. Blanchard likewise commits the reasonable amount to the circumstances rather than a rigid measure. The remand permits a substantially reduced award or zero after the required analysis and does not restore the $280,000 award automatically. The Court does not categorically forbid affirmance on a sufficient alternative fee ground in another properly presented case.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 — applied; actual success, unrelated unsuccessful work and a reasoned discretionary assessment govern amount.
- City of Riverside v. Rivera, 477 U.S. 561 — applied within its authority; no mechanical proportionality requirement, without guaranteed fees for nominal relief.
- Blanchard v. Bergeron, 489 U.S. 87 — applied; the court must determine a reasonable statutory fee in the circumstances.
- City of Burlington v. Dague, June 24, 1992 — distinguished; contingency enhancement under the two environmental statutes does not decide this eligibility or remand question.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, an enforceable nominal judgment establishes §1988 prevailing-party status. Actual success governs reasonable amount, including possible zero; no mandatory damages/fees proportion or presumption of substantial fees follows. This case requires remand rather than immediate appellate fixation at zero. Dague’s separate contingency rule remains unchanged.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Carey, Hewitt, Rhodes and Garland establish the eligibility distinction; Hensley, Rivera and Blanchard govern the bounded amount remand.

#### District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/District_of_Columbia_v_Greater_Washington_Board_of_Trade_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### Covered-plan content as a mandatory legal measure

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §514(a) preempts this continuation obligation insofar as it incorporates an ERISA-covered health plan’s benefit content as the legal measure of mandatory replacement coverage, even when an employer may administer the replacement benefits separately through an exempt workers’ compensation arrangement. No §514(b) savings claim is decided, and this ruling does not preempt requirements measured solely by an ERISA-exempt governmental or church plan.

**Authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; eight Justices adopt the same operative rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that separate administration does not remove the obligation’s legal dependence on a covered plan. Shaw supplies the connection-or-reference construction of §514(a), while preserving separately administered plans maintained solely to meet workers’ compensation, unemployment-compensation or disability-insurance requirements. Here the District’s command fixes replacement coverage by the employee’s existing covered benefits, for the statutory continuation period. Mackey and Ingersoll-Rand establish that such operative reference can require preemption even when a law addresses another subject. Alessi likewise prevents a workers’ compensation label from insulating interference with a covered plan. Section 4(b)(3) excludes the separately maintained arrangement from ERISA coverage; it does not transform the referenced covered plan into an exempt one. Fort Halifax’s one-time-payment rule does not apply to this conceded health plan. The Court leaves unpresented §514(b) savings questions and applications measured solely by exempt plans outside this holding. It decides no general rule that incidental economic influence or every mention of benefits suffices.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85 — applied; its separate-unit permission concerns independently fixed exempt benefits and does not eliminate operative reference to a different covered plan.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825 — applied; specific legal reference is distinguished from ordinary general garnishment.
- Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133 — applied; dependence on a covered plan can trigger preemption despite the nominal subject of the state rule.
- Alessi v. Raybestos-Manhattan, Inc., 451 U.S. 504 — applied; regulation through workers’ compensation does not avoid ERISA’s covered-plan restriction.
- Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 — distinguished; a one-time payment without ongoing administration differs from this health plan.
- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724 — preserved; its insurance-saving holding supplies no additional §4(b) escape from a covered-plan reference.
- Morales v. Trans World Airlines, June 1, 1992 — consistent similar-language analogy only; its aviation holding does not replace ERISA’s own coverage and saving provisions.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, mandatory replacement health coverage legally measured by an existing covered plan is preempted in this application notwithstanding separate exempt administration. The 52-week statutory cap describes the invalidated obligation’s scope, not a federal safe harbor. Exempt-only plan measures and unpresented §514(b) savings questions remain outside this application; ordinary indirect economic effects remain unresolved here.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Existing connection/reference law is applied to the mandatory covered-plan benchmark. Separate exempt-plan administration and §514(b) saving provisions retain their distinct operation.

#### Mississippi v. Louisiana, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/Mississippi_v_Louisiana_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### An interstate complaint belongs to the exclusive original forum

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1251(a) denies a federal district court jurisdiction to grant relief on an actual controversy between two states, even when the complaint is joined to private litigation and this Court previously declined an original action. The appellate judgment is reversed insofar as it adjudicates Louisiana’s complaint against Mississippi, with instructions to direct dismissal of that complaint for want of jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices; unanimous controlling holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that exclusive jurisdiction cannot be shared by implication. Section 1251(a) reserves controversies between states to this Court. California v. Arizona recognizes that a district court cannot hear one state’s claim against another under that allocation. Louisiana’s third-party complaint expressly seeks relief against Mississippi; its origin in a private title suit does not change those parties. The earlier refusal to entertain an original action therefore supplies no affirmative district-court power. Illinois v. Milwaukee and Ohio v. Wyandotte address the circumstances warranting an original forum, and Texas v. New Mexico recognizes discretion in its exercise. Those authorities do not authorize transferring a statutorily exclusive controversy to another federal court. The general federal-question statute cannot overcome the specific restriction. Mississippi’s own argument for district jurisdiction, and the convenience of deciding all questions together, cannot cure the defect. The Court accordingly removes the interstate adjudication without determining the boundary or the merits of acquiescence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- California v. Arizona, 440 U.S. 59 — applied; this Court is the exclusive federal forum for a state’s claim against another state.
- Illinois v. City of Milwaukee, 406 U.S. 91 — distinguished as to different defendants and applied to preserve original-forum discretion.
- Ohio v. Wyandotte Chemicals Corp., 401 U.S. 493 — preserved; discretion does not grant another court exclusive interstate jurisdiction.
- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 — preserved; case-specific discretion does not negate §1251(a).
- Wyoming v. Oklahoma, January 22, 1992 — distinguished; its original-forum exercise and separate objections supply no district-court authority here.

#### Private title questions remain distinct from binding interstate relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1251(a) does not bar an otherwise lawful private-title action merely because deciding title requires locating a state boundary; such adjudication does not bind the states on their boundary. Louisiana’s intervention that seeks no relief against Mississippi is unaffected, and the remaining title claims return for a determination whether the existing record permits fair resolution or additional proceedings are necessary.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices; unanimous controlling holding and remand limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that jurisdiction follows the parties to the controversy, not every geographic issue a court may need to consider. The District Court’s authority over the private plaintiffs’ claims against the private defendants is conceded. Durfee explains that a boundary determination in private litigation does not bind the states themselves. A court may therefore decide private title within its lawful jurisdiction without issuing an authoritative interstate boundary decree. Louisiana’s intervention in that title dispute does not itself seek relief against Mississippi and survives the statutory ruling. But the courts below intermingled private ownership with the purported interstate adjudication. This Court cannot assume that excising the latter leaves a complete and fair basis for the former. The Court of Appeals must examine that question and require additional District Court proceedings if needed. No private claimant’s title is determined here, no state is bound by a private boundary finding, and no new original action is commenced.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Durfee v. Duke, 375 U.S. 106 — applied; adjudication between private parties does not bind the states on their boundary.
- California v. Arizona, 440 U.S. 59 — applied to the actual interstate controversy; it does not withdraw otherwise lawful private-title jurisdiction.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, §1251(a) excludes district adjudication of a state-against-state complaint notwithstanding joinder to private litigation or earlier denial of original leave. A lawful private-title action, including Louisiana’s nonadverse intervention, remains possible; it cannot bind the states on their boundary. No replacement original-jurisdiction or boundary test is adopted.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Statutory exclusivity is applied to the actual interstate parties; original-jurisdiction discretion and private boundary-related adjudication remain distinct. No rule of accretion, avulsion or sovereign acquiescence is decided.

#### Republic National Bank of Miami v. United States, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/Republic_National_Bank_of_Miami_v_United_States_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### Removal does not itself terminate perfected forfeiture jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** Once valid attachment has established in rem jurisdiction, removal of the property or substituted proceeds does not by itself defeat appellate jurisdiction. The bank’s failure to obtain a stay or bond therefore does not foreclose this appeal where a favorable judgment could still provide lawful effectual relief; an entirely useless judgment presents a distinct limit.

**Authority:** Parts I, II and IV of Blackmun’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; eight Justices adopt this rule. Thomas supports the judgment on a different statutory ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that initial control of the property and continuing custody are different requirements. The Rio Grande rejects the proposition that removal automatically destroys jurisdiction already acquired, and The Little Charles identifies practical inability to effectuate a decree as a different concern. The Brig Ann addresses abandonment before proceedings began; Taylor v. Carryl concerns the original seizure needed to obtain jurisdiction. Neither establishes the Government’s proposed perpetual-custody rule. Here the parties substituted identifiable sale proceeds for property validly attached, and the Government then placed those proceeds in its own Treasury account. The bank’s failure to secure a stay did not undo the original attachment. Church of Scientology likewise confirms that possible effectual relief can preserve review despite an intervening event. The statutory-payment holding supplies that possibility here. The Court reserves a case where a judgment would be entirely useless, the amendment’s temporal application, and every question about whether this bank should prevail on the forfeiture merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- The Rio Grande, 23 Wall. 458 — applied; removal alone does not terminate jurisdiction properly acquired.
- The Little Charles, 26 F. Cas. 979 — applied; the ability to effectuate relief is distinct from uninterrupted custody.
- The Brig Ann, 9 Cranch 289 — distinguished; abandonment before institution differs from removal after valid attachment.
- Taylor v. Carryl, 20 How. 583 — distinguished; initial jurisdiction does not establish perpetual custody as a condition.
- Church of Scientology of California v. United States, November 16, 1992 — applied by analogy on effectual relief; its IRS-copy rule supplies no payment authority.

#### The return statute and judgment appropriation permit effective relief upon success

**Holding and operative rule:** If the bank prevails in the underlying forfeiture and obtains the requisite judgment, 28 U.S.C. §2465 supplies a substantive right to return of the substituted proceeds; 31 U.S.C. §1304 and 28 U.S.C. §2414 supply the judgment-payment route when payment is not otherwise provided, the Comptroller General certifies payment, and the obligation satisfies the statutory final-judgment and payable-judgment requirements. That conditional lawful route defeats the Government’s claim that this appeal can produce no effective relief, without ordering payment now or recognizing judicial spending power apart from an applicable appropriation.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s statutory-payment opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six express joins and independently controls. Stevens expressly accepts the same sufficient statutory proposition in his own writing, making seven supporters without an invented formal join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress has supplied a lawful means of effectuating a favorable forfeiture judgment. Section 2465 commands return when the claimant prevails, and sale of the attached property does not eliminate that right; the proceeds stand in its place. Section 1304 appropriates sums for qualifying final judgments when payment is not otherwise provided and the certification and statutory-payment conditions are met. Section 2414 supplies the relevant final district-court judgment route. Richmond cautions that the Judgment Fund is not an all-purpose judicial account: a substantive statutory right must support payment. Section 2465 supplies that right here if the bank succeeds. The Government’s Treasury transfer therefore does not make relief legally impossible. Knote’s restriction on payment without legislative authority need not be qualified because the necessary statutory route exists. The Court does not decide whether disputed funds may ever be returned without an appropriation, whether another payment source applies, or whether the bank is entitled to prevail. No money is awarded today.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Office of Personnel Management v. Richmond, 496 U.S. 414 — applied; a substantive statutory entitlement and applicable appropriation, not equity alone, are required for this payment route.
- Knote v. United States, 95 U.S. 149 — preserved; the statutory resolution leaves the broader constitutional question about contested forfeiture proceeds undecided.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, perfected civil-forfeiture jurisdiction is not lost solely by transfer of the res or proceeds, where lawful effectual relief remains possible. In this setting §2465 reaches substituted proceeds, and §§1304 and 2414 permit payment upon the required favorable judgment and their statutory conditions. The bank must still establish its entitlement. The majority leaves §1355(c)’s application to pre-enactment events and any appropriation-free return theory unresolved; the statute’s already-effective text remains in force independently of this decision.

**Precedent treatment:** No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The traditional attachment authorities are applied within their initial-versus-continuing-custody distinction. Richmond’s requirement of a substantive statutory basis is satisfied conditionally through §2465 and the judgment appropriation. The Eleventh Circuit’s continuous-custody rule cannot sustain this dismissal. The majority adopts no appropriation-free constitutional return doctrine and no construction of the intervening amendment’s temporal reach.

#### 1993-01-12 — Puerto Rico Aqueduct & Sewer Authority v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc.

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/puerto-rico-aqueduct-v-metcalf-eddy-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding and operative rule.** A State or entity claiming to be an arm of a State may immediately appeal under §1291 a conclusive denial of Eleventh Amendment immunity from federal suit when the immunity issue is separate from the merits and the protection would be effectively lost by waiting for final judgment. Appealability does not establish that the claimant possesses immunity, and a dispute over arm-of-State status does not by itself require postponing review until trial.

**Authority.** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: seven votes for this rule and its controlling explanation. Stone-Zsela supplies an eighth vote for reversal and the narrower procedural rule in his judgment concurrence. No fractured-decision analysis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the protection from federal suit cannot ordinarily be restored after the State has undergone the litigation. Cohen permits immediate review of a small class of orders whose separate rights would otherwise be lost; Coopers & Lybrand requires conclusiveness, separation from the merits and effective unreviewability. Mitchell applies those principles to immunity from litigation. The denied claim here concerns that protection, not whether the engineering firm can prove breach of contract. The States’ protected immunity and their interests in avoiding federal coercive process distinguish this claim from an ordinary objection to the chosen forum. Ex parte Young does not reduce state immunity to a defense against liability: it permits qualifying prospective relief against officials, while Green v. Mansour excludes a declaration of a past federal violation and Cory v. White preserves immunity of the State or agency itself. The Court decides neither whether PRASA is an arm of Puerto Rico nor whether Puerto Rico must receive a State’s treatment. Those questions are not resolved by recognizing appellate jurisdiction.

**Precedent treatment.**
- Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541 (1949), and Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463 (1978): applied; the three collateral-order requirements remain mandatory.
- Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985): its immunity-from-suit rationale is applied to asserted Eleventh Amendment immunity; this does not extend substantive immunity to new entities.
- Edelman v. Jordan, 415 U.S. 651 (1974), and In re Ayers, 123 U.S. 443 (1887): relied on for the existing protection from federal suit and its federalism basis, without enlarging the classes of immune defendants.
- Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), Green v. Mansour, 474 U.S. 64 (1985), and Cory v. White, 457 U.S. 85 (1982): preserved as distinct rules concerning prospective official relief, past-violation declarations, and suits against the State or agency itself.
- Van Cauwenberghe v. Biard, 486 U.S. 517 (1988), and Catlin v. United States, 324 U.S. 229 (1945): distinguished; their ordinary forum-jurisdiction finality rules remain.
- Mt. Healthy City Board of Education v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274 (1977): retained; entity status remains a distinct inquiry.

Effective January 12, 1993, qualifying conclusive denials of Eleventh Amendment immunity are immediately appealable under the existing collateral-order requirements. Before this decision, the Court had not resolved the circuit conflict over this category. The addition concerns appellate timing; entitlement, waiver, abrogation, the unchallenged Puerto Rico premise, and alternative contract remedies remain undecided. Existing prospective-officer-relief limitations remain as described in Holdings. The court below may examine PRASA’s status on remand.

#### 1993-01-12 — Bath Iron Works Corp. v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/bath-iron-works-v-director-owcp-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding 1 — compensation branch and injury date.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Noise-induced occupational hearing loss sustained during workplace exposure is a scheduled, presumptively disabling injury governed by §908(c)(13), whether claimed before or after retirement; the injury is complete at last exposure, which supplies the injury date for the wage basis. Later awareness or filing does not make it an occupational disease that does not immediately result in disability under §910(i), and §908(c)(23)’s special retiree formula therefore does not apply.

**Authority.** Stevens’s unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; nine votes for the rule and application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the time a worker learns of hearing loss is distinct from the time the disabling injury occurs. Potomac Electric establishes that a scheduled injury receives its prescribed compensation without proof that earnings actually fell. The undisputed medical description establishes that noise-related hearing loss occurs during exposure and is complete when exposure ends. Section 910(i), by contrast, addresses an occupational disease that does not immediately cause death or disability. Its awareness rule cannot apply until that predicate is satisfied. The employer’s reading would remove that limiting language merely because the worker has retired. The provisions cross-referencing §910(i) and §910(d)(2) must operate together; they do not authorize combining a latent-disease wage measure with the hearing-loss schedule. The Court therefore affirms the scheduled classification. It does not decide a claim based on a disease that genuinely becomes disabling later, create a continuing-wage-loss requirement for scheduled benefits, or disturb the waived wage-calculation issue and unchallenged impairment percentage in this case.

**Precedent treatment.** Potomac Electric Power Co. v. Director, OWCP, 449 U.S. 268 (1980), is applied: scheduled compensation is mandatory within its statutory branch and does not require actual earning loss. Todd Shipyards Corp. v. Black, 717 F.2d 1280 (9th Cir. 1983), is distinguished as a latent-disease case; its rationale does not move the completed hearing injury to the awareness date. The contrary retiree classifications in Ingalls Shipbuilding and Alabama Dry Dock do not survive this statutory construction.

**Holding 2 — the distinct filing protection.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 908(c)(13)(D) postpones the running of the §912 notice period and §913 claim period for hearing loss until the employee receives an audiogram and its accompanying report indicating hearing loss; it does not postpone the substantive time of injury to awareness. The 1984 amendments accordingly provide a hearing-specific filing protection, independently defeating the contention that congressional concern about late hearing claims necessarily places them in the latent-disease compensation system.

**Authority.** The same unanimous Court opinion; all nine Justices join this independently sufficient answer to the employer’s legislative-history argument as well as the text-based classification in Holding 1.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Congress addressed delayed hearing claims through a separate filing rule. Section 908(c)(13)(D) directs when the notice and claim periods begin; it does not use the awareness language or substantive predicate of §910(i). That distinction permits a worker to claim an injury already completed during employment without converting it into a disease that becomes disabling after retirement. Potomac Electric supplies the existing schedule’s operation, and the 1984 hearing amendment preserves access to that system. The employer relies on a Senator’s passing criticism of Redick, a Board decision denying a retiree’s hearing claim. The carefully read statutory text is unambiguous and is not displaced by that single statement. Independently, the hearing-specific amendment shows why congressional concern about that denial does not entail use of §908(c)(23). These are two sufficient answers, not competing injury-date rules. Brown’s timely filing therefore remains consistent with scheduled compensation. The Court does not establish an alternative audiogram date, reopen timeliness, or create a universal rule for genuinely delayed occupational disease.

**Precedent treatment.** Potomac Electric remains the source of the schedule’s fixed statutory operation. Redick v. Bethlehem Steel Corp., 16 BRBS 155 (1984), cannot justify denying a scheduled claim solely because hearing loss was discovered after voluntary retirement; reference to it in debate does not change either statutory trigger.

From January 12, 1993, occupational hearing loss completed during exposure is compensated under the schedule, using last exposure as the injury date for the wage basis, even when awareness and claim follow retirement. The schedule gives two-thirds of the applicable average weekly wage for the prescribed weeks: bilateral total loss uses 200 weeks, with proportionate treatment for partial loss. Actual postinjury earnings need not decline. The special retiree formula instead remains available only for the qualifying delayed-disease category: within the first retirement year the relevant wage is one fifty-second of earnings in the preceding 52 weeks; after that year it is the national average wage at the statutory injury time, with two-thirds multiplied by the permanent-impairment percentage under the referenced medical guides for the duration of impairment. These systems cannot be hybridized. The distinct audiogram-and-report filing protection remains. The present award is affirmed under preservation limits, not recomputed.

#### 1993-01-12 — Commissioner v. Soliman

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/commissioner-v-soliman-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding and operative rule.** Under then-effective §280A(c)(1)(A), identifying the principal place of business requires comparing the relative importance of activities performed at each business location and the time spent there; necessary administration or the lack of another office alone does not make a home office principal. Customer contact or service delivery receives great but nonconclusive weight, special facilities may add weight, time is particularly useful when the functions do not settle the comparison, and a home office does not qualify by default when no place emerges as principal.

**Authority.** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter: six votes for this formulation and its application. Thomas and Scalia agree only with the judgment on their separately stated approach; Stevens dissents. The Court opinion controls without a fractured-decision inquiry.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Soliman’s home office does not qualify because the statute asks which place is principal, not merely whether an office is legitimate or necessary. Malat v. Riddell and Crane v. Commissioner direct attention to ordinary meaning; principal requires comparison among business locations. Section 162’s ordinary-expense rule does not eliminate §280A’s additional restriction. Administering anesthesia and caring for patients in specially equipped hospitals are the most important services of this practice, and Soliman also spends substantially more working time there. His essential records, preparation and correspondence do not outweigh those converging facts. The contrary headquarters approach would turn almost any indispensable administrative room into the principal place without making the comparison Congress required. The Court adopts no universal customer-contact requirement and does not bar a home office where the main business activities actually occur there. The separate patient-meeting and unattached-structure branches retain independent operation; alternative office availability may bear on an employee’s employer-convenience condition, but not on the principal-place inquiry. No later statutory amendment enters this decision.

**Precedent treatment.**
- Malat v. Riddell, 383 U.S. 569 (1966), and Crane v. Commissioner, 331 U.S. 1 (1947): applied for ordinary meaning in revenue statutes; neither is displaced.
- Welch v. Helvering, 290 U.S. 111 (1933): retained for the fact-sensitive character of deduction questions; it does not prevent articulating consistent statutory considerations.
- The Fourth Circuit’s necessity/substantial-time/no-other-office test is rejected because it fails to compare locations; no earlier Supreme Court principal-place test is overruled.

**Application and limits.** Exclusive and regular business use remain prerequisites. Visits from patients or clients are not indispensable under subsection (A); subsection (B) is a separate alternative for normal-course meetings or dealings, and subsection (C) separately covers qualifying unattached structures. Neither alternative is presented as a basis for this deduction; their general boundaries are not adjudicated. The employee-only employer-convenience requirement is not a condition for this self-employed taxpayer. The Court disallows this home-office deduction and reaches no other deduction.

Effective January 12, 1993, the principal-place branch uses comparative functional importance and time with the qualifications in Holdings. This settles the previously open Supreme Court question; necessity, lack of another office and general deductibility under §162 are insufficient. The Court does not require delivery to clients at home, does not select an inflexible arithmetic weighting, and recognizes that no location need qualify. Other branches and the additional employer-convenience condition for employee use retain distinct operation. The 1983 deduction at issue fails on the combined hospital-service and time facts.

#### 1993-01-12 — Rowland v. California Men’s Colony, Unit II Men’s Advisory Council

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/rowland-v-california-mens-colony-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding 1 — associations may apply.**

**Holding and operative rule.** An association is not categorically excluded from then-effective §1915: the Dictionary Act’s inclusion of associations within person applies because §1915’s affidavit, financial-inability and counsel provisions can operate through ordinary agency and representation rules. The context exception remains operative, but these features do not establish an incompatible statutory setting or authorize a natural-person-only restriction.

**Authority.** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five votes for this construction and its operative limits. Kennedy’s separate account of permissible workability analysis adds no condition to the Court’s holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Congress’s express definition includes the Council within the class allowed to seek fee relief. United States v. A & P Trucking Co. recognizes that an artificial entity can act through people; Wilson v. Omaha Indian Tribe gives effect to the Dictionary Act while testing the actual statutory context. Here an association can litigate through lawful counsel, establish facts through an authorized agent and lack resources to pay costs. The personal language of an affidavit and poverty does not make those ordinary legal acts impossible. Nor does the permission to request counsel imply that every eligible applicant must be able to appear without counsel. Mallard preserves that permission as discretionary, while Osborn preserves the entity’s representation requirement. The officers’ administrative objections deserve consideration when the court examines a particular application; they do not establish categorical exclusion. Neitzke cautions that efficient docket management cannot substitute for the enacted screening standard. The Court preserves the contextual exception and decides association eligibility, not an entitlement to fee relief or a comprehensive insolvency test for every entity.

**Precedent treatment.**
- United States v. A & P Trucking Co., 358 U.S. 121 (1958): applied for giving statutory entity language effective operation through ordinary legal mechanisms; criminal-liability rules are not imported.
- Wilson v. Omaha Indian Tribe, 442 U.S. 653 (1979): applied for the Dictionary Act’s inclusive default subject to actual context; no requirement of an express contrary definition is created.
- Neitzke v. Williams, 490 U.S. 319 (1989): applied to preserve statutory construction against a policy substitution based on administrative convenience.
- Mallard v. United States District Court, 490 U.S. 296 (1989): preserved; §1915(d) permits a request for counsel and does not compel service.
- Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738 (1824): preserved; an entity’s lawful counsel requirement is separate from financial eligibility.

**Holding 2 — eligibility does not establish the showing.**

**Holding and operative rule.** An eligible association must submit a properly authorized affidavit establishing its actual inability to pay costs or give security and its asserted entitlement to redress; the court may examine legally available resources and sham concealment without automatically attributing every member’s assets to it. Inability to hold an entity bank account or a balance-sheet deficit alone does not compel fee relief, so the Council’s application returns for the actual financial and representation determinations; §1915 neither licenses lay entity advocacy nor guarantees appointed counsel.

**Authority.** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies this remand rule and limitation. The dissenters would require denial because of entity status and do not join the Court’s application process.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Ninth Circuit moved too quickly from eligibility to an unconditional grant. Section 1915(a) makes the affidavit and inability-to-pay showing necessary and gives the court permission to grant relief once the statutory requirements are satisfied. Adkins explains the need to examine financial ability in the applicant’s actual circumstances; its personal necessities-of-life language does not itself define an association’s resources. The Council’s prohibition on maintaining funds may be relevant, but it does not alone resolve whether resources can lawfully be made available for the litigation, including through direct payment. The court must verify the affiant’s authority and assess the showing without simply aggregating every member’s property. Osborn keeps entity representation separate, and Mallard prevents converting a discretionary counsel request into compulsory appointment. Neitzke and Denton preserve lawful screening for frivolous or malicious proceedings and clearly baseless facts. The Court finds neither the Council indigent nor its underlying claim meritorious. It remands those application questions without announcing a universal organizational insolvency formula.

**Precedent treatment.**
- Adkins v. E. I. DuPont de Nemours & Co., 335 U.S. 331 (1948): applied to actual financial inability; its individual-applicant facts do not categorically exclude associations or prescribe a corporate insolvency test.
- Osborn and Mallard: preserved as distinct counsel and representation limits rather than grounds for automatic financial disqualification.
- Neitzke and Denton v. Hernandez (May 4, 1992): preserved for their respective legal and factual screening rules, with Denton’s abuse-of-discretion review and independent correction of legal error unchanged.

Effective January 12, 1993, an association may seek §1915 relief through an authorized affidavit and lawful representation. Courts must decide actual financial inability and the remaining statutory conditions; eligibility does not compel a grant, aggregate every member’s assets, excuse concealment, authorize lay advocacy or compel counsel. The Court adds this eligibility/remand rule to the opening law without changing Neitzke/Denton screening. It does not establish that every insolvent corporation qualifies, adopt a universal organizational means test, resolve the Council’s tobacco-policy claims, or decide a constitutional right to subsidized organizational litigation. The statutory resolution makes the asserted First Amendment avoidance theory unnecessary to decide.

#### 1993-01-13 — Nixon v. United States

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Nixon_v_United_States_merits_1993-01-13.md).

#### The presented committee-evidence challenge is not judicially revisable

**Holding and operative rule:** The Senate’s use of a committee to receive testimony, while the full Senate receives the record, hears argument and retains deliberation and judgment, falls within the impeachment-trial procedure committed to the Senate and is not judicially revisable on this claim. This holding does not decide whether courts may enforce the oath, presidential-trial presiding-officer or two-thirds requirements, or review a purported proceeding that abandons adjudication altogether.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Six of nine Justices adopt this exact ground and its limits; White and Blackmun’s merits ground is noncontrolling, and Souter does not formally join the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this challenge asks the Judiciary to choose the Senate’s evidentiary method rather than enforce an independently prescribed constitutional procedure. Baker requires examination of the particular constitutional commitment and the standards available for judicial decision. Article I gives the Senate sole responsibility to try impeachments but does not require every Senator to receive live testimony. The committee assembled evidence; the full Senate retained deliberation and judgment. Powell enforced qualifications independently fixed by the Constitution and therefore does not authorize appellate supervision of this method. Commerce v. Montana likewise permits review of a defined population constraint, not judicial selection of another branch’s trial procedure. Political importance alone would not foreclose adjudication. Here, however, imposing the ordinary courtroom model would transfer supervisory authority the constitutional text assigns elsewhere. The Court accordingly affirms dismissal without deciding whether the procedure was constitutionally sufficient on the merits, or whether violation of an express command or abandonment of adjudication would present a reviewable claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 — applied through its issue-specific textual-commitment and manageable-standards inquiry; political subject matter alone remains insufficient.
- Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 — distinguished because independently fixed legislative qualifications differ from the evidentiary method left to the Senate.
- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, decision — preserved and distinguished; its apportionment review holding does not create impeachment-procedure review.
- United States v. Muñoz-Flores, 495 U.S. 385 — preserved; interbranch disagreement alone does not bar constitutional review.

Effective January 13, 1993, the Rule XI evidentiary-allocation objection presented here is nonjusticiable. This adds an impeachment-specific application of Baker without a general immunity for politically significant action. The Senate’s procedure receives no controlling merits approval. Judicial enforceability of independently determinate impeachment commands and the consequences of a proceeding abandoning adjudication remain open. White and Blackmun’s contrary reviewability rule and Souter’s exceptional-case formulation are noncontrolling.

#### 1993-01-13 — Bray v. Alexandria Women’s Health Clinic

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Bray_v_Alexandria_Womens_Health_Clinic_merits_1993-01-13.md).

#### Private conspiracy requires purposeful interference with a privately protected right

**Holding and operative rule:** A purely private conspiracy under the deprivation clause of §1985(3) must aim at interfering with a right protected against private as well as official encroachment; incidental effects are insufficient. Clinic obstruction applied to interstate and local patients alike does not establish that interference with interstate travel was a conscious objective merely because substantial numbers of patients crossed state lines.

**Authority:** Scalia Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters. Souter separately agrees with rejection of this theory without a formal join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory purpose requirement must be proved for the particular right invoked. Griffin recognizes that interstate travel may receive protection against private conspiracies, but it does not convert every injury suffered during such travel into a federal civil-rights violation. Scott requires the conspiracy to aim at interference with an independently protected right. Guest similarly distinguishes a robbery of an interstate traveler from a conspiracy directed at travel itself. Here the District Court found that substantial numbers of patients crossed state lines. That finding establishes clientele, not the object of the conspiracy. Respondents conceded that the obstruction did not distinguish interstate patients from local patients, and the demonstrated object was stopping abortions at the clinics. The serious consequences of the blockades cannot supply the missing statutory element. The Court rejects this claim without deciding that an interstate traveler loses protection upon reaching a destination, or that every nondiscriminatory local obstruction can never interfere with interstate passage.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Griffin v. Breckenridge, 403 U.S. 88 — applied; coverage of private conspiracies retains purposeful, invidiously discriminatory deprivation requirements.
- United Brotherhood of Carpenters & Joiners v. Scott, 463 U.S. 825 — applied; a private conspiracy must target a privately protected right.
- United States v. Guest, 383 U.S. 745 — applied by analogy; injury to a traveler differs from purposeful interference with interstate travel.

#### Constitutional abortion liberty does not itself supply the private-right element

**Holding and operative rule:** Constitutional protection of abortion choice against governmental interference does not itself create a right against the purely private obstruction proved here. Without necessary state involvement or another right protected against private encroachment, the deprivation clause cannot sustain this theory merely because the conspiracy deliberately seeks to prevent abortions.

**Authority:** Scalia Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters of this distinct sufficient ground addressing the alternative privacy theory. Souter separately accepts Scott’s private-right limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional source and scope of a right remain separate from the statutory remedy invoked to enforce it. Scott rejected the use of the deprivation clause to protect First Amendment rights against a wholly private conspiracy when the underlying guarantee constrained governmental action. The same distinction governs here. Roe and Casey preserve the protected abortion decision and limit governmental interference; Casey’s complete framework governs enacted duties and their effects on access, purpose, clinical judgment and relief. It does not create a private-action cause of action or change §1985(3)’s elements. The organizers’ deliberate opposition to abortions therefore identifies the object of their conduct without supplying the required independently protected private right. Unlawful blockades remain subject to applicable state law. The Court neither retreats from Casey nor equates unlawful obstruction with protected persuasion. Whether a properly presented conspiracy to hinder state protection meets the separate statutory clause is left unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Scott, 463 U.S. 825 — applied; the underlying constitutional guarantee’s state/private distinction remains.
- Roe v. Wade, 410 U.S. 113 — preserved as carried forward and modified by Casey; no direct private-obstruction cause follows.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, June 29, 1992 — preserved; its six-step state-regulation framework does not change §1985(3)’s elements.
- Great American Federal Savings & Loan Assn. v. Novotny, 442 U.S. 366 — applied; §1985(3) does not itself create the substantive right.

#### Federal merits failure does not retroactively destroy jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** These §1985(3) claims were not wholly insubstantial and frivolous, so their failure on the merits does not remove original federal jurisdiction. Power over the related state claims remains subject to the governing pendent-jurisdiction principles rather than automatic denial because of today’s federal merits ruling.

**Authority:** Scalia Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters. This does not establish entitlement to any particular injunction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unsuccessful federal claim and a jurisdictionally nonexistent claim are different things. Bell v. Hood permits the federal court to decide a substantial asserted federal right even when the plaintiff ultimately fails to establish a cause of action. The clinics’ theory relied on Griffin’s recognition of private conspiracies and on interstate travel; the dispute over its reach was sufficiently substantial to invoke federal jurisdiction. The Court’s rejection of that theory does not retrospectively make the litigation frivolous. Gibbs therefore remains the source of authority for the related state claims in this action, which began before §1367’s enactment. Those claims were actually adjudicated, and petitioners cannot obtain dismissal of the entire action simply by prevailing on the federal merits. Whether the remaining state judgments and the proper exercise of pendent jurisdiction support the injunction’s particular scope requires further consideration below. The Court neither establishes a new jurisdictional basis nor commands retention of every unsuccessful federal case’s state claims.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678 — applied; substantial federal jurisdiction differs from failure on the merits.
- United Mine Workers v. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 — preserved and applied to pendent jurisdiction in this pre-§1367 action.

#### Federal fees fall while independent state relief requires examination

**Holding and operative rule:** The existing §1988 fee-and-cost award cannot stand on the rejected §1985(3) liability and is vacated. The injunction returns for examination of its state trespass/public-nuisance foundations, jurisdiction and lawful scope; federal merits failure alone neither sustains it nor requires its dissolution.

**Authority:** Scalia Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters. Souter agrees to remove the present federal basis but would additionally direct consideration of hindrance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that relief must rest on the legal ground that supports it. The fee-and-cost award rested on success under §1985(3), and that adjudicated basis fails today. Farrar recognizes prevailing-party eligibility when a plaintiff has an enforceable federal nominal-damages judgment, while leaving reasonable amount distinct. These plaintiffs retain no successful deprivation-clause judgment; Farrar’s amount inquiry cannot preserve this award. The result is not a determination that all state-law success in civil-rights litigation can never support fees under any circumstances. The injunction presents a separate issue because the District Court also entered state trespass and public-nuisance judgments. Gibbs prevents treating the federal merits loss as an automatic jurisdictional eraser, but it does not establish that every term of this injunction follows from state law. The lower court must identify the independent authority and permissible scope of continued relief. No replacement fee, new damages award, broadened injunction or predicted result on remand is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Farrar v. Hobby, December 14, 1992 — distinguished; enforceable federal nominal relief differs from reversal of the only adjudicated federal liability basis.
- Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 — applied; pendent adjudication and support for particular relief remain distinct.

#### The unadjudicated hindrance theory is not resolved here

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court does not adjudicate liability under §1985(3)’s separate clause concerning conspiracies to prevent or hinder constituted state authorities from giving or securing equal protection. That theory was not adjudicated below or fairly included in the granted deprivation-clause questions, and no finding establishes the required purpose to hinder state protection; the remand does not direct liability under that theory.

**Authority:** Scalia Part V, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters control scope and mandate. Ultimate substantive requirements of the separate clause remain undecided.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a distinct, undeveloped statutory theory cannot be converted into an adjudicated basis for relief on this review. The lower courts resolved deprivation-clause travel liability and state claims, not a purpose to prevent or hinder state protection. The District Court’s account of demonstrators outnumbering police describes effects and enforcement capacity; it does not itself supply the omitted finding of conspiratorial purpose. Respondents sought an opportunity to develop the theory, while petitioners disputed its pleading and availability. The question-presented limits preserved in Yee and Kamen’s distinction between a claim and additional arguments do not permit resolution of that separate claim here. The Court does not infer that the clause incorporates every requirement of the deprivation clause, nor that it dispenses with them. A later effort to pursue a properly pleaded and supported theory remains subject to ordinary procedural and substantive requirements. No such claim succeeds or fails on its merits today.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido, April 1, 1992 — applied; a distinct claim outside the grant differs from additional argument on a presented claim.
- Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services, Inc., 500 U.S. 90 — applied at that scope; additional legal arguments do not erase claim limits.

The full scope of Griffin’s nonracial class category and whether these blockades show invidious sex-based animus remain unresolved. Scalia Part III has four supporters and no controlling alternative holding. No Marks synthesis is needed because five direct joins support each holding. The Court does not decide whether §1985(3)’s text independently authorizes injunctive relief on a successful federal claim.

Effective January 13, 1993, this private-blockade record fails the deprivation clause’s targeted-private-right requirement. Constitutional abortion liberty does not itself supply that element. Federal liability and its fee basis fail; original jurisdiction survives and independent state relief requires examination. The Court does not decide whether women are a qualifying class or whether opposition to abortion proves or excludes sex-based animus. No controlling hindrance construction, categorical intrastate-obstruction rule or new constitutional abortion standard is adopted.

#### 1993-01-13 — Crosby v. United States

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Crosby_v_United_States_merits_1993-01-13.md).

### Trial cannot begin under the voluntary-absence exception without initial presence

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule43 does not permit a criminal trial to begin in a defendant’s absence merely because that absence is voluntary; its voluntary-absence continuation exception requires that the defendant was initially present when trial began. Crosby’s convictions from such a trial must be set aside and the case remanded for proceedings complying with the rule; no constitutional minimum or change to another enumerated exception is decided.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine participating Justices adopt the rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the rule’s express boundary governs even when a defendant deliberately flees. Rule43 begins with a requirement of presence and limits exceptions to those the rule itself supplies. The continuation provision applies to a defendant initially present who voluntarily absents himself after trial begins. Diaz permits completion of an ongoing trial following such a departure; Taylor applies that same rule without requiring an additional warning that the trial will continue. Neither decision authorizes beginning the trial of an absent defendant. The Government’s burden of rescheduling witnesses and proceedings does not create an additional exception. Initial presence also gives a workable assurance that a later departure abandons a proceeding the defendant knows is under way. The district court’s finding of knowing flight therefore does not save this trial. Reversal requires a lawful proceeding; it does not excuse flight or decide what separate sanctions may be available. Because the rule resolves the case, the Court leaves the constitutional question open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Diaz v. United States, 223 U.S.442 — applied at its actual scope: voluntary departure permits continuation after initial presence, not commencement in absence.
- Taylor v. United States, 414 U.S.17 — applied: no special warning is required for voluntary midtrial absence; its initial-presence premise remains essential.
- United States v. Crosby, 917 F.2d362 — rejected on Rule43: practical burdens and knowing pretrial flight cannot replace the rule’s express commencement condition.

Effective January 13, 1993, the Court settles Rule43’s initial-presence boundary and rejects an implied voluntary-pretrial-flight exception. Other enumerated Rule43 exceptions, independent lawful sanctions for flight and constitutional questions remain unchanged or unaddressed. No constitutional waiver or general harmless-error doctrine is announced.

#### 1993-01-19 — Dobbs v. Zant

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Dobbs_v_Zant_summary_merits_1993-01-19.md).

#### Accurate transcript before law-of-the-case review

**Holding and operative rule:** When a recovered capital-sentencing transcript materially contradicts the factual account supporting an earlier habeas decision, an appellate court may not exclude it without justification and then invoke that exclusion to defeat its own manifest-injustice exception to law of the case. On this record, delay substantially caused by the State's erroneous representations that no transcript existed does not justify exclusion; the transcript must receive consideration through proper further proceedings.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; six of nine participating Justices adopt this rule and the remand. Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment; White would use plenary review.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appellate court must consider the actual argument before deciding whether its earlier ruling rests on a materially mistaken premise. Gardner identifies the importance of a complete record in capital review, and Gregg recognizes Georgia's transcript requirement as a safeguard against arbitrary sentencing. Here both federal courts relied on counsel's account when no transcript was thought available. The recovered transcript calls that account into serious question. The appellate court excluded it without explanation and then treated its absence as preventing application of the manifest-injustice exception. That circular treatment supplies no sufficient procedural justification. The State's erroneous assertions substantially explain the delay, and the District Court recognized Dobbs's legitimate reliance on them. The State's interest in finality does not require preserving an avoidable factual error. Strickland's performance and prejudice requirements remain for application below; the transcript's significance is not itself a finding of ineffective assistance. The Court leaves proper procedural implementation and any assessment of harmlessness to the courts familiar with this record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349 — its plurality's complete-record concern supports this confined correction; no broader plurality proposition becomes a holding by citation.
- Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 — its joint opinion's approval of complete capital-review records supplies the reliability rationale; Georgia's entire sentencing scheme is not reexamined.
- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 — performance and prejudice remain unchanged and undecided here.
- Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, May 4, 1992 — preserved; its Townsend deliberate-bypass rule for inadequate state factual development does not displace the distinct record-correction question or independent claim bars.

Effective January 19, 1993, the described circular exclusion of a material recovered transcript cannot sustain this law-of-the-case refusal where the State substantially caused its delayed discovery. The lower courts retain the ineffective-assistance merits and proper procedural implementation. Neither transcript contradiction nor this remand establishes prejudice.

#### 1993-01-25 — Graham v. Collins

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Graham_v_Collins_merits_1993-01-25.md).

#### The youth and background claims apply law settled before finality

**Holding and operative rule:** Teague does not bar Graham's claim that Texas's submitted sentencing questions prevented effective use of his youth and unstable background as material mitigation independent of deliberateness or future danger. Jurek's conditional approval, Lockett and Eddings dictated that functional requirement before his conviction became final on September 10, 1984; no new procedural-rule exception is created.

**Authority:** Parts I–III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor; five Justices adopt this prefinality-law holding, its application to youth and background, and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that applying an existing protection to these facts does not announce a new rule. Teague and Butler protect reasonable reliance on law at finality; they do not require an earlier case with identical evidence. Jurek approved the Texas scheme on the understanding that relevant mitigation could receive effective consideration. Lockett rejected legal barriers to mitigating aspects of character, record and offense, and Eddings expressly protected youth and disturbed background. Those decisions preceded Graham's final judgment. Penry later applied the same duty when the special issues failed to express a material mitigating judgment; it did not create that duty. The State's reliance on Jurek is substantial, but facial approval subject to effective mitigation cannot authorize its material exclusion in a particular application. Franklin's treatment of prison conduct and Saffle's rejection of unrestricted sympathy do not negate the preexisting requirement. Stringer confirms that a later application need not create new law merely because judges disagree. The Court reaches these claims without invoking either Teague exception.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, and Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407 — applied; the relevant result must be dictated at finality, not merely supported by a later plausible extension.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — preserved as qualified approval; it does not immunize every application of the special issues.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied as the pre-1984 effective-mitigation rule.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied as recognition of that existing rule, not as law inserted retroactively into 1984.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992 — applied at its existing Teague scope; disagreement alone does not establish novelty.
- Franklin v. Lynaugh, 487 U.S. 164, and Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484 — distinguished; evidence whose mitigating use fits the questions, and unrestricted sympathy, differ from material culpability mitigation excluded by them.

#### Youth requires an effective route for its independent mitigating force

**Holding and operative rule:** When the record supports a material mitigating judgment about culpability that the authorized sentencing answers cannot express, a reasonable likelihood that the submitted mechanism prevents that use requires an effective corrective instruction or procedure. Graham's youth at seventeen has that independent significance notwithstanding deliberate conduct or predicted future danger, and the three submitted special issues provide no adequate route for it here.

**Authority:** Parts II and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground for sentencing relief and its conditional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prediction of future violence does not answer every question about a young offender's culpability. Eddings recognizes immaturity, inexperience and undeveloped judgment as material mitigation. A juror could accept those considerations while finding that Graham deliberately killed and might remain dangerous. Jurek's approval therefore does not end the inquiry: the permitted answers must actually allow the material mitigating judgment. The provocation question supplies no route for youth's independent significance either. Texas correctly observes that youth may predict rehabilitation, and the jury could consider that prediction. But permitting one material use does not authorize exclusion of another. Penry confirms this distinction between admitting evidence and allowing its mitigating effect. Boyde's reasonable-likelihood formulation assesses the submitted mechanism; the substantive duty remains the prefinality rule. The Court requires an effective sentencing vehicle, not favorable weight, acquittal or an unrestricted mercy question. It announces no categorical prohibition on executing all seventeen-year-old offenders and does not decide the constitutionality of Texas's later statute.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied to youth's independent culpability significance; jurors may assign weight but cannot be legally prevented from giving it effect.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — its conditional framework remains; this defective application does not establish facial invalidity.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied beyond a permanent-handicap limitation; a mitigating trait need not have only aggravating relevance to future danger to require an effective vehicle.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 — applied as the reasonable-likelihood method of assessing instructional operation, not as the source of a new substantive rule at 1984 finality.

#### The submitted background evidence also requires effective consideration

**Holding and operative rule:** A capital sentencing system may not prevent a jury from acting on the material culpability significance it finds in a defendant's disturbed upbringing merely because that evidence also bears on future dangerousness or lacks a uniquely severe permanent handicap. The evidence of Graham's mother's recurring mental illness and hospitalizations and his shifting family care supports such a mitigating judgment, which the submitted special issues cannot adequately express; this independently requires the same sentencing remedy.

**Authority:** Parts III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor; five Justices adopt this independent background-evidence ground and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the sentencing jury must have a lawful way to act on the material mitigating significance of this upbringing. Eddings protects consideration of disturbed family background, particularly for a young defendant; it does not demand proof of a uniquely severe permanent handicap. Graham's grandmother described his mother's repeated hospitalization and his movement between family caregivers. The jury could find that history reduced his culpability without denying that the killing was deliberate or that future violence was probable. The provocation question does not express that judgment. The State argues that the favorable testimony about his behavior shows no lasting harm and that the evidence can support rehabilitation. Those arguments properly address weight, but cannot establish that every material mitigating use fits the authorized answers. Penry requires an effective means of expressing the supported culpability judgment; admission alone is insufficient. The Court neither finds a psychiatric disability nor requires jurors to infer a particular causal effect. It requires a lawful opportunity for sentencing assessment and leaves the weight to the new sentencer.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 — applied to relevant background proffered as a basis for less than death.
- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied; disturbed upbringing cannot be excluded from mitigating effect as a matter of law.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied; neither a uniquely severe permanent handicap nor complete absence of relevance to the special issues is an exclusive trigger.

#### Positive character as an independent ground

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The separate contention that Graham's household assistance, religious observance and financial support of his children independently require additional sentencing relief is rejected by all nine Justices, on different grounds. Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter regard its asserted independent culpability significance as insubstantial on this record and its future-behavior use as available under the second issue; White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would bar the requested mitigation relief under Teague. Stone-Zsela joins the denial of this independent component in the judgment only. These grounds do not yield a common general rule or a Marks subset; the youth and background holdings independently require sentencing relief.

**Limits:** No universal rule about positive character evidence or its value apart from the asserted culpability and future-danger uses is decided. The jury remains free to hear constitutionally relevant evidence at the new sentencing proceeding. Neither denial of this independent ground nor the sentencing remedy disturbs the murder conviction.

Effective January 25, 1993, Texas's former special-issue mechanism is unconstitutional as applied to the material culpability significance of Graham's youth and disturbed background, and relief on those grounds is not new law under Teague. Lower courts must ask whether supported material mitigating use can actually receive effect, using the reasonable-likelihood instruction inquiry; mere admission or some relevance to future danger is insufficient. This is no categorical fourth-question requirement, entitlement to favorable weight, facial invalidation, or juvenile-execution prohibition. The positive-character component yields no new general controlling rule.

#### 1993-01-25 — Zafiro v. United States

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Zafiro_v_United_States_merits_1993-01-25.md).

### Petitioner-specific omission on appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** When Gloria Zafiro did not pursue her severance denial on appeal and the court of appeals did not decide her personal claim, the Court declines to consider that claim without a supported exceptional reason; her codefendants’ preservation supplies none. Her judgment remains undisturbed on that prudential basis, with no merits rejection, jurisdictional bar or finding of intentional constitutional waiver.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; eight participating Justices adopt the explanation. Stevens agrees in the disposition but does not join the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that review must track each petitioner’s actual appellate claim. Youakim states the ordinary practice against deciding questions neither raised nor resolved below, while allowing exceptions supported by the case. Williams permits review when the court of appeals actually decided an important federal question despite a party’s failure to renew opposition to binding circuit precedent. That circumstance is absent here. The Seventh Circuit considered the severance challenges of Garcia, Soto and Martinez, but addressed a different sufficiency question for Gloria Zafiro. Her trial motion and appeal from conviction do not establish appellate presentation of every possible objection. Nor does considering her testimony as part of Martinez’s defense restore her personal claim. No exceptional reason for doing so is shown. The Court therefore leaves her judgment undisturbed without deciding whether her severance denial was substantively correct. The separate sufficiency question, ineffective assistance and possible collateral proceedings remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Youakim v. Miller,425 U.S.231 — applied: nonreview is ordinary practice, not an inflexible jurisdictional rule.
- United States v. Williams,May4,1992 — distinguished at its stated scope: a passed-upon question may be reviewed; Gloria’s personal claim was not passed upon.
- Yee v. City of Escondido,April1,1992 — applied: an unrelated sufficiency question does not enter the granted severance review.

### Concrete prejudice and effective relief under Rule14

**Holding and operative rule:** For properly joined defendants, mutually antagonistic defenses alone do not require severance; the district court evaluates whether a joint trial presents a serious risk of compromising a specific trial right or preventing a reliable individualized verdict, and chooses effective relief in its sound discretion. Severance is not required for every showing of prejudice if instructions or other safeguards adequately cure the risk, but instructions are not presumed to cure every risk; Garcia, Soto and Martinez have shown no uncured risk requiring separate trials here.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; eight Justices adopt the rule and its application. Stevens’s narrower concurrence supplies no join in this general formulation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the prejudice inquiry concerns the trial’s actual operation, not the label attached to a defense. Richardson recognizes the legitimate benefits of joint trials and the ordinary effectiveness of properly directed instructions. Bruton preserves a different result where a specific confrontation danger cannot be cured in that way. Rule14 likewise reaches serious threats to reliable individual verdicts even without a separate constitutional violation. Relevant risks include prejudicial evidence admissible only against another defendant, complex proof involving markedly different culpability, or essential exculpatory evidence unavailable in a joint trial; those examples do not exhaust the inquiry. Here the petitioners rely on blame shifting rather than a demonstrated impairment of a trial right or reliable assessment. The instructions kept the prosecution’s burden separate for each defendant and distinguished evidence from argument. A better chance of acquittal in isolation is not itself cognizable prejudice. Because these safeguards suffice on this record, denying the three preserved severance motions was within the district court’s discretion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Richardson v. Marsh,481 U.S.200 — applied for joint-trial benefits and the ordinary effectiveness of instructions, not a universal cure.
- Bruton v. United States,391 U.S.123 — preserved: a specific confrontation risk may require exclusion, redaction or severance when instructions are inadequate.
- United States v. Lane,474 U.S.438 — applied only to Rule14’s discretion over effective prejudice relief; no Rule8 misjoinder ruling.
- Schaffer v. United States,362 U.S.511 — applied to adequate individualized jury instructions on this record.

Effective January25,1993, Rule14 receives the stated serious-risk and tailored-relief formulation; neither mutual antagonism nor some prejudice imposes an automatic severance duty. Review remains for abuse of discretion, with independent rights protected. The prudential preservation application leaves Gloria’s personal claim undecided. Rules8 and43, proof beyond reasonable doubt and confrontation law remain distinct and unchanged. No Marks synthesis is required.

#### 1993-01-25 — Spectrum Sports v. McQuillan

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Spectrum_Sports_v_McQuillan_merits_1993-01-25.md).

### Conduct and intent cannot replace dangerous probability

**Holding and operative rule:** Attempted monopolization under Sherman Act §2 requires anticompetitive or predatory conduct, specific intent to monopolize, and a dangerous probability of achieving monopoly power, evaluated through the relevant product and geographic market and the defendant’s economic power there. Conduct may support intent and other permissible evidentiary inferences, but neither conduct nor intent eliminates independent proof of dangerous probability; no categorical small-firm immunity or prohibition of circumstantial proof follows.

**Authority:** White’s opinion of the Court for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine Justices adopt the rule and its explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that injury to a rival cannot stand in for the threatened monopoly that the statute addresses. Swift requires proximity to successful monopolization, not merely an intention to achieve it. Copperweld explains why unilateral competition is governed by that boundary: forceful competitive conduct must not become unlawful simply because it harms another firm. The Ninth Circuit’s Lessig approach permits the jury to infer both intent and dangerous probability from unfair conduct without establishing a market or the defendant’s power within it. That removes an element rather than identifying evidence that satisfies it. The McQuillans may rely on circumstantial evidence, but its force must be evaluated against actual competitive alternatives and economic power. Kodak permits a single-brand market when evidence warrants one; it does not presume power from a proprietary product or a terminated distributorship. The Court therefore rejects the challenged instruction while leaving the relevant market, probability of success and ultimate liability for proper determination below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Swift & Co. v. United States,196 U.S.375 — applied: dangerous probability requires proximity and degree beyond intent alone.
- Copperweld Corp. v. Independence Tube Corp.,467 U.S.752 — applied: unilateral conduct must actually monopolize or dangerously threaten monopolization for these §2 offenses.
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services,June8,1992 — preserved at its stated scope: evidence may establish a single-brand market and power; no presumption replaces separate attempt elements.
- Lessig v. Tidewater Oil Co.,327 F.2d459 — rejected insofar as its conduct-based inference dispenses with market and dangerous-probability proof.

### The undifferentiated verdict requires further proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** The judgment against these petitioners cannot stand on a §2 verdict that may rest solely on an attempted-monopolization instruction dispensing with dangerous-probability proof, when the appellate affirmance rests on that legally erroneous route. Reverse and remand for application of the correct elements and consideration of any properly preserved independent grounds; this decision establishes neither defeat nor success on those grounds and orders no automatic new trial or judgment for petitioners.

**Authority:** White’s unanimous opinion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas adopt the remedial holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ambiguity in this verdict matters because one submitted route was legally defective. The jury’s finding allowed monopolization, attempted monopolization or conspiracy to monopolize, without specifying which it accepted. The court of appeals sustained the judgment on the attempt theory and did not resolve the challenges to independent RICO and state-law grounds. Sunkist Growers supplies the applicable reason for reversal where a general verdict may rest on a legally erroneous route. Griffin’s distinct treatment of inadequate evidence on a legally valid criminal object does not rescue an instruction that removes an element. The Court cannot choose a different ground the jury might have accepted and treat that possibility as an adjudicated basis for affirmance. Nor does correcting the instruction establish that respondents must lose every remaining claim. The appropriate remedy is further proceedings in the court of appeals under the proper elements and whatever preserved independent grounds remain available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sunkist Growers, Inc. v. Winckler & Smith Citrus Products Co.,370 U.S.19 — applied: the possibility of reliance on a legally erroneous submitted ground requires reversal here.
- Griffin v. United States,December3,1991 — distinguished at its stated scope: its factual-insufficiency rule does not validate an element-removing instruction or decide the unreviewed civil claims.

Effective January25,1993, dangerous probability in a relevant product and geographic market remains an independent element, now expressly foreclosing Lessig’s substitute inference. Kodak’s empirical market inquiry and absence of automatic patent or brand power remain controlling. No market-share minimum, competitor-protection tort, small-firm immunity or general supply duty is created. Proof of antitrust injury and other elements of recovery remains necessary under existing law but is not separately resolved in this grant. The remand does not decide RICO, California claims or other parties’ judgments.

#### Lockhart v. Fretwell — 1993-01-25

[Controlling Decision Record](records/Lockhart_v_Fretwell_merits_1993-01-25.md).

#### 1. Loss of a displaced substantive rule is not cognizable prejudice here

**Holding and operative rule:** Counsel's failure to obtain a favorable application of a substantive rule subsequently rejected does not establish Strickland prejudice where the lost benefit is one the governing law does not recognize and the omission otherwise deprived the defendant of no fair or reliable sentencing protection. This limited rule does not replace ordinary reasonable-probability analysis, excuse deficient performance, or require an additional showing of unfairness in the usual ineffective-assistance case.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt the rule and its application to the Collins objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this omitted objection supplies no constitutional ground for setting aside the sentence. Strickland v. Washington measures prejudice through the fairness and reliability of the adversarial proceeding and excludes a favorable result attributable to an erroneous legal standard. Nix v. Whiteside demonstrates that a probable change in outcome is insufficient when the claimed benefit is not legally protected. Lowenfield v. Phelps permits the required narrowing of death eligibility at the guilt stage; the duplication objection accepted in Collins has since been rejected. Counsel's conceded deficiency therefore did not remove a substantive protection to which Fretwell is entitled. Kimmelman v. Morrison preserves ineffective assistance as a distinct claim even when direct habeas review of the underlying search claim is unavailable, but it still requires a meritorious underlying objection. That procedural restriction differs from the displaced substantive rule here. Ordinarily, a reasonable probability of a different result establishes prejudice. The Court does not reopen counsel's conceded deficiency or decide the separate, unresolved penalty-phase preparation claims.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S.668 (1984): applied and clarified for loss of a subsequently rejected substantive rule; ordinary reasonable-probability analysis remains the usual test.
- Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S.157 (1986): applied by analogy for the distinction between a changed outcome and loss of a legally protected benefit; no finding of perjury by Fretwell is made.
- Kimmelman v. Morrison, 477 U.S.365 (1986): distinguished; a procedural bar on direct habeas consideration does not eliminate a meritorious underlying claim, unlike the substantive premise here.
- Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S.231 (1988): applied for constitutionally sufficient narrowing at the guilt stage despite duplication at sentencing.
- Collins v. Lockhart, 754 F.2d258 (8th Cir.1985), and Perry v. Lockhart, 871 F.2d1384 (8th Cir.1989): Collins's displaced double-counting rule supplies no cognizable benefit; Perry's rejection of that rule supplies the accepted litigation premise. Whether Collins could independently be revived is not decided.

#### 2. Teague does not preserve the displaced rule for this prejudice inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** Teague's limitation on new rules supporting collateral relief does not require a federal habeas court to disregard a subsequent legal correction favorable to the State when deciding whether an omitted objection caused cognizable Strickland prejudice. Performance remains assessed from counsel's perspective at the time, while the prejudice inquiry here does not preserve an entitlement to the rejected Collins rule.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this answer to the temporal objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the different purposes of the two inquiries answer the objection based on hindsight. Strickland v. Washington protects counsel from an unreasonable retrospective assessment of professional performance. Deficient performance is conceded, so that protection is not in dispute. The remaining question concerns the constitutional consequence of the omission, not whether competent counsel should have recognized Collins. Teague v. Lane limits collateral disruption of state judgments through newly announced rules; its finality rationale does not give a prisoner a corresponding right to preserve a favorable but erroneous substantive interpretation. A State may therefore invoke the correction relevant to the claimed prejudice. The contrary approach would transform protection against new collateral obligations into an enduring right to a result that present governing law rejects. This conclusion does not eliminate ordinary prejudice analysis or resolve every retroactivity question. The Court has no need to adopt the Solicitor General's separate argument based on the present-custody wording of section 2254(a).

**Precedent treatment:**
- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S.288 (1989): distinguished from an affirmative entitlement to an obsolete rule; its collateral finality limitation is not made reciprocal.
- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S.668 (1984): preserves the contemporaneous perspective for evaluating performance without mechanically fixing the prejudice inquiry to every favorable rule then available.

**Current scope:** Effective January25, 1993, ordinary Strickland remains governing law, with the stated displaced-substantive-rule limitation and Teague distinction. No actual-innocence prerequisite, general harmless-error rule, or broad habeas deference standard is adopted. Sawyer's eligibility gateway, Wright's limited sufficiency result, and the Stringer/Sochor/Espinosa invalid-weighing requirements remain distinct. The Court does not decide that Collins remains valid, the SG's alternative section2254(a) theory, or the unresolved independent assistance claims. No Marks issue arises.

#### Bufferd v. Commissioner — 1993-01-25

**Holding and operative rule.** For an assessment of an S corporation shareholder’s individual income tax, the return that starts the ordinary § 6501(a) limitations period is the shareholder’s return, including applicable extensions and statutory exceptions. Enacted special audit and limitation regimes remain governed by their own terms; later unified audit provisions are not applied to this 1979 liability. No general presumption favoring the Commissioner, ruling on the disputed extension wording, or expansion of assessment time beyond an applicable exception or agreement is adopted. The unanimous rule requires no Marks analysis.

#### United States v. Nachtigal — 1993-02-22

[Controlling Decision Record](records/United_States_v_Nachtigal_summary_merits_1993-02-22.md).

**Holding and operative rule.** An offense carrying no more than six months’ imprisonment is presumptively petty for the constitutional jury-trial right, but the defendant may overcome that presumption by showing that the additional authorized penalties, considered with the maximum incarceration, are so severe that they clearly reflect a legislative judgment that the offense is serious. For this national-park DUI scheme, the six-month imprisonment ceiling, $5,000 fine and alternative probation of up to five years with its authorized conditions do not rebut the presumption; the defendant has no constitutional jury entitlement on that basis.

**Authority.** The per curiam opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas; all nine support both the complete Blanton rule and this application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court applies Blanton’s complete inquiry to the penalties the law authorizes. Blanton makes the maximum prison term the primary indication of seriousness while allowing unusually severe additional penalties to overcome the petty-offense presumption. Congress fixed a six-month ceiling for this offense even though the Secretary promulgated the regulation. The $5,000 fine and alternative probation do not establish the exceptional severity required. Probation may restrict work, residence and movement, require treatment or community service, and include community-facility residence or monitored home confinement; those restrictions differ from imprisonment. Any authorized intermittent custody remains limited by the offense’s imprisonment ceiling. The Court considers those possible conditions, not merely Nachtigal’s actual fine and one year of unsupervised probation. Other States’ jury rules do not replace Congress’s judgment about this federal offense. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary distinction from Blanton therefore fails. This application does not declare that money penalties or probation can never make a six-month offense serious, and it decides no other challenge to the conviction.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Blanton v. North Las Vegas, 489 U.S. 538 (1989), applied: the six-month presumption remains rebuttable after considering additional authorized penalties; the governing legislature’s choices, rather than other jurisdictions’ practices, supply the relevant seriousness judgment.
- United States v. Craner, 652 F.2d 23 (9th Cir. 1981), displaced to the extent its jury-entitlement rule for this federal offense conflicts with Blanton as applied here. Delegated rulemaking does not remove Congress’s enacted imprisonment ceiling from the analysis.
No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. This decision establishes no universal rule that financial sanctions or probation can never overcome the petty-offense presumption.

**Current scope:** Effective February 22, 1993, Blanton controls the national-park DUI penalty scheme at issue. The existing rebuttable test is unchanged; the present holding rejects this scheme as a sufficient rebuttal. The maximum legally authorized sanctions govern rather than the punishment actually selected. The distinction between five years of probation and five years of imprisonment does not erase the need to examine probation conditions. The conditional alternative fine based on gain or victim loss was invoked in respondent’s papers without a record showing either predicate; the Court neither declares that provision inapplicable in every DUI prosecution nor decides whether a differently triggered fine package would be serious. No broader financial-sanction rule, proof burden beyond Blanton’s showing, or holding on another challenge to the conviction follows. The ancillary in forma pauperis grant supplies no new indigence doctrine. There is no Marks issue because the rule has nine direct votes.

#### Growe v. Emison — 1993-02-23

[Controlling Decision Record](records/Growe_v_Emison_merits_1993-02-23.md).

#### 1. Timely state redistricting includes the State's courts

**Holding and operative rule:** When a State's legislature or judiciary is timely undertaking redistricting, a federal court must defer its own redistricting action unless the record shows that the State will not produce a lawful plan in time for the election. The federal court may set a reasonable deadline and retain jurisdiction as a safeguard, but may neither obstruct timely state work nor displace it merely because it prefers another plan; completion of every state appeal before the election is not required.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion on state priority, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt this rule. The separate eight-Justice dismissal direction concerns this completed record, not the general availability of retained jurisdiction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Minnesota's own courts must receive the same opportunity to complete lawful redistricting as its legislature. Scott v. Germano expressly recognized state judicial responsibility and allowed a federal deadline and reserve jurisdiction; Reynolds v. Sims supplies the obligation to correct unequal districts while giving the State an initial opportunity. The federal panel instead directed its deadline solely to the legislature and treated the state court's provisional work as interference. It then relied on delay to which its own injunction had contributed. The state court had adopted its legislative plan and was prepared to act on congressional districts in time for the election. The presence of a federal statutory claim in only one complaint does not change the shared remedial task. Nor does the possibility of an unfinished state appeal justify taking that task away. Federal power remains available for a proved federal defect or an actual failure of timely state action; this decision establishes no universal rule of priority based on filing dates.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Scott v. Germano, 381 U.S.407 (1965): applied to both legislative and congressional redistricting through state courts; its timely-action condition and reserve federal jurisdiction remain.
- Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S.533 (1964): applied for primary state responsibility and the obligation to remedy unconstitutional apportionment; population-equality requirements are unchanged.

#### 2. A superseding state judgment must receive legal effect

**Holding and operative rule:** Once a state court's final judgment replaces the challenged legislative plan, the federal court must give the replacement legal effect rather than treat it as one optional proposal. A challenge to the old plan becomes moot unless it also reaches the superseding plan; allegations against the old plan alone do not establish a violation in the replacement.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion on the live-plan issue, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the stated rule. No party-preclusion rule beyond that necessary legal effect is announced.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the federal court must address the plan that actually governs. The state court's final legislative judgment changed Minnesota law before the federal panel selected its own districts. Section 1738 and the principles explained in Atlantic Coast Line Railroad v. Locomotive Engineers require respect for a state judgment's legal effect; they do not permit federal judges to demote it to an optional map. The earlier allegations concerned the superseded 1983 districts. No party established that those allegations remained a live challenge to the replacement, and the federal panel neither held a hearing nor requested argument addressing a particular replacement plan's asserted statutory defect. Wise v. Lipscomb supplies the related requirement to examine a lawful replacement before imposing judicial relief. A general preference for a larger minority population in one district cannot supply the missing violation. The Court does not decide an unpleaded replacement-plan challenge or immunize the State's work from an appropriately presented federal claim.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co. v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 398 U.S.281 (1970): applied for respect for state judgments and the federal-state judicial relationship; no enlarged preclusion rule adopted.
- Wise v. Lipscomb, 437 U.S.535 (1978): applied for the need to evaluate the legally operative replacement before federal remedial displacement.

#### 3. Fragmentation claims require proof of the causal voting conditions

**Holding and operative rule:** A §2 claim that single-member districts fragment a minority group's potential electoral majority requires proof that the group is sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a majority in a single-member district, is politically cohesive, and faces majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate. Without the required cohesion and majority-bloc evidence, the court may not order a minority district by adding distinct groups' populations; the Court assumes without deciding that a supported coalition claim is permissible and leaves influence-only claims and the population denominator unresolved.

**Authority:** Scalia's statutory opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the stated single-member extension and record application. No fractured-opinion or Marks inference is required.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a remedial district requires proof of dilution, not a demographic assumption. Thornburg v. Gingles identifies the conditions connecting an electoral structure to lost minority opportunity. Size and compactness establish a potential electoral majority; cohesion identifies a common electoral preference; majority bloc voting explains how the challenged arrangement ordinarily defeats that preference. Those reasons apply when single-member boundaries fragment voters just as they apply to their submergence in a multimember district. Gingles reserved that extension, and the Court now adopts it for this claim. Even assuming compactness and the permissibility of combining distinct minority groups, the record contains neither statistical nor anecdotal evidence of the necessary common cohesion or majority bloc voting in Minneapolis. National generalizations cannot replace local proof. The statute's totality inquiry does not dispense with those necessary conditions or guarantee proportional representation. No particular statistical technique is universally prescribed, and the Court does not decide every possible coalition or influence-district theory.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S.30 (1986): extended from multimember dilution to the single-member fragmentation claim at issue; its causal prerequisites and totality inquiry remain distinct, and its influence-only reservation remains open.

**Current scope:** State judicial redistricting receives the same timely opportunity as state legislative work. Federal reserve jurisdiction remains available under the stated conditions, although this action must be dismissed. A superseding plan must be tested on a properly presented claim concerning that plan. Gingles's three causal prerequisites now expressly apply to this fragmentation claim, with totality still required. Coalition cognizability, influence-only claims and total-population versus voting-age-population measurement remain unresolved. Other asserted errors are not reached.

#### Itel Containers International Corp. v. Huddleston — 1993-02-23

[Controlling Decision Record](records/Itel_Containers_International_Corp_v_Huddleston_merits_1993-02-23.md).

#### 1. The Container Conventions exempt importation charges, not every container transaction

**Holding and operative rule:** The 1956 and 1972 Container Conventions prohibit direct or indirect taxes imposed by reason of, or in connection with, importation of qualifying temporarily admitted containers, rather than every tax involving those containers. Tennessee's nondiscriminatory tax on the transfer of possession under a local lease is not such an importation charge; the 1972 definition also excludes fees and charges limited to the approximate cost of services rendered.

**Authority:** Kennedy's treaty portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas: eight Justices adopt this construction and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the reason for imposing the tax controls, not the historical reason why the container is present in Tennessee. Air France v. Saks directs attention first to treaty text. Both Conventions connect the exemption to importation, and removing that connection would make their limiting words ineffective. Qualifying containers receive temporary admission subject to reexportation within three months unless customs authorities extend the period; the protection does not establish permanent tax immunity for their commercial use. Itel's lease proceeds are taxed because possession changes in Tennessee, without discrimination between domestic and foreign goods. Other nations' practices deserve attention under Factor v. Laubenheimer, but their use of different tax systems does not establish the asserted exemption. Direct and indirect taxes alike are prohibited when they attach to importation; neither form is prohibited merely because a container contributes to taxable commercial value. The Executive's supporting interpretation is persuasive support, not a substitute for construing the Conventions.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S.392 (1985): applied for treaty interpretation beginning with text while considering relevant context.
- Factor v. Laubenheimer, 290 U.S.276 (1933): applied for consideration of treaty parties' practical construction; the practice asserted here does not displace the operative importation limitation.

#### 2. Federal container regulation does not preempt this local lease tax

**Holding and operative rule:** The Container Conventions, 19 U.S.C.1322 and their implementing regulations do not occupy all container taxation or preempt a nondiscriminatory local lease tax that neither taxes importation nor obstructs the federal temporary-admission policy. Tennessee's tax therefore survives both the asserted field-preemption and obstacle-preemption grounds; the distinct federal rules governing bonded warehouses do not confer the same exemption on these lease transactions.

**Authority:** Kennedy's regulatory-preemption portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas: eight Justices adopt the stated rejection of both grounds.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the scope of the federal arrangement must be identified before state taxation can be displaced. Hines v. Davidowitz supplies the obstacle inquiry, but the pertinent federal objective is protected temporary admission of containers, not freedom from every ordinary local tax. McGoldrick and Xerox involved a different arrangement: goods held under continuous customs supervision for reexport, with duty treatment intended to encourage use of American facilities. Taxing those goods could defeat that particular benefit. The container rules instead concern admission, certification and specified customs treatment, and Tennessee's local lease tax leaves those protections intact. Nor does extensive federal regulation alone establish occupation of the entire field. R. J. Reynolds rejected that inference even for the more detailed warehouse regime. The Court rejects both the alleged obstacle and the asserted complete occupation without denying that another tax could conflict with federal law. Nondiscrimination is relevant here but does not by itself answer every preemption question.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S.52 (1941): applied for conflict with federal purposes; no such obstacle established here.
- McGoldrick v. Gulf Oil Corp., 309 U.S.414 (1940): distinguished because the bonded-warehouse benefit and supervision differ from container leasing.
- Xerox Corp. v. County of Harris, 459 U.S.145 (1982): distinguished on the same specific federal warehouse purpose; its protection remains.
- R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Durham County, 479 U.S.130 (1986): applied against inferring complete field occupation from detailed customs regulation.

#### 3. The local lease tax satisfies the additional foreign-commerce requirements

**Holding and operative rule:** In addition to Complete Auto's substantial nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services, a tax affecting foreign commerce must avoid a substantial risk of international multiple taxation and interference with the Nation's ability to speak with one voice. With Complete Auto compliance conceded here, Tennessee's discrete local transaction and credit for tax properly paid to another domestic or foreign jurisdiction on that same transaction, together with the demonstrated federal arrangement, satisfy those additional requirements; Executive support informs but does not decide their application.

**Authority:** Kennedy's foreign-commerce portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas: seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia agrees only in the judgment on this issue; Blackmun dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that potential foreign taxing authority alone does not make a local transaction constitutionally untaxable. Japan Line supplies separate multiple-taxation and national-uniformity inquiries; domestic ownership does not eliminate them. Container Corp. distinguishes unavoidable overlapping taxation from the mere possibility that another sovereign could tax, while Wardair recognizes the significance of a discrete local transaction. Tennessee taxes delivery under the lease and credits tax properly paid elsewhere on the same event, reducing the relevant duplication risk. Itel accepts the lower court's Complete Auto determination, so the Court does not independently reconsider those four requirements. The tax also fits the actual treaty, statutory and regulatory arrangement and does not conflict with a demonstrated international practice requiring exemption of this transaction. The United States' submission supports that conclusion but cannot authorize an otherwise unconstitutional tax. The absence of a demonstrated foreign conflict supports this application; it does not reduce the one-voice inquiry to express statutory preemption or dispense with apportionment in another case.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S.274 (1977): its four requirements remain mandatory; their satisfaction is conceded in this case.
- Japan Line, Ltd. v. County of Los Angeles, 441 U.S.434 (1979): retained and distinguished; foreign-owned property taxed fully abroad presented a different multiple-taxation and uniformity problem.
- Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S.159 (1983): applied to distinguish inevitable duplication from potential foreign taxation and to assess foreign-policy evidence.
- Wardair Canada Inc. v. Florida Department of Revenue, 477 U.S.1 (1986): applied for the significance of a discrete local transaction and the actual federal arrangement.

#### 4. A local lease transaction is not the import or export taxed in Richfield

**Holding and operative rule:** Under Michelin, this nondiscriminatory local lease tax does not offend national commercial uniformity, divert federal import revenue or disrupt interstate harmony, and therefore does not violate the Import-Export Clause. The tax attaches to temporary possession transferred in Tennessee, rather than to the containers or their cargo as imports or exports in transit; the Court leaves unresolved whether Michelin otherwise altered Richfield's rule for direct taxation of goods in transit.

**Authority:** Kennedy's Import-Export portion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas: seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia concurs only in this component's judgment on a distinct export-status ground; Blackmun does not supply a separate Import-Export disposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Import-Export Clause requires its own analysis even when the Commerce Clause challenge fails. Michelin identifies protection of national commercial policy, federal import revenue and harmony among States. The national-policy inquiry is satisfied for the reasons already stated, and Washington Stevedoring explains why a properly connected, apportioned and evenhanded charge for local commercial activity respects interstate harmony. The revenue concern also fails here because Tennessee collects revenue from a local transfer of possession, not from importing goods. Richfield involved a direct tax upon the export transaction in goods already committed to export. This lease tax falls on a distinct service and its proceeds. The fact that a container will carry goods abroad does not transform every earlier local commercial event into an import duty or export impost. The Court need not decide how Michelin affects other direct taxes on goods in transit, and it neither overrules Richfield nor grants a general exemption from constitutional review.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Michelin Tire Corp. v. Wages, 423 U.S.276 (1976): applied through its three constitutional concerns.
- Department of Revenue v. Association of Washington Stevedoring Cos., 435 U.S.734 (1978): applied for distinct local services and the interstate-harmony inquiry.
- Richfield Oil Corp. v. State Board of Equalization, 329 U.S.69 (1946): distinguished; its direct-export rule is not overruled or finally reconciled with every implication of Michelin.

**Current scope:** The four source-specific holdings above govern this transaction effective February23, 1993. No test is abandoned: Complete Auto remains mandatory, Japan Line's two additional inquiries remain independent, federal preemption remains distinct from constitutional review, and Michelin's three concerns govern the Import-Export holding. Before this decision those general rules and the limited temporary-admission regime existed; this decision adds their controlling construction and application to a nondiscriminatory domestic container-lease transaction. Neither domestic ownership nor Executive support eliminates the foreign-commerce inquiry. No general local-tax immunity, apportionment exemption, express-conflict-only rule or overruling of Richfield follows. No Marks analysis is needed because every holding has a direct majority.

#### Fex v. Michigan — 1993-02-23

[Controlling Decision Record](records/Fex_v_Michigan_merits_1993-02-23.md).

### Article III(a): actual receipt starts the trial period

**Holding and operative rule.** When a prisoner invokes Article III while serving a term of imprisonment and an untried charge in another participating State is the subject of a lodged detainer, the 180-day trial period begins only when the required written notice and request for final disposition have actually reached both the prosecuting officer and the appropriate court in that State. Delivery to the custodian alone does not start it; a necessary or reasonable continuance may be allowed for good cause shown in open court with the prisoner or counsel present, Article VI(a) tolls the period while the prisoner is unable to stand trial as determined by the court having jurisdiction, and Article V(c) requires dismissal with prejudice when the applicable period is violated.

**Authority.** Scalia's opinion, joined by White, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six votes for this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the compact starts the prosecution period on receipt by the officials who must act on the request. Article III(a) names the prosecutor and court as the recipients; Article III(b) separately makes the custodian responsible for forwarding the papers and accompanying certificate. Requiring a return receipt reinforces a definite, verifiable starting point. Carchman v. Nash supplies the treatment of this congressionally approved compact as federal law and requires attention to its operative terms. Houston v. Lack protects a prisoner's own appeal filing upon delivery to prison authorities; it does not determine when another jurisdiction's period for bringing a prosecution begins. The contrary interpretation addresses a serious risk of official delay, but it could consume the receiving State's trial period before that State receives the request. Article IX's liberal-construction direction does not eliminate the named recipients. Because trial began 177 days after both recipients received Fex's papers, Article III(a) does not require dismissal. The Court leaves any separate notice violation, independent constitutional remedy, and the availability of a continuance first sought after expiration unresolved.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Carchman v. Nash, 473 U.S. 716 (1985): applied for the compact’s federal-law character and attention to operative language; its exclusion of probation-violation detainers remains intact.
- Houston v. Lack, 487 U.S. 266 (1988): distinguished because a prisoner’s own appeal-filing deadline differs from commencement of the receiving State’s prosecution period; its mailbox rule remains in force within its setting.

**Current scope:** Effective February 23, 1993, Article III(a)'s 180-day period starts on actual receipt by both prosecutor and court. The custodian's prompt-forwarding duty and the Article III(c) prompt-notice duty remain obligations; a delay in performing either is not converted into compliance by this trigger holding. No automatic grace period, bad-faith prerequisite, constitutional speedy-trial rule, damages remedy, or remedial rule for Article III(c) is adopted. Article III(a)'s good-cause continuance and Article VI(a)'s judicially determined inability-to-stand-trial exclusion retain their separate terms. No earlier Supreme Court decision had resolved this exact receipt trigger, and none is overruled.

#### United States v. Dunnigan — 1993-02-23

[Controlling Decision Record](records/United_States_v_Dunnigan_merits_1993-02-23.md).

#### Constitutional permission and the Guideline’s consequence

**Holding and operative rule.** A sentence increase under U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 for independently established trial perjury—willfully false testimony under oath or affirmation about a material matter—does not violate the defendant’s right to testify. Once the court properly determines that the defendant committed such perjury, the applicable Guideline requires its two-level obstruction adjustment; neither exercising the trial right nor a guilty verdict alone supplies that predicate.

**Authority.** Kennedy’s opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas; all nine Justices adopt this proposition, its explanation and its record application, so it controls directly.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the right to testify does not protect deliberate material falsehood under oath. Nix v. Whiteside supplies that boundary: a defendant’s opportunity to present a defense does not include a right to perjury. United States v. Grayson permits sentencing courts to consider a defendant’s willful false testimony while warning against a reflex increase whenever a testifying defendant is convicted. Section 3C1.1 respects that distinction when the court independently finds perjury. The Fourth Circuit’s reliance on the Guidelines’ reduced emphasis on rehabilitation does not justify a categorical exemption, because intentional corruption of the trial process also bears on retribution and incapacitation. Nor does the mandatory consequence erase the factual prerequisites that make the increase lawful. A defendant may testify inaccurately through confusion or faulty memory, or truthfully advance a defense the jury rejects, without committing perjury. The Court rejects the constitutional prohibition here while preserving those limits; it does not require a separate perjury prosecution or decide a later sentencing framework.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S. 157 (1986), applied to the right’s boundary: the right to present testimony includes no entitlement to perjury; the decision does not authorize assuming falsehood from an unsuccessful defense.
- United States v. Grayson, 438 U.S. 41 (1978), applied: actual willful trial falsehood may inform sentencing; its rehabilitation discussion is not an exclusive justification and its warning against reflex increases remains operative.

#### Perjury predicates, independent findings and application

**Holding and operative rule.** When a defendant objects to a § 3C1.1 increase based on trial testimony, the court must review the evidence and independently find willfully false sworn testimony concerning a material matter, excluding confusion, mistake and faulty memory. Separate clear findings for each element are preferable, but a single determination encompassing all the factual predicates is sufficient; the district court’s findings here meet that standard.

**Authority.** Kennedy’s opinion, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas; all nine Justices adopt this proposition, its explanation and its record application, so it controls directly.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court requires a finding of perjury, not merely a finding that the defendant lost at trial. Section 1621 supplies the established elements of sworn, willfully false testimony about a material matter. Grayson’s caution against reflex sentencing requires the judge to examine the evidence rather than treat the guilty verdict as the missing proof of those elements. Separate findings on falsity, materiality and intent are preferable because they make the basis for review clear. A combined finding is nevertheless sufficient when it actually encompasses each predicate. Here the district court reviewed the evidence, identified Dunnigan’s denials of participation, and found untruthful testimony on material matters designed substantially to affect the outcome. That determination encompasses intentional material falsehood; its reference to the verdict does not reduce it to the verdict alone. Respondent’s contrary reading overlooks the judge’s own assessment and the purpose attributed to the testimony. The Court requires no special verbal formula and leaves confusion, mistake and faulty memory outside perjury.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Grayson, 438 U.S. 41 (1978), applied with its safeguard intact: a sentencing response requires an actual assessment of deliberate false testimony, not an automatic consequence of conviction.
- Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S. 157 (1986), left intact in its factual boundary: absence of a protected right to perjury does not establish that disputed testimony was perjurious.

**Current scope:** Effective February 23, 1993, § 3C1.1 constitutionally requires the two-level adjustment once trial perjury is properly established. The reusable finding standard requires independent review after an objection and all elements of sworn material falsehood and willful intent. A combined finding may suffice; separate findings are preferable, not indispensable. Before this decision Grayson allowed consideration of actual perjury in discretionary sentencing and Nix denied a right to commit perjury; this decision rejects a categorical exception for the mandatory Guidelines and supplies the required finding rule. Honest mistake, confusion or faulty memory, an unsuccessful but truthful defense, and the verdict alone do not establish perjury. No separate prosecution is required. The Court selects no new general proof quantum, announces no rule governing all commentary, and imports no later jury-finding doctrine. Both holdings have a direct nine-Justice coalition; no Marks analysis is needed.

#### Negonsott v. Samuels — 1993-02-24

[Controlling Decision Record](records/Negonsott_v_Samuels_merits_1993-02-24.md).

### Kansas-specific concurrent authority over state offenses

**Holding and operative rule.** Under 18 U.S.C. §3243, Kansas may prosecute offenses committed by or against Indians on Indian reservations, including trust or restricted allotments, within Kansas to the same extent its courts may prosecute offenses elsewhere in the State under state law, even when the conduct also violates the Indian Major Crimes Act. Federal courts retain their jurisdiction over federally defined offenses; this concurrent authority over conduct does not authorize Kansas courts to adjudicate federal criminal charges.

**Authority.** White's unanimous opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, controls the grant, the saving clause and the disposition.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Kansas Act gives effect to both state and federal authority. Its first sentence grants Kansas jurisdiction over offenses involving Indians on reservations without excluding the major offenses. Its second sentence preserves the jurisdiction of federal courts over offenses defined by federal law. A single act may violate both bodies of law without allowing a state court to try a federal charge. Reading the second sentence to preserve exclusive federal authority over the conduct would withdraw a substantial part of the first sentence's unqualified grant. The otherwise exclusive operation of the Indian Major Crimes Act therefore does not defeat this specific congressional authorization. County of Yakima illustrates the need to identify the precise state power Congress authorized; it supplies no general state power over Indian country. Here Congress supplied the necessary authorization expressly. Because the text resolves the allocation, the canon favoring Indians in cases of statutory ambiguity does not change the result. Kansas could prosecute Negonsott for aggravated battery. The Court decides neither the independent scope of tribal adjudicatory authority nor any question of successive prosecutions.

**Precedent treatment.**

- County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation (January 14, 1992): its requirement of specific congressional authorization is applied at that level; its land-tax holding is neither converted into criminal jurisdiction nor enlarged.
- No earlier decision is overruled. The ordinary federal allocation under the Indian Major Crimes Act remains subject to this express Kansas-specific enactment.

**Current scope:** Effective February 24, 1993, the Kansas Act authorizes state-law prosecutions within its described Indian-reservation and allotment coverage even when the conduct also constitutes a listed federal major crime. Federal jurisdiction over federal offenses is preserved. The grant derives from this enactment, not Kansas's general police power or a general theory of diminished tribal sovereignty. The decision grants no corresponding power to another State, adjudicates no extinction of tribal authority, and resolves no double-jeopardy or successive-prosecution issue.

#### United States v. A Parcel of Land Known as 92 Buena Vista Avenue — 1993-02-24

[Controlling Decision Record](records/United_States_v_92_Buena_Vista_Avenue_merits_1993-02-24.md).

### The innocent-owner defense is available to an owner who received a gift

**Holding and operative rule.** An actual owner is not excluded from §881(a)(6)'s innocent-owner defense merely because the owner acquired the interest by gift rather than purchase for value. To prevent forfeiture to the extent of that interest, the owner must establish that the relevant act or omission occurred without the owner's knowledge or consent as the statute requires; eligibility does not establish that showing or decide its precise temporal measure.

**Authority.** Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter, supplies five votes for this proposition; Scalia and Thomas also accept donee eligibility in their judgment concurrence. The Court opinion independently controls.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statutory word owner includes a person who receives property as a gift. Congress protected an owner's interest without limiting the protection to purchasers for value. Russello v. United States instructs courts to respect differences in language Congress chose for forfeiture provisions; a restriction stated in one provision cannot simply be inserted into another. The criminal-forfeiture provision considered in Caplin & Drysdale contains a distinct scheme for third-party transferees, including an express purchaser qualification, and does not supply that missing term here. Goodwin holds title to the residence, but title and the alleged source of the purchase money do not establish innocence. She must prove the absence of knowledge or consent required by §881(a)(6). The Government's relation-back argument presents a separate question about when its title becomes effective; it does not rewrite the defense as a purchaser-only protection. The Court therefore affirms eligibility and leaves the claimant's proof, the precise temporal knowledge rule, and any unpresented constitutional protection unresolved.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S.16 (1983): applied to respect Congress’s different property-forfeiture language; it does not itself decide the claimant’s knowledge or establish an innocent-owner finding.
- Caplin & Drysdale, Chartered v. United States, 491 U.S.617 (1989): distinguished because its criminal-forfeiture and third-party-transfer provisions differ; its attorney-fee and criminal-forfeiture holdings remain intact.

### Relation back operates after the court determines that forfeiture is authorized

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 881(h) is not a self-executing adjudication that eliminates an asserted §881(a)(6) innocent-owner defense before forfeiture is determined. The claimant may establish that defense first; if the property or the relevant interest is lawfully forfeitable, the resulting governmental title relates back to the act giving rise to forfeiture, subject to the statutory protection of any interest for which the defense succeeds.

**Authority.** Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter, controls with five votes. Scalia and Thomas concur in this practical result on their separately stated relation-back analysis; no narrower-ground inference is needed.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the relation-back provision must operate with the defense Congress placed in the same forfeiture scheme. Section 881(h) refers to property described in subsection (a), and subsection (a)(6) excludes an owner's protected interest when that owner establishes the statutory defense. The Government cannot establish that an interest is forfeitable by assuming away the defense that determines whether it may be forfeited. United States v. Stowell explains the traditional operation of relation back: condemnation perfects a title that then reaches back to the forfeiture-producing act. That mechanism does not dispense with the determination authorizing forfeiture. Caplin & Drysdale enforces relation back within a different criminal-forfeiture scheme; it does not erase this civil provision's express owner protection. Goodwin therefore may attempt to prove her defense before governmental title is perfected against her interest. If the defense fails and forfeiture is authorized, relation back remains effective. The Court leaves knowledge timing, any properly raised equitable bar, and the factual entitlement to forfeiture for appropriate further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- United States v. Stowell, 133 U.S.1 (1890): applied for the requirement of condemnation to perfect forfeiture title and its retrospective effect; relation back survives but does not bypass the statutory defense.
- Caplin & Drysdale, Chartered v. United States, 491 U.S.617 (1989): distinguished as a different statutory scheme; no weakening of valid relation back after authorized forfeiture follows.

**Current scope:** Effective February 24, 1993, a donee with an ownership interest may invoke §881(a)(6), and §881(h) does not defeat that defense before the court determines forfeiture. Both rules have an Opinion of the Court with five joins, with two additional judgment concurrences. The claimant bears the statutory showing as to absence of knowledge or consent; this decision does not resolve the precise temporal knowledge standard, choose a general construction of knowledge versus consent, decide source tracing or actual innocence, resolve an unpresented estoppel issue, or announce a constitutional innocent-owner guarantee. Section 881(a)(7)'s separate real-property defense is not construed. The 1984 enactment of §881(h) postdates the 1982 acquisition, but the defense opportunity also exists under traditional relation back, so no retroactivity holding is needed.

#### Voinovich v. Quilter — 1993-03-02

[Controlling Decision Record](records/Voinovich_v_Quilter_merits_1993-03-02.md).

#### H1. Section 2 does not make a State prove remedial necessity before choosing majority-minority districts

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 2 does not prohibit a State from choosing majority-minority districts merely because no federal violation compels their creation; challengers bear at least the initial burden of showing, under the totality of circumstances, unequal electoral opportunity caused by the challenged arrangement. A federal court may order such districts only when their creation is necessary to remedy an established federal-law violation, but that limit does not become a categorical restriction on state redistricting authority; independently applicable federal constitutional and statutory requirements remain enforceable.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part II.B, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt the complete proposition; it directly controls the rejection of the lower court's statutory prohibition and burden allocation.

**Controlling explanation:** Section 2 protects equal electoral opportunity rather than a preferred distribution of minority voters among districts. Gingles requires challengers to demonstrate unequal access under the totality of circumstances; it does not declare a particular district form inherently unlawful. Concentrating minority voters can improve their ability to elect preferred candidates, diminish that ability, or leave it unchanged. The consequences require proof. The District Court instead required Ohio to demonstrate a prior statutory violation before creating majority-minority districts. That reverses the statute's initial allocation of the burden and mistakes an absence of federal compulsion for a federal prohibition. Growe recognizes the State's primary responsibility for apportionment and limits federal judicial intervention to an established federal defect. A State derives its districting authority independently of a federal court's remedial power. Its choice remains subject to federal requirements, but Section 2 supplies no categorical ban here. The Court does not decide the distinct constitutional challenge to conscious racial classification, which was neither presented below nor raised in this appeal.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): followed for the challengers' burden and the totality-of-circumstances inquiry; no district form is automatically unlawful under Section 2.
- Growe v. Emison, simulated decision of February 23, 1993: applied to distinguish the State's primary districting authority from federal judicial remedial authority; its actual majority holdings remain controlling.

#### H2. The assumed influence-dilution claim fails for lack of the required majority bloc voting

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming that Section 2 reaches the asserted loss of minority influence districts, the claim fails because challengers did not establish sufficiently cohesive white majority voting usually to defeat the minority's preferred candidates. The Gingles prerequisites apply with attention to the claim's nature: an influence claim would require modification or elimination of the first prerequisite that the minority could constitute a district majority, but the Court need not select that adaptation or decide whether influence claims are cognizable because the independently necessary bloc-voting showing is absent.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Parts II.A and II.C, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt this independently dispositive rejection of the presented Section 2 claim and each stated reservation.

**Controlling explanation:** Packing can impair electoral opportunity, just as dispersing a cohesive minority can. Gingles recognizes both possible mechanisms, and Growe applies its threshold framework to challenges involving single-member districts. Here, however, challengers seek additional districts in which black voters would be an influential minority relying on crossover votes, rather than districts in which they could themselves form a majority. The Court assumes this theory is cognizable and does not mechanically demand satisfaction of a majority-size prerequisite inconsistent with that assumption. Whether that first prerequisite should be modified or eliminated can remain open. The third prerequisite independently defeats the claim: the District Court found an absence of racially polarized voting, and challengers accept that finding. Without sufficiently strong white bloc voting to frustrate the minority's preferred candidates, this record does not establish the unequal opportunity asserted. The lower court cannot replace that missing causal proof with the bare observation that creating majority-minority districts removes black voters from other districts. Neither all influence claims nor all packing claims are rejected.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): applied to the necessity of majority bloc voting and the causal mechanism of dilution; its reservations about influence claims remain unresolved.
- Growe v. Emison, simulated decision of February 23, 1993: followed for application of the threshold framework to single-member district challenges, with its influence-claim reservation preserved rather than silently decided.

#### H3. The particular finding of intentional discrimination is clearly erroneous

**Holding and operative rule:** Assuming that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted vote-dilution claim, the finding that Ohio intentionally injured black voters cannot stand on this record: attention to perceived federal-law requirements and possession of an opponent's speculative documents, without evidence of reliance, do not establish the prohibited purpose the District Court inferred. The Court reverses that finding as clearly erroneous without deciding the Amendment's coverage of dilution or its relationship to race-conscious districting generally.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt this record-specific constitutional disposition; none adopts a general immunity for political objectives or racial awareness.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court assumes that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted dilution claim. Beer v. United States leaves that coverage question unresolved, and this case does not require an answer. The asserted violation depends on the intentional injury to black voters that the District Court found. Neither cited piece of evidence supports that inference. Tilling's decision to honor federal requirements when he perceived a conflict with state law demonstrates attention to the Supremacy Clause, not a purpose to abridge minority voting strength. The documents he possessed were an opponent's speculation about possible partisan strategies; they were not his adopted instructions, and no evidence shows that he relied on them. The affirmative record instead shows participation by minority organizations and incorporation of substantial features of the Ohio NAACP's proposal. Possible political objectives do not eliminate the need for evidence connecting those objectives to prohibited racial injury. On this record the finding is clearly erroneous. The Court expresses no broader view on race-conscious districting or the unpresented racial-classification claim.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Beer v. United States, 425 U.S. 130 (1976): followed in leaving unresolved whether the Fifteenth Amendment reaches vote-dilution claims; the assumption made here creates no new coverage holding.
- Mobile v. Bolden, 446 U.S. 55 (1980): its plurality and separate writings are background to the alleged intentional-discrimination claim, not treated as a single majority rationale or a new categorical rule governing every race-conscious map.

#### H4. Population deviations above ten percent require a genuine justification inquiry

**Holding and operative rule:** A state legislative plan whose maximum total population deviation exceeds ten percent establishes a prima facie case requiring the State to justify the disparity; crossing that threshold does not make preservation of county boundaries legally unavailable as a justification. The District Court must determine whether the actual plan reasonably advances that rational state policy, including its substantiated connection to the deviations and consistency of implementation, and whether the resulting disparities nevertheless exceed constitutional limits; the Court does not decide that Ohio has met its burden.

**Authority:** O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt this standard and the population-only remand; neither the State's success nor a particular remedial map receives a controlling vote.

**Controlling explanation:** Equal protection requires substantially equal population in state legislative districts, but it does not demand mathematical identity in every district. Brown v. Thomson explains that deviations below ten percent are generally minor, while larger disparities establish a prima facie case requiring state justification. Ohio's plan crosses that threshold, so its officials must substantiate their explanation. The District Court nevertheless erred by treating county preservation as legally incapable of justifying deviations above ten percent. Mahan v. Howell permits a rational policy preserving political subdivisions to justify some disparities; Brown preserves both the need for a reasonable connection between plan and policy and an independent constitutional limit on the resulting inequality. Invoking county lines by name does not establish that connection. The inquiry concerns the actual plan, the consistency of the policy's application, and the magnitude of the departures it produces. The record has not received that analysis under the proper standard. The Court therefore remands this issue alone, without approving the deviations or ordering any particular racial arrangement.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Brown v. Thomson, 462 U.S. 835 (1983): followed for the prima facie threshold, the required connection to a rational state policy, and the separate constitutional limit; departures below ten percent remain generally minor, not categorically immune.
- Mahan v. Howell, 410 U.S. 315 (1973): followed in allowing a substantiated political-subdivision policy to justify departures above ten percent; it does not automatically validate Ohio's particular departures.

**Current scope:** Effective March 2, 1993, the four holdings above govern. Section 2 requires proof of the challenged arrangement's unequal electoral consequences; a State need not first demonstrate a remedial federal violation to choose majority-minority districts. The assumed influence claim fails for absent majority bloc voting while cognizability and adaptation of the first Gingles prerequisite remain open. The purpose ruling is confined to the deficient evidence in this record. Population departures above ten percent require substantiated state justification under Brown and Mahan; neither automatic invalidity nor automatic approval follows. These are source-specific applications and clarifications of the inherited rules, not a replacement districting framework. Growe's effective holdings remain intact; its dismissal mandate addressed its exhausted record and does not dismiss Ohio's independently live population claim. No decision is made about the distinct racial-classification claim, Fifteenth Amendment dilution coverage or race-conscious districting generally. No later Shaw rule enters. No precedent is overruled.

#### Leatherman v. Tarrant County Narcotics Intelligence and Coordination Unit — 1993-03-03

Source: [Decision Record](records/leatherman-v-tarrant-county-merits-1993-03-03.md).

### The Federal Rules supply the pleading standard for municipal § 1983 claims

**Holding and operative rule:** A federal court may not impose a judge-created heightened factual-particularity requirement on a § 1983 complaint against a municipality beyond the then-operative Federal Rules. Rule 8 governs the complaint, Rule 9(b)'s fraud-or-mistake categories do not create a municipal-liability category, and Monell and Canton retain their separate substantive requirements.

**Authority:** Stone's Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; unanimous controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that litigation expense cannot amend the Federal Rules. Conley v. Gibson explains that Rule 8 requires fair notice of the claim and its grounds rather than detailed factual proof in the complaint. Rule 9(b) expressly requires particularity for fraud and mistake, but does not identify municipal civil-rights claims as another category. Monell v. Department of Social Services requires municipal policy or custom causing the constitutional injury; it does not turn the absence of employer-based liability into immunity from suit. Owen v. City of Independence rejects municipal qualified immunity and therefore defeats the asserted immunity justification for demanding more particulars. City of Canton v. Harris preserves the substantive failure-to-train requirements without supplying a different pleading rule. The court of appeals affirmed these dismissals because the complaints did not meet its special standard. That basis cannot stand. Summary judgment, Rule 11 and lawful control of discovery remain available within their own requirements. The Court leaves individual-officer immunity pleading, collateral estoppel and the unreviewed alternative summary-judgment issues undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41 (1957): applied for fair-notice pleading; no subsequent plausibility rule is imported.
- Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978): preserved; municipal policy/custom and causation remain necessary, while freedom from employer-based liability supplies no immunity from suit.
- Owen v. City of Independence, 445 U.S. 622 (1980): applied to reject municipal qualified immunity and the pleading argument built on it.
- City of Canton v. Harris, 489 U.S. 378 (1989): preserved as substantive failure-to-train attribution law, not authority for greater pleading particularity.
- Elliott v. Perez, 751 F.2d 1472 (5th Cir.1985), and Palmer v. City of San Antonio, 810 F.2d 514 (5th Cir.1987): rejected insofar as their rule demands heightened particularity for municipal § 1983 complaints; individual-immunity pleading remains unresolved.

**Law after the event:** Effective March 3, 1993, the Court resolves the circuit conflict by foreclosing a special municipal § 1983 particularity requirement absent authority in the Rules or applicable legislation. Ordinary pleading and Rule 11 duties, discovery control and Rule 56 remain distinct. Collins v. City of Harker Heights, February 26, 1992, continues to separate the underlying constitutional wrong from municipal attribution; Soldal v. Cook County, December 8, 1992, does not establish the merits or attribution of these allegations. No individual-officer pleading rule or substantive relaxation of Monell/Canton is adopted. Collateral estoppel was not addressed by the Fifth Circuit or included in the questions presented and is not decided.

#### Reves v. Ernst & Young — 1993-03-03

Source: [Decision Record](records/reves-v-ernst-young-merits-1993-03-03.md).

### Some role in directing the enterprise is necessary, without an executive-status requirement

**Holding and operative rule:** Liability under § 1962(c) requires participation in the operation or management of the enterprise itself: some part in directing its affairs through the prohibited racketeering pattern, not merely conducting the defendant's own affairs or rendering services to the enterprise. Primary responsibility or a formal executive position is unnecessary; an associated outsider can qualify by actually directing enterprise affairs, and lower-level participants operating under upper management can qualify, while the exact downward reach is reserved.

**Authority:** The statutory-construction portion of Blackmun's Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: seven Justices adopt the stated rule at the same level of generality. Souter and White reject the directional requirement.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that participation must concern the direction of enterprise affairs. Turkette supplies the starting point in the enacted language, and Sorenson supports a consistent meaning for repeated uses of the same word. Reading conduct to include direction gives effect to its repeated use; reading participation as taking some part in that direction gives independent work to the broader phrase. The words directly or indirectly prevent a restriction to titled executives or those with primary responsibility. Russello's recognition that participation is broad does not erase the conduct limitation. Sedima rejects artificial restrictions on civil RICO, but its liberal-construction principle cannot replace the elements Congress enacted. An outside accountant therefore has no professional-status immunity, yet the importance of the accountant's services does not alone make the accountant a director of the enterprise. Association and actual function remain distinct inquiries. The Court leaves the lowest qualifying level of enterprise operation unresolved and does not determine a general aiding-and-abetting theory, conspiracy liability, predicate acts, injury or causation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Turkette, 452 U.S. 576 (1981): applied for text-based interpretation of distinct RICO elements; its inclusion of criminal enterprises remains unchanged.
- Sorenson v. Secretary of Treasury, 475 U.S. 851 (1986): applied for consistent treatment of repeated statutory language.
- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16 (1983): preserved as recognizing broad participation language; breadth operates within the enacted conduct requirement.
- Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., 473 U.S. 479 (1985): preserved; broad construction and rejection of special racketeering injury do not eliminate § 1962(c)'s conduct element.
- Bennett v. Berg, 710 F.2d 1361 (8th Cir.1983): its operation-or-management formulation is adopted with the stated limits.
- Yellow Bus Lines v. Drivers Local 639, 913 F.2d 948 (D.C.Cir.1990): operation/management approach accepted, but a requirement of significant control is rejected; some role in direction suffices.
- Bank of America National Trust & Savings Assn. v. Touche Ross & Co., 782 F.2d 966 (11th Cir.1986): its rejection of an operation-or-management requirement is disapproved.

### This auditing and reporting record does not establish the required direction

**Holding and operative rule:** The accounting firm's preparation and explanation of the cooperative's audit reports, using the financial records and disclosing an investment-based plant valuation, do not establish participation in directing the cooperative merely because the firm failed to require or communicate a fair-market valuation. On the RICO component reviewed here, that failure is insufficient under § 1962(c), and the firm's summary judgment is affirmed.

**Authority:** The application portion of Blackmun's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, controls by six votes. Stone, White and Souter reject this application; their judgments cannot be counted as joins.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory role cannot be established merely by assigning management's professional responsibility for financial statements to the outside auditor who helps prepare them. The lower courts applied the operation-or-management formulation recognized today, and their judgment remains correct on the record before the Court. Arthur Young worked with the cooperative's financial records, prepared audits, explained them to the board and made presentations at annual meetings. Its reports disclosed that the plant's stated value rested on the cooperative's investment. The objection that a fair-market valuation would have exposed insolvency identifies a serious defect in the work, but does not by itself show that the firm directed the cooperative. Accounting standards inform the professional relationship; they do not supply the statutory meaning of enterprise management. Rule 56 still requires favorable treatment of supported evidence and reasonable inferences for the nonmovant. This conclusion concerns the particular statutory role shown here, not a categorical exemption for accountants or a finding that every disputed accounting choice is immaterial in another record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bennett v. Berg, 710 F.2d 1361 (8th Cir.1983): applied as the lower courts' statutory-role formulation, subject to Holding 1's qualifications.
- Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986): remains the governing favorable-evidence and reasonable-inference rule at summary judgment; no alteration of its standard is adopted.
- Holmes v. Securities Investor Protection Corp., March 24, 1992: its simulated factual/proximate causation requirement is preserved, not decided or replaced by the failure to show statutory direction.

**Law after the event:** Effective March 3, 1993, operation/management is the governing § 1962(c) participation requirement, with some directional role sufficient and no professional or outsider immunity. The exact lower-rung boundary, general aiding-and-abetting liability, conspiracy, predicate acts and the unadjudicated elements remain unresolved. Section1962(a), (b) retain their distinct acquisition/investment functions; subsection(d)'s conspiracy question is not decided. The Court does not invoke lenity as an alternative holding. Holmes's March 24, 1992 direct-causation requirement under § 1964(c) remains independent and unchanged. The lower court's separate securities, damages, fees, costs and contribution decisions are not adopted as Supreme Court law. Four-Justice historical confirmation is noncontrolling; no Marks synthesis is necessary because both holdings have express majorities.

#### Building & Construction Trades Council v. Associated Builders & Contractors (Boston Harbor) — 1993-03-08

Source: [Decision Record](records/building-construction-trades-v-associated-builders-boston-harbor-merits-1993-03-08.md).

### Genuine proprietary project purchases are not state labor regulation

**Holding and operative rule:** Garmon and Machinists preemption do not prohibit a State's project-specific contracting condition when the State pursues its own proprietary interests as owner and purchaser, the analogous private conduct is lawful, and Congress has not expressly or impliedly displaced that proprietary conduct. MWRA's Bid Specification 13.1 meets those conditions; it governs performance of the Boston Harbor project rather than imposing general labor policy on unrelated conduct.

**Authority:** Blackmun's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: unanimous controlling authority in both dockets.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these labor-preemption rules address regulation, and that this purchase is not regulation. Garmon protects the federal scheme governing conduct protected or prohibited, or arguably so, by the NLRA; Machinists preserves the economic choices Congress meant to leave unregulated. Gould bars procurement used to punish labor-law violations unrelated to the State's purchase, while leaving ordinary purchasing interests distinct. MWRA owns, pays for and supervises this construction and seeks stable performance of a particular job. Those interests are the interests a private project owner may pursue. The construction provisions reinforce the lawful private comparison: § 8(f) permits qualifying prehire agreements, and § 8(e)'s construction proviso permits the relevant project subcontracting condition. McNeff and Woelke explain those separate permissions. Employees retain the election opportunities preserved by § 8(f)'s final proviso. The association's objection based on governmental economic leverage does not make every public purchase regulation. Ownership and a contract label alone are insufficient, but the project's actual operation supplies the proprietary basis here. The Court leaves independent statutory prohibitions and the unreviewed objections undecided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- San Diego Building Trades Council v. Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 (1959): preserved as protecting the NLRA/NLRB field against state regulation; genuine proprietary purchasing does not trigger that rule.
- Machinists v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Commission, 427 U.S. 132 (1976): preserved as protecting Congress's intended free play of economic forces; a lawful project purchase is not regulatory displacement of those forces.
- Wisconsin Department of Industry v. Gould Inc., 475 U.S. 282 (1986): distinguished; debarment to enforce labor law against unrelated conduct was regulatory, while this contract concerns the State's purchased performance.
- Golden State Transit Corp. v. Los Angeles, 475 U.S. 608 (1986): distinguished; conditioning a sovereign operating license on labor settlement is regulation, not a purchase of the licensee's services.
- Jim McNeff, Inc. v. Todd, 461 U.S. 260 (1983): applied for construction prehire agreements under § 8(f); no general immunity from the Act follows.
- Woelke & Romero Framing, Inc. v. NLRB, 456 U.S. 645 (1982): applied for the construction-industry subcontracting proviso under § 8(e).
- NLRB v. Iron Workers, 434 U.S. 335 (1978): preserved concerning the § 8(f) proviso permitting employees to use elections under §§ 9(c), (e) to reject representation or union-security arrangements.
- Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218 (1947), and Maryland v. Louisiana, 451 U.S. 725 (1981): applied as caution against inferring congressional displacement not supplied by the federal scheme.

**Law after the event:** Effective March 8, 1993, the regulatory scope of Garmon and Machinists expressly leaves this genuine proprietary project condition outside their preemptive reach. A procurement label or public ownership alone supplies no blanket exemption. Government cannot use a purchase to regulate unrelated market conduct or impose general labor policy. The private agreement's validity under §§ 8(e), (f) is undisputed for this review; those provisions are distinct and do not affirmatively make MWRA an NLRA employer, since the Act's employer definition excludes States and MWRA is purchasing here. Section 8(f)'s final proviso preserves employee elections under §§ 9(c), (e). The Court does not reach the § 8(d) contention, which was not the appellate ground and was outside the grant, or the ERISA, equal-protection, due-process, Sherman Act and state-law theories left outside this review.

#### Smith v. United States (Antarctica) — 1993-03-08

Source: [Decision Record](records/smith-v-united-states-antarctica-merits-1993-03-08.md).

### The FTCA does not authorize this Antarctic tort action

**Holding and operative rule:** The foreign-country exception in § 2680(k), read with the FTCA's enacted liability and place-of-law provisions, excludes this claim based entirely on acts or omissions in Antarctica even though no other sovereign is recognized there. Sections 1346(b) and2674 do not authorize substituting the claimant's Oregon law for the law of the Antarctic place of the alleged negligence merely because she resides in Oregon; her valid residence venue does not supply the missing substantive consent.

**Authority:** The statutory portion of White's Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: eight Justices adopt this holding. Stevens dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the territorial exception must be read within the liability scheme Congress enacted. Spelar explains the exception's territorial setting and the refusal to expose the United States to liability based on foreign law; it does not make foreign sovereignty the only permissible meaning of country. Richards requires the whole law of the place where the negligent act occurred. Here every alleged act occurred in Antarctica, which has no local civil tort system furnishing that statutory starting point. Oregon law might govern some private dispute, but neither the claimant's residence nor ordinary forum conflicts reasoning changes Congress's place-based direction. The Court does not regard the proposed domestic rule as nonexistent; it rejects the statutory authority to substitute that rule here. Kubrick requires fidelity to Congress's waiver, without either enlarging or narrowing it by judicial preference. Oregon venue is available, and possible venue difficulties for other plaintiffs are context only. The holding concerns this FTCA claim and decides neither negligence nor an independently authorized remedy.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Spelar, 338 U.S. 217 (1949): its territorial understanding of the exception is applied to Antarctica; its foreign-sovereignty facts do not fix an exclusive definition requiring another recognized sovereign.
- Richards v. United States, 369 U.S. 1 (1962): applied; the whole law of the place of the negligent act controls the statutory choice, and the claimant's residence does not authorize a different starting point.
- United States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111 (1979): applied to respect the intended scope of the FTCA waiver without judicial expansion or contraction.
- Beattie v. United States, 756 F.2d 91 (D.C.Cir.1984): its conclusion permitting the Antarctic FTCA action is disapproved; the territorial/choice-of-law objection in Scalia's dissent is consistent with the adopted statutory construction.
- United States v. Nordic Village, February 25, 1992: preserved at its actual simulated scope concerning clear consent to affirmative bankruptcy monetary relief; it does not independently decide this different express waiver.
- Molzof v. United States, January 14, 1992: preserved; its construction of punitive damages and independent local-law entitlement do not establish Antarctic territorial coverage.

**Law after the event:** Effective March 8, 1993, § 2680(k) excludes the Antarctic claim on the stated statutory ground. No blanket rule defeats every offshore FTCA, constitutional or other statutory channel. White's additional discussion, joined by Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, treats the presumption described in EEOC v. Arabian American Oil Co., 499 U.S. 244, as confirmation where Congress has supplied no indication of Antarctic coverage; the presumption is not adopted as an independently sufficient universal territorial rule. Stone joins none of that additional discussion. The FTCA's maritime history and separate statutory waivers remain to be addressed according to their own terms; this case resolves none of them. No Marks analysis is needed because eight expressly join the controlling statutory proposition.

#### Ortega-Rodriguez v. United States — 1993-03-08

Source: [Decision Record](records/Ortega-Rodriguez_v_United_States_merits_1993-03-08.md).

### A sufficient appellate connection is required

**Holding and operative rule:** When a defendant's flight and recapture both precede a timely appeal, fugitive status alone does not justify dismissal: the sanction must respond to a sufficient connection between that misconduct and the appellate process. Courts may adopt reasonable rules for recurring classes of appellate disruption, and may dismiss for sufficiently established interference, inability to conduct meaningful review, or prejudice to a legally available retrial; retrial prejudice cannot justify dismissing a sufficiency appeal whose successful resolution would prohibit retrial.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; six Justices adopt the rule, qualifications and remand.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that appellate dismissal must protect the appellate proceeding it terminates. Molinaro permits dismissal when a defendant invokes review while evading the court's authority. Dorrough preserves deterrence and orderly appellate administration even after recapture, but its case concerned flight during an appeal. Neither decision makes every earlier escape an abandonment of later review. Thomas v. Arn requires a reasoned exercise of supervisory power, and Appellate Rule 47 concerns practice before the appellate court. Here, custody removes the ordinary enforcement problem. District courts can punish disobedience occurring before them; a separate appellate penalty requires its own connection. The Government correctly identifies possible disruption from severed codefendant appeals. That concern may justify dismissal if sufficiently substantial, but the unexplained application of Holmes does not establish that assessment. Retrial prejudice does no work on this sufficiency challenge. The Court leaves the significance of separate appellate consideration to the Eleventh Circuit and neither guarantees review irrespective of disruption nor decides the underlying criminal claim.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Molinaro v. New Jersey, 396 U.S. 365: retained for flight connected to pending review; its disentitlement rationale does not establish automatic forfeiture for every completed preappeal flight.
- Estelle v. Dorrough, 420 U.S. 534: retained for deterrence and appellate order in its pending-appeal setting; it does not settle the distinct preappeal boundary.
- Smith v. United States, 94 U.S. 97, Bohanan v. Nebraska, 125 U.S. 692, and Eisler v. United States, 338 U.S. 189: their enforcement rationale remains applicable to fugitives beyond the reviewing court's effective control, a condition absent here.
- Allen v. Georgia, 166 U.S. 138: postappeal escape may support continued dismissal after recapture; that setting remains distinct.
- Thomas v. Arn, 474 U.S. 140: applied to require reasonable appellate supervision; reasonable circuit rules need not be identical.
- United States v. Holmes, 680 F.2d 1372: its automatic conviction-appeal forfeiture rule is rejected insofar as it dispenses with a sufficient appellate connection; its exception for absence beyond the defendant's control and its preservation of sentencing-error review do not cure that defect.

**Law after the event:** Effective March 8, 1993, a completed preappeal escape must have a sufficient appellate connection before dismissal is justified. The change concerns the scope of appellate disentitlement, not jurisdiction: flight does not eliminate an adjudicable controversy. Recurring rules remain permissible if their covered situations meet the connection requirement. The Eleventh Circuit may assess the significance of separated codefendant appeals on remand; it may not treat flight alone as conclusive. No merits innocence, automatic reinstatement irrespective of interference, or constitutional right to a criminal appeal is held.

#### Reiter v. Cooper — 1993-03-08

Source: [Decision Record](records/Reiter_v_Cooper_merits_1993-03-08.md).

### The statutory claim may be asserted before payment

**Holding and operative rule:** A shipper may assert its claim for unreasonable-rate damages under 49 U.S.C.§ 11705(b)(3) as a counterclaim in the carrier's action for undercharges on the same shipments; Rule 8(c) allows correction of its designation as a defense when justice requires. Under§ 11706(g), the claim accrues upon delivery or tender of delivery, so payment of the disputed tariff charge is not a prerequisite; a negotiated rate alone supplies no common-law defense to the filed tariff.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this statutory rule and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the filed-rate obligation and the enacted remedy for an unreasonable rate must each operate. Maislin rejected an administrative policy allowing a negotiated price to defeat the tariff; it expressly left rate reasonableness available. Section 11705(b)(3) creates a cause of action, so the technically correct objection to calling it a defense does not prevent its use as a counterclaim under Rules 8(c) and13. These claims concern the very shipments for which the carrier seeks collection. The proposed payment-first rule also fails under the accrual text. Louisville Cement construed an earlier statute to require payment before accrual; Congress thereafter specified delivery or tender of delivery. Nothing now separates accrual for limitation purposes from accrual for suit. The Court therefore permits present assertion without excusing the shipper from proving its statutory claim. It neither enforces the negotiated prices as such nor decides that the filed rates were unlawful.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Maislin Industries, U.S., Inc. v. Primary Steel, Inc., 497 U.S. 116: applied; its rejection of the negotiated-rate unreasonable-practice policy remains, alongside the statutory unreasonable-rate remedy.
- United States ex rel. Louisville Cement Co. v. ICC, 246 U.S. 638: its payment-based accrual construction was superseded by Congress's delivery-accrual provision; it cannot establish the proposed prerequisite.
- Southern Construction Co. v. Pickard, 371 U.S. 57: applied for assertion of related counterclaims under ordinary federal procedure.
- Texas & Pacific Railway v. Mugg, 202 U.S. 242; Louisville & Nashville Railroad v. Maxwell, 237 U.S. 94; Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, Chicago & St.Louis Railway v. Fink, 250 U.S. 577: their bars on common-law avoidance of filed tariffs remain; statutory reparations are distinct.
- T.I.M.E. Inc. v. United States, 359 U.S. 464: its rejection of a free-standing unreasonable-rate defense does not eliminate the later express statutory cause of action.

### Same-transaction recoupment survives the affirmative limitation

**Holding and operative rule:** The two-year period in§ 11706(c)(2) for an affirmative unreasonable-rate damages action does not bar a shipper from reducing liability in a timely carrier action through recoupment arising from the same shipments. This defensive recovery extends only to the asserted liability; the decision creates no unlimited extension for unrelated claims or affirmative recovery beyond it.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices support this independent limitation ruling.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a carrier cannot turn the limitation period into immunity from a related defensive claim by waiting to collect. Bull supplies the traditional rule that recoupment remains available while the main action is timely. Western Pacific applied the same principle to an Interstate Commerce Act limitation, rejecting an interpretation that would let a carrier collect unreasonable charges merely by delaying suit. The Government's special setoff statute mattered there to shipments outside the particular collection claims, but it does not undermine the same-transaction rationale applicable here. These shippers seek to reduce the very undercharge demands before the court. Bankruptcy does not erase that defense: it determines the proper amount of the debtor's claim rather than transferring estate property to satisfy an unrelated preference. Rules 8, 13 and54 remain applicable through the relevant Bankruptcy Rules. The Court does not decide entitlement to affirmative damages beyond recoupment or authorize aggregation of unrelated shipments.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247: applied; same-transaction recoupment survives while the principal action is timely.
- United States v. Western Pacific Railroad, 352 U.S. 59: applied for the analogous statutory limitation rationale; the Government's distinct setoff power is not made a condition of private recoupment.

### Primary jurisdiction does not withdraw judicial jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** A court hearing the statutory unreasonable-rate claim must allow a reasonable opportunity for the parties to obtain the ICC's determination of rate reasonableness, but that primary-jurisdiction procedure does not deprive the court of jurisdiction. The court may retain the action, ordinarily staying proceedings for that purpose, or dismiss without prejudice only if the parties would not be unfairly disadvantaged; neither prior payment nor a completed ICC ruling is required to file the civil claim.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices adopt this allocation and its qualifications.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that agency expertise and judicial jurisdiction perform different tasks. Western Pacific places an issue within the ICC's special competence before that agency while a properly cognizable judicial claim remains in court. Under this statute, referral means allowing a party time to file an administrative complaint; it does not mean that the court can command the ICC to answer. Mitchell Coal describes that procedure, and Carnation preserves the choice between retention and a dismissal that does not unfairly disadvantage the parties. Respondents instead seek an exhaustion requirement. The ICC has long construed its motor-carrier authority as permitting rate determinations but not an initial decree of reparations; that reasonable statutory interpretation is respected under Chevron. Nor does the Act require an agency decision before suit: its delivery-based limitation could otherwise expire while administrative proceedings continued. The Court therefore rejects premature-suit dismissal without taking the rate question from the ICC or deciding its answer.

**Precedent treatment:**
- United States v. Western Pacific Railroad, 352 U.S. 59: applied to allocate the expert rate issue while preserving the judicial claim.
- Mitchell Coal & Coke Co. v. Pennsylvania Railroad, 230 U.S. 247: applied for a stay affording a reasonable opportunity to request an agency ruling.
- Carnation Co. v. Pacific Westbound Conference, 383 U.S. 213: applied to retention or nondisadvantaging dismissal without prejudice.
- Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, 467 U.S. 837: applied to the ICC's longstanding reasonable construction of its reparations authority, without new deference doctrine.
- Myers v. Bethlehem Shipbuilding Corp., 303 U.S. 41, and Heckler v. Ringer, 466 U.S. 602: distinguished; their exhaustion setting does not supply the missing statutory prerequisite here.

### Separate judgment requires the ordinary discretionary determination

**Holding and operative rule:** A separate final judgment on the carrier's claim requires Rule 54(b)'s express determination that there is no just reason for delay, considering judicial administration and the actual equities. Prompt suit by a solvent carrier ordinarily favors separate judgment, while actual or threatened insolvency of the party seeking judgment weighs against it without creating an absolute bar; appropriate security and an enforcement stay under Rule 62(h) remain available where other equities justify them.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight Justices support the rule and remand rather than deciding discretion initially.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a statutory counterclaim changes case management without necessarily postponing every collection judgment. A true defense cannot be separated from the claim it defeats; a counterclaim can be resolved separately under Rule 54(b). Sears and Cold Metal preserve that distinction, and Curtiss-Wright requires attention to judicial administration, the policy against piecemeal appeals and the parties' equities. The filed tariff remains the legal rate unless disapproved, so agency delay may weigh in a solvent carrier's favor. Insolvency matters because later repayment may be worthless, but it does not mechanically defeat separate judgment. If a shipper's possible insolvency creates competing risk, a conditional enforcement stay and deposit may protect both sides. Crancer upheld a discretionary refusal to stay on different circumstances; it did not prohibit a stay or abolish counterclaim procedure. Neither lower court made the required express determination here. The Court returns that decision to the proper court and does not award collection priority to either side.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Mackey, 351 U.S. 427, and Cold Metal Process Co. v. United Engineering & Foundry Co., 351 U.S. 445: applied for Rule 54(b)'s separate-judgment authority and safeguards.
- Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. General Electric Co., 446 U.S. 1: applied; actual or threatened insolvency is relevant, not an absolute bar, and protective conditions may address competing risks.
- Crancer v. Lowden, 315 U.S. 631: distinguished; it sustained discretionary collection without an available judicial reparations counterclaim after the shippers chose the separate rail-agency route, rather than requiring collection in every case.
- Keogh v. Chicago & Northwestern Railway, 260 U.S. 156; Arizona Grocery Co. v. Atchison,Topeka & Santa Fe Railway, 284 U.S. 370; Lowden v. Simonds-Shields-Lonsdale Grain Co., 306 U.S. 516: applied for the continuing force of filed rates not disapproved by the ICC.

**Law after the event:** From March 8, 1993, statutory unreasonable-rate counterclaims may proceed before payment, and defensive same-shipment recoupment is not defeated by the affirmative two-year limitation when the main action is timely. The ICC initially determines rate reasonableness; courts retain adjudicative responsibility and discretion over a proper stay or nondisadvantaging dismissal. Rule 54(b), rather than automatic carrier priority or an automatic insolvency prohibition, governs separate final judgment. No negotiated price is judicially substituted for the filed tariff.

#### Delo v. Lashley — 1993-03-08

Source: [Decision Record](records/Delo_v_Lashley_summary_review_1993-03-08.md).

### An evidentiary predicate for the requested mitigating circumstance

**Holding and operative rule:** The Constitution does not require a capital sentencer to receive an instruction on a particular mitigating circumstance when no evidence supports that circumstance; Missouri may require some supporting evidence for its no-significant-prior-criminal-history instruction while leaving the sentencer free to consider and give effect to mitigation actually presented. This production requirement neither requires exhaustive proof of a negative, compels the defendant to testify, authorizes an adverse inference from silence, nor reduces the State's burden to establish aggravating circumstances.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; seven Justices adopt the rule and reversal of this ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that protection of mitigating evidence does not supply evidence missing from the record. Lockett, Eddings and Penry require meaningful consideration of mitigation offered by a defendant. Graham applies that requirement to supported youth and background evidence; it does not establish a different fact from silence. Hopper permits an evidentiary predicate for an instruction, and Walton preserves a defendant's mitigation burden while leaving aggravation to the State. Missouri asks only for some supporting evidence here. Counsel made no proffer, and the trial court did not rule that evidence could not be offered. The prosecution suggested testimony from acquaintances, so this was not a demand that Lashley surrender his privilege against self-incrimination. His age is distinct from the claimed absence of significant criminal activity. The State's access to criminal records does not eliminate the available defense evidence. Whether counsel improperly failed to develop it is a separate claim with separate procedural requirements. No actual criminal history is found from materials the jury never heard.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586; Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104; Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302: applied to preserve meaningful consideration of supported mitigation; they do not require this unsupported instruction.
- Graham v. Collins,January 25, 1993: preserved in its actual controlling scope; supported youth/background culpability evidence must receive effect, without presuming a distinct mitigating fact.
- Hopper v. Evans, 456 U.S. 605: applied by analogy for an evidentiary predicate, not for an obligation to submit unsupported alternatives.
- Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639: applied to the permitted allocation of mitigation proof, subject to the State's undiminished aggravation burden; Missouri's lesser some-evidence requirement is all that is adjudicated here.

### The proposed innocence instruction does not sustain this judgment

**Holding and operative rule:** Even assuming that a presumption of innocence of other offenses applies at capital sentencing, its existence does not automatically require an instruction; the relevant circumstances must create a genuine danger that jurors will attribute other criminal conduct to the defendant on a basis other than lawful evidence. The silent criminal-history record, undiminished reasonable-doubt instructions on aggravation, and absence of a circumstance creating that danger do not require such an instruction here; the Court does not decide the general existence or scope of that sentencing presumption.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas; five Justices expressly adopt this alternative response. Stone-Zsela and Souter do not join or reach it.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the suggested presumption does not establish a right to the instruction on this record. Taylor protects a fair guilt determination when surrounding circumstances risk a verdict based on extraneous suspicion. Whorton explains that an innocence instruction is not automatically required and directs attention to the full setting. Assuming that analogous protection applies to unrelated offenses at sentencing, no circumstance here creates the relevant risk. The jury received no criminal-history evidence and was instructed that the State must establish aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant's youth and the seriousness of the penalty do not themselves suggest other offenses. Counsel's concerns during a discussion outside the jury's presence likewise do not explain how jurors would infer a record from silence. The dissent's proposed inference of mitigating status differs from protection against unsupported adverse inference. This alternative addresses only entitlement to the instruction here; it neither resolves a general sentencing presumption nor rests on a categorical loss of innocence-related protections after conviction.

**Precedent treatment:**
- Taylor v. Kentucky, 436 U.S. 478: applied for the danger of a verdict based on extraneous considerations, while preserving its context-sensitive instruction rule.
- Kentucky v. Whorton, 441 U.S. 786: applied for the totality-of-circumstances inquiry and rejection of an automatic instruction requirement.
- Herrera v. Collins,January 25, 1993: not extended; its actual record-specific rejection of federal relief supplies no categorical rule extinguishing innocence protections for other purposes.

**Law after the event:** Effective March 8, 1993, supported mitigation must still receive meaningful effect, but silence alone does not compel the requested no-history instruction. Five Justices additionally reject automatic instruction on an assumed presumption concerning other offenses under these circumstances. That alternative does not establish a general postconviction extinction of innocence protections. The State’s aggravation burden and the defendant’s privilege remain intact. Part I independently requires the judgment; no Marks synthesis is needed because each adopted proposition has a named majority.

#### Reno v. Flores — 1993-03-23

Source: [Decision Record](records/Reno_v_Flores_merits_1993-03-23.md).

### 1. Statutory authority for the juvenile release regulation

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 1252(a)(1) permits the Attorney General to use the family-custodian and discretionary-release arrangements in 8 C.F.R. §242.24 for temporary juvenile custody pending deportation proceedings; the regulation is not facially beyond that authority because it does not mandate release to every willing responsible adult. Custody remains subject to the statute's reasonable-dispatch limitation, its habeas remedy upon the requisite conclusive showing of unnecessary delay, the regulation's release avenues and the existing conditions agreement.

**Authority.** Scalia's opinion for the Court, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six votes for this rule. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on a narrower statutory and facial-review ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Congress left room for reasonable arrangements governing detention and release while deportability is determined. Carlson v. Landon recognizes discretion within the immigration statute and requires a reasonable foundation for its exercise. The National Center for Immigrants' Rights decision permits general release conditions serving the immigration scheme, with individual safeguards, and does not confine every condition to physical attendance alone. Caring for a juvenile for whom an approved custodian is unavailable fits that statutory responsibility. The regulation permits parental designation and exceptional release, so it does not establish an absolute family-only barrier. The contrary reading would turn a discretionary custody statute into a command to entrust every otherwise releasable child to any willing adult. Neither the text nor the facial record requires that command. The Court does not decide the legality of particular prolonged custody, eliminate habeas review for unnecessary delay, or excuse breach of the conditions agreement.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S.524 (1952): applies discretion bounded by the immigration statute; it does not authorize indefinite custody.
- National Center for Immigrants' Rights, December 16, 1991, decision: applies general immigration-release authority with preserved individual safeguards; does not enlarge that decision into universal detention authority.
- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S.739 (1987): applies facial-review limits to the whole-regulation remedy.

### 2. The facial substantive-due-process challenge fails

**Holding and operative rule.** The Constitution does not give a juvenile without an available parent, guardian or close adult relative a fundamental right to placement with a willing unrelated adult instead of temporary, nonpunitive government care under this regulation and the binding conditions agreement. On this facial record, the family preferences and other release avenues reasonably serve care and attendance interests; individual unconstitutional confinement and enforcement of the agreement remain open.

**Authority.** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. O'Connor and Souter also explain the substantial liberty interest in avoiding institutional confinement in a separate concurrence; Stone-Zsela does not join this formulation.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the particular placement entitlement asserted here is not a fundamental constitutional right. Schall v. Martin recognizes that juveniles are subject to protective custody and that their liberty interests operate within the need for responsible adult care. Parham v. J. R. likewise rejects an assumption that a minor can simply choose complete freedom from adult custody. The challenged rule selects custodians and provides alternatives; it does not prescribe punishment. The conditions agreement supplies the protective setting against which this facial case must be judged, without establishing that every facility in fact complies. Youngberg v. Romeo preserves constitutional limits on institutional treatment, and Foucha requires a lawful ground for continued confinement after an authorizing predicate ends. Neither establishes a general right to unrelated-adult placement during lawful temporary immigration custody. The Court rejects the claim that the regulation must fall in all applications. Particular unlawful restraints and violations of the agreement remain subject to appropriate proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S.253 (1984): applies the distinction between protective juvenile custody and punishment.
- Parham v. J. R., 442 U.S.584 (1979): applies the special circumstances of juvenile care; does not extinguish a child's liberty interest.
- Youngberg v. Romeo, 457 U.S.307 (1982): preserves constitutional limits on institutional care.
- Foucha v. Louisiana, May 18, 1992, decision: distinguishes psychiatric confinement after its predicate ends; no universal custody test is inferred.

### 3. Request-based review is not facially inadequate

**Holding and operative rule.** The described examination, notice and opportunity to obtain immigration-judge review on request do not facially require replacement with an automatic hearing for every detained juvenile. That result depends on the available procedures and the absence of a record establishing general inability to invoke them; it does not approve ineffective notice, denial of requested review, improper delay or unlawful custody in a particular case.

**Authority.** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela agrees with setting aside the universal automatic-hearing remedy but reserves a distinct Mathews-based implementation inquiry.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the facial record does not justify an automatic immigration-judge hearing in every case. For a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure, the examination and custody decision occur within twenty-four hours; a different officer examines unless no qualified alternative is available. The notice form provides English and Spanish notice of review, and a waiver can be withdrawn by requesting a hearing. Immigration-judge review, followed by administrative and judicial review, supplies a means to contest custody. Fare v. Michael C. recognizes that juveniles can knowingly waive even criminal protections; age alone therefore does not prove universal inability to request this review. The Japanese Immigrant Case supplies the underlying entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings. A proposed individualized comparison with every willing adult restates the rejected placement entitlement. Respondents have not established universal ignorance of the review option or inevitable excessive delay. The Court does not decide a supported claim that notice, assistance or delay makes review ineffective for a child or class.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Fare v. Michael C., 442 U.S.707 (1979): applies the recognition that juveniles can make a knowing waiver; no universal finding of comprehension follows.
- The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S.86 (1903): applies aliens' entitlement to due process in deportation proceedings.

### 4. The challenged classifications do not establish facial unequal treatment

**Holding and operative rule.** The regulation's distinction between juveniles with available family custodians and those without them survives the facial equal-protection objection because family availability bears a rational relation to safe placement and attendance. The separate federal juvenile-delinquency release scheme does not require identical release rules for immigration custody, and this ruling does not resolve unequal administration in a particular case.

**Authority.** Scalia's Court opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: six. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on this component and adds no ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the classifications challenged here do not make the regulation facially unconstitutional. Family members and unrelated applicants for custody stand differently in relation to the child's continuing care, and the government may use that difference when arranging temporary custody. The regulation does not make family status an absolute bar to other placement: it provides parental designation and unusual-and-compelling-circumstances discretion. The objection therefore cannot proceed as though every child without an available relative must remain confined regardless of the regulation's alternatives. Schall v. Martin supplies the relevant recognition that protective juvenile arrangements may account for responsibility and care. The existence of different release provisions for federal juvenile-delinquency proceedings does not erase differences between that statutory setting and deportation proceedings. The Court does not hold that all children in the former system are citizens, that aliens lack equal-protection safeguards, or that discriminatory administration would be permissible. Its conclusion concerns the challenged classifications on this facial record.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Schall v. Martin, 467 U.S.253 (1984): applies the legitimate relevance of protective care in juvenile custody; no general exemption from equal protection follows.

**Law after the event:** Section 242.24 survives this facial attack. Subsection (b)(1) requires release in order of preference to a nondetained parent, legal guardian, or adult brother, sister, aunt, uncle or grandparent unless detention is required to secure timely appearance before the Service or immigration court, or ensure the juvenile's safety or that of others. If the only listed custodian is detained, (b)(2) provides discretionary case-by-case consideration of simultaneous release. Under (b)(3), a parent or guardian detained or outside the United States may designate another capable and willing person by sworn affidavit before an immigration or consular officer; that person must agree to care for the child and ensure presence at all future proceedings. Subsection (b)(4) permits other agreeing adult custodians only in unusual and compelling circumstances, at the district director's or chief patrol agent's discretion. These are distinct release avenues, not one undifferentiated exception.

Requested custody review and the enforceable 1987 conditions agreement remain. Section1252(a)(1) makes the custody determination reviewable by habeas upon a conclusive showing that the Attorney General is not proceeding with the reasonable dispatch warranted by the particular facts and circumstances to determine deportability. No thirty-day legal cap is created. The twenty-four-hour examination and custody process described in the holding concerns a juvenile not seeking voluntary departure; the separate voluntary-departure safeguards are not adjudicated as universally sufficient here. No right to release to every willing unrelated adult and no automatic hearing for every detained juvenile is created. The Court does not decide particular excessive detention, defective notice, ineffective access to review or properly supported systemic implementation claims. Stone-Zsela's proposed means of remedying those defects, including necessary assistance or automatic review, remain noncontrolling.

#### Saudi Arabia v. Nelson — 1993-03-23

Source: [Decision Record](records/Saudi_Arabia_v_Nelson_merits_1993-03-23.md).

### 1. The first commercial-activity clause requires the action to rest on that activity

**Holding and operative rule.** Under the first clause of 28 U.S.C. §1605(a)(2), commercial activity carried on in the United States—activity having substantial United States contact under §1603(e)—must supply the basis of the action, rather than merely precede the injury or establish a factual connection with it. The inquiry identifies the conduct constituting the elements that would entitle the plaintiff to relief; recruitment, a United States employment contract and a commercial retaliatory motive do not by themselves make the intentional detention-and-torture claims an action based on domestic commercial activity.

**Authority.** Souter's opinion for the Court, excluding its warning paragraph, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: six. White and Blackmun agree in the intentional-claim judgment on a separate domestic-nexus ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the statute asks what conduct the action is based upon. Congress used a narrower connection in this clause than mere relationship to a commercial transaction. Nelson's recruitment and contract explain how he came to work at the hospital, but proof of them would not establish the intentional arrests, imprisonment and abuse for which he seeks damages. Republic of Argentina v. Weltover supplies the distinction between a private market transaction and sovereign conduct; its third-clause direct-effect rule does not replace this first-clause requirement. The alleged injuries arise from the coercive conduct, not from a failure to perform the employment bargain as such. Treating every later wrong as based on the earlier recruitment would erase the statutory limitation. The Court does not hold that all conduct in an action must always be commercial or settle every possible combination of sovereign and commercial acts. It addresses the intentional claims presented and the particular asserted jurisdictional clause. No ruling on the sufficiency of the recruitment's United States contacts is necessary to that disposition.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, June 12, 1992, decision: applies the nature-of-conduct rule; distinguishes its third-clause direct-effect holding from the first-clause basis requirement.

### 2. Alleged abuse of sovereign police power is not commercial conduct

**Holding and operative rule.** Arrest, imprisonment and torture alleged to have been inflicted through a foreign state's police authority are sovereign in nature for §1605(a)(2), even if undertaken to retaliate against a commercial employee's safety complaints. The asserted commercial-activity exception therefore does not support jurisdiction over these intentional-tort claims; this statutory immunity ruling neither legalizes the abuse nor decides an unasserted exception or liability of absent individual actors.

**Authority.** Souter's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas: six. Intentional-claim reversal receives eight judgment votes; White and Blackmun reach it without joining the sovereign-nature characterization.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that alleged misuse of police authority remains an exercise of power peculiar to a sovereign. Weltover directs attention to the nature of an act that a private market participant could undertake, rather than the public or commercial objective motivating it. A private employer can recruit workers and retaliate against them, but it does not thereby possess a state's police power to arrest and imprison. The allegations place the coercive acts in that sovereign channel. Their asserted unlawfulness does not convert them into the commercial exchange that the statutory exception covers. The contrary approach would classify the very same exercise of police authority differently whenever a claimant alleged a business motive. That would substitute purpose for the distinction Congress prescribed. The Court does not minimize the seriousness of torture, determine the tort merits, or infer an immunity exception from the gravity of the allegations. Other statutory exceptions and claims against individuals not before the Court remain undecided.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, June 12, 1992, decision: applies nature rather than purpose; its market-participant distinction remains controlling.

**Law after the event:** The first-clause basis inquiry and the sovereign character of alleged police coercion control. The warning claims in this action cannot proceed under the asserted exception, by a five-to-four judgment. There is no five-Justice general rule that every independent recruitment-warning omission is barred. Souter's four focus on the underlying sovereign wrong despite a negligence label; White treats retaliatory employment conduct as commercial but locates the actionable conduct abroad, with insufficient domestic nexus. Neither rationale is a logical subset of the other. Marks therefore supplies no broader warning rule. The four Justices favoring warning proceedings do not establish jurisdiction, duty, attribution, knowledge, causation, entity identity or preclusion for this action.

#### Cincinnati v. Discovery Network — 1993-03-24

Source: [Decision Record](records/Cincinnati_v_Discovery_Network_merits_1993-03-24.md).

### 1. The selected commercial category lacks the required fit

**Holding and operative rule.** When truthful, nonmisleading commercial publications concerning lawful activity and newspapers cause the same asserted physical harms through their newsracks, the City must justify why its selected commercial category reasonably fits its safety and aesthetic interests. Cincinnati has not done so: removing respondents' sixty-two racks while retaining approximately 1,500–2,000 physically similar newspaper racks violates the First Amendment; a small reduction is not invalid merely for being small, and no least-restrictive-means or all-sources-at-once requirement follows.

**Authority.** Stevens's opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven, throughout.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the content distinction must do constitutional work connected to the City's asserted objectives. Central Hudson requires a substantial interest, direct advancement and an appropriately limited restriction of protected commercial speech. Board of Trustees v. Fox makes that fit reasonable and proportional, not necessarily perfect or the least restrictive available. Here safety and appearance are substantial concerns, but respondents' racks do not create different physical harms from the many newspaper racks the City leaves untouched. The lower value assigned to commercial speech does not explain the choice of this category to address those harms. Removing sixty-two racks produces some reduction; its size alone is not decisive. The defect is the unexplained selection among equivalent sources of the stated problem. Neutral size, location, number and maintenance rules illustrate alternatives relevant to fit without becoming mandatory alternatives. The Court does not abolish commercial-speech distinctions or adopt the view that all truthful commercial speech receives full strict scrutiny.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S.557 (1980): applies the existing commercial-speech test to the accepted classification.
- Board of Trustees v. Fox, 492 U.S.469 (1989): applies reasonable proportional fit and government burden; preserves rejection of least restrictive means.
- Metromedia, Inc. v. San Diego, 453 U.S.490 (1981): distinguishes the billboard treatment; does not permit an unrelated content distinction without the required fit.

### 2. The selective prohibition is not content neutral

**Holding and operative rule.** A newsrack prohibition determined by whether its publication is a commercial handbill is content based when the asserted safety and aesthetic harms do not justify that distinction without reference to the publication's content. Cincinnati cannot sustain this selective ban as a content-neutral time, place and manner restriction merely because its objective is benign; otherwise lawful neutral regulation of newsracks remains available.

**Authority.** Stevens's Court opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter: seven. This rejects the City's independent time, place and manner defense.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the City cannot convert this selective prohibition into a neutral regulation by describing its objective as safety or appearance. Ward v. Rock Against Racism and Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence permit appropriate regulation of the time, place and manner of speech when it is justified without reference to content. The operative distinction here requires looking at the publication and excludes commercial handbills while leaving newspapers in the same physical setting. The City's interests explain concern about racks generally but do not explain that selection. Renton v. Playtime Theatres permits attention to distinct secondary effects; the record identifies no comparable difference between the physical effects of these categories of racks. Benign motive therefore does not establish content neutrality. The Court does not hold that every regulation touching commercial expression is invalid or that the City lacks authority over sidewalks. A properly drawn neutral regulation remains subject to the established standards and may address the demonstrated physical problems.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S.781 (1989): applies justification without reference to content; a benign objective alone does not validate the selective line.
- Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S.288 (1984): preserves valid content-neutral time, place and manner regulation.
- Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc., 475 U.S.41 (1986): distinguishes different secondary effects; no such difference justifies this rack classification.

**Law after the event:** The particular selective ban fails reasonable fit and cannot be upheld as content-neutral time, place and manner regulation. Incremental regulation remains possible, and alternatives matter without imposing least restrictive means. A city must explain a content distinction's relation to the harms it invokes; physical rack rules remain available if otherwise lawful. No claim about all newsrack restrictions, misleading advertising, unlawful transactions or every commercial classification is resolved.

#### Pioneer Investment Services Co. v. Brunswick Associates Limited Partnership — 1993-03-24

Source: [Decision Record](records/Pioneer_Investment_Services_Co_v_Brunswick_Associates_Limited_Partnership_merits_1993-03-24.md).

### 1. Excusable neglect can include carelessness

**Holding and operative rule.** For a Chapter 11 proof-of-claim deadline subject to Bankruptcy Rule 9006(b)(1), neglect includes inadvertence, mistake or carelessness as well as circumstances beyond the actor's control, but relief after expiration requires a motion and excusability. Excusability depends on all relevant circumstances, including prejudice to the debtor, length of delay and its effect on proceedings, the reason for delay including reasonable control, and good faith; this authority does not enlarge deadlines governed by Rule 9006(b)(2)'s prohibitions or (b)(3)'s specific-rule limitations.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela's opinion for the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that neglect is not confined to events a diligent person could not control. The ordinary meaning includes a failure to act through carelessness, and the rule's separate word excusable determines which failures warrant relief. The bankruptcy rules distinguish the broad enlargement authority in subsection (b)(1) from express exceptions in subsections (b)(2) and (b)(3). Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz enforced one such specific limitation; it does not eliminate the discretion the different rule grants here. Excusability is an equitable judgment considering the circumstances of the omission, including its reason and consequences. The contrary construction would exclude inadvertence before considering whether an unusual setting makes it excusable. Yet the rule does not make every missed deadline forgivable. Ordinary ignorance of clear rules or inadvertence ordinarily will not suffice, and good faith or absence of prejudice alone is not a substitute for explaining the failure. No ruling is made for deadlines whose own express provisions foreclose this enlargement.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz, April 21, 1992, decision: distinguishes the Rule4003(b)/9006(b)(3) exemption-objection limit; preserves its strict operation.
- Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S.871 (1990): distinguishes the requirement of a supported post-deadline request; does not confine every qualifying neglect to uncontrollable events.

### 2. Counsel's conduct is attributed to the creditor

**Holding and operative rule.** A creditor seeking relief under Rule 9006(b)(1) is accountable for the acts and omissions of its freely selected counsel; the court must evaluate the neglect of both client and lawyer together. Reasonable client reliance on counsel does not create an exception from that attribution or independently require an extension.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela's Court opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five; the dissent does not dispute attorney attribution, but its disagreement about excusability does not create another rationale majority.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that retaining a lawyer does not transfer the consequences of litigation conduct away from the client. Link v. Wabash Railroad treats the attorney as the party's representative and holds the party accountable for the representative's omissions. United States v. Boyle likewise rejects reliance on an agent as an automatic excuse for missing a known filing duty. These principles apply when a bankruptcy creditor requests an extension after the claims deadline. The Court of Appeals therefore erred to the extent it treated the creditors' reliance on counsel's assurance as a reason to disregard counsel's neglect. That error does not require reversal of the result because excusability must still be decided under the rule's equitable standard with the lawyer's conduct included. The Court does not absolve experienced counsel of responsibility, guarantee relief to an innocent client, or decide a dispute between client and attorney. It evaluates this application on the full attributed conduct and the circumstances of this notice.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Link v. Wabash Railroad Co., 370 U.S.626 (1962): applies attribution of freely selected counsel's conduct to the client.
- United States v. Boyle, 469 U.S.241 (1985): applies the rejection of reliance on an agent as an automatic filing excuse.

### 3. This record supports allowing the late proofs of claim

**Holding and operative rule.** With counsel's conduct attributed to the creditors, the unusually placed and ambiguous bar-date notice, the twenty-day delay, and the undisturbed findings of no prejudice, no adverse administrative effect and good faith support excusing the missed Chapter 11 deadline here. The late claims may be filed; the decision does not establish their substantive validity or amount, excuse ordinary disregard of a clear notice, or give dispositive weight to counsel's office disruption.

**Authority.** Stone-Zsela's Court opinion, joined by White, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five; the Sixth Circuit's judgment is affirmed on the corrected attribution analysis.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the particular notice and the undisturbed equitable findings justify relief. The deadline appeared inconspicuously in a notice styled as a meeting-of-creditors notice, without the clarity of the usual separate bar-date notice. That unusual presentation matters even though the creditors received notice and counsel bore responsibility for reading it. The bankruptcy court found no prejudice, no adverse effect from the twenty-day delay, and good faith by both creditors and counsel. The Court gives little weight to counsel's withdrawal from his firm and disruption of his practice; Link still charges his negligence to the creditors. The contrary view gives controlling force to responsibility for the omission while excluding the unusual notice and other circumstances from a single equitable evaluation. The Court does not excuse every inattentive lawyer or require prejudice before a deadline can be enforced. It permits these proofs of claim under this record, leaving their merits and the ordinary administration of the estate for the bankruptcy proceedings.

**Precedent treatment.**

- Link v. Wabash Railroad Co., 370 U.S.626 (1962): applies full attorney attribution while permitting the separate excusability inquiry authorized by this rule.

**Law after the event:** A Chapter 11 movant under Rule9006(b)(1) may establish excusable neglect even when the omission involves carelessness, but must justify it on all relevant circumstances with counsel's conduct attributed. No factor alone guarantees relief. Rule9006(b)(2) bars enlargement for the actions governed by Rules1007(d),1017(b)(3),2003(a) and (d),7052,9023 and9024; Rule9006(b)(3) permits enlargement only to the extent and on the conditions stated in Rules1006(b)(2),1017(e),3002(c),4003(b),4004(a),4007(c),8002 and9033. Those express limits remain distinct from the present Chapter 11 authority; in particular Chapter 7 Rule3002(c) and Taylor's Rule4003(b) do not gain a general equitable exception. Claims allowed to be filed remain subject to ordinary merits adjudication.

#### Fex v. Michigan — 1993-02-23

**Blackmun, joined by Stevens, dissenting on Article III(a).** The proper custodial submission completes the act the compact requires from a prisoner. Article III(b) channels the request through officials whom the prisoner cannot supervise; reading Article III(a) together with that prescribed channel and Article IX places transmission risk on the participating governments. Houston v. Lack supplies the analogous protection for a prisoner who has surrendered the paper to the mandatory intermediary. Blackmun's Carchman opinion recognizes the serious burdens associated with unresolved detainers, which explain why prompt disposition matters independently of ultimate conviction. The actual-receipt rule permits an indefinite official delay after the prisoner has done what the compact asks. The prosecutor's interest in adequate trial preparation is substantial, but a necessary or reasonable continuance remains possible upon Article III(a)'s actual good-cause showing in open court with the prisoner or counsel present. These Justices would reverse and remand under the custodial trigger, preserving the compact's actual exclusions rather than granting an automatic extension. They do not decide whether a continuance can first be obtained after the period has expired.

**Stone, separately dissenting on Article III(a).** Stone would begin the period when the prisoner gives the responsible custodial official the required written notice and final-disposition request for transmission through Article III(b). The custodian's later preparation of the required certificate or forwarding does not defer that date. The claimant must establish both a qualifying request and its delivery date under ordinary evidentiary rules: an oral inquiry, a writing missing the required notice or request, or an unexplained delivery to an unrelated official does not suffice. The prosecutor and court remain entitled to receive the papers. Stone acknowledges that their actual receipt offers a clear administrative date and preserves their entire preparation period, but reads the mandatory custodial channel and Article IX to assign transmission risk to the governments that operate it. Under his proposed rule the responsible custodian must record receipt, forward request and certificate promptly, and preserve delivery records. He would reverse and remand to apply the September 7 submission date and decide any Article III good-cause continuance or Article VI exclusion on its own predicates. If the lawful period expired before trial, Article V(c) requires dismissal with prejudice. Delay creates no automatic extension; bad faith is not required; no damages remedy or constitutional override is proposed.

#### United States v. A Parcel of Land Known as 92 Buena Vista Avenue — 1993-02-24

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** They agree that Goodwin may assert the defense and that gift status alone is not disqualifying. Their analysis starts with the conventional operation of forfeiture, reflected in Stowell: the Government must complete the forfeiture process, after which its title reaches back to the offense. Section 881(d)'s incorporation of customs procedures confirms that Congress retained an adjudicative process and did not make §881(h) an instantly completed transfer immune to the other statutory conditions. A claimant may invoke the conditions that prevent forfeiture during that process. The concurrence therefore rejects the premise that the Government has already conclusively eliminated Goodwin's ownership before her defense can be considered. It does not accept the Court's full account that the subsection (a)(6) exception, by defining the property described in subsection (a), alone resolves what subsection (h) covers; ordinary relation back and the statutory procedures supply the necessary explanation. Nor would these Justices turn statements in Goodwin's submissions into an unlitigated estoppel or a binding election of the date at which knowledge must be measured. The Court's express reservation of that date is consistent with their conclusion, but does not remove their distinct structural objection. Their concurrence preserves the Government's retrospective title once forfeiture is validly completed. It establishes no immunity for a donee who cannot prove the statutory defense, no exemption for fictitious ownership, and no rule under the separate real-property provision.

**Kennedy, joined by White, dissenting.** They would give greater effect to the Government's offense-date claim to forfeitable property. A recipient ordinarily cannot receive a better title by gift than the donor possessed. In their view the donor's title was already subject to the Government's superior forfeiture claim, and the gift did not displace that claim merely because the recipient lacked knowledge. Stowell's avoidance of intermediate transfers and Caplin & Drysdale's application of offense-date governmental title support that conclusion. This is a property-title argument, not an insertion of the words purchaser for value into §881(a)(6) as an independent defense requirement. They distinguish an innocent purchaser who gives value, and a person succeeding to a genuinely protected title, from a donee taking directly from the holder of forfeitable property. They would reverse the categorical defense-eligibility ruling and leave room below to examine governing property law and any legally sufficient consideration or other protected title Goodwin could establish. They do not adjudge those possible factual predicates on this record. They reject the Court's sequencing because they regard the Government's superior claim as limiting the ownership that can support the defense, even though formal forfeiture proceedings remain necessary. These views are noncontrolling.
