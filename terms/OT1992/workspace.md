#### Church of Scientology of California v. United States, 1992-11-16

Source: [Decision Record](records/church-of-scientology-v-united-states-merits-1992-11-16.md).

**Holding and operative rule.** Compliance with an IRS summons-enforcement order does not moot a timely appeal when, if enforcement proves unlawful, a court can afford meaningful partial relief by ordering return or destruction of copies the Government retains. Complete restoration of secrecy is unnecessary; this ruling establishes the availability of review, not entitlement to that remedy.

**Authority.** Stevens’s opinion for the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas: nine votes for this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an appeal remains a live controversy when judicial relief can still protect a concrete interest. Mills v. Green supplies the requirement of possible effectual relief; it does not demand that a court undo every consequence of the challenged order. The IRS has learned the contents of these recordings, but it also retains copies. An order requiring their return or destruction could address the Church’s continuing interest in possession and privacy. The Government’s argument confuses irreversible disclosure with the absence of every remedy. Reisman v. Caplin establishes that IRS enforcement orders are appealable. Sections 7402(b) and 7604(a), which authorize judicial enforcement, do not withdraw that appellate review after compliance. The Court need not determine the whole extent of a district court’s power to condition enforcement. Nor does recognizing possible relief decide whether these recordings are privileged, whether the crime-fraud exception applies, or whether the Government may later use information derived from them. Those questions do not prevent review of the order.

**Precedent treatment.** Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), applied: some effectual relief suffices. Reisman v. Caplin, 375 U.S. 440, 449 (1964), applied: summons-enforcement orders remain judicially reviewable. United States v. Zolin, 491 U.S. 554 (1989), left undisturbed: its privilege ruling and divided disposition of enforcement-condition jurisdiction are not reopened.

**Current-law effect:** From November 16, 1992, the governing mootness inquiry in this setting asks whether a court can still grant meaningful relief concerning retained materials. The availability of partial return/destruction relief suffices despite irreversible acquisition of knowledge. Existing appellate review of IRS enforcement orders remains available. The merits, scope of district-court enforcement conditions, future-use restrictions, claimed preclusion, and a proposed third-party exception are reserved. No privilege, tax-exemption, damages, or suppression doctrine changes.

**Precedent treatment:** Mills and Reisman are applied as stated in Holdings. Zolin is left undisturbed. The Ninth Circuit’s Kersting-based rule that completed production alone moots the appeal cannot stand in circumstances where retained copies allow effectual relief. United States v. Ryan, 402 U.S. 530 (1971), and Perlman v. United States, 247 U.S. 7 (1918), concern finality and immediate appeal, not an automatic mootness rule; this decision establishes no general third-party exception. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

#### Parke v. Raley, 1992-12-01

Source: [Decision Record](records/parke-v-raley-merits-1992-12-01.md).

**Holding 1 — initial production burden.**

**Holding and operative rule.** In Kentucky’s collateral challenge to a final counseled conviction used for sentence enhancement, due process permits the Commonwealth to prove the judgment and require the defendant to produce evidence of infringement or procedural irregularity. If the defendant refutes regularity, the Commonwealth must affirmatively establish constitutional validity under Kentucky’s procedure; a missing old transcript alone, without alleged official misconduct, does not establish invalidity.

**Authority.** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight votes at this level. Blackmun agrees with the result in a separate writing limited to sentencing.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the State may begin with the regularity of a proved final judgment while allowing a genuine challenge to its validity. Boykin v. Alabama requires a knowing and voluntary guilty plea and an affirmative record on direct review. It does not allocate every burden when a final judgment is later challenged in a separate sentencing proceeding. Johnson v. Zerbst recognizes that collateral constitutional claims may carry a challenger’s evidentiary burden. Kentucky allows rebuttal, provides a hearing when needed, and requires affirmative state proof once regularity is refuted. The defendant participated in the earlier proceeding, while the State may face comparable difficulty reconstructing it. A missing transcript therefore does not itself demonstrate fundamental unfairness. Burgett v. Texas preserves the prohibition against use of an invalid uncounseled conviction; it does not make every unavailable plea record proof of a constitutional defect. This decision neither transfers the burden of proving an offense nor permits courts to ignore evidence that a particular plea was involuntary.

**Precedent treatment.** Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), distinguished as direct review without weakening its valid-waiver requirement. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468–469 (1938), applied to collateral regularity. Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), preserved for constitutionally defective uncounseled convictions. Medina v. California (June 22, 1992), distinguished: its narrow competence-burden holding does not establish a controlling categorical ban on practical due-process analysis.

**Holding 2 — measure of proof.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Due process does not require the clear-and-convincing extrarecord proof demanded by the Sixth Circuit to establish validity of an old guilty plea in this collateral sentencing proceeding. Kentucky’s retention of the ultimate persuasion burden does not entail that heightened constitutional quantum; the fact of prior conviction and the validity inquiry remain distinct.

**Authority.** The same eight-Justice Court opinion; Blackmun independently agrees on his sentencing ground.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Sixth Circuit demanded more than the Constitution requires when it prescribed clear and convincing extrarecord evidence. Boykin did not decide the measure of proof in a collateral proceeding, and its direct-review record requirement does not supply that missing rule. Johnson v. Zerbst permits ordinary collateral proof of constitutional invalidity; it does not require every uncertainty from an aged record to be resolved by a heightened state burden. Reconstruction can be difficult for both parties. Kentucky responds by recognizing an initial presumption, allowing contrary evidence and requiring a responsive state showing when the presumption is refuted. Neither the constitutional importance of a voluntary plea nor that responsive burden establishes the particular quantum imposed below. The Court rejects that mandatory quantum without announcing one evidentiary measure for every state system. Kentucky’s separate requirement to prove the fact of prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt remains unaffected, as do the prosecution’s burdens concerning the present offense.

**Precedent treatment.** Boykin is not extended into a clear-and-convincing collateral proof requirement. Zerbst supports the distinction between constitutional validity and a universally heightened burden. The Sixth Circuit’s Dunn v. Simmons, 877 F.2d 1275 (1989), cannot govern insofar as it demands that constitutional quantum and shifts the entire production burden solely because no transcript survives.

**Holding 3 — the 1981 plea.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Under then-applicable 28 U.S.C. §2254(d), a competent state court’s factual determination, evidenced by written findings, an opinion or other reliable and adequate written indicia after a merits hearing involving the applicant and the State receives a presumption of correctness subject to the conditions and exceptions below; the ultimate federal question whether a plea was knowing and voluntary remains independently determined. On the established earlier warnings, contemporaneous 1981 charge form and jury warning, representation, education, unimpaired condition and testimony here, Raley’s 1981 plea was knowing and voluntary and the Sixth Circuit’s conditional new-hearing remedy is unwarranted.

**Statutory qualifications.** The presumption does not govern if the applicant establishes, it otherwise appears, or the State admits that (1) the state hearing did not resolve the merits of the factual dispute; (2) its factfinding procedure was inadequate to afford a full and fair hearing; (3) material facts were not adequately developed; (4) the state court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction or jurisdiction over the applicant; (5) an indigent applicant was denied appointed counsel in deprivation of his constitutional right; (6) the applicant did not receive a full, fair and adequate hearing; or (7) the applicant was otherwise denied due process in that proceeding. Under paragraph (8), the presumption also does not govern when the pertinent state record is produced and the federal court, considering that record as a whole, concludes that the factual determination lacks fair record support. At a federal evidentiary hearing, once the state factual determination is duly proved, and absent those circumstances, the applicant must establish its error by convincing evidence. That federal factual-review burden is distinct from Kentucky’s allocation and measure of proof of a prior plea’s constitutional validity. Inferences receive deference only when fairly supported by the facts; no presumption attaches to the ultimate federal legal conclusion.

**Authority.** The same eight-Justice Court opinion; Blackmun concurs in reversal without a separate contrary factual conclusion.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the record supports the Kentucky courts’ inference of continued understanding. Marshall v. Lonberger distinguishes historical findings, which receive statutory deference when fairly supported, from the ultimate federal validity of the plea. Raley’s earlier advice is established by a rights-and-waiver form and counsel’s confirmation, not by the bare existence of a conviction. In 1981 he again had counsel, understood the charge form and acknowledged a jury-trial warning. His education, lack of impairment and testimony about sentencing and bargaining support the inference that he understood the choice he made. His inability to recall additional warnings does not compel the contrary inference. The state hearing resolved the factual dispute through adequately developed evidence and fair procedures; the whole pertinent record fairly supports its findings. No jurisdictional, counsel or other due-process defect in that hearing is shown. Applying Boykin and North Carolina v. Alford independently to those facts, the plea was knowing and voluntary. The Court does not presume understanding from any prior conviction or make every experienced defendant’s plea valid. It reverses the conditional writ on this particular showing; the unchallenged 1979 determination remains intact.

**Precedent treatment.** Marshall v. Lonberger, 459 U.S. 422, 431–438 (1983), applied to supported historical inferences and independent legal review. Boykin and North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25, 31 (1970), applied to an informed voluntary choice. No later habeas deference standard enters.

**Current-law effect:** Kentucky may apply its rebuttable production allocation in this collateral challenge; it must follow its own responsive affirmative-proof rule after rebuttal. The Sixth Circuit cannot require the State to bear the entire burden by clear and convincing evidence solely because old transcripts are unavailable. Current law does not establish a universal collateral proof quantum or a categorical no-right-to-collateral-review rule. Then-§2254(d) governs historical facts subject to the hearing, factual-development, jurisdiction, counsel, due-process and whole-record-support exceptions set out in Holding 3; federal validity remains independently determined. The 1981 conditional writ is reversed on the actual evidentiary showing. Direct-review Boykin, the exclusion of invalid uncounseled convictions, criminal-element proof, and the Medina fracture remain undisturbed.

**Precedent treatment:** Boykin, Zerbst, Burgett, Lonberger, Alford, and the limited Medina rule are treated separately as described in Holdings. The Sixth Circuit’s Dunn v. Simmons allocation and compulsory proof quantum are rejected; no Supreme Court precedent is overruled. Kentucky’s Dunn v. Commonwealth, 703 S.W.2d 874 (1985), supplies the actual state procedure, not an independently created federal rule. The statutory sentencing provisions and Kentucky’s Gadd construction remain distinct from the federal due-process permission.

#### Richmond v. Lewis, 1992-12-01

Source: [Decision Record](records/richmond-v-lewis-merits-1992-12-01.md).

**Holding 1 — an actual cure is necessary.**

**Holding and operative rule.** When a death sentence in a State that weighs aggravation against mitigation rests on an unconstitutionally vague aggravating factor, remaining valid factors do not alone cure the error. The sentence may stand only after an authorized sentencer actually makes a constitutionally valid new sentencing determination, including lawful appellate reweighing, or the State establishes constitutional harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Authority.** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: eight votes. Thomas also writes separately; Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that an invalid factor cannot remain uncorrected in the process choosing death. Arizona’s statute directs consideration of aggravating and mitigating circumstances and permits death when an aggravator exists and mitigation is not sufficiently substantial to call for leniency. Its courts construe that direction as weighing. The survival of two valid aggravators therefore establishes eligibility without establishing a lawful selection decision. Clemons v. Mississippi permits an authorized state appellate court to correct the error through actual reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review. The operative Stringer, Sochor and Espinosa decisions require the substance of that correction, not an assumption that the same outcome would follow. No particular formula of words is indispensable, but a general obligation of independent review is not performance of a new sentencing calculus. The federal habeas court cannot supply Arizona’s missing weighing itself. This rule leaves the State free to choose an authorized constitutional cure and does not require an automatic life sentence.

**Precedent treatment.** Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 (1990), applied: lawful state appellate cure is permitted, automatic factor-count affirmance is not. Stringer v. Black (March 9), Sochor v. Florida (June 8) and Espinosa v. Florida (June 29), all 1992, applied at their controlling levels: actual weighing error requires actual constitutional correction. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), preserves the State’s beyond-reasonable-doubt harmlessness obligation.

**Holding 2 — Arizona’s adjudication did not cure this sentence.**

**Holding and operative rule.** On the parties’ premise that a majority of the Arizona Supreme Court had to cast valid curative votes, this sentence cannot stand: even assuming the two Justices retaining the narrowed aggravator lawfully reweighed, the two other affirming Justices relied conclusively on the remaining criminal history without a new balance of mitigation. The denial of sentence relief is reversed for a conditional writ allowing a reasonable period for constitutional correction or vacation of the death sentence and imposition of a lawful lesser sentence.

**Authority.** The same eight-Justice Court opinion. No rule demanding identical reasoning from every member of a state appellate majority is adopted.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the state judgment lacks enough votes for a lawful cure on the premise the parties accept. Chief Justice Holohan and Justice Hays retained the heinous-or-depraved factor and balanced mitigation against three aggravators. The Court assumes, without deciding, that their application of the later narrowing construction and their reweighing were valid. Justices Cameron and Gordon rejected that factor, but their separate explanation treated Richmond’s murder and kidnapping history as conclusive justification for death. They did not perform a new balance against the mitigation, which included evidence of rehabilitation. Their general agreement with an independent-review obligation cannot overcome that specific explanation. Justice Feldman would have imposed life. Thus at most two valid votes supported affirmance, and one more was necessary. The Court need not decide whether Richmond drove over the victim, whether the principal opinion permissibly applied the narrowed factor, or whether separately valid rationales could otherwise combine. The conviction remains untouched; only the uncorrected death sentence requires relief.

**Precedent treatment.** Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), and Lewis v. Jeffers, 497 U.S. 764 (1990), preserved: narrowing constructions and appropriately reviewed state applications may be valid, but do not supply a curative vote absent here. State v. Gretzler, 135 Ariz.42, 659 P.2d1 (1983), remains the state narrowing source; its proper application by Holohan and Hays is not decided. Clemons and the operative weighing-error decisions require the stated remedy.

**Current-law effect:** Arizona is a weighing State for this sentencing rule. Remaining valid aggravators do not cure a vague factor’s effect on the selection process. An actual lawful state cure or constitutional harmlessness is necessary, and the federal habeas court cannot replace that cure with its own sentencing balance. On the parties’ majority-vote premise, the1983 adjudication lacks a sufficient curative affirmance. This application strengthens no general rule about fractured state judgments beyond the accepted premise. Existing weighing doctrine remains operative; Scalia’s eligibility-only theory and Thomas’s preserved retroactivity objection remain noncontrolling.

**Precedent treatment:** The weighing-error cases and Chapman are applied as stated in Holdings. Walton/Jeffers permit properly narrowed aggravators and appropriate habeas review; they do not authorize an automatic affirmance with remaining factors. The historical Ninth Circuit’s alternative treatment of Arizona as outside weighing is rejected under the statutory direction and authoritative Arizona construction. The Court assumes rather than resolves the validity of Holohan-Hays’s Gretzler application. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled, and no rule requiring a single common state appellate rationale is established.

#### Soldal v. Cook County, 1992-12-08

Source: [Decision Record](records/Soldal_v_Cook_County_merits_1992-12-08.md).

#### A seizure of a home protects possession independently of privacy

**Holding and operative rule:** Meaningful government-attributable interference with possession of a home is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment even without a search, invasion of privacy or liberty, or criminal investigation. The family’s alleged forcible dispossession meets that threshold, but coverage alone does not establish unreasonableness or every element of §1983 liability.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine Justices adopt the same rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that taking a home implicates the Fourth Amendment even when officials do not search it. Jacobsen distinguishes a search’s invasion of privacy from a seizure’s interference with possession; Place likewise examines the detention of property independently of a search. Hicks confirms that the absence of a new privacy intrusion does not remove a seizure from constitutional scrutiny. The alleged removal here deprived the family of its home while deputies prevented resistance. Adickes permits the joint-action inference accepted below at summary judgment, and respondents’ alternative ground does not require disturbing that determination. The concern that this rule federalizes every repossession confuses coverage with liability. Private conduct alone is insufficient, reasonableness remains to be adjudicated, and municipal policy, causation, immunity and relief retain their separate requirements. The Court does not decide those matters or extend the Amendment to every category of property. A home is expressly protected by its text.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Jacobsen, 466 U.S. 109 — applied; possession and privacy remain independently protected interests.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696 — applied; seizure scrutiny does not require an accompanying search.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321 — applied; plain-view seizure remains subject to the Fourth Amendment.
- Adickes v. S. H. Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144 — applied to the summary-judgment joint-action inference, without a final conspiracy finding.
- Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 — preserved; seizure coverage does not establish municipal liability.
- Wyatt v. Cole, May 18, 1992 — preserved; attribution does not settle immunity or a private merits defense.

#### A due-process characterization does not displace the specific seizure claim

**Holding and operative rule:** A court may not reject an otherwise applicable Fourth Amendment claim merely because the same conduct also concerns a deprivation of property under the Due Process Clause. Each applicable constitutional protection must be examined on its own terms; this disposition does not adjudicate the family’s separate substantive-due-process claim.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; unanimous holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a general description of the controversy cannot erase a specific constitutional protection. Graham directs courts to use the explicit constitutional source governing the challenged conduct instead of replacing it with generalized substantive due process. It does not authorize treating the dominant character of a dispute as a reason to disregard another applicable constitutional command. Here, the seizure of a home falls within the Fourth Amendment’s own terms. The landlord-tenant setting and the availability of state remedies therefore cannot end that inquiry. Parratt addresses the adequacy of process for a property deprivation; it does not decide whether a government-attributable seizure is reasonable. Hudson’s prison-specific treatment of possession does not establish a general exclusion for homes outside prison. The family may pursue the restored seizure claim without obtaining a ruling here on its separate substantive-due-process contention. The Court leaves that question, and the remaining liability and remedial issues, unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 — applied; the specific Fourth Amendment source governs its covered conduct and is not displaced by a dominant-character theory.
- Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527 — distinguished; postdeprivation procedural remedies do not answer the distinct seizure claim.
- Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517 — confined to its prison setting for the relevant coverage proposition; no rule excluding possession of a home follows.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 8, 1992, Fourth Amendment seizure coverage protects government-attributable meaningful interference with possession of a home independently of privacy, liberty, a search, or criminal investigative purpose. A competing due-process characterization does not displace the specific claim. Ultimate reasonableness and separate §1983 liability/remedy requirements remain open.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Jacobsen, Place, Hicks, Adickes and Graham are applied; Parratt and Hudson do not supply the proposed categorical bar. The general §1983 and immunity frameworks remain unchanged.

#### Farrar v. Hobby, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/Farrar_v_Hobby_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### Enforceable nominal relief establishes eligibility

**Holding and operative rule:** A civil-rights plaintiff who obtains an enforceable nominal-damages judgment against the defendant from whom fees are sought is a prevailing party under §1988. The judgment must directly benefit the plaintiff by changing the defendant’s legal obligation; its nominal amount does not defeat eligibility.

**Authority:** Part I of White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; all nine Justices support this controlling proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an enforceable dollar judgment is actual relief. Carey recognizes nominal damages for a proved denial of procedural due process when compensatory injury cannot be shown. That judgment obliges Hobby to pay money he otherwise had no duty to pay. Hewitt denied prevailing-party status where favorable judicial language produced no relief, and Rhodes did so where a declaration could benefit neither plaintiff. Those limitations do not describe this enforceable award. Garland makes a material alteration in the parties’ legal relationship the statutory threshold and assigns the extent of success to the amount inquiry. Its discussion of an insignificant technical victory does not convert an enforceable monetary judgment into no judgment at all. The estate therefore qualifies as a prevailing party. This conclusion neither revisits the jury’s liability or causation findings nor guarantees any positive fee; §1988 still requires that any amount awarded be reasonable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 — applied; nominal relief vindicates the established procedural violation without presumed compensatory injury.
- Hewitt v. Helms, 482 U.S. 755 — distinguished; favorable language without enforceable relief differs from a payment judgment.
- Rhodes v. Stewart, 488 U.S. 1 — distinguished; ineffective declaratory relief differs from a judgment directly benefiting the estate.
- Texas State Teachers Assn. v. Garland Independent School District, 489 U.S. 782 — applied and clarified; magnitude affects amount, not eligibility where nominal relief is enforceable.

#### Reasonable amount requires reconsideration on this review

**Holding and operative rule:** When the court of appeals denies fees solely because a nominal-damages plaintiff is ineligible, correction of that error does not itself determine a reasonable amount. On this record, the fee judgment is vacated and remanded for preserved reasonableness objections and an explained Hensley assessment of actual success, allowing reduction to zero without automatically reinstating the prior award or requiring proportionality to damages.

**Authority:** Part II of White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter; five Justices control the remedy and stated fee principles.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the amount should be reconsidered under the correct eligibility rule. Hensley makes the degree of success central, requires exclusion of unrelated unsuccessful work, and calls for an explanation of the fee awarded. The failed demand for $17 million plainly bears on that assessment. Hobby expressly argues for zero fees or a substantial reduction. Petitioners object that the Fifth Circuit never decided amount and seek remand of that question. The Court chooses to leave the preserved alternative to lower-court review of the discretionary award; it does not treat that argument as waived or unbriefed. Rivera forecloses a mechanical ratio between damages and fees; it does not justify compensating unsuccessful work or inventing a public benefit. Blanchard likewise commits the reasonable amount to the circumstances rather than a rigid measure. The remand permits a substantially reduced award or zero after the required analysis and does not restore the $280,000 award automatically. The Court does not categorically forbid affirmance on a sufficient alternative fee ground in another properly presented case.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 — applied; actual success, unrelated unsuccessful work and a reasoned discretionary assessment govern amount.
- City of Riverside v. Rivera, 477 U.S. 561 — applied within its authority; no mechanical proportionality requirement, without guaranteed fees for nominal relief.
- Blanchard v. Bergeron, 489 U.S. 87 — applied; the court must determine a reasonable statutory fee in the circumstances.
- City of Burlington v. Dague, June 24, 1992 — distinguished; contingency enhancement under the two environmental statutes does not decide this eligibility or remand question.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, an enforceable nominal judgment establishes §1988 prevailing-party status. Actual success governs reasonable amount, including possible zero; no mandatory damages/fees proportion or presumption of substantial fees follows. This case requires remand rather than immediate appellate fixation at zero. Dague’s separate contingency rule remains unchanged.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Carey, Hewitt, Rhodes and Garland establish the eligibility distinction; Hensley, Rivera and Blanchard govern the bounded amount remand.

#### District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/District_of_Columbia_v_Greater_Washington_Board_of_Trade_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### Covered-plan content as a mandatory legal measure

**Holding and operative rule:** ERISA §514(a) preempts this continuation obligation insofar as it incorporates an ERISA-covered health plan’s benefit content as the legal measure of mandatory replacement coverage, even when an employer may administer the replacement benefits separately through an exempt workers’ compensation arrangement. No §514(b) savings claim is decided, and this ruling does not preempt requirements measured solely by an ERISA-exempt governmental or church plan.

**Authority:** Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; eight Justices adopt the same operative rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that separate administration does not remove the obligation’s legal dependence on a covered plan. Shaw supplies the connection-or-reference construction of §514(a), while preserving separately administered plans maintained solely to meet workers’ compensation, unemployment-compensation or disability-insurance requirements. Here the District’s command fixes replacement coverage by the employee’s existing covered benefits, for the statutory continuation period. Mackey and Ingersoll-Rand establish that such operative reference can require preemption even when a law addresses another subject. Alessi likewise prevents a workers’ compensation label from insulating interference with a covered plan. Section 4(b)(3) excludes the separately maintained arrangement from ERISA coverage; it does not transform the referenced covered plan into an exempt one. Fort Halifax’s one-time-payment rule does not apply to this conceded health plan. The Court leaves unpresented §514(b) savings questions and applications measured solely by exempt plans outside this holding. It decides no general rule that incidental economic influence or every mention of benefits suffices.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85 — applied; its separate-unit permission concerns independently fixed exempt benefits and does not eliminate operative reference to a different covered plan.
- Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825 — applied; specific legal reference is distinguished from ordinary general garnishment.
- Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133 — applied; dependence on a covered plan can trigger preemption despite the nominal subject of the state rule.
- Alessi v. Raybestos-Manhattan, Inc., 451 U.S. 504 — applied; regulation through workers’ compensation does not avoid ERISA’s covered-plan restriction.
- Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 — distinguished; a one-time payment without ongoing administration differs from this health plan.
- Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724 — preserved; its insurance-saving holding supplies no additional §4(b) escape from a covered-plan reference.
- Morales v. Trans World Airlines, June 1, 1992 — consistent similar-language analogy only; its aviation holding does not replace ERISA’s own coverage and saving provisions.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, mandatory replacement health coverage legally measured by an existing covered plan is preempted in this application notwithstanding separate exempt administration. The 52-week statutory cap describes the invalidated obligation’s scope, not a federal safe harbor. Exempt-only plan measures and unpresented §514(b) savings questions remain outside this application; ordinary indirect economic effects remain unresolved here.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Existing connection/reference law is applied to the mandatory covered-plan benchmark. Separate exempt-plan administration and §514(b) saving provisions retain their distinct operation.

#### Mississippi v. Louisiana, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/Mississippi_v_Louisiana_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### An interstate complaint belongs to the exclusive original forum

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1251(a) denies a federal district court jurisdiction to grant relief on an actual controversy between two states, even when the complaint is joined to private litigation and this Court previously declined an original action. The appellate judgment is reversed insofar as it adjudicates Louisiana’s complaint against Mississippi, with instructions to direct dismissal of that complaint for want of jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices; unanimous controlling holding.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that exclusive jurisdiction cannot be shared by implication. Section 1251(a) reserves controversies between states to this Court. California v. Arizona recognizes that a district court cannot hear one state’s claim against another under that allocation. Louisiana’s third-party complaint expressly seeks relief against Mississippi; its origin in a private title suit does not change those parties. The earlier refusal to entertain an original action therefore supplies no affirmative district-court power. Illinois v. Milwaukee and Ohio v. Wyandotte address the circumstances warranting an original forum, and Texas v. New Mexico recognizes discretion in its exercise. Those authorities do not authorize transferring a statutorily exclusive controversy to another federal court. The general federal-question statute cannot overcome the specific restriction. Mississippi’s own argument for district jurisdiction, and the convenience of deciding all questions together, cannot cure the defect. The Court accordingly removes the interstate adjudication without determining the boundary or the merits of acquiescence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- California v. Arizona, 440 U.S. 59 — applied; this Court is the exclusive federal forum for a state’s claim against another state.
- Illinois v. City of Milwaukee, 406 U.S. 91 — distinguished as to different defendants and applied to preserve original-forum discretion.
- Ohio v. Wyandotte Chemicals Corp., 401 U.S. 493 — preserved; discretion does not grant another court exclusive interstate jurisdiction.
- Texas v. New Mexico, 462 U.S. 554 — preserved; case-specific discretion does not negate §1251(a).
- Wyoming v. Oklahoma, January 22, 1992 — distinguished; its original-forum exercise and separate objections supply no district-court authority here.

#### Private title questions remain distinct from binding interstate relief

**Holding and operative rule:** Section 1251(a) does not bar an otherwise lawful private-title action merely because deciding title requires locating a state boundary; such adjudication does not bind the states on their boundary. Louisiana’s intervention that seeks no relief against Mississippi is unaffected, and the remaining title claims return for a determination whether the existing record permits fair resolution or additional proceedings are necessary.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices; unanimous controlling holding and remand limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that jurisdiction follows the parties to the controversy, not every geographic issue a court may need to consider. The District Court’s authority over the private plaintiffs’ claims against the private defendants is conceded. Durfee explains that a boundary determination in private litigation does not bind the states themselves. A court may therefore decide private title within its lawful jurisdiction without issuing an authoritative interstate boundary decree. Louisiana’s intervention in that title dispute does not itself seek relief against Mississippi and survives the statutory ruling. But the courts below intermingled private ownership with the purported interstate adjudication. This Court cannot assume that excising the latter leaves a complete and fair basis for the former. The Court of Appeals must examine that question and require additional District Court proceedings if needed. No private claimant’s title is determined here, no state is bound by a private boundary finding, and no new original action is commenced.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Durfee v. Duke, 375 U.S. 106 — applied; adjudication between private parties does not bind the states on their boundary.
- California v. Arizona, 440 U.S. 59 — applied to the actual interstate controversy; it does not withdraw otherwise lawful private-title jurisdiction.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, §1251(a) excludes district adjudication of a state-against-state complaint notwithstanding joinder to private litigation or earlier denial of original leave. A lawful private-title action, including Louisiana’s nonadverse intervention, remains possible; it cannot bind the states on their boundary. No replacement original-jurisdiction or boundary test is adopted.

**Precedent treatment:** No precedent is overruled. Statutory exclusivity is applied to the actual interstate parties; original-jurisdiction discretion and private boundary-related adjudication remain distinct. No rule of accretion, avulsion or sovereign acquiescence is decided.

#### Republic National Bank of Miami v. United States, 1992-12-14

Source: [Decision Record](records/Republic_National_Bank_of_Miami_v_United_States_merits_1992-12-14.md).

#### Removal does not itself terminate perfected forfeiture jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** Once valid attachment has established in rem jurisdiction, removal of the property or substituted proceeds does not by itself defeat appellate jurisdiction. The bank’s failure to obtain a stay or bond therefore does not foreclose this appeal where a favorable judgment could still provide lawful effectual relief; an entirely useless judgment presents a distinct limit.

**Authority:** Parts I, II and IV of Blackmun’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter; eight Justices adopt this rule. Thomas supports the judgment on a different statutory ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that initial control of the property and continuing custody are different requirements. The Rio Grande rejects the proposition that removal automatically destroys jurisdiction already acquired, and The Little Charles identifies practical inability to effectuate a decree as a different concern. The Brig Ann addresses abandonment before proceedings began; Taylor v. Carryl concerns the original seizure needed to obtain jurisdiction. Neither establishes the Government’s proposed perpetual-custody rule. Here the parties substituted identifiable sale proceeds for property validly attached, and the Government then placed those proceeds in its own Treasury account. The bank’s failure to secure a stay did not undo the original attachment. Church of Scientology likewise confirms that possible effectual relief can preserve review despite an intervening event. The statutory-payment holding supplies that possibility here. The Court reserves a case where a judgment would be entirely useless, the amendment’s temporal application, and every question about whether this bank should prevail on the forfeiture merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- The Rio Grande, 23 Wall. 458 — applied; removal alone does not terminate jurisdiction properly acquired.
- The Little Charles, 26 F. Cas. 979 — applied; the ability to effectuate relief is distinct from uninterrupted custody.
- The Brig Ann, 9 Cranch 289 — distinguished; abandonment before institution differs from removal after valid attachment.
- Taylor v. Carryl, 20 How. 583 — distinguished; initial jurisdiction does not establish perpetual custody as a condition.
- Church of Scientology of California v. United States, November 16, 1992 — applied by analogy on effectual relief; its IRS-copy rule supplies no payment authority.

#### The return statute and judgment appropriation permit effective relief upon success

**Holding and operative rule:** If the bank prevails in the underlying forfeiture and obtains the requisite judgment, 28 U.S.C. §2465 supplies a substantive right to return of the substituted proceeds; 31 U.S.C. §1304 and 28 U.S.C. §2414 supply the judgment-payment route when payment is not otherwise provided, the Comptroller General certifies payment, and the obligation satisfies the statutory final-judgment and payable-judgment requirements. That conditional lawful route defeats the Government’s claim that this appeal can produce no effective relief, without ordering payment now or recognizing judicial spending power apart from an applicable appropriation.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s statutory-payment opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six express joins and independently controls. Stevens expressly accepts the same sufficient statutory proposition in his own writing, making seven supporters without an invented formal join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress has supplied a lawful means of effectuating a favorable forfeiture judgment. Section 2465 commands return when the claimant prevails, and sale of the attached property does not eliminate that right; the proceeds stand in its place. Section 1304 appropriates sums for qualifying final judgments when payment is not otherwise provided and the certification and statutory-payment conditions are met. Section 2414 supplies the relevant final district-court judgment route. Richmond cautions that the Judgment Fund is not an all-purpose judicial account: a substantive statutory right must support payment. Section 2465 supplies that right here if the bank succeeds. The Government’s Treasury transfer therefore does not make relief legally impossible. Knote’s restriction on payment without legislative authority need not be qualified because the necessary statutory route exists. The Court does not decide whether disputed funds may ever be returned without an appropriation, whether another payment source applies, or whether the bank is entitled to prevail. No money is awarded today.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Office of Personnel Management v. Richmond, 496 U.S. 414 — applied; a substantive statutory entitlement and applicable appropriation, not equity alone, are required for this payment route.
- Knote v. United States, 95 U.S. 149 — preserved; the statutory resolution leaves the broader constitutional question about contested forfeiture proceeds undecided.

**Current-law effect:** Effective December 14, 1992, perfected civil-forfeiture jurisdiction is not lost solely by transfer of the res or proceeds, where lawful effectual relief remains possible. In this setting §2465 reaches substituted proceeds, and §§1304 and 2414 permit payment upon the required favorable judgment and their statutory conditions. The bank must still establish its entitlement. The majority leaves §1355(c)’s application to pre-enactment events and any appropriation-free return theory unresolved; the statute’s already-effective text remains in force independently of this decision.

**Precedent treatment:** No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The traditional attachment authorities are applied within their initial-versus-continuing-custody distinction. Richmond’s requirement of a substantive statutory basis is satisfied conditionally through §2465 and the judgment appropriation. The Eleventh Circuit’s continuous-custody rule cannot sustain this dismissal. The majority adopts no appropriation-free constitutional return doctrine and no construction of the intervening amendment’s temporal reach.

#### 1993-01-12 — Puerto Rico Aqueduct & Sewer Authority v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc.

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/puerto-rico-aqueduct-v-metcalf-eddy-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding and operative rule.** A State or entity claiming to be an arm of a State may immediately appeal under §1291 a conclusive denial of Eleventh Amendment immunity from federal suit when the immunity issue is separate from the merits and the protection would be effectively lost by waiting for final judgment. Appealability does not establish that the claimant possesses immunity, and a dispute over arm-of-State status does not by itself require postponing review until trial.

**Authority.** White’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: seven votes for this rule and its controlling explanation. Stone-Zsela supplies an eighth vote for reversal and the narrower procedural rule in his judgment concurrence. No fractured-decision analysis is necessary.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the protection from federal suit cannot ordinarily be restored after the State has undergone the litigation. Cohen permits immediate review of a small class of orders whose separate rights would otherwise be lost; Coopers & Lybrand requires conclusiveness, separation from the merits and effective unreviewability. Mitchell applies those principles to immunity from litigation. The denied claim here concerns that protection, not whether the engineering firm can prove breach of contract. The States’ protected immunity and their interests in avoiding federal coercive process distinguish this claim from an ordinary objection to the chosen forum. Ex parte Young does not reduce state immunity to a defense against liability: it permits qualifying prospective relief against officials, while Green v. Mansour excludes a declaration of a past federal violation and Cory v. White preserves immunity of the State or agency itself. The Court decides neither whether PRASA is an arm of Puerto Rico nor whether Puerto Rico must receive a State’s treatment. Those questions are not resolved by recognizing appellate jurisdiction.

**Precedent treatment.**
- Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541 (1949), and Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463 (1978): applied; the three collateral-order requirements remain mandatory.
- Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985): its immunity-from-suit rationale is applied to asserted Eleventh Amendment immunity; this does not extend substantive immunity to new entities.
- Edelman v. Jordan, 415 U.S. 651 (1974), and In re Ayers, 123 U.S. 443 (1887): relied on for the existing protection from federal suit and its federalism basis, without enlarging the classes of immune defendants.
- Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), Green v. Mansour, 474 U.S. 64 (1985), and Cory v. White, 457 U.S. 85 (1982): preserved as distinct rules concerning prospective official relief, past-violation declarations, and suits against the State or agency itself.
- Van Cauwenberghe v. Biard, 486 U.S. 517 (1988), and Catlin v. United States, 324 U.S. 229 (1945): distinguished; their ordinary forum-jurisdiction finality rules remain.
- Mt. Healthy City Board of Education v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274 (1977): retained; entity status remains a distinct inquiry.

Effective January 12, 1993, qualifying conclusive denials of Eleventh Amendment immunity are immediately appealable under the existing collateral-order requirements. Before this decision, the Court had not resolved the circuit conflict over this category. The addition concerns appellate timing; entitlement, waiver, abrogation, the unchallenged Puerto Rico premise, and alternative contract remedies remain undecided. Existing prospective-officer-relief limitations remain as described in Holdings. The court below may examine PRASA’s status on remand.

#### 1993-01-12 — Bath Iron Works Corp. v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/bath-iron-works-v-director-owcp-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding 1 — compensation branch and injury date.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Noise-induced occupational hearing loss sustained during workplace exposure is a scheduled, presumptively disabling injury governed by §908(c)(13), whether claimed before or after retirement; the injury is complete at last exposure, which supplies the injury date for the wage basis. Later awareness or filing does not make it an occupational disease that does not immediately result in disability under §910(i), and §908(c)(23)’s special retiree formula therefore does not apply.

**Authority.** Stevens’s unanimous Opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; nine votes for the rule and application.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the time a worker learns of hearing loss is distinct from the time the disabling injury occurs. Potomac Electric establishes that a scheduled injury receives its prescribed compensation without proof that earnings actually fell. The undisputed medical description establishes that noise-related hearing loss occurs during exposure and is complete when exposure ends. Section 910(i), by contrast, addresses an occupational disease that does not immediately cause death or disability. Its awareness rule cannot apply until that predicate is satisfied. The employer’s reading would remove that limiting language merely because the worker has retired. The provisions cross-referencing §910(i) and §910(d)(2) must operate together; they do not authorize combining a latent-disease wage measure with the hearing-loss schedule. The Court therefore affirms the scheduled classification. It does not decide a claim based on a disease that genuinely becomes disabling later, create a continuing-wage-loss requirement for scheduled benefits, or disturb the waived wage-calculation issue and unchallenged impairment percentage in this case.

**Precedent treatment.** Potomac Electric Power Co. v. Director, OWCP, 449 U.S. 268 (1980), is applied: scheduled compensation is mandatory within its statutory branch and does not require actual earning loss. Todd Shipyards Corp. v. Black, 717 F.2d 1280 (9th Cir. 1983), is distinguished as a latent-disease case; its rationale does not move the completed hearing injury to the awareness date. The contrary retiree classifications in Ingalls Shipbuilding and Alabama Dry Dock do not survive this statutory construction.

**Holding 2 — the distinct filing protection.**

**Holding and operative rule.** Section 908(c)(13)(D) postpones the running of the §912 notice period and §913 claim period for hearing loss until the employee receives an audiogram and its accompanying report indicating hearing loss; it does not postpone the substantive time of injury to awareness. The 1984 amendments accordingly provide a hearing-specific filing protection, independently defeating the contention that congressional concern about late hearing claims necessarily places them in the latent-disease compensation system.

**Authority.** The same unanimous Court opinion; all nine Justices join this independently sufficient answer to the employer’s legislative-history argument as well as the text-based classification in Holding 1.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Congress addressed delayed hearing claims through a separate filing rule. Section 908(c)(13)(D) directs when the notice and claim periods begin; it does not use the awareness language or substantive predicate of §910(i). That distinction permits a worker to claim an injury already completed during employment without converting it into a disease that becomes disabling after retirement. Potomac Electric supplies the existing schedule’s operation, and the 1984 hearing amendment preserves access to that system. The employer relies on a Senator’s passing criticism of Redick, a Board decision denying a retiree’s hearing claim. The carefully read statutory text is unambiguous and is not displaced by that single statement. Independently, the hearing-specific amendment shows why congressional concern about that denial does not entail use of §908(c)(23). These are two sufficient answers, not competing injury-date rules. Brown’s timely filing therefore remains consistent with scheduled compensation. The Court does not establish an alternative audiogram date, reopen timeliness, or create a universal rule for genuinely delayed occupational disease.

**Precedent treatment.** Potomac Electric remains the source of the schedule’s fixed statutory operation. Redick v. Bethlehem Steel Corp., 16 BRBS 155 (1984), cannot justify denying a scheduled claim solely because hearing loss was discovered after voluntary retirement; reference to it in debate does not change either statutory trigger.

From January 12, 1993, occupational hearing loss completed during exposure is compensated under the schedule, using last exposure as the injury date for the wage basis, even when awareness and claim follow retirement. The schedule gives two-thirds of the applicable average weekly wage for the prescribed weeks: bilateral total loss uses 200 weeks, with proportionate treatment for partial loss. Actual postinjury earnings need not decline. The special retiree formula instead remains available only for the qualifying delayed-disease category: within the first retirement year the relevant wage is one fifty-second of earnings in the preceding 52 weeks; after that year it is the national average wage at the statutory injury time, with two-thirds multiplied by the permanent-impairment percentage under the referenced medical guides for the duration of impairment. These systems cannot be hybridized. The distinct audiogram-and-report filing protection remains. The present award is affirmed under preservation limits, not recomputed.

#### 1993-01-12 — Commissioner v. Soliman

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/commissioner-v-soliman-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding and operative rule.** Under then-effective §280A(c)(1)(A), identifying the principal place of business requires comparing the relative importance of activities performed at each business location and the time spent there; necessary administration or the lack of another office alone does not make a home office principal. Customer contact or service delivery receives great but nonconclusive weight, special facilities may add weight, time is particularly useful when the functions do not settle the comparison, and a home office does not qualify by default when no place emerges as principal.

**Authority.** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter: six votes for this formulation and its application. Thomas and Scalia agree only with the judgment on their separately stated approach; Stevens dissents. The Court opinion controls without a fractured-decision inquiry.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Soliman’s home office does not qualify because the statute asks which place is principal, not merely whether an office is legitimate or necessary. Malat v. Riddell and Crane v. Commissioner direct attention to ordinary meaning; principal requires comparison among business locations. Section 162’s ordinary-expense rule does not eliminate §280A’s additional restriction. Administering anesthesia and caring for patients in specially equipped hospitals are the most important services of this practice, and Soliman also spends substantially more working time there. His essential records, preparation and correspondence do not outweigh those converging facts. The contrary headquarters approach would turn almost any indispensable administrative room into the principal place without making the comparison Congress required. The Court adopts no universal customer-contact requirement and does not bar a home office where the main business activities actually occur there. The separate patient-meeting and unattached-structure branches retain independent operation; alternative office availability may bear on an employee’s employer-convenience condition, but not on the principal-place inquiry. No later statutory amendment enters this decision.

**Precedent treatment.**
- Malat v. Riddell, 383 U.S. 569 (1966), and Crane v. Commissioner, 331 U.S. 1 (1947): applied for ordinary meaning in revenue statutes; neither is displaced.
- Welch v. Helvering, 290 U.S. 111 (1933): retained for the fact-sensitive character of deduction questions; it does not prevent articulating consistent statutory considerations.
- The Fourth Circuit’s necessity/substantial-time/no-other-office test is rejected because it fails to compare locations; no earlier Supreme Court principal-place test is overruled.

**Application and limits.** Exclusive and regular business use remain prerequisites. Visits from patients or clients are not indispensable under subsection (A); subsection (B) is a separate alternative for normal-course meetings or dealings, and subsection (C) separately covers qualifying unattached structures. Neither alternative is presented as a basis for this deduction; their general boundaries are not adjudicated. The employee-only employer-convenience requirement is not a condition for this self-employed taxpayer. The Court disallows this home-office deduction and reaches no other deduction.

Effective January 12, 1993, the principal-place branch uses comparative functional importance and time with the qualifications in Holdings. This settles the previously open Supreme Court question; necessity, lack of another office and general deductibility under §162 are insufficient. The Court does not require delivery to clients at home, does not select an inflexible arithmetic weighting, and recognizes that no location need qualify. Other branches and the additional employer-convenience condition for employee use retain distinct operation. The 1983 deduction at issue fails on the combined hospital-service and time facts.

#### 1993-01-12 — Rowland v. California Men’s Colony, Unit II Men’s Advisory Council

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/rowland-v-california-mens-colony-merits-1993-01-12.md).

**Holding 1 — associations may apply.**

**Holding and operative rule.** An association is not categorically excluded from then-effective §1915: the Dictionary Act’s inclusion of associations within person applies because §1915’s affidavit, financial-inability and counsel provisions can operate through ordinary agency and representation rules. The context exception remains operative, but these features do not establish an incompatible statutory setting or authorize a natural-person-only restriction.

**Authority.** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy: five votes for this construction and its operative limits. Kennedy’s separate account of permissible workability analysis adds no condition to the Court’s holding.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that Congress’s express definition includes the Council within the class allowed to seek fee relief. United States v. A & P Trucking Co. recognizes that an artificial entity can act through people; Wilson v. Omaha Indian Tribe gives effect to the Dictionary Act while testing the actual statutory context. Here an association can litigate through lawful counsel, establish facts through an authorized agent and lack resources to pay costs. The personal language of an affidavit and poverty does not make those ordinary legal acts impossible. Nor does the permission to request counsel imply that every eligible applicant must be able to appear without counsel. Mallard preserves that permission as discretionary, while Osborn preserves the entity’s representation requirement. The officers’ administrative objections deserve consideration when the court examines a particular application; they do not establish categorical exclusion. Neitzke cautions that efficient docket management cannot substitute for the enacted screening standard. The Court preserves the contextual exception and decides association eligibility, not an entitlement to fee relief or a comprehensive insolvency test for every entity.

**Precedent treatment.**
- United States v. A & P Trucking Co., 358 U.S. 121 (1958): applied for giving statutory entity language effective operation through ordinary legal mechanisms; criminal-liability rules are not imported.
- Wilson v. Omaha Indian Tribe, 442 U.S. 653 (1979): applied for the Dictionary Act’s inclusive default subject to actual context; no requirement of an express contrary definition is created.
- Neitzke v. Williams, 490 U.S. 319 (1989): applied to preserve statutory construction against a policy substitution based on administrative convenience.
- Mallard v. United States District Court, 490 U.S. 296 (1989): preserved; §1915(d) permits a request for counsel and does not compel service.
- Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738 (1824): preserved; an entity’s lawful counsel requirement is separate from financial eligibility.

**Holding 2 — eligibility does not establish the showing.**

**Holding and operative rule.** An eligible association must submit a properly authorized affidavit establishing its actual inability to pay costs or give security and its asserted entitlement to redress; the court may examine legally available resources and sham concealment without automatically attributing every member’s assets to it. Inability to hold an entity bank account or a balance-sheet deficit alone does not compel fee relief, so the Council’s application returns for the actual financial and representation determinations; §1915 neither licenses lay entity advocacy nor guarantees appointed counsel.

**Authority.** The same five-Justice Opinion of the Court supplies this remand rule and limitation. The dissenters would require denial because of entity status and do not join the Court’s application process.

**Controlling explanation.** The Court holds that the Ninth Circuit moved too quickly from eligibility to an unconditional grant. Section 1915(a) makes the affidavit and inability-to-pay showing necessary and gives the court permission to grant relief once the statutory requirements are satisfied. Adkins explains the need to examine financial ability in the applicant’s actual circumstances; its personal necessities-of-life language does not itself define an association’s resources. The Council’s prohibition on maintaining funds may be relevant, but it does not alone resolve whether resources can lawfully be made available for the litigation, including through direct payment. The court must verify the affiant’s authority and assess the showing without simply aggregating every member’s property. Osborn keeps entity representation separate, and Mallard prevents converting a discretionary counsel request into compulsory appointment. Neitzke and Denton preserve lawful screening for frivolous or malicious proceedings and clearly baseless facts. The Court finds neither the Council indigent nor its underlying claim meritorious. It remands those application questions without announcing a universal organizational insolvency formula.

**Precedent treatment.**
- Adkins v. E. I. DuPont de Nemours & Co., 335 U.S. 331 (1948): applied to actual financial inability; its individual-applicant facts do not categorically exclude associations or prescribe a corporate insolvency test.
- Osborn and Mallard: preserved as distinct counsel and representation limits rather than grounds for automatic financial disqualification.
- Neitzke and Denton v. Hernandez (May 4, 1992): preserved for their respective legal and factual screening rules, with Denton’s abuse-of-discretion review and independent correction of legal error unchanged.

Effective January 12, 1993, an association may seek §1915 relief through an authorized affidavit and lawful representation. Courts must decide actual financial inability and the remaining statutory conditions; eligibility does not compel a grant, aggregate every member’s assets, excuse concealment, authorize lay advocacy or compel counsel. The Court adds this eligibility/remand rule to the opening law without changing Neitzke/Denton screening. It does not establish that every insolvent corporation qualifies, adopt a universal organizational means test, resolve the Council’s tobacco-policy claims, or decide a constitutional right to subsidized organizational litigation. The statutory resolution makes the asserted First Amendment avoidance theory unnecessary to decide.

#### 1993-01-13 — Nixon v. United States

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Nixon_v_United_States_merits_1993-01-13.md).

#### The presented committee-evidence challenge is not judicially revisable

**Holding and operative rule:** The Senate’s use of a committee to receive testimony, while the full Senate receives the record, hears argument and retains deliberation and judgment, falls within the impeachment-trial procedure committed to the Senate and is not judicially revisable on this claim. This holding does not decide whether courts may enforce the oath, presidential-trial presiding-officer or two-thirds requirements, or review a purported proceeding that abandons adjudication altogether.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. Six of nine Justices adopt this exact ground and its limits; White and Blackmun’s merits ground is noncontrolling, and Souter does not formally join the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this challenge asks the Judiciary to choose the Senate’s evidentiary method rather than enforce an independently prescribed constitutional procedure. Baker requires examination of the particular constitutional commitment and the standards available for judicial decision. Article I gives the Senate sole responsibility to try impeachments but does not require every Senator to receive live testimony. The committee assembled evidence; the full Senate retained deliberation and judgment. Powell enforced qualifications independently fixed by the Constitution and therefore does not authorize appellate supervision of this method. Commerce v. Montana likewise permits review of a defined population constraint, not judicial selection of another branch’s trial procedure. Political importance alone would not foreclose adjudication. Here, however, imposing the ordinary courtroom model would transfer supervisory authority the constitutional text assigns elsewhere. The Court accordingly affirms dismissal without deciding whether the procedure was constitutionally sufficient on the merits, or whether violation of an express command or abandonment of adjudication would present a reviewable claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 — applied through its issue-specific textual-commitment and manageable-standards inquiry; political subject matter alone remains insufficient.
- Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 — distinguished because independently fixed legislative qualifications differ from the evidentiary method left to the Senate.
- United States Department of Commerce v. Montana, March 31, 1992, decision — preserved and distinguished; its apportionment review holding does not create impeachment-procedure review.
- United States v. Muñoz-Flores, 495 U.S. 385 — preserved; interbranch disagreement alone does not bar constitutional review.

Effective January 13, 1993, the Rule XI evidentiary-allocation objection presented here is nonjusticiable. This adds an impeachment-specific application of Baker without a general immunity for politically significant action. The Senate’s procedure receives no controlling merits approval. Judicial enforceability of independently determinate impeachment commands and the consequences of a proceeding abandoning adjudication remain open. White and Blackmun’s contrary reviewability rule and Souter’s exceptional-case formulation are noncontrolling.

#### 1993-01-13 — Bray v. Alexandria Women’s Health Clinic

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Bray_v_Alexandria_Womens_Health_Clinic_merits_1993-01-13.md).

#### Private conspiracy requires purposeful interference with a privately protected right

**Holding and operative rule:** A purely private conspiracy under the deprivation clause of §1985(3) must aim at interfering with a right protected against private as well as official encroachment; incidental effects are insufficient. Clinic obstruction applied to interstate and local patients alike does not establish that interference with interstate travel was a conscious objective merely because substantial numbers of patients crossed state lines.

**Authority:** Scalia Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters. Souter separately agrees with rejection of this theory without a formal join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory purpose requirement must be proved for the particular right invoked. Griffin recognizes that interstate travel may receive protection against private conspiracies, but it does not convert every injury suffered during such travel into a federal civil-rights violation. Scott requires the conspiracy to aim at interference with an independently protected right. Guest similarly distinguishes a robbery of an interstate traveler from a conspiracy directed at travel itself. Here the District Court found that substantial numbers of patients crossed state lines. That finding establishes clientele, not the object of the conspiracy. Respondents conceded that the obstruction did not distinguish interstate patients from local patients, and the demonstrated object was stopping abortions at the clinics. The serious consequences of the blockades cannot supply the missing statutory element. The Court rejects this claim without deciding that an interstate traveler loses protection upon reaching a destination, or that every nondiscriminatory local obstruction can never interfere with interstate passage.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Griffin v. Breckenridge, 403 U.S. 88 — applied; coverage of private conspiracies retains purposeful, invidiously discriminatory deprivation requirements.
- United Brotherhood of Carpenters & Joiners v. Scott, 463 U.S. 825 — applied; a private conspiracy must target a privately protected right.
- United States v. Guest, 383 U.S. 745 — applied by analogy; injury to a traveler differs from purposeful interference with interstate travel.

#### Constitutional abortion liberty does not itself supply the private-right element

**Holding and operative rule:** Constitutional protection of abortion choice against governmental interference does not itself create a right against the purely private obstruction proved here. Without necessary state involvement or another right protected against private encroachment, the deprivation clause cannot sustain this theory merely because the conspiracy deliberately seeks to prevent abortions.

**Authority:** Scalia Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters of this distinct sufficient ground addressing the alternative privacy theory. Souter separately accepts Scott’s private-right limitation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the constitutional source and scope of a right remain separate from the statutory remedy invoked to enforce it. Scott rejected the use of the deprivation clause to protect First Amendment rights against a wholly private conspiracy when the underlying guarantee constrained governmental action. The same distinction governs here. Roe and Casey preserve the protected abortion decision and limit governmental interference; Casey’s complete framework governs enacted duties and their effects on access, purpose, clinical judgment and relief. It does not create a private-action cause of action or change §1985(3)’s elements. The organizers’ deliberate opposition to abortions therefore identifies the object of their conduct without supplying the required independently protected private right. Unlawful blockades remain subject to applicable state law. The Court neither retreats from Casey nor equates unlawful obstruction with protected persuasion. Whether a properly presented conspiracy to hinder state protection meets the separate statutory clause is left unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Scott, 463 U.S. 825 — applied; the underlying constitutional guarantee’s state/private distinction remains.
- Roe v. Wade, 410 U.S. 113 — preserved as carried forward and modified by Casey; no direct private-obstruction cause follows.
- Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, June 29, 1992 — preserved; its six-step state-regulation framework does not change §1985(3)’s elements.
- Great American Federal Savings & Loan Assn. v. Novotny, 442 U.S. 366 — applied; §1985(3) does not itself create the substantive right.

#### Federal merits failure does not retroactively destroy jurisdiction

**Holding and operative rule:** These §1985(3) claims were not wholly insubstantial and frivolous, so their failure on the merits does not remove original federal jurisdiction. Power over the related state claims remains subject to the governing pendent-jurisdiction principles rather than automatic denial because of today’s federal merits ruling.

**Authority:** Scalia Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters. This does not establish entitlement to any particular injunction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an unsuccessful federal claim and a jurisdictionally nonexistent claim are different things. Bell v. Hood permits the federal court to decide a substantial asserted federal right even when the plaintiff ultimately fails to establish a cause of action. The clinics’ theory relied on Griffin’s recognition of private conspiracies and on interstate travel; the dispute over its reach was sufficiently substantial to invoke federal jurisdiction. The Court’s rejection of that theory does not retrospectively make the litigation frivolous. Gibbs therefore remains the source of authority for the related state claims in this action, which began before §1367’s enactment. Those claims were actually adjudicated, and petitioners cannot obtain dismissal of the entire action simply by prevailing on the federal merits. Whether the remaining state judgments and the proper exercise of pendent jurisdiction support the injunction’s particular scope requires further consideration below. The Court neither establishes a new jurisdictional basis nor commands retention of every unsuccessful federal case’s state claims.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678 — applied; substantial federal jurisdiction differs from failure on the merits.
- United Mine Workers v. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 — preserved and applied to pendent jurisdiction in this pre-§1367 action.

#### Federal fees fall while independent state relief requires examination

**Holding and operative rule:** The existing §1988 fee-and-cost award cannot stand on the rejected §1985(3) liability and is vacated. The injunction returns for examination of its state trespass/public-nuisance foundations, jurisdiction and lawful scope; federal merits failure alone neither sustains it nor requires its dissolution.

**Authority:** Scalia Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters. Souter agrees to remove the present federal basis but would additionally direct consideration of hindrance.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that relief must rest on the legal ground that supports it. The fee-and-cost award rested on success under §1985(3), and that adjudicated basis fails today. Farrar recognizes prevailing-party eligibility when a plaintiff has an enforceable federal nominal-damages judgment, while leaving reasonable amount distinct. These plaintiffs retain no successful deprivation-clause judgment; Farrar’s amount inquiry cannot preserve this award. The result is not a determination that all state-law success in civil-rights litigation can never support fees under any circumstances. The injunction presents a separate issue because the District Court also entered state trespass and public-nuisance judgments. Gibbs prevents treating the federal merits loss as an automatic jurisdictional eraser, but it does not establish that every term of this injunction follows from state law. The lower court must identify the independent authority and permissible scope of continued relief. No replacement fee, new damages award, broadened injunction or predicted result on remand is ordered.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Farrar v. Hobby, December 14, 1992 — distinguished; enforceable federal nominal relief differs from reversal of the only adjudicated federal liability basis.
- Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 — applied; pendent adjudication and support for particular relief remain distinct.

#### The unadjudicated hindrance theory is not resolved here

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court does not adjudicate liability under §1985(3)’s separate clause concerning conspiracies to prevent or hinder constituted state authorities from giving or securing equal protection. That theory was not adjudicated below or fairly included in the granted deprivation-clause questions, and no finding establishes the required purpose to hinder state protection; the remand does not direct liability under that theory.

**Authority:** Scalia Part V, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Kennedy and Thomas: five direct supporters control scope and mandate. Ultimate substantive requirements of the separate clause remain undecided.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a distinct, undeveloped statutory theory cannot be converted into an adjudicated basis for relief on this review. The lower courts resolved deprivation-clause travel liability and state claims, not a purpose to prevent or hinder state protection. The District Court’s account of demonstrators outnumbering police describes effects and enforcement capacity; it does not itself supply the omitted finding of conspiratorial purpose. Respondents sought an opportunity to develop the theory, while petitioners disputed its pleading and availability. The question-presented limits preserved in Yee and Kamen’s distinction between a claim and additional arguments do not permit resolution of that separate claim here. The Court does not infer that the clause incorporates every requirement of the deprivation clause, nor that it dispenses with them. A later effort to pursue a properly pleaded and supported theory remains subject to ordinary procedural and substantive requirements. No such claim succeeds or fails on its merits today.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Yee v. City of Escondido, April 1, 1992 — applied; a distinct claim outside the grant differs from additional argument on a presented claim.
- Kamen v. Kemper Financial Services, Inc., 500 U.S. 90 — applied at that scope; additional legal arguments do not erase claim limits.

The full scope of Griffin’s nonracial class category and whether these blockades show invidious sex-based animus remain unresolved. Scalia Part III has four supporters and no controlling alternative holding. No Marks synthesis is needed because five direct joins support each holding. The Court does not decide whether §1985(3)’s text independently authorizes injunctive relief on a successful federal claim.

Effective January 13, 1993, this private-blockade record fails the deprivation clause’s targeted-private-right requirement. Constitutional abortion liberty does not itself supply that element. Federal liability and its fee basis fail; original jurisdiction survives and independent state relief requires examination. The Court does not decide whether women are a qualifying class or whether opposition to abortion proves or excludes sex-based animus. #### 1993-01-13 — Crosby v. United States

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Crosby_v_United_States_merits_1993-01-13.md).

### Trial cannot begin under the voluntary-absence exception without initial presence

**Holding and operative rule:** Rule43 does not permit a criminal trial to begin in a defendant’s absence merely because that absence is voluntary; its voluntary-absence continuation exception requires that the defendant was initially present when trial began. Crosby’s convictions from such a trial must be set aside and the case remanded for proceedings complying with the rule; no constitutional minimum or change to another enumerated exception is decided.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s opinion of the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine participating Justices adopt the rule and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the rule’s express boundary governs even when a defendant deliberately flees. Rule43 begins with a requirement of presence and limits exceptions to those the rule itself supplies. The continuation provision applies to a defendant initially present who voluntarily absents himself after trial begins. Diaz permits completion of an ongoing trial following such a departure; Taylor applies that same rule without requiring an additional warning that the trial will continue. Neither decision authorizes beginning the trial of an absent defendant. The Government’s burden of rescheduling witnesses and proceedings does not create an additional exception. Initial presence also gives a workable assurance that a later departure abandons a proceeding the defendant knows is under way. The district court’s finding of knowing flight therefore does not save this trial. Reversal requires a lawful proceeding; it does not excuse flight or decide what separate sanctions may be available. Because the rule resolves the case, the Court leaves the constitutional question open.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Diaz v. United States, 223 U.S.442 — applied at its actual scope: voluntary departure permits continuation after initial presence, not commencement in absence.
- Taylor v. United States, 414 U.S.17 — applied: no special warning is required for voluntary midtrial absence; its initial-presence premise remains essential.
- United States v. Crosby, 917 F.2d362 — rejected on Rule43: practical burdens and knowing pretrial flight cannot replace the rule’s express commencement condition.

Effective January 13, 1993, the Court settles Rule43’s initial-presence boundary and rejects an implied voluntary-pretrial-flight exception. Other enumerated Rule43 exceptions, independent lawful sanctions for flight and constitutional questions remain unchanged or unaddressed. No constitutional waiver or general harmless-error doctrine is announced.

#### 1993-01-19 — Dobbs v. Zant

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Dobbs_v_Zant_summary_merits_1993-01-19.md).

#### Accurate transcript before law-of-the-case review

**Holding and operative rule:** When a recovered capital-sentencing transcript materially contradicts the factual account supporting an earlier habeas decision, an appellate court may not exclude it without justification and then invoke that exclusion to defeat its own manifest-injustice exception to law of the case. On this record, delay substantially caused by the State's erroneous representations that no transcript existed does not justify exclusion; the transcript must receive consideration through proper further proceedings.

**Authority:** The per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter; six of nine participating Justices adopt this rule and the remand. Scalia and Thomas concur only in the judgment; White would use plenary review.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appellate court must consider the actual argument before deciding whether its earlier ruling rests on a materially mistaken premise. Gardner identifies the importance of a complete record in capital review, and Gregg recognizes Georgia's transcript requirement as a safeguard against arbitrary sentencing. Here both federal courts relied on counsel's account when no transcript was thought available. The recovered transcript calls that account into serious question. The appellate court excluded it without explanation and then treated its absence as preventing application of the manifest-injustice exception. That circular treatment supplies no sufficient procedural justification. The State's erroneous assertions substantially explain the delay, and the District Court recognized Dobbs's legitimate reliance on them. The State's interest in finality does not require preserving an avoidable factual error. Strickland's performance and prejudice requirements remain for application below; the transcript's significance is not itself a finding of ineffective assistance. The Court leaves proper procedural implementation and any assessment of harmlessness to the courts familiar with this record.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349 — its plurality's complete-record concern supports this confined correction; no broader plurality proposition becomes a holding by citation.
- Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 — its joint opinion's approval of complete capital-review records supplies the reliability rationale; Georgia's entire sentencing scheme is not reexamined.
- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 — performance and prejudice remain unchanged and undecided here.
- Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, May 4, 1992 — preserved; its Townsend deliberate-bypass rule for inadequate state factual development does not displace the distinct record-correction question or independent claim bars.

Effective January 19, 1993, the described circular exclusion of a material recovered transcript cannot sustain this law-of-the-case refusal where the State substantially caused its delayed discovery. The lower courts retain the ineffective-assistance merits and proper procedural implementation. Neither transcript contradiction nor this remand establishes prejudice.

**Holding and operative rule:** Herrera's delayed and materially inconsistent affidavits, considered with the identification, physical and documentary evidence supporting his convictions, do not establish entitlement to the requested federal habeas relief or justify the district court's innocence-based stay, even assuming an exceptionally persuasive post-trial showing could constitutionally bar execution. #### 1993-01-25 — Graham v. Collins

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Graham_v_Collins_merits_1993-01-25.md).

#### The youth and background claims apply law settled before finality

**Holding and operative rule:** Teague does not bar Graham's claim that Texas's submitted sentencing questions prevented effective use of his youth and unstable background as material mitigation independent of deliberateness or future danger. Jurek's conditional approval, Lockett and Eddings dictated that functional requirement before his conviction became final on September 10, 1984; no new procedural-rule exception is created.

**Authority:** Parts I–III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor; five Justices adopt this prefinality-law holding, its application to youth and background, and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that applying an existing protection to these facts does not announce a new rule. Teague and Butler protect reasonable reliance on law at finality; they do not require an earlier case with identical evidence. Jurek approved the Texas scheme on the understanding that relevant mitigation could receive effective consideration. Lockett rejected legal barriers to mitigating aspects of character, record and offense, and Eddings expressly protected youth and disturbed background. Those decisions preceded Graham's final judgment. Penry later applied the same duty when the special issues failed to express a material mitigating judgment; it did not create that duty. The State's reliance on Jurek is substantial, but facial approval subject to effective mitigation cannot authorize its material exclusion in a particular application. Franklin's treatment of prison conduct and Saffle's rejection of unrestricted sympathy do not negate the preexisting requirement. Stringer confirms that a later application need not create new law merely because judges disagree. The Court reaches these claims without invoking either Teague exception.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, and Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407 — applied; the relevant result must be dictated at finality, not merely supported by a later plausible extension.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — preserved as qualified approval; it does not immunize every application of the special issues.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied as the pre-1984 effective-mitigation rule.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied as recognition of that existing rule, not as law inserted retroactively into 1984.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992 — applied at its existing Teague scope; disagreement alone does not establish novelty.
- Franklin v. Lynaugh, 487 U.S. 164, and Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484 — distinguished; evidence whose mitigating use fits the questions, and unrestricted sympathy, differ from material culpability mitigation excluded by them.

#### Youth requires an effective route for its independent mitigating force

**Holding and operative rule:** When the record supports a material mitigating judgment about culpability that the authorized sentencing answers cannot express, a reasonable likelihood that the submitted mechanism prevents that use requires an effective corrective instruction or procedure. Graham's youth at seventeen has that independent significance notwithstanding deliberate conduct or predicted future danger, and the three submitted special issues provide no adequate route for it here.

**Authority:** Parts II and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground for sentencing relief and its conditional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prediction of future violence does not answer every question about a young offender's culpability. Eddings recognizes immaturity, inexperience and undeveloped judgment as material mitigation. A juror could accept those considerations while finding that Graham deliberately killed and might remain dangerous. Jurek's approval therefore does not end the inquiry: the permitted answers must actually allow the material mitigating judgment. The provocation question supplies no route for youth's independent significance either. Texas correctly observes that youth may predict rehabilitation, and the jury could consider that prediction. But permitting one material use does not authorize exclusion of another. Penry confirms this distinction between admitting evidence and allowing its mitigating effect. Boyde's reasonable-likelihood formulation assesses the submitted mechanism; the substantive duty remains the prefinality rule. The Court requires an effective sentencing vehicle, not favorable weight, acquittal or an unrestricted mercy question. It announces no categorical prohibition on executing all seventeen-year-old offenders and does not decide the constitutionality of Texas's later statute.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied to youth's independent culpability significance; jurors may assign weight but cannot be legally prevented from giving it effect.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — its conditional framework remains; this defective application does not establish facial invalidity.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied beyond a permanent-handicap limitation; a mitigating trait need not have only aggravating relevance to future danger to require an effective vehicle.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 — applied as the reasonable-likelihood method of assessing instructional operation, not as the source of a new substantive rule at 1984 finality.

#### The submitted background evidence also requires effective consideration

**Holding and operative rule:** A capital sentencing system may not prevent a jury from acting on the material culpability significance it finds in a defendant's disturbed upbringing merely because that evidence also bears on future dangerousness or lacks a uniquely severe permanent handicap. The evidence of Graham's mother's recurring mental illness and hospitalizations and his shifting family care supports such a mitigating judgment, which the submitted special issues cannot adequately express; this independently requires the same sentencing remedy.

**Authority:** Parts III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor; five Justices adopt this independent background-evidence ground and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the sentencing jury must have a lawful way to act on the material mitigating significance of this upbringing. Eddings protects consideration of disturbed family background, particularly for a young defendant; it does not demand proof of a uniquely severe permanent handicap. Graham's grandmother described his mother's repeated hospitalization and his movement between family caregivers. The jury could find that history reduced his culpability without denying that the killing was deliberate or that future violence was probable. The provocation question does not express that judgment. The State argues that the favorable testimony about his behavior shows no lasting harm and that the evidence can support rehabilitation. Those arguments properly address weight, but cannot establish that every material mitigating use fits the authorized answers. Penry requires an effective means of expressing the supported culpability judgment; admission alone is insufficient. The Court neither finds a psychiatric disability nor requires jurors to infer a particular causal effect. It requires a lawful opportunity for sentencing assessment and leaves the weight to the new sentencer.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 — applied to relevant background proffered as a basis for less than death.
- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied; disturbed upbringing cannot be excluded from mitigating effect as a matter of law.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied; neither a uniquely severe permanent handicap nor complete absence of relevance to the special issues is an exclusive trigger.

#### Positive character as an independent ground

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The separate contention that Graham's household assistance, religious observance and financial support of his children independently require additional sentencing relief is rejected by all nine Justices, on different grounds. Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter regard its asserted independent culpability significance as insubstantial on this record and its future-behavior use as available under the second issue; White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would bar the requested mitigation relief under Teague. Stone-Zsela joins the denial of this independent component in the judgment only. These grounds do not yield a common general rule or a Marks subset; the youth and background holdings independently require sentencing relief.

**Limits:** No universal rule about positive character evidence or its value apart from the asserted culpability and future-danger uses is decided. The jury remains free to hear constitutionally relevant evidence at the new sentencing proceeding. Neither denial of this independent ground nor the sentencing remedy disturbs the murder conviction.

Effective January 25, 1993, Texas's former special-issue mechanism is unconstitutional as applied to the material culpability significance of Graham's youth and disturbed background, and relief on those grounds is not new law under Teague. Lower courts must ask whether supported material mitigating use can actually receive effect, using the reasonable-likelihood instruction inquiry; mere admission or some relevance to future danger is insufficient. This is no categorical fourth-question requirement, entitlement to favorable weight, facial invalidation, or juvenile-execution prohibition. The positive-character component yields no new general controlling rule.

#### 1993-01-25 — Zafiro v. United States

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Zafiro_v_United_States_merits_1993-01-25.md).

### Petitioner-specific omission on appeal

**Holding and operative rule:** When Gloria Zafiro did not pursue her severance denial on appeal and the court of appeals did not decide her personal claim, the Court declines to consider that claim without a supported exceptional reason; her codefendants’ preservation supplies none. Her judgment remains undisturbed on that prudential basis, with no merits rejection, jurisdictional bar or finding of intentional constitutional waiver.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; eight participating Justices adopt the explanation. Stevens agrees in the disposition but does not join the Court’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that review must track each petitioner’s actual appellate claim. Youakim states the ordinary practice against deciding questions neither raised nor resolved below, while allowing exceptions supported by the case. Williams permits review when the court of appeals actually decided an important federal question despite a party’s failure to renew opposition to binding circuit precedent. That circumstance is absent here. The Seventh Circuit considered the severance challenges of Garcia, Soto and Martinez, but addressed a different sufficiency question for Gloria Zafiro. Her trial motion and appeal from conviction do not establish appellate presentation of every possible objection. Nor does considering her testimony as part of Martinez’s defense restore her personal claim. No exceptional reason for doing so is shown. The Court therefore leaves her judgment undisturbed without deciding whether her severance denial was substantively correct. The separate sufficiency question, ineffective assistance and possible collateral proceedings remain outside this decision.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Youakim v. Miller,425 U.S.231 — applied: nonreview is ordinary practice, not an inflexible jurisdictional rule.
- United States v. Williams,May4,1992 — distinguished at its stated scope: a passed-upon question may be reviewed; Gloria’s personal claim was not passed upon.
- Yee v. City of Escondido,April1,1992 — applied: an unrelated sufficiency question does not enter the granted severance review.

### Concrete prejudice and effective relief under Rule14

**Holding and operative rule:** For properly joined defendants, mutually antagonistic defenses alone do not require severance; the district court evaluates whether a joint trial presents a serious risk of compromising a specific trial right or preventing a reliable individualized verdict, and chooses effective relief in its sound discretion. Severance is not required for every showing of prejudice if instructions or other safeguards adequately cure the risk, but instructions are not presumed to cure every risk; Garcia, Soto and Martinez have shown no uncured risk requiring separate trials here.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; eight Justices adopt the rule and its application. Stevens’s narrower concurrence supplies no join in this general formulation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the prejudice inquiry concerns the trial’s actual operation, not the label attached to a defense. Richardson recognizes the legitimate benefits of joint trials and the ordinary effectiveness of properly directed instructions. Bruton preserves a different result where a specific confrontation danger cannot be cured in that way. Rule14 likewise reaches serious threats to reliable individual verdicts even without a separate constitutional violation. Relevant risks include prejudicial evidence admissible only against another defendant, complex proof involving markedly different culpability, or essential exculpatory evidence unavailable in a joint trial; those examples do not exhaust the inquiry. Here the petitioners rely on blame shifting rather than a demonstrated impairment of a trial right or reliable assessment. The instructions kept the prosecution’s burden separate for each defendant and distinguished evidence from argument. A better chance of acquittal in isolation is not itself cognizable prejudice. Because these safeguards suffice on this record, denying the three preserved severance motions was within the district court’s discretion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Richardson v. Marsh,481 U.S.200 — applied for joint-trial benefits and the ordinary effectiveness of instructions, not a universal cure.
- Bruton v. United States,391 U.S.123 — preserved: a specific confrontation risk may require exclusion, redaction or severance when instructions are inadequate.
- United States v. Lane,474 U.S.438 — applied only to Rule14’s discretion over effective prejudice relief; no Rule8 misjoinder ruling.
- Schaffer v. United States,362 U.S.511 — applied to adequate individualized jury instructions on this record.

Effective January25,1993, Rule14 receives the stated serious-risk and tailored-relief formulation; neither mutual antagonism nor some prejudice imposes an automatic severance duty. Review remains for abuse of discretion, with independent rights protected. The prudential preservation application leaves Gloria’s personal claim undecided. Rules8 and43, proof beyond reasonable doubt and confrontation law remain distinct and unchanged. No Marks synthesis is required.

#### 1993-01-25 — Spectrum Sports v. McQuillan

Authority: [Canonical Decision Record](records/Spectrum_Sports_v_McQuillan_merits_1993-01-25.md).

### Conduct and intent cannot replace dangerous probability

**Holding and operative rule:** Attempted monopolization under Sherman Act §2 requires anticompetitive or predatory conduct, specific intent to monopolize, and a dangerous probability of achieving monopoly power, evaluated through the relevant product and geographic market and the defendant’s economic power there. Conduct may support intent and other permissible evidentiary inferences, but neither conduct nor intent eliminates independent proof of dangerous probability; no categorical small-firm immunity or prohibition of circumstantial proof follows.

**Authority:** White’s opinion of the Court for Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas; nine Justices adopt the rule and its explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that injury to a rival cannot stand in for the threatened monopoly that the statute addresses. Swift requires proximity to successful monopolization, not merely an intention to achieve it. Copperweld explains why unilateral competition is governed by that boundary: forceful competitive conduct must not become unlawful simply because it harms another firm. The Ninth Circuit’s Lessig approach permits the jury to infer both intent and dangerous probability from unfair conduct without establishing a market or the defendant’s power within it. That removes an element rather than identifying evidence that satisfies it. The McQuillans may rely on circumstantial evidence, but its force must be evaluated against actual competitive alternatives and economic power. Kodak permits a single-brand market when evidence warrants one; it does not presume power from a proprietary product or a terminated distributorship. The Court therefore rejects the challenged instruction while leaving the relevant market, probability of success and ultimate liability for proper determination below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Swift & Co. v. United States,196 U.S.375 — applied: dangerous probability requires proximity and degree beyond intent alone.
- Copperweld Corp. v. Independence Tube Corp.,467 U.S.752 — applied: unilateral conduct must actually monopolize or dangerously threaten monopolization for these §2 offenses.
- Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Technical Services,June8,1992 — preserved at its stated scope: evidence may establish a single-brand market and power; no presumption replaces separate attempt elements.
- Lessig v. Tidewater Oil Co.,327 F.2d459 — rejected insofar as its conduct-based inference dispenses with market and dangerous-probability proof.

### The undifferentiated verdict requires further proceedings

**Holding and operative rule:** The judgment against these petitioners cannot stand on a §2 verdict that may rest solely on an attempted-monopolization instruction dispensing with dangerous-probability proof, when the appellate affirmance rests on that legally erroneous route. Reverse and remand for application of the correct elements and consideration of any properly preserved independent grounds; this decision establishes neither defeat nor success on those grounds and orders no automatic new trial or judgment for petitioners.

**Authority:** White’s unanimous opinion; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas adopt the remedial holding and explanation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the ambiguity in this verdict matters because one submitted route was legally defective. The jury’s finding allowed monopolization, attempted monopolization or conspiracy to monopolize, without specifying which it accepted. The court of appeals sustained the judgment on the attempt theory and did not resolve the challenges to independent RICO and state-law grounds. Sunkist Growers supplies the applicable reason for reversal where a general verdict may rest on a legally erroneous route. Griffin’s distinct treatment of inadequate evidence on a legally valid criminal object does not rescue an instruction that removes an element. The Court cannot choose a different ground the jury might have accepted and treat that possibility as an adjudicated basis for affirmance. Nor does correcting the instruction establish that respondents must lose every remaining claim. The appropriate remedy is further proceedings in the court of appeals under the proper elements and whatever preserved independent grounds remain available.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sunkist Growers, Inc. v. Winckler & Smith Citrus Products Co.,370 U.S.19 — applied: the possibility of reliance on a legally erroneous submitted ground requires reversal here.
- Griffin v. United States,December3,1991 — distinguished at its stated scope: its factual-insufficiency rule does not validate an element-removing instruction or decide the unreviewed civil claims.

Effective January25,1993, dangerous probability in a relevant product and geographic market remains an independent element, now expressly foreclosing Lessig’s substitute inference. Kodak’s empirical market inquiry and absence of automatic patent or brand power remain controlling. No market-share minimum, competitor-protection tort, small-firm immunity or general supply duty is created. Proof of antitrust injury and other elements of recovery remains necessary under existing law but is not separately resolved in this grant. The remand does not decide RICO, California claims or other parties’ judgments.

#### 1993-01-12 — Puerto Rico Aqueduct & Sewer Authority v. Metcalf & Eddy, Inc.

Blackmun joins the Court’s procedural decision while retaining his view, expressed in his Atascadero dissent, that the Constitution does not support the full breadth of the Court’s state-immunity doctrine. He accepts immunity in the narrower class of state-law actions by another State’s or a foreign country’s citizen in federal court; that protection is enough to justify immediate review, independently of whether this particular claimant ultimately qualifies.

Stone-Zsela concurs only in the judgment. Cohen and Mitchell supply the procedural result because a substantial, conclusively denied claim to protection from suit cannot be restored after trial. Sovereign-dignity reasoning is unnecessary to that conclusion. Frivolous or nonconclusive assertions remain subject to ordinary screening. He leaves substantive immunity, PRASA’s status, waiver, abrogation, prospective-officer relief and alternative contract remedies open.

Stevens dissents. Under Van Cauwenberghe and Catlin, an objection to federal jurisdiction ordinarily awaits final judgment. He regards this state-immunity claim as a choice-of-forum defense, unlike officials’ protection against the distracting threat of liability that supports Mitchell. Allowing piecemeal review imposes costs on the claimant and courts that a governmental dignity interest does not justify. He would affirm dismissal without resolving immunity.

#### 1993-01-12 — Commissioner v. Soliman

Source: published writings preserved in the [Decision Record](records/commissioner-v-soliman-merits-1993-01-12.md); each separate position remains noncontrolling beyond the propositions adopted by the controlling coalition.

Blackmun joins the Court and emphasizes that a deduction must satisfy the statutory conditions. Soliman’s only office may be important without being his principal business place; the bulk of professional service, time and remuneration arises at the hospitals. Papers and telephone calls do not themselves establish the patient-meeting branch. His concurrence creates no separate Court rule.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, concurs in the judgment. He would ordinarily locate the principal place where the taxpayer delivers the services or goods for which he is paid. Here all anesthesiology services occur at hospitals, so that inquiry would end. When the home itself is one of multiple places of delivery or income production, he would use a totality inquiry guided by relative importance and time. His approach thus has an escape valve, but he regards the Court’s general two-consideration inquiry as unnecessarily uncertain where the service-delivery facts already resolve the case.

Stevens dissents. He would treat the sole office maintained for management of this self-employed practice as its principal place of business. Exclusive regular use, the necessity of administration and absence of other office space satisfy the statute’s antiabuse purpose. He considers the three statutory alternatives independent and objects that heavy service-delivery emphasis risks collapsing principal business location into the patient-meeting branch. He would affirm the deduction; his headquarters construction does not control.

#### 1993-01-12 — Rowland v. California Men’s Colony, Unit II Men’s Advisory Council

Source: published writings preserved in the [Decision Record](records/rowland-v-california-mens-colony-merits-1993-01-12.md); each separate position remains noncontrolling beyond the propositions adopted by the controlling coalition.

Kennedy joins the Court. He considers it permissible to ask whether the Dictionary Act’s broad meaning can work within the particular statute; that contextual inquiry need not be illegitimate policymaking. But authorized affidavits, an actual resources inquiry and separate counsel rules supply a workable application here. He therefore agrees that practical difficulties do not overcome the inclusive definition or justify categorical exclusion. His concurrence does not guarantee the Council relief on remand.

Souter, joined by White, O’Connor and Scalia, dissents. He regards four features together as sufficient contextual indication that §1915 addresses natural persons: the possibility of proceeding without requested counsel, language of personal poverty, the applicant’s own sworn assertion and its perjury deterrent, and the absence of an organizational means standard. A permissive counsel provision fits individuals able to appear on their own, while entities require licensed counsel. Ordinary personal-indigency measures do not resolve which corporate or member resources count, and agency affidavits do not remove all authorization and deterrence concerns. He would reverse the Ninth Circuit and require denial of this entity’s motion. His natural-person restriction does not control.

#### 1993-01-13 — Nixon v. United States

Source: published writings preserved in the [Decision Record](records/Nixon_v_United_States_merits_1993-01-13.md); each separate position remains noncontrolling beyond the propositions adopted by the controlling coalition.

**Stevens, concurring, no joins.** Stevens joins the Court’s opinion and emphasizes the constitutional decision to place impeachment in the Legislative Branch. That allocation deserves respect in evaluating this ordinary procedural objection. He sees no occasion to assume that the Senate would abandon its constitutional responsibility. His additional institutional emphasis supplies no holding concerning a future violation of an express command.

**White, joined by Blackmun, concurring in the judgment.** They would decide the claim on the merits. Powell requires courts to interpret the limits of a constitutionally assigned power; assigning the Senate the trial does not necessarily make the Senate the final interpreter of the duty to conduct one. Review does not require imposing every criminal-trial formality. The committee developed a record, the full Senate received it and heard the parties, and the full Senate made the decision. In their view that process satisfies the constitutional requirement to try Nixon. They reject the premise that possible disruption or interbranch disagreement independently removes ordinary constitutional interpretation from the courts. Their ground supports affirmance but commands only two votes.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment, no joins.** Souter regards the present allocation of evidentiary work as falling within the Senate’s broad responsibility for impeachment trials. The institutional disruption of judicial review reinforces restraint here. He would leave room for judicial intervention in an extraordinary proceeding whose arbitrariness seriously threatens the integrity of the result, such as conviction unrelated to adjudication. This reservation is a separate, noncontrolling position; the Court itself leaves that outer boundary undecided.

#### 1993-01-13 — Bray v. Alexandria Women’s Health Clinic

Source: published writings preserved in the [Decision Record](records/Bray_v_Alexandria_Womens_Health_Clinic_merits_1993-01-13.md); each separate position remains noncontrolling beyond the propositions adopted by the controlling coalition.

**Scalia Part III, joined by White, Kennedy and Thomas; plurality only.** These four would additionally find no class-based invidious animus here. A group defined merely by the activity the alleged tortfeasor opposes is insufficient; opposition to abortion differs from targeting women because of sex. They leave open whether women can be a qualifying class. Their purpose analysis uses Griffin, Scott and Feeney’s distinction between action because of a group’s disadvantage and action despite its effect. They also favor a broader exclusion of purely local obstruction applied without discrimination against interstate travelers. Neither additional ground has five votes.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring in part and in the judgment, no joins.** The independent private-right defect suffices. Opposition to abortion alone neither proves nor disproves sex discrimination; Griffin’s full class boundary need not be decided. He joins jurisdiction, fees, state remand and hindrance nonreach, but does not endorse the plurality’s categorical local-obstruction formulation. Those reservations prevent attribution of the additional grounds to the Court.

**Kennedy, concurring, no joins.** Kennedy joins Scalia throughout. Limited federal coverage does not remove state responsibility to enforce ordinary trespass and obstruction laws. Respect for separate federal and state authority cannot justify tolerating serious unlawful conduct. This observation adds no enforcement order or remand direction.

**Souter, concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part, no joins.** Souter accepts Griffin/Scott as settled statutory constructions and rejects the present deprivation theory and its fee basis. He would consider hindrance separately. A purpose to impede constituted state authorities supplies an additional limiting element and, in his view, need not carry the deprivation clause’s entire class-animus and privately protected-right restrictions. Overwhelmed police warrant further examination, not automatic liability. He disputes exclusion of that opportunity from affirmative remand directions. His view establishes no purpose finding and no successful federal claim.

**Stevens, joined by Blackmun, dissenting.** They would affirm federal relief. Deliberate exclusion from abortion services targets a choice available only to women, and organized blockades impair actual access by interstate patients. Griffin and travel protection reach this purposeful deprivation; equal obstruction of local patients is not a complete answer. Stevens also reads hindrance to reach organized interference with state protection. They would uphold relief or at least allow development of that theory. Neither their interpretation nor the asserted inference of purpose controls.

**O’Connor, joined by Blackmun, dissenting.** They regard organized blockades as deliberate denial of equal legal protection to women seeking lawful medical care. O’Connor’s position, consistent with her Scott dissent join, treats collective coercion preventing a protected class from enjoying its rights as the statute’s concern. They reject characterization of the exclusion as incidental disadvantage and would uphold federal relief; the distinct hindrance provision reinforces their position. This does not convert Casey’s governmental-regulation framework into a freestanding constitutional prohibition on private acts.

#### 1993-01-25 — Herrera v. Collins

Source: published writings preserved in the [Decision Record](records/Herrera_v_Collins_merits_1993-01-25.md); each separate position remains noncontrolling beyond the propositions adopted by the controlling coalition.

O'Connor, joined by Kennedy, emphasizes in a separate concurrence that execution of a person established to be factually innocent would be constitutionally intolerable, but that the difficult question of a freestanding federal remedy need not be decided on this submission. The plurality's account of ordinary habeas practice and Texas's late-new-trial and clemency procedures does not attract White's join and supplies no new five-Justice holding on categorical cognizability or the adequacy of clemency.

White concurs in the judgment alone. Assuming that a persuasive showing of innocence could bar execution despite an expired new-evidence deadline, he would require at least a showing that no rational trier could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt upon the old and new evidence together. He expressly agrees that Herrera's proof falls short for the reasons in the plurality. This does not adopt an established freestanding claim or a definitive burden.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, also joins the plurality and separately rejects a constitutional entitlement to reopen a lawfully completed conviction on newly discovered innocence evidence alone. Townsend, historical procedural practice and finality support his position; the arguendo disposition does not cause him to accept a new constitutional right. His categorical position has two votes.

Blackmun dissents, joined by Stevens and Souter in the legal discussion and proposed remand. He would recognize an independent Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment claim because execution of a person shown to be innocent is itself a constitutional injury. Ford and Johnson show that a valid judgment does not resolve every later objection to execution; Beck connects capital punishment to reliable guilt determinations. He would require the prisoner to show probable actual innocence on the whole evidence, exceeding a mere likelihood that a juror would entertain reasonable doubt. He rejects leaving that constitutional claim conclusively to clemency and would remand for district-court consideration of threshold factual and procedural requirements, without declaring Herrera innocent. Blackmun alone adds his broader criticism of restrictions on capital review; Stevens and Souter do not join that concluding commentary.

Stone-Zsela dissents separately. He would vacate only the categorical exclusion of a properly presented capital-innocence claim, preserving exhaustion and then-governing abuse-of-the-writ rules. His proposed merits burden is clear and convincing affirmative proof, on the relevant old and new evidence together, that the prisoner did not commit the crime for which execution is sought. Ford and Beck support extending substantive protection; Penry's exception for substantive limits on capital punishment would extend to that protected class. This is a proposed extension, not a claim that those cases already decided freestanding innocence. Clemency is additional protection, not a conclusive answer to an established constitutional prohibition. Allegations provisionally credited must be capable of satisfying that burden before factual development becomes material. Townsend and Keeney then retain deliberate bypass, while ordinary counsel negligence alone does not bar a necessary hearing. Any temporary stay requires an express Barefoot/Delo showing; no indefinite stay follows. If innocence is proved, execution for that crime is barred and another sentencing hearing cannot cure the defect. His proposal neither orders acquittal nor disturbs independent lawful custody, revives the Brady claim, changes the trial burden or resolves all noncapital claims.

#### 1993-01-25 — Graham v. Collins

Source: published writings preserved in the [Decision Record](records/Graham_v_Collins_merits_1993-01-25.md); each separate position remains noncontrolling beyond the propositions adopted by the controlling coalition.

White, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, would affirm because reasonable jurists in 1984 could read Jurek, Lockett and Eddings as allowing Texas to channel this mitigation through deliberateness and future dangerousness. In their view a new instruction requirement is not dictated by prefinality law; it neither removes a class of conduct or persons from punishment nor supplies a watershed accuracy procedure, so neither Teague exception saves it. They distinguish Penry's disabling evidence from Graham's evidence supporting rehabilitation and do not adopt the Court's as-applied merits rule.

Thomas also writes separately. He regards Penry's expansive account of mitigating effect as inconsistent with Furman's requirement that capital discretion be structured against arbitrariness and discrimination. He would permit reasonable state definitions of relevance and sentencing criteria while allowing defendants to present constitutionally relevant information. He would overrule Penry to that extent; the Court does not do so. His view is separate from White's sufficient Teague ground.

Stevens concurs while joining the controlling opinion. He regards narrowing eligibility, avoiding vague aggravators, proportionality limits and individualized mitigation as complementary protections. Requiring a sentencer to act on relevant individual circumstances reduces resort to irrelevant considerations such as race; it does not demand an unreasoned preference. His discussion responds to Thomas without creating an additional test or changing the conditional remedy.

Souter's Part IV, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and O'Connor, explains the limited character-evidence disposition. Ordinary chores, religious observance and support for children can inform future dangerousness through the second special issue. On the asserted theory here, their separate bearing on culpability for this offense is insubstantial. The four do not decide the value of such traits apart from the submitted culpability/future-danger arguments. Stone-Zsela joins only the denial of independent relief on that component.
