### McNeil v. United States, No. 92-6033

Merits decision, 1993-05-17

Argued 1993-04-19. Decided 1993-05-17. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit under §1254(1).

Judgment under review: Seventh Circuit, 964 F.2d 647 (1992).

Question: Whether exhaustion completed after an FTCA action begins permits that original action to continue because no substantial litigation has occurred.

McNeil commenced the action on March 6, 1989, before presenting an administrative claim. The agency later denied his claim. The district court dismissed the prematurely filed action, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed. The question whether his later papers themselves commenced a new action was not included in the question accepted for review.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-04-19) and decision (1993-05-17).

Affirmed, 9–0.

On the question presented, the votes were as follows: Affirm: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no opposing vote.

Stevens delivered the Opinion of the Court. Joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 total). Entire opinion; affirmance on the prefiling rule.

An FTCA claimant must present the claim to the appropriate federal agency and receive its final written denial, sent by certified or registered mail, before instituting the action; after six months without final disposition, the claimant may instead elect to treat the inaction as final denial. Exhaustion achieved after filing does not cure the original premature action, even when little litigation has occurred; §2675(a) expressly excepts claims asserted under the Federal Rules by third-party complaint, crossclaim or counterclaim.

This holding is adopted in Stevens’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

The Court holds that the statute fixes the order in which the claimant and the courts must proceed. Presenting a claim and awaiting the agency’s disposition are steps preceding institution of the action, not conditions that may be completed while it remains pending. Hallstrom enforces a comparable statutory sequence and rejects an invitation to replace it with an assessment of practical progress in the litigation. McCarthy preserves the primacy of an express congressional command, while excusing a different, judicially imposed exhaustion requirement where Congress supplied none. Administrative settlement and orderly processing also require a rule that does not turn on how quickly the parties litigate after an unlawful filing. McNeil’s lack of counsel does not authorize a different sequence: Haines concerns reading pleadings fairly, not dispensing with statutory procedure. This conclusion addresses the original action alone. The Court does not decide whether later papers commenced another action or how any limitations defense to that action should be resolved.

Hallstrom v. Tillamook County, 493 U.S. 20: applied to enforce a congressionally fixed prefiling sequence. McCarthy v. Madigan, March 4, 1992: statutory primacy applied; its damages-only BOP exception is distinguished. Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519: liberal pleading construction preserved; it does not excuse the prefiling requirement.

§2675(a) required prior administrative presentation and exhaustion. After this decision, the Court rejects a no-substantial-progress cure of the original premature action. The six-month election and third-party-complaint, crossclaim and counterclaim exceptions remain; new-action and limitations questions remain open.

[Official published report, 508 U.S. 106](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508106/usrep508106.pdf) supports the public record, dates and statutory text. [Lower-court judgment](https://static.case.law/f2d/964/html/0647-01.html) supplies the reviewed proceedings. Statutory terms and case citations are supplied; opinion language is paraphrased, with no unverified quotation offered. The source record does not establish a later filing, later lower-court outcome or additional mandate date.

The Seventh Circuit judgment dismissing the original premature FTCA action is affirmed. No tort liability is adjudicated. Whether later papers constituted a new action, and the timeliness or other defenses applicable to such an action, are not resolved. No remand or special mandate date is ordered. No special mandate date or subsequent filing is supplied.

**End of entry: McNeil v. United States, Merits decision, 1993-05-17.**

### Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation, No. 92-259

Merits decision, 1993-05-17

Argued 1993-03-23. Decided 1993-05-17. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit under §1254(1).

Judgment under review: Tenth Circuit, 967 F.2d 1425 (1992).

Question: Whether Oklahoma may impose the challenged income and vehicle taxes on tribal members without determining their residence in Indian country, and whether only a surviving reservation can qualify.

The Nation challenged income taxes on tribal employees and excise and registration taxes on tribally registered vehicles. The Tenth Circuit protected member employees without deciding their residence. Oklahoma sought delinquent state vehicle assessments when vehicles moved from tribal registration into the state system. The unfavorable nonmember rulings are not reopened by the Nation through this review.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-03-23) and decision (1993-05-17).

Vacated and remanded, 9–0, on the reviewed member income and vehicle-tax components.

On tribal-member income taxes, the votes were as follows: Vacate and remand: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no opposing vote.

On tribal-member vehicle excise and registration taxes, the votes were as follows: Vacate and remand: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no opposing vote.

O'Connor delivered the Opinion of the Court. Joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 total). Entire opinion; both reviewed tax components.

Absent express congressional authorization, a State may not tax a tribal member’s income earned in Indian country when the member resides there; tribal employment alone does not establish that protection. The residence inquiry includes §1151 reservations, dependent Indian communities and allotments whose Indian titles have not been extinguished, including rights-of-way, rather than only an undiminished formal reservation.

This holding is adopted in O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

The Court holds that the place where a tribal member lives is an indispensable part of the inquiry. McClanahan protected a member who both lived and earned the relevant income on the reservation. The lower court therefore could not extend that result to every member employee merely because the employer was the Tribe. But Oklahoma’s competing reservation-only rule is also mistaken. Congress’s Indian-country definition includes dependent communities and qualifying allotments, and Potawatomi confirms the significance of federally protected tribal land beyond a formal reservation label. The residence inquiry can consequently be resolved on an applicable allotment without deciding every contested reservation boundary. Oklahoma identifies no express congressional permission for these taxes; its failure to assume jurisdiction under the applicable Public Law 280 procedures supplies none. Yakima concerns a different, expressly authorized fee-land levy. The Court remands for the missing residence predicates and leaves open any independently supported protection of tribal self-government for members living outside Indian country.

McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission, 411 U.S. 164: applied with both residence and income-source predicates. Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe, 498 U.S. 505: applied against a reservation-label-only geography. County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes, January 14, 1992: distinguished; limited land-tax authorization supplies no income-tax authority.

For tribal members residing in Indian country, the challenged vehicle excise and registration levies fall within the prohibition on state property taxation absent congressional authorization; collecting the same forbidden charges from later purchasers does not save them. Their application to the established residents must be reconsidered on remand; the Court does not decide a genuinely apportioned off-country road-use charge or every tax connected to a vehicle sale.

This holding is adopted in O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

The Court holds that the substance of these levies, not their labels, controls. Moe and Colville prohibit the state vehicle property taxes imposed on protected resident tribal members. Oklahoma’s excise is distinct from its sales tax, and the registration levy is imposed in place of personal property taxation. The State has not shown that either assessment measures the amount of travel outside Indian country. Exempting nonresident vehicles further weakens the claim that these are simply charges for using the roads. Requiring a later purchaser to pay the accumulated amounts for the tribal-registration period burdens the same protected ownership and cannot accomplish indirectly what the State cannot do directly. The ruling nevertheless requires the resident-member predicate absent from the lower court’s analysis. It does not transform tribal registration into immunity for every owner, invalidate a demonstrated and properly tailored road-use charge, or decide the different position of nonmembers.

Moe v. Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, 425 U.S. 463: applied to member vehicle property taxation. Washington v. Confederated Tribes of the Colville Indian Reservation, 447 U.S. 134: applied to the substance of the vehicle levies; genuinely tailored off-country use charges remain distinct.

Congressional authorization, membership, residence and the particular tax remain separate inquiries. Indian country is not restricted to formal reservations. The reviewed judgment must be reconsidered under the actual residence predicates; the broader outside-residence self-government issue remains open. Yakima and Negonsott keep their distinct statutory scopes.

[Official published report, 508 U.S. 114](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep508/usrep508114/usrep508114.pdf) supports the public record, dates and statutory text. [Lower-court judgment](https://static.case.law/f2d/967/html/1425-01.html) supplies the reviewed proceedings. Statutory terms and case citations are supplied; opinion language is paraphrased, with no unverified quotation offered. The source record does not establish a later filing, later lower-court outcome or additional mandate date.

The Tenth Circuit judgment is vacated in the reviewed respects. On remand, determine member residence in Indian country and apply the income-source and vehicle-tax rules to the established facts. A final determination of every historical reservation boundary is unnecessary if qualifying allotments or other Indian country resolve residence. No blanket exemption for all members, relief for nonmembers, or tax refund amount is adjudicated. No special mandate date or subsequent filing is supplied.

**End of entry: Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation, Merits decision, 1993-05-17.**

### Deal v. United States, No. 91-8199

Merits decision, 1993-05-17

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-01. Decided 1993-05-17. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit under §1254(1).

Judgment under review: Fifth Circuit, 954 F.2d 262 (1992).

Question: Whether the second-or-subsequent-conviction enhancement in the applicable §924(c)(1) reaches additional distinct firearm counts adjudicated in the same prosecution.

Deal committed six bank robberies on separate dates in 1990. The jury convicted him on six firearm counts in a single prosecution. The district court imposed five years for the first §924(c) conviction and twenty years for each of the other five, consecutively, totaling 105 years under that provision. The Fifth Circuit affirmed. The convictions and separate robbery sentences are not challenged on this question.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-03-01) and decision (1993-05-17).

#### Judgment

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Additional convictions in the same prosecution trigger the enhanced tier

**Controlling proposition:** Under the applicable §924(c)(1), a conviction means the finding of guilt; a second or subsequent conviction on a distinct firearm count may therefore occur in the same prosecution as the first. The statute does not require commission of the later offense after an earlier conviction, and the enhanced terms remain subject to its consecutive-service command.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute counts convictions, not prosecutions separated by an opportunity to reoffend. In this sentencing provision, conviction denotes the finding of guilt rather than a final judgment already including sentence. Each distinct finding after the first is an additional conviction even when the findings arise in the same proceeding; making the sentence part of the predicate would confuse what triggers punishment with the punishment being imposed. The dissent’s recidivist construction supplies an intervening-offense condition that Congress did not write. Bell and the Court’s earlier firearm cases require attention to ambiguity in the enacted scheme, but they do not direct courts to disregard a meaning settled by this context. The seriousness of the resulting sentence does not establish textual uncertainty or authorize a different statutory sequence. Deal’s six findings thus support one base term and five enhanced terms. The Court does not decide a constitutional proportionality claim or the operation of later legislation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bell v. United States, 349 U.S. 81: residual lenity preserved; no unresolved ambiguity found in this sentencing sequence.
- Simpson v. United States, 435 U.S. 6: distinguished; cumulative statutory punishment question does not settle the present conviction sequence.
- Busic v. United States, 446 U.S. 398: distinguished on the same limited basis; its earlier statutory problem is not reopened.
- United States v. Thompson/Center Arms Co., June 8, 1992: no general new lenity method displaced; its different statutory ambiguity does not control this phrase.

#### Current-Law Effect

The same-prosecution reach of the enhanced tier was open in this Court. After this decision, five Justices authorize it for distinct additional convictions under the applicable statute. Neither dissent is current law. Convictions, consecutive service and the unreviewed sentence remain distinct; no appellate-finality or proportionality rule is announced.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Dissent — Stevens; joined by Blackmun, O'Connor:** Stevens, joined by Blackmun and O’Connor, reads the phrase as a recidivist provision addressing renewed offending after a prior conviction. The long-established operation of repeat-offender statutes and the development of §924(c) matter more than abstractly counting findings of guilt. Bell, Simpson and Busic support resolving the remaining uncertainty against multiplying the aggravated tier in the first prosecution. The dissent objects that the Court’s construction makes the enhancement depend on the prosecutor’s decision to collect counts in one case and produces repeated enhanced punishment without an intervening opportunity to conform conduct after conviction. It would preserve the underlying convictions and consecutive base penalties. This view does not control. Stevens’s discussion invokes an earlier conviction becoming final; Stone’s distinct dissent expressly leaves appellate finality undecided and is not a join in that language.

**Dissent — Stone-Zsela; no other joins:** Stone-Zsela would require a qualifying conviction before commission of the later offense. He acknowledges that the Government’s ordinary ordinal reading is substantial, but reads the phrase in its recidivist setting and statutory development. Bell, Simpson and Busic support disciplined construction without themselves deciding the present phrase. Residual uncertainty is resolved through lenity after considering those tools. The proposed remedy removes only the aggravated tiers, retaining all six convictions, the consecutive-service command and unrelated sentencing components. He does not decide that the prior conviction must be final on appeal and does not adopt a proportionality limit. His separate writing is noncontrolling.

### El Vocero de Puerto Rico v. Puerto Rico, No. 92-949

Reasoned summary decision, 1993-05-17

#### Chronology and Posture

Submitted on the petition; no oral argument. Decided 1993-05-17. On certiorari to the Supreme Court of Puerto Rico under §1258, in a reasoned summary decision.

Judgment under review: Supreme Court of Puerto Rico, AC-90-181, July 8, 1992.

Question: Whether Rule 23(c) may make closure of this trial-like preliminary hearing the default unless the accused requests public proceedings.

A reporter and newspaper challenged denial of access to preliminary hearings under Rule 23(c). The Puerto Rico Supreme Court sustained the rule, relying on local practice and its view of preliminary hearings. The hearings determine whether an accused will be held for trial before a neutral magistrate, with counsel, cross-examination and the opportunity to present a defense. The governing rule allows exclusion of witnesses; that is distinct from blanket exclusion of the press and public. The lower court also recognized opening upon implied waiver by the accused or circumstances making the asserted privacy protection unnecessary. Those qualifications do not impose the constitutionally required burden and findings on the party seeking closure and do not cure the default.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate on submission and at decision.

#### Judgment

Certiorari granted; judgment reversed and case remanded by reasoned per curiam opinion, 9–0.

The lower courts may protect an established overriding interest through specific findings, consideration of reasonable alternatives and a narrowly confined order. No automatic release of every transcript, grand-jury opening, damages award or special deadline is directed. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by and scope | Relationship to judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court, per curiam | Per curiam | Supported by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 total). Qualified access rule and express implementing remand; no separate writing. | Grant review, reverse and remand |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Trial-like preliminary hearings require qualified public access

**Controlling proposition:** The First Amendment’s qualified right of access applies to these adversarial preliminary hearings; the history inquiry concerns this type of proceeding, not only Puerto Rico’s own practice. Closure requires specific findings supporting an overriding interest and a narrowly tailored order after reasonable alternatives are considered; where fair-trial prejudice is invoked, there must be a substantial probability of prejudice that closure would prevent and no adequate reasonable alternative.

**Authority:** Per curiam Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a defendant’s preference cannot replace the constitutional justification for closing a judicial proceeding. Press-Enterprise II applies experience and function to preliminary hearings, and these hearings contain the adversarial safeguards that make public observation useful: a neutral decisionmaker, counsel, cross-examination and an opportunity to present a defense. Globe Newspaper rejects a categorical substitute for the particularized inquiry. A jurisdiction cannot defeat that inquiry merely by pointing to its own tradition of closure, for the relevant experience concerns the kind of hearing across the country. Fair-trial concerns remain substantial, but their existence must be demonstrated with the required probability of prejudice and inadequacy of reasonable alternatives. Witness safety can likewise support a properly justified and limited protective order. Rule 23(c)’s default therefore cannot stand. The Court does not establish access to every pretrial activity, open grand-jury proceedings, or order disclosure of material covered by a valid narrow protection.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Richmond Newspapers, Inc. v. Virginia, 448 U.S. 555: access’s public oversight function applied.
- Globe Newspaper Co. v. Superior Court, 457 U.S. 596: categorical closure rejected in favor of a particularized justification.
- Press-Enterprise Co. v. Superior Court (II), 478 U.S. 1: trial-like preliminary-hearing access and the fair-trial closure requirements applied.

#### Current-Law Effect

Qualified access, not absolute access, governs. The framework is applied to Puerto Rico’s trial-like preliminary hearings; local closure tradition and an accused’s election cannot replace specific justification. The express remand supplies implementation without deciding a particular protective order.

### Commissioner v. Keystone Consolidated Industries, Inc., No. 91-1677

Merits decision, 1993-05-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-02-22. Decided 1993-05-24. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit under §1254(1).

Judgment under review: Fifth Circuit, 951 F.2d 76 (1992).

Question: Whether an employer’s transfer of unencumbered property in satisfaction of pension-funding obligations is a prohibited sale or exchange under §4975(c)(1)(A).

Keystone transferred unencumbered truck terminals and real property to its pension trust and credited their values against minimum funding obligations. It treated the transfers as sales or exchanges for income-tax purposes. The Commissioner assessed excise-tax deficiencies; the Tax Court and Fifth Circuit rejected the sale-or-exchange classification. The stated property values are not contested here. Classification, rather than a new valuation or independently adjudicated exemption, is the question reviewed.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-02-22) and decision (1993-05-24).

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 8–1; Stevens dissents.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whether an employer’s transfer of unencumbered property in satisfaction of pension-funding obligations is a prohibited sale or exchange under §4975(c)(1)(A). | Reverse and remand | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8) | Stevens (1) | The judgment excluding the transfers from §4975(c)(1)(A) is reversed and the case expressly remanded for proceedings under the correct classification and applicable tax conditions. Unencumbered status alone supplies no exclusion. No new valuation, final tax computation, unpresented exemption or automatic second-tier assessment is adjudicated; statutory correction and exemption conditions continue to govern if properly applicable. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The established tax meaning includes property exchanged for release of a funding duty

**Controlling proposition:** In §4975(c)(1)(A), the established tax meaning of sale or exchange includes an employer’s transfer of property in satisfaction of an enforceable pension-funding obligation. An unencumbered transfer meeting that condition is not excluded merely because it is called a contribution.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Part III-A; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress used a familiar tax expression against an established legal background. Hammel recognizes that sale or exchange can encompass a disposition discharging an obligation, even when the transaction does not resemble a voluntary cash sale. Sorenson supports reading repeated tax language consistently where the statutory setting warrants it. Keystone receives release from its funding obligation in exchange for the property transferred, and indeed treated the disposition as a sale or exchange in reporting its own income-tax consequences. Atlantic Cleaners warns against mechanically transferring a meaning between different settings, but the prohibited-transaction provision’s breadth supplies no reason to exclude this exchange. The dissent’s concern about voluntary contributions does not alter the consideration furnished when an existing obligation is discharged. This rationale independently supports classification of the reviewed transfers. It does not decide every gratuitous contribution, settle an unpresented exemption, or calculate the remaining excise-tax consequences.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Helvering v. Hammel, 311 U.S. 504: settled tax treatment of obligation-discharging transfers applied.
- Sorenson v. Secretary of Treasury, 475 U.S. 851: consistent tax-language construction applied.
- Atlantic Cleaners & Dyers, Inc. v. United States, 286 U.S. 427: contextual qualification preserved; it does not justify a different meaning here.
- Commissioner v. Lester, 366 U.S. 299: internal consistency of tax language supports the established-meaning rationale.
- Albernaz v. United States, 450 U.S. 333: prior settled construction informs the meaning Congress selected.

##### Discharge supplies consideration even without importing a technical tax meaning

**Controlling proposition:** Independently of a settled technical tax meaning, transferring property to discharge an employer’s pension-funding duty is an exchange under §4975(c)(1)(A): the plan receives property and the employer receives release from its obligation. Section 4975(f)(3)’s inclusion of property subject to a mortgage or similar lien assumed by the plan or placed by a disqualified person within ten years does not exclude other exchanges supported by consideration.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Parts III-C and III-D; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the actual legal effects of this transfer suffice even without relying on a specialized tax usage. The plan obtains property; the employer obtains satisfaction of a duty it otherwise must perform. Those reciprocal legal benefits constitute an exchange under the ordinary meaning of the enacted words. Keystone’s reading of the encumbrance provision mistakes an express inclusion for an exhaustive definition. Section 4975(f)(3) ensures coverage when a plan assumes a mortgage or similar lien or the disqualified person placed the lien within the stated ten-year period; it does not say that a transfer discharging a separate funding obligation is excluded. The statutory text thus answers the strongest structural objection without relying on policy hostility toward property funding. ### United States Department of Justice v. Landano, No. 91-2054

Question: Whether every source furnishing information to the FBI in a criminal investigation may be presumed confidential under FOIA Exemption 7(D), and what narrower inferences are permissible.

Landano sought FBI files concerning the murder for which he had been convicted. The FBI withheld material under several exemptions. The reviewed question is the showing of source confidentiality under Exemption 7(D), not whether Landano is innocent, whether a Brady violation occurred, or the independent Exemption 7(C) balancing ruling. The lower court rejected the universal presumption and required individualized evidence in terms that may unduly exclude reasonable categorical inferences.

On the question presented, the votes were as follows: Vacate and remand: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9); no opposing vote.

Under Exemption 7(D), a source is confidential when the information was furnished under an express assurance or circumstances supporting a reasonable inference of confidentiality; an assurance of absolute secrecy is unnecessary. The Government bears the burden and cannot presume every FBI criminal-investigation source confidential, but may rely on a supported, rebuttable inference tied to particular circumstances or a properly defined category, including the crime and the source’s relationship to it.

This holding is adopted in O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

The Court holds that the statutory word confidential describes the circumstances of a source’s cooperation, not simply the identity of the investigating agency. A person may expect restricted law-enforcement sharing while reasonably expecting that the information will not become publicly available. The FBI’s sources nevertheless range too widely to support one inference for everyone. Reporters Committee approved categorical treatment under a different privacy provision and does not remove the factual predicate in Exemption 7(D). At the same time, requiring separate direct testimony for every source would disregard circumstances that themselves support the inference. Payment to an informant, the violence of a crime, or a witness’s relationship to it may support an appropriately confined category when the agency explains the connection. The requester remains free to challenge that inference with nonprotected evidence. The Court reserves whether later public testimony waives protection and does not decide the prisoner’s guilt, Brady claim or entitlement to every withheld page.

Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, 489 U.S. 749: distinguished; privacy categories do not establish every source’s confidentiality. John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp., 493 U.S. 146: compilation analysis preserved; it does not eliminate the confidentiality requirement. Department of State v. Ray, December16,1991: distinct Exemption 6 privacy holding preserved.

Exemption 7(D) protects identity when law-enforcement disclosure could reasonably be expected to reveal a confidential source, including an agency, authority or private institution furnishing information confidentially; it also protects information furnished by a confidential source in a criminal investigation conducted by a criminal-law-enforcement authority. The statute separately covers a lawful national-security intelligence investigation; reasonably segregable nonexempt material remains subject to release, but redacting identity alone does not remove protection for covered source information.

This holding is adopted in O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

The Court holds that the two statutory protections must be applied according to their different objects. One prevents disclosure that could reasonably reveal the confidential source; the other protects information furnished by such a source in the specified criminal-investigation setting. Landano therefore cannot obtain protected source information merely by proposing deletion of the source’s name. Nor can the FBI withhold an entire file simply because it contains material from one protected source: confidentiality must first be established and segregation must address material outside the exemption. John Doe Agency requires attention to the statutory law-enforcement compilation requirement rather than the original location of the paper, while Rose preserves FOIA’s disclosure duty for nonexempt material. The present remand concerns FBI criminal-investigation records, not the scope of the separate national-security clause. Protected submissions and in-camera examination may assist the court, but no universal requirement to use either procedure is imposed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The sentencing remand does not prevent review of this completed conviction ruling

**Controlling proposition:** Under §1257 and Cox’s surviving-federal-question category, the separate sentencing remand does not prevent review of the finally resolved federal challenge to Sullivan’s conviction, which will remain whatever lawful sentence follows. Review does not reach the correctness of the penalty-phase relief or create a general exception for interlocutory criminal rulings.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the unresolved sentencing proceedings do not remove the federal conviction question from review. Cox permits review when further proceedings cannot eliminate the already determined federal issue that survives them. Louisiana conclusively sustained guilt against this constitutional challenge, while granting relief for a distinct failure of counsel in the penalty phase. A new sentencing hearing cannot supply the missing constitutionally adequate jury determination of guilt and cannot reconsider the federal ruling sustaining the conviction. The two components must therefore remain separate both in assessing finality and in defining the Court’s mandate. The Court accordingly reaches the harmless-error issue while leaving the defendant’s independently obtained sentencing relief intact.

**Controlling proposition:** When an unconstitutional reasonable-doubt charge vitiates the jury’s governing standard for all factual findings, the defendant has received no constitutionally valid jury verdict of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. **Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the required finding belongs to a properly instructed jury. Winship supplies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard, and Duncan assigns the criminal verdict to the jury. Chapman asks whether a constitutional error contributed to the verdict actually rendered; it does not authorize a reviewing court to make the missing verdict itself. Here the conceded Cage defect reaches the standard governing all findings, so no valid jury determination remains against which to measure the error. Rose involved a separable erroneous presumption and therefore does not establish harmlessness where the entire adjudicative foundation is missing. Fulminante identifies defects of that structure as outside ordinary harmless-error analysis. The strength of the evidence cannot transfer the jury’s constitutional responsibility to appellate judges. The Court reserves different instructional omissions and inaccuracies, accepts the conceded defect rather than reopening it under Estelle, and leaves Brecht’s separate preserved-Doyle habeas rule unchanged.

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-23. Decided 1993-06-01. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit under §1254(1).

Judgment under review: Eleventh Circuit, 957 F.2d 835 (1992).

Question: Whether offering a firearm as consideration for drugs constitutes use during and in relation to drug trafficking under the charged version of §924(c)(1).

Smith offered a MAC-10 automatic firearm and silencer in exchange for cocaine in a transaction with an undercover officer. The exchange was not completed. The firearm count charged use, not carrying. The underlying trafficking offenses included the transaction’s conspiracy and attempt. The reviewed firearm conviction carried a thirty-year mandatory consecutive term; the separate drug convictions are outside this question.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-03-23) and decision (1993-06-01).

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Offering a firearm in exchange for drugs is use within the charged statute

**Controlling proposition:** Offering a firearm as consideration in a drug exchange constitutes using the firearm under the applicable §924(c)(1), even though the firearm is employed as merchandise rather than fired, brandished or otherwise employed as a weapon. The separate during-and-in-relation-to requirement must also be met; the statute is not satisfied by possession alone under this holding.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a firearm is used when it is deliberately employed as the consideration offered to obtain drugs. Perrin directs attention to the ordinary meaning of the enacted word, and Timbers requires the surrounding statute to inform that reading. Use is not limited to the object’s most familiar purpose, and §924(d)’s treatment of firearm transactions confirms that Congress can describe employment in exchange as firearm use. That contextual confirmation does not import the forfeiture provision’s distinct elements or burden of proof. The dissent’s weapon-only reading would add a limitation absent from this criminal provision. The separate relation-to requirement supplies an additional limit rather than a reason to narrow use to discharge or threats. Bass and Moskal preserve lenity after genuine unresolved ambiguity; the Court finds none after these ordinary tools. This ruling concerns the offered exchange proved here, not uncharged carrying, mere possession or later statutory amendments.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Perrin v. United States, 444 U.S. 37: ordinary meaning applied.
- United Savings Association v. Timbers of Inwood Forest, 484 U.S. 365: whole-statute context applied.
- United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, and Moskal v. United States, 498 U.S. 103: residual lenity preserved; no remaining ambiguity found.
- Deal v. United States, May17,1993: conviction sequence remains distinct; it does not decide use.

##### The offered firearm has the required connection to the trafficking crime

**Controlling proposition:** The firearm must have a purpose or effect with respect to the trafficking crime, rather than being present by accident or coincidence; at minimum, it must facilitate or have the potential to facilitate that crime. Offering the firearm as the consideration for the charged drug transaction establishes that relationship here without deciding that facilitation alone is sufficient in every other setting.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; White, Blackmun, O'Connor, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the gun’s role in this transaction was direct. It was the item offered to procure the cocaine, not an object coincidentally present while a separate offense occurred. The phrase in relation to prevents an unrelated firearm use from triggering the mandatory penalty, and the transaction here meets that limit because the proposed exchange itself advances the charged trafficking. The statutory inquiry remains separate from whether the conduct counts as use; neither requirement may swallow the other. Stewart and Long describe the facilitative relationship as a useful minimum, but the Court need not decide its outer boundary when the firearm supplies the proposed payment. Nor does the officer’s failure to complete the barter defeat the nexus to the conspiracy and attempt actually charged. Blackmun’s concurrence confirms that no universal facilitation-only sufficiency rule is adopted. The decision does not sustain the conviction through carrying, which the indictment did not charge.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Stewart, 779 F.2d 538: facilitation formulation used as a minimum connection, not a universal sufficiency rule.
- United States v. Long, 905 F.2d 1572: facilitative relationship formulation applied within the proved exchange setting.

#### Current-Law Effect

This Court had not settled whether merchandise barter is firearm use. After this decision, five Justices hold that the offered exchange is use and has the required trafficking relationship. The exact outer boundary of sufficient facilitation remains open. No carrying, mere-possession, later-amendment or constitutional-sentencing rule is entered.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Concurrence — Blackmun; no other joins:** Blackmun joins the Court’s construction and its application to the offered exchange. He emphasizes that this firearm was tendered as the consideration for the cocaine, so its relationship to the charged trafficking was established without deciding every marginal form of facilitation. He does not understand the opinion’s minimum formulation to make facilitation alone sufficient in every case. That reservation preserves the separate relation-to element and leaves its outer boundary open; it does not withhold a vote from the result or from the Court’s rule for this transaction.

**Dissent — Scalia; joined by Stevens, Souter:** Scalia, joined by Stevens and Souter, reads the entire phrase in its ordinary setting: using a firearm means employing it as a weapon, not merely doing something useful with it. The argument does not restrict weapon use to firing; threats or striking can be weapon employment. The broader transaction language in the forfeiture provision addresses different objects and does not establish the criminal element in §924(c). Treating the gun as merchandise lacks the contextual weapon use charged here, even if trading it assists the drug transaction. The separate relation-to requirement cannot cure that missing element. The dissent would reverse the firearm-use conviction and cannot sustain it on carrying, which was not charged. It does not control.

**Dissent — Stone-Zsela; no other joins:** Stone-Zsela accepts the force of the Government’s instrumental-use reading but finds that the criminal phrase in context addresses employment as a weapon. Perrin and Timbers require contextual ordinary meaning; Bass and Moskal call for lenity only after the ordinary tools leave genuine uncertainty. The independent connection to trafficking does not answer whether the gun was used in the charged sense. Threats, intimidation or striking may qualify without firing, whereas merchandise-only barter does not. He would remove the firearm count and its thirty-year mandatory term and remand for the resulting sentence correction. The separate drug convictions remain, and the remand would not authorize conviction for uncharged carrying or a new trial to cure the rejected legal theory. This position is noncontrolling.

### Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, No. 91-1671

Merits decision, 1993-06-01

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-02-22. Decided 1993-06-01. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit under §1254(1).

Judgment under review: Ninth Circuit, 948 F.2d 607 (1991).

Question: Whether §502(a)(3)’s appropriate-equitable-relief provision authorizes the requested compensatory monetary recovery from an admitted nonfiduciary alleged knowingly to have participated in a fiduciary breach.

Plan participants alleged that the actuary knowingly participated in conduct causing pension losses. Review follows dismissal; allegations of breach, participation, knowledge and causal loss are not findings. Hewitt’s nonfiduciary status is fixed. The restitution ruling is unchallenged, and additional parties and claims were dismissed without a preserved appeal. The Ninth Circuit’s separate revival of the state malpractice claim is not under review. Hewitt expressly disclaimed the alternative argument that §502(a)(3) supplies no cause of action against a nonfiduciary at all.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-02-22) and decision (1993-06-01).

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The reviewed compensatory award is not appropriate equitable relief

**Controlling proposition:** Section 502(a)(3)’s appropriate equitable relief encompasses traditionally equitable categories of relief, not compensatory damages merely because a court of equity could award them in trust litigation. The requested monetary compensation from this admitted nonfiduciary for the plan’s losses is therefore unavailable under that provision; the Court does not bar every monetary equitable remedy or decide the antecedent, expressly disclaimed cause-of-action question.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court; Blackmun, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (5 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted remedy limitation must retain independent force. Curtis and Terry distinguish compensation for loss, ordinarily legal relief, from traditionally equitable categories. Reading equitable relief to include everything an equity court could award in a trust controversy would deprive that limitation of its work and disregard ERISA’s use of different remedial language elsewhere. Firestone directs consultation of trust law where it fits the statute; it does not authorize replacing the statute’s express limit with the full remedial jurisdiction of historical equity. Clews establishes that trust courts could award money, but that fact does not determine the category Congress selected here. Russell reinforces respect for ERISA’s differentiated enforcement structure without itself settling this question. The participants seek compensation rather than a preserved restitution remedy. The Court rejects that claim while leaving the disclaimed antecedent action question, unreviewed remedies and the separate malpractice disposition untouched; no allegations become findings of liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189: traditional legal compensation distinction applied.
- Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers v. Terry, 494 U.S. 558: remedy classification applied; trust forum alone does not decide the statutory category.
- Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101: trust-law guidance preserved subject to express statutory limits.
- Clews v. Jamieson, 182 U.S. 461: equity’s monetary powers acknowledged but distinguished from the statutory category.
- Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell, 473 U.S. 134: enforcement-structure caution applied; its reserved question is now resolved only within this holding.
- United States v. Burke, May26,1992: its fractured judgment supplies no controlling general remedial-range test.
- Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February26,1992: appropriate-remedy presumption distinguished because Congress expressly limited this provision to equitable relief.

#### Current-Law Effect

The availability of this compensatory nonfiduciary remedy remained open. After this decision, five Justices reject it under §502(a)(3)’s equitable-relief limit. The Court does not decide whether a cause against the nonfiduciary otherwise exists, does not prohibit every equitable monetary remedy and does not reopen restitution. Stone’s bounded statutory-coverage and restoration proposals remain noncontrolling. Burke’s actual fractured authority and Franklin’s cause/remedy distinction remain unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Dissent — White; joined by Stevens, O'Connor:** White, joined by Stevens and O’Connor, reads equitable relief in the particular trust-law setting that ERISA incorporates. Firestone makes that background central, and Clews illustrates the traditional power of equity to restore trust losses through a monetary award, including against a knowing participant. The dissent regards the majority’s general legal-versus-equitable categories as an insufficient account of this particular remedy. It argues that the equitable limitation still matters because punitive relief and remedies arising only from the later merger of law and equity were not traditional trust restoration. Russell does not decide the different §502(a)(3) issue. The express redress language and §502(l)’s treatment of knowing participants further support a claim against a participating nonfiduciary without making every professional a fiduciary. This position would remove the categorical dismissal, not establish liability or an amount of recovery. It does not control.

**Dissent — Stone-Zsela; no other joins:** Stone-Zsela would permit traditional monetary restoration for losses caused by actual knowing participation in an ERISA fiduciary’s breach. Firestone supports consulting trust law, and Clews identifies this restorative remedy as equitable; the argument is not that every award once obtainable incidentally in equity becomes available. Russell’s respect for the statutory enforcement structure remains, but does not settle the distinct redress provision presented here. Section 502(a)(3)’s express authority to redress the fiduciary violation supplies the bounded cause; §502(l) offers context without creating an independent private action. Hewitt’s disclaimer is a presentation limit, not a jurisdictional flaw. On remand the participants would still need to prove breach, actual knowing participation and causal loss, and the court would determine the proper amount and recipient within equitable limits. No professional-negligence or punitive claim is authorized. Fixed nonfiduciary status, unchallenged restitution, unappealed parties and claims, and the separate malpractice disposition remain closed. His conclusion on statutory coverage is a dissenting proposition, not an answer supplied by the Court.

### Musick, Peeler & Garrett v. Employers Insurance of Wausau, No. 92-34

Merits decision, 1993-06-01

Argued 1993-03-01. Decided 1993-06-01. On certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit under §1254(1).

Judgment under review: Ninth Circuit, 954 F.2d 575 (1992).

Question: Whether persons jointly liable under the existing implied Rule 10b-5 action may seek contribution, as a bounded incident of that remedy.

Insurers as subrogees of settling securities defendants sought contribution from attorneys and accountants alleged to share responsibility for the underlying fraud. The district court dismissed; the Ninth Circuit reinstated the action. The question here is the existence of contribution under the established implied action, not a determination that any professional actually violated the securities laws or the proper amount of an allocation.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1993-03-01) and decision (1993-06-01).

Affirmed, 6–3.

On the question presented, the votes were as follows: Affirm: Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (6); opposed by Blackmun, O'Connor, Thomas (3).

Kennedy delivered the Opinion of the Court. Joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Souter (6 total). Contribution as a bounded incident of the established implied action.

Thomas filed a dissent. Joined by Blackmun, O'Connor (3 total). Would reject a judicially supplied contribution action.

Persons jointly liable for the same violation under the established implied Rule 10b-5 action may seek contribution, subject to proof of their shared underlying liability and the applicable allocation requirements. The rule is a bounded incident of that received action and does not create general professional liability, an automatic aiding-and-abetting rule, indemnity, or contribution under unrelated statutes.

This holding is adopted in Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (6 Justices). The named Justices form a direct majority for this rule.

The Court holds that defining this incident of the established implied remedy calls for the same confined method used to define its other boundaries. Lampf looks to closely analogous express securities provisions, and §§9(e) and 18(b) provide contribution among persons answerable for comparable misconduct. Blue Chip Stamps and Ernst & Ernst likewise show that the received action must have judicially identified limits rather than an undefined remedial scope. Northwest Airlines and Texas Industries concern attempts to add contribution to different congressionally supplied schemes; they do not settle every incident of this already judicially developed action. The dissent’s new-action objection has force, but the Court concludes that allocation among persons already liable is part of defining that existing liability, not a new substantive prohibition. The insurers must still establish the defendants’ own shared liability and a permissible allocation. Neither participation in a professional engagement nor payment of a settlement proves those predicates.

Lampf, Pleva, Lipkind, Prupis & Petigrow v. Gilbertson, 501 U.S. 350: close express securities analogies applied to define an incident of the received action. Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores, 421 U.S. 723: bounded judicial definition of the established action applied. Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder, 425 U.S. 185: limits on implied securities liability preserved. Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. Transport Workers Union, 451 U.S. 77: different express remedial scheme distinguished; no universal contribution presumption announced. Texas Industries, Inc. v. Radcliff Materials, Inc., 451 U.S. 630: different statutory scheme distinguished. Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, February26,1992: existing-action/remedy distinction preserved; no freestanding professional cause created.

Contribution within the received Rule 10b-5 action was unsettled in this Court. After this decision, six Justices recognize it through closely analogous securities remedies, subject to actual shared liability and allocation. No new substantive violation or universal federal contribution rule follows; the dissent’s authorization objection remains noncontrolling.

Thomas, joined by Blackmun and O’Connor, distinguishes the accepted private action of an injured investor from a new action by a wrongdoer seeking reimbursement. Northwest Airlines and Texas Industries require congressional authorization rather than a judicial preference for fair allocation. The existence of contribution in other express securities sections shows that Congress knew how to provide it; it does not itself enact a contribution right under §10(b). Lampf’s task of defining a limitation period for a received action does not necessarily justify creating a distinct claimant and claim. The dissent would reject the asserted federal contribution right without deciding whether these professionals committed securities violations or fixing anyone’s share of loss. Its disagreement concerns remedial authority, not a general rule immunizing advisers from their own otherwise established liability. It is noncontrolling.
