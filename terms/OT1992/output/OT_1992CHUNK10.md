### Godinez v. Moran, No. 92-725

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-21; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Ninth Circuit, 972 F.2d 263 (1992).

**Question:** Whether the constitutional competence standard for pleading guilty or waiving counsel is higher than the standard for standing trial, and how competence relates to a knowing and voluntary waiver.

**Material record:** Two psychiatrists found Moran competent to stand trial. About two and a half months later he sought to discharge counsel and plead guilty, explaining that he wanted to prevent presentation of mitigation. The trial court conducted a colloquy and expressly found competence and knowing, intelligent, voluntary waivers. Moran referred to prescribed medication; the court did not inquire further into it. A later state postconviction hearing rejected incompetence. The Ninth Circuit relied on the suicide attempt, medication, answers, and desire to waive mitigation in finding an inadequate inquiry under its higher standard. Direct review had already reduced the death sentence for the ex-wife's murder to life without parole; the other two death sentences remained.

**Relief sought:** Nevada’s warden seeks reversal of the conditional-writ judgment. Moran seeks preservation of relief allowing withdrawal of the pleas and proceedings with counsel.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 7–2.

| Component | Disposition | Supporting Justices | Opposing or other positions | Remedy / scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Higher constitutional competence threshold and associated conditional writ | Reverse and remand (7–2) | Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens | Remove the conditional-writ direction based on the higher standard; consider the existing inquiry and actual-waiver claims under the proper doctrines and pre-AEDPA treatment of state findings. Blackmun and Stevens would preserve conditional relief. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A common capacity standard and a separate actual waiver

**Controlling proposition:** Dusky supplies the constitutional mental-capacity standard for standing trial, pleading guilty and choosing to waive counsel: sufficient present ability to consult with counsel with rational understanding, and rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings. A competent defendant may relinquish the rights only through a knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver; competence does not establish that the particular choice was informed and uncoerced.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that mental capacity and the validity of a choice are separate questions. Dusky requires rational understanding, not a bare awareness of the courtroom or the charges. Faretta protects a competent defendant’s informed choice of self-representation without requiring the legal skill of a lawyer. That distinction defeats the claim that the decision to waive counsel necessarily requires professional ability to conduct a defense. It does not relieve the judge of examining the choice actually made. Boykin requires an informed guilty plea, and Zerbst requires a valid relinquishment of counsel. Westbrook’s additional inquiry is preserved through those waiver requirements rather than a second, higher constitutional capacity threshold. The Ninth Circuit therefore cannot sustain its conditional writ on the premise that ordinary trial competence is constitutionally insufficient as a capacity standard. States remain free to provide further safeguards. The Court decides neither competence for execution nor a distinct rule for the later conduct of a trial without counsel.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S.402: the functional competence standard governs each presented choice.
- Faretta v. California, 422 U.S.806: legal skill is distinct from competence to choose representation.
- Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S.238, and Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S.458: actual informed and voluntary waiver remains separately required.
- Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S.150: additional waiver inquiry preserved; a higher constitutional capacity quantum is not required.

##### Existing findings do not eliminate the distinct inquiry on remand

**Controlling proposition:** A further competence determination is required when circumstances give the court reason to doubt present competence; no automatic hearing is required for every plea or waiver. Reversal of the higher-standard ruling leaves the existing claims about adequate inquiry and informed, voluntary relinquishment for consideration under the correct standards and the applicable pre-AEDPA treatment of state findings.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that rejecting the Ninth Circuit’s legal standard does not adjudicate every factual and remedial issue in Nevada’s favor. The trial court expressly found competence and valid waivers, and a later state hearing considered the challenge. Those findings must be addressed, not described as nonexistent. Pate and Drope nevertheless require attention to circumstances suggesting present incapacity, including relevant changes after an earlier examination. The medication and other circumstances therefore bear on the questions the proper doctrines ask; they are neither automatic proof of incompetence nor matters conclusively answered by older reports. Medina leaves that inquiry duty intact, and the Riggins decision does not equate medication with incapacity. The reviewing court must also consider whether any necessary retrospective determination can reliably be made and whether each relinquishment was informed and voluntary. The unraised Teague defense is not introduced here. The conditional-writ direction resting on the higher standard falls; no already-reduced death sentence is restored.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S.375, and Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S.162: reasonable doubt concerning present competence triggers inquiry.
- Medina, June 22, 1992, and Riggins, May 18, 1992: their limited rules are preserved, not enlarged into conclusive findings here.
- Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S.37: the Court may decline to introduce an unraised Teague defense.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, the common Dusky capacity standard and independent waiver obligation govern the presented choices. Inquiry and application remain distinct; the higher-capacity basis of the Ninth Circuit’s conditional writ is rejected. No automatic validation of convictions or sentences, no restoration of the already-reduced sentence, and no new general retrospective-hearing rule follows.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by Scalia, accepts the common standard and separate waiver requirements but relies on the longstanding application of one competence standard throughout the proceeding rather than comparisons of decisional difficulty. That historical rationale is noncontrolling. Thomas, joined by White, O’Connor and Souter, additionally regards the choices involved in a plea as no more demanding than the combined decisions during a trial; four votes do not make that additional rationale controlling. Stone-Zsela emphasizes that prior reports and express findings do not automatically resolve a later informed-choice or renewed-inquiry claim; he would assess reliable retrospective review and pre-AEDPA treatment of findings before selecting further relief. Blackmun, joined by Stevens, would sustain conditional relief: capacity to assist a lawyer does not adequately test capacity to proceed alone, and Westbrook, Massey and Rees require a context-specific inquiry. The recent suicide attempt, depression, medication and answers at the colloquy make reliance on the earlier reports insufficient in their view.

### Heller v. Doe, No. 92-351

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-22; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Sixth Circuit, Doe v. Cowherd, 965 F.2d 109 (1992).

**Question:** Whether Kentucky’s different burdens of proof and family-participation rules for involuntary commitment of persons with intellectual disability and persons with mental illness violate equal protection, and whether family party status violates procedural due process.

**Material record:** Kentucky required proof of dangerousness, likely benefit from treatment, and a least restrictive placement predicate in the relevant commitment proceedings. For the category described in the then-operative statute as “mental retardation,” proof was clear and convincing and guardians or immediate family could participate as parties. For mental illness, proof was beyond a reasonable doubt and that party status was not supplied. The class challenged these differences. The medical and developmental distinctions offered as rationales concern typical onset, diagnostic history, prediction, and the information held by families; they are not individual findings that any class member is dangerous or incompetent.

**Relief sought:** Kentucky seeks reversal of the challenged federal rulings. The committed class seeks affirmance. Other district-court grounds outside the questions under review remain separate.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. Unequal proof burdens invalidated, 5–4; categorical invalidation of family-party participation reversed, 6–3.

| Family/guardian party status: equal protection and categorical due-process invalidation | Reverse categorical invalidation (6–3) | Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Party participation may continue with counsel, effective contest and independent adjudication. Blackmun, Stevens and Souter would invalidate on equal protection and do not reach the independent due-process question. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The lower overall proof protection lacks a rational relation to the disadvantage imposed

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky’s clear-and-convincing burden for involuntary commitment of adults with intellectual disability, compared with proof beyond a reasonable doubt for mental illness, violates equal protection on the preserved rational-basis challenge: the offered diagnostic, prediction and treatment differences do not rationally justify assigning this class the greater risk of mistaken confinement. Addington’s federal minimum remains unchanged; the remedy must address lawful equalization and severability rather than impose a national reasonable-doubt standard or order every class member released.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory distinction must relate to the particular procedural disadvantage. Cleburne permits relevant distinctions between conditions but does not allow a label to substitute for that relationship. A longer history may make diagnosis or some predictions easier. Commitment, however, also requires dangerousness, likely treatment benefit and a least restrictive placement; the interests at stake remain physical liberty and protection from erroneous confinement. Addington explains the allocation of that error risk and supplies a constitutional minimum, not immunity from an equality challenge when a State provides greater protection to another class. The asserted difference in the intrusiveness of treatment likewise does not adequately explain the lower overall safeguard against confinement. Beach does not demand formal findings or perfect empirical fit, and the Court imposes neither requirement. It rejects the proffered relationship, not legislative generalization as such. The appropriate limited remedy must respect severability and the State’s lawful choices, while every continuing confinement still needs an independent lawful basis.

**Precedent treatment:**

- City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S.432: rational relationship to the actual disadvantage required; no new scrutiny tier adopted.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S.418: the clear-and-convincing minimum survives; unequal safeguards remain separately reviewable.
- FCC v. Beach Communications, June 1, 1993: no formal-findings or empirical-certainty requirement imposed; its specific economic justification does not decide this classification.
- Foucha, May 18, 1992: current lawful confinement predicates preserved; noncontrolling equal-protection reasoning is not promoted to law.

##### Family-party participation has a distinct rational basis

**Controlling proposition:** Kentucky may distinguish the two commitment proceedings by allowing guardians and immediate family members to participate as parties for adults with a lifelong developmental disability. Continuing family knowledge rationally supports that participation; the availability of testimony or a less extensive form of participation does not by itself defeat ordinary rational-basis review.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that gathering useful information is different from assigning a person less protection against erroneous confinement. A developmental condition and sustained family involvement plausibly give relatives and guardians knowledge of abilities, daily functioning and available care that assists the proceeding. Parham recognizes the value of family information without giving a relative authority to decide whether an adult should be confined. Cleburne requires a rational relationship, and that informational relationship supports the participation distinction here. Kentucky need not show that every family has useful knowledge or that no less extensive procedure could work. Nor does the existence of mental illness within some families eliminate the permissible general distinction. The Court does not presume that family members always share the adult’s interests. Their preferences or convenience establish none of the statutory grounds for confinement, and the independent adjudicator must evaluate their evidence under the lawful burden. This holding sustains party participation as a category, not every application of it.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Cleburne, 473 U.S.432: relevant differences may support a particular procedural distinction.
- Parham v. J.R., 442 U.S.584: family information may be useful; adult confinement is not delegated to relatives.
- Schweiker v. Wilson, 450 U.S.221: ordinary review does not require the least restrictive legislative alternative.

##### Party participation does not itself deprive the adult of fair adjudication

**Controlling proposition:** Allowing relatives or guardians to participate as parties does not categorically violate procedural due process where the adult has counsel and an effective opportunity to contest adverse evidence before an independent decisionmaker. Relatives may neither supply a substitute for the statutory commitment grounds nor control the decision or suppress the adult’s position.

**Authority:** Kennedy’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the challenged party status must be examined within the actual procedural safeguards. Mathews requires consideration of the private interest, the risk of error and value of safeguards, and the government’s interests. The liberty interest is substantial. Kentucky nevertheless provides counsel, professional examination, an opportunity to testify and present evidence, cross-examination and independent adjudication. Relevant family information can improve accuracy when tested through those procedures. The possibility of adverse family interests requires such testing; it does not establish that adding a party inherently makes an accurate decision impossible. Parham does not permit the State to call an adult’s involuntary placement voluntary merely because a relative requested it. The adjudicator must find the statutory grounds under the lawful burden, regardless of the family’s preference. Proceedings that defeat effective contest or independent decisionmaking remain challengeable. The Court reverses the categorical due-process invalidation without determining that every class member’s hearing was fair.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S.319: private interest, error risk and safeguards, and government interests applied separately from equality review.
- Parham, 442 U.S.584: informational participation distinguished from final decisional control.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, the proof distinction is invalid under rational-basis review, with severability and lawful equalization left for remand. The family distinction and party status survive the categorical challenges subject to independent adjudication and effective contest. Addington, the substantive confinement predicates and the unresolved heightened-scrutiny question remain distinct. Kentucky’s repealed voluntary-admission rule and other unreviewed grounds are not decided.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, would sustain the proof distinction. They regard easier diagnosis, a longer behavioral history supporting prediction, and generally less intrusive treatment as independently sufficient rational grounds. Addington’s minimum is met, and ordinary review permits imperfect generalizations. Souter, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, would also invalidate family-party status: useful information can be obtained through witnesses, while party status adds an adversary able to call and cross-examine witnesses and appeal a refusal to confine. They find no rational connection between that additional burden and the different condition and therefore do not reach due process. Blackmun separately preserves his view that disability classifications and substantial restraints on liberty warrant heightened review, while accepting that the proof distinction fails the ordinary standard used here.

Supreme Court review of this merits event is complete. Determine severability and lawful state choices to equalize safeguards; no national reasonable-doubt rule or blanket release. ### Buckley v. Fitzsimmons, No. 91-7849

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-02-22; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Seventh Circuit, 952 F.2d 965 (1992).

**Question:** Whether prosecutors have absolute immunity from § 1983 damages claims for allegedly fabricating evidence during preliminary investigation and making false statements at a press conference announcing an indictment.

**Material record:** Buckley alleges that prosecutors sought a favorable bootprint identification after other laboratory examinations failed reliably to connect him to the crime, using an expert allegedly willing to fabricate evidence. He also challenges public statements announcing an indictment. He was jailed, tried before a jury unable to reach a verdict, and released after charges were dropped following the expert’s death. The fabrication and wrongdoing allegations are accepted at this pleading stage, not adjudicated facts. The challenged preliminary evidence work preceded probable cause to arrest or initiate proceedings against him.

**Relief sought:** Buckley seeks reversal of the immunity dismissal and further proceedings on his damages claims. The prosecutors seek affirmance of absolute immunity.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded: 6–3 on preliminary investigative fabrication and 9–0 on press statements.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preliminary investigative fabrication | Reverse absolute-immunity dismissal (6–3) | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas | White, Kennedy, Souter | Remand for remaining elements and defenses; allegations are not findings. |
| Press-conference statements | Reverse absolute-immunity dismissal (9–0) | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | No absolute prosecutorial immunity for the press function; constitutional injury and other defenses remain unresolved. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Investigative fabrication is not protected by absolute prosecutorial immunity

**Controlling proposition:** A prosecutor who performs ordinary investigative work to obtain evidence identifying a perpetrator before probable cause has no absolute prosecutorial immunity for alleged fabrication in that function; later courtroom use does not transform the earlier work into advocacy. Genuine evaluation and preparation in an advocate’s role remain protected, and neither all pre-indictment acts nor all post-indictment acts receive a categorical classification.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the prosecutor must justify immunity for the function actually performed. Imbler protects initiating and presenting a prosecution and genuine preparation for those acts. Burns, Forrester and the Antoine decision reject immunity based merely on official title or an activity’s importance to litigation. The complaint describes a joint effort with detectives to secure a boot identification during the preliminary investigation of an unsolved crime. On those allegations the prosecutors were searching for evidence needed to build a case, not preparing an established advocate’s presentation. Their later use of the evidence before a grand jury or at trial does not change the earlier function. The competing view correctly observes that advocacy can include out-of-court preparation, but that principle does not establish the protected function alleged here. The Court does not decide qualified immunity, ultimate constitutional injury or damages. Unreviewed immunity for the actual in-court presentations remains undisturbed, and unresolved allegations about paid or coerced witness statements require initial treatment below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Imbler v. Pachtman, 424 U.S.409: protected advocacy and genuine preparation preserved; ordinary investigation distinguished.
- Burns v. Reed, 500 U.S.478: function-specific limits applied to investigation.
- Forrester v. White, 484 U.S.219, and Antoine, June 7, 1993: function and claimant’s burden control; importance to litigation is insufficient.

##### A press conference does not carry an advocate’s absolute immunity

**Controlling proposition:** A prosecutor’s statements to the press announcing an indictment have no absolute prosecutorial immunity merely because they concern a pending prosecution or cause injury through later judicial proceedings. The ordinary qualified-immunity inquiry and the requirement of an actionable constitutional deprivation remain separate.

**Authority:** Stevens’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that informing the public is not the judicial advocacy function protected by Imbler. The challenged press conference neither initiates a prosecution in court nor presents the State’s case to an adjudicator. Burns requires historical and functional support for absolute immunity, and none supports this out-of-court press function. The Seventh Circuit’s focus on whether injury depends on a later judge’s decision confuses the merits of a damages claim with immunity for the challenged act. A prosecutor’s title cannot convert public statements into judicial advocacy when other executive officials making comparable statements receive no such absolute protection. Tower also forecloses creating a new immunity simply because the Court considers it desirable policy. Rejecting absolute immunity does not establish that defamation alone violates the Constitution, that these statements caused compensable harm, or that qualified immunity is unavailable. Those questions remain for the appropriate proceedings after reversal of the categorical immunity ruling.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Imbler, 424 U.S.409, and Burns, 500 U.S.478: press activity lacks the protected advocacy function.
- Tower v. Glover, 467 U.S.914: policy alone cannot create a new §1983 absolute immunity.
- Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S.800, and Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S.635: separate qualified-immunity law left unchanged.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, the pleaded investigative fabrication and press statements lack absolute prosecutorial immunity. Genuine advocacy preparation and unreviewed in-court presentation immunity survive. No constitutional liability, damages award, witness-immunity change or qualified-immunity revision results.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White and Souter, agrees that press statements lack absolute immunity but views the expert consultation as an advocate’s preparation for presenting evidence. Imbler protects preparatory evaluation as well as the eventual use of testimony; denying that protection, in their view, allows an immunity challenge to be relabeled as a challenge to preparation. They reject probable cause as a universal line between investigation and advocacy and would affirm the fabrication ruling. Scalia joins the Court but emphasizes the claimant’s obligation to establish historical immunity. He also distinguishes immunity from an actionable deprivation: mere manufacture of evidence, without constitutionally harmful use, may not establish a §1983 claim. His observation does not decide that remaining question.

### Shalala v. Schaefer, No. 92-311

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-31; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit, No. 91-3494, judgment reported at 960 F.2d 1053 (1992).

**Question:** When an EAJA fee application is timely following a Social Security remand, and how a sentence-four judgment differs from a sentence-six remand under 42 U.S.C. § 405(g).

**Material record:** The district court entered a sentence-four remand order in 1989 but did not enter the separate judgment required by Rule 58. Benefits were later awarded in the administrative proceedings, and the claimant then sought EAJA fees. The absence of the separate judgment, rather than simply the later benefits award, is material to when the appeal and fee periods began.

**Relief sought:** The Secretary seeks reversal on timeliness grounds; Schaefer seeks to retain the fee award.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed, 9–0 in the judgment.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| EAJA application and reviewed fee award | Affirm (9–0) | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | Application was timely; sustain the reviewed award and complete proper judgment entry where still necessary. No new administrative-service award or reopening of unreviewed fee questions. |

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Sentence-four judgment ends the action and confers prevailing status

**Controlling proposition:** A sentence-four judgment reversing the Secretary’s denial of benefits with a remand ends the §405(g) civil action and makes the successful claimant a prevailing party; the court must enter judgment rather than retain the merits action pending an administrative award. Sentence-six remands instead retain jurisdiction for the statutorily specified proceedings and later return to court.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statute supplies distinct remand routes with different procedural consequences. Sentence four authorizes a judgment affirming, modifying or reversing the Secretary’s decision, with or without remand. Finkelstein and Melkonyan give effect to that judgment and distinguish it from sentence six, which allows a pre-answer remand on the Secretary’s good-cause motion or a remand for new material evidence with good cause for its earlier omission. A claimant who obtains reversal through sentence four has secured judicial relief, not merely a favorable statement during ongoing litigation. Garland’s prevailing-party rule, consistent with the Farrar eligibility holding, therefore applies without waiting for the agency to award benefits. That status does not establish every EAJA requirement or the reasonable amount. Administrative success cannot replace the court judgment as the statutory filing event. The Court preserves the different sentence-six sequence, including return to court and a final judgment after the further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sullivan v. Finkelstein, 496 U.S.617, and Melkonyan v. Sullivan, 501 U.S.89: the two statutory remand routes applied.
- Texas State Teachers Assn. v. Garland Independent School District, 489 U.S.782, and Farrar, December 14, 1992: enforceable judicial relief establishes prevailing status, not automatic fee amount.

##### The missing separate judgment prevents the fee deadline from expiring

**Controlling proposition:** Under the then-effective rules, EAJA’s thirty-day application period follows a properly entered judgment and expiration of the applicable sixty-day appeal period; the absence of the required Rule 58 separate judgment prevents that deadline from expiring. Schaefer’s application is timely although the sentence-four remand was itself a final merits disposition.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (7 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that substantive finality and the formal entry starting appellate time must not be collapsed. Section 2412(d) measures its thirty days from a judgment that is final and no longer appealable. Rule 4(a) gives sixty days to appeal in this action involving a federal officer and requires entry in compliance with Rule 58. The district court’s April 4, 1989 remand resolved the judicial merits but lacked the separate judgment document. Indrelunas gives that formality effect. Mallis permits an appeal without insisting on the document in appropriate circumstances; it does not deem the unstarted appeal period expired against a party seeking fees. The Secretary therefore cannot establish untimeliness from the remand date or the later administrative decision. Proper entry remains necessary where still absent. This conclusion sustains the award reviewed here without importing a later deemed-entry deadline, enlarging recoverable services or reopening EAJA issues outside the appeal.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Indrelunas, 411 U.S.216: separate-document requirement applied as then in force.
- Bankers Trust Co. v. Mallis, 435 U.S.381: ability to take an appeal without the document distinguished from expiration of appellate time.
- Melkonyan, 501 U.S.89: final court judgment, not agency decision, governs the EAJA clock.

##### Hudson does not preserve administrative-fee coverage after an ordinary terminated sentence-four action

**Controlling proposition:** Hudson’s integrated administrative-proceeding fee rationale does not apply to a sentence-four remand properly entered without retained jurisdiction, or to retention of jurisdiction that is challenged. It remains applicable to qualifying sentence-six proceedings in which the court retains the action, subject to EAJA’s other requirements.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Hudson must be read with the statutory structure clarified in Finkelstein and Melkonyan. Hudson involved a remand treated, without objection, as part of a continuing judicial action. Its fee rationale depended on that continuing jurisdiction and the administrative work’s relationship to the judicial case. Sentence four, however, requires a terminating judgment; a court cannot preserve the action simply to postpone the fee clock until benefits are awarded. The argument that effective representation on remand favors broader coverage identifies a policy concern but does not supply authority to retain an action the statute ends. The qualifying sentence-six route remains different because the Secretary returns to the court after further proceedings. The holding neither eliminates Hudson in that setting nor awards new administrative fees in this appeal. Prevailing status after a sentence-four reversal and entitlement to compensation for later administrative work remain separate questions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Sullivan v. Hudson, 490 U.S.877: retained-jurisdiction rationale limited in light of the statutory distinction; sentence-six coverage preserved.
- Finkelstein, 496 U.S.617, and Melkonyan, 501 U.S.89: sentence-four termination controls; statutory retention is reserved for sentence six.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 24, sentence-four reversal/remand confers prevailing status and ends the action, while then-required separate judgment entry and expiration of appellate time govern EAJA timeliness. The Hudson limitation has six direct votes. No later Rule 58 deemed-entry provision or universal administrative-fee entitlement is adopted.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by Blackmun, concurs in the judgment but would preserve Hudson’s broader administrative-fee approach. Representation before the agency after a judicial remand may be essential to obtaining the benefits sought; in their view, EAJA’s purposes support retaining authority to enter the final fee judgment after those proceedings. They regard the distinction drawn for immediate appealability in Finkelstein as insufficient to eliminate Hudson’s protection and would reject the conflicting language in Melkonyan. Their position supports affirmance here but does not control the sentence-four rule. Stone-Zsela joins the finality, prevailing-status and missing-entry holdings without joining the additional categorical administrative-fee limitation.

### United States v. Edge Broadcasting Co., No. 92-486

Merits disposition, 1993-06-25

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-21; decided 1993-06-25. On writ of certiorari to Fourth Circuit, 956 F.2d 263 (1992).

### St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks, No. 92-602

Merits disposition, 1993-06-25

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-20; decided 1993-06-25. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit,970 F.2d487 (1992).

**Question:** Whether a Title VII plaintiff is entitled to judgment as a matter of law once the factfinder rejects all of the employer’s stated nondiscriminatory reasons, or must still persuade the factfinder of intentional discrimination.

**Material record:** Hicks, a Black correctional officer and shift commander, was demoted and discharged. He sued St. Mary's under Title VII and superintendent Steve Long under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for the alleged racial discrimination. Following a bench trial, the district court found a prima facie case, found the employer’s articulated disciplinary explanations unworthy of belief, but did not find racial motivation proved. It suggested personal animosity rather than race. The court of appeals did not complete ordinary clear-error review of that finding because it treated rejection of all offered reasons as dispositive. Whether the alternative explanation is supported and was fairly litigated is distinct from the abstract burden rule.

**Relief sought:** The employer seeks reversal of the automatic-liability rule. Hicks seeks affirmance or review of the adverse factual finding under the correct evidentiary framework.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Pretext alone does not command judgment, and circumstantial evidence need not be supplemented by a special category of proof

**Controlling proposition:** After an employer produces admissible evidence of nondiscriminatory reasons, a proven prima-facie case and rejection of the offered explanations do not automatically command judgment for the employee. Those circumstances may support a finding of intentional discrimination without a mandatory additional category of direct or independent proof; the Eighth Circuit’s automatic-liability mandate is vacated, while no majority here adopts a replacement persuasion allocation.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the appellate mandate cannot stand merely because the employer’s reasons were disbelieved. Title VII addresses the prohibited cause of an employment decision, not false explanation as an independent statutory wrong. McDonnell Douglas structures indirect proof, and Burdine requires the employer to produce admissible evidence rather than persuade at the ordinary explanation stage. That structure does not justify eliminating every opportunity to consider whether discrimination actually caused the action. At the same time, Aikens recognizes the practical force of circumstantial proof: a prima-facie showing and rejection of the employer’s material account may establish discrimination without eyewitness testimony or a separate kind of evidence. The Court therefore removes the compulsory-liability direction and remands. Five Justices do not agree on a new allocation after established pretext. The inherited rules remain governing to the extent not displaced by this narrow holding; neither Stone’s persuasion shift nor the dissent’s automatic-result rule gains controlling force. No finding that personal hostility actually motivated these decisions is made.

**Precedent treatment:**

- McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green,411 U.S.792: indirect framework retained; no automatic liability holding adopted.
- Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine,450 U.S.248: production stage retained; no five-Justice replacement of its persuasion rule.
- Postal Service Board of Governors v. Aikens,460 U.S.711: actual discrimination and circumstantial evidence remain central.
- Hazen, April 20, 1993: prohibited motive remains distinct from general unfairness; no added evidence category follows.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 25, the automatic-liability mandate is removed and the narrow five-Justice holding governs. Stone’s proposed trigger, shifted burden and extra remedial instructions remain published noncontrolling positions. No majority changes Burdine’s persuasion allocation or establishes a universal Title VII/§1983 equivalence. The appellate court proceeds under existing governing law consistent with the limited mandate; the Court does not decide clear error, retaliation, remaining defenses, relief or applicability of the 1991 Act.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia, joined by O’Connor, Kennedy and Thomas, would retain the employee’s ultimate persuasion burden throughout. Rule 301 and Burdine distinguish evidence production from credibility, so a rejected explanation does not revive a compulsory presumption. They regard Aikens as requiring an actual discrimination finding and would review the district court’s finding for clear error under Rule 52(a), without a pretext-plus demand. Souter, joined by White, Blackmun and Stevens, would affirm. Their reading of Burdine gives operative force to the employer’s obligation to identify the reasons the employee must contest; after all are disproved, the indirect route is complete. They object to allowing a factfinder to search for a different unexplained motive after trial and invoke statutory stare decisis. Stone proposes a distinct middle rule, not joined by another Justice. The employee must prove the applicable prima-facie case and affirmatively establish that every material nondiscriminatory explanation offered was not an actual reason. Inaccuracy alone, peripheral discrepancies, or an actual remaining material reason do not trigger the shift; none prevents an otherwise sufficient discrimination claim. After the trigger, the employer must prove absence of the prohibited cause by a preponderance, using the whole admissible record. A merely possible lawful reason or unsupported personal hostility is insufficient; hostility itself may be racial. No fixed documentary form is required, and the employee receives notice and an opportunity to contest any decisive factual theory. Equipoise after the shift favors the employee on causation, while other elements, defenses and relief retain their own sources. Stone openly departs from Burdine’s perpetual persuasion assignment as a Title VII-specific proof construction; Rule 301 supplies no authority for that change, and Price Waterhouse’s different predicate is illustrative rather than controlling. He would retain valid findings, set aside the Title VII judgment, and permit a focused supplemental presentation only where fairness under the changed allocation requires it, with an equivalent response opportunity. The rule grants no routine retrial, successive explanations, same-decision defense or 1991 Act retroactivity determination. Stone would separately reconsider Long’s §1983 judgment without automatically importing the Title VII allocation. The two four-Justice groups do not join this departure: one insists on continuing employee persuasion, the other on completed indirect proof requiring judgment. Their theories and Stone’s are not logical subsets yielding a common affirmative allocation. No Marks conversion establishes the proposed shift.

### Shaw v. Reno, No. 92-357

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-20; decided 1993-06-28. On direct appeal from three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of North Carolina, 808 F. Supp.461 (1992).

**Question:** Whether the complaint states an Equal Protection claim against North Carolina officials for allegedly arbitrary racial construction of congressional districts, notwithstanding the asserted Voting Rights Act purpose; separately, whether the claims against federal preclearance officials may proceed in this district.

**Material record:** After the 1990 census, North Carolina received a twelfth congressional seat. The Attorney General objected to its initial one-majority-Black-district plan under § 5; the State then adopted a two-majority-Black-district plan. The Attorney General’s objection identified a possible additional district in the southeast; the revised second district instead followed roughly 160 miles of the Interstate 85 corridor farther north. Forty counties were covered by § 5, and the State did not pursue the alternative declaratory-judgment preclearance route. The complaint alleges arbitrary concentration by race rather than ordinary geographic criteria. Its five Durham County voters include two assigned to District 12 and three to adjacent District 2. Racial purpose, a legally sufficient remedial predicate, and the actual relationship between the challenged boundaries and voting-rights compliance have not been resolved at trial.

**Relief sought:** The voters seek reversal of dismissal, declaratory relief, and an injunction against the challenged plan. State officials seek affirmance; federal officials defend the jurisdictional and preclearance-review bars.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Territorial racial design alone does not state the asserted deprivation

**Controlling proposition:** Alleged deliberate or predominant consideration of race in drawing territorial constituencies, even with departures from ordinary districting criteria, does not by itself state this equal-protection claim where no racial exclusion, intentional dilution, materially unequal participation, legally inferior civic membership or other independently cognizable constitutional deprivation is alleged. This is a substantive disposition of the design-only theory, not heightened pleading, a universal requirement of additional downstream loss, or an added constitutional-injury prerequisite for an independent statutory claim.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the alleged design alone does not deny these voters equal membership in their territorial constituency. UJO supports the limited distinction between racial awareness and electoral discrimination, although its separate rationales establish no comprehensive test. Croson protects each person against racial allocation of competitive opportunities; statewide racial strength therefore cannot answer a personal deprivation. Here the alleged demographic design does not itself prescribe racial eligibility or limit the representative’s constituency to one race. Anderson forbids official racial ballot cues, and Gomillion forbids boundaries used to expel a racial population from municipal voting; both remain enforceable without this theory. The contrary view reasonably identifies a danger of racial stereotyping, but that danger does not make every deliberate demographic boundary an official rule of unequal membership. The Court accepts the allegations rather than inventing benign geography. Conley supplies ordinary pleading rules, while the asserted substantive theory fails. No finding of federal compulsion, final compliance with other law, or right to a particular racial composition follows.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United Jewish Organizations v. Carey, 430 U.S.144: its fractured reasoning supports a limited distinction, not a comprehensive controlling test or a statewide-white-representation defense.
- Wright v. Rockefeller, 376 U.S.52: distinguished; racial purpose was not established there, so it did not decide the present assumed-purpose question.
- City of Richmond v. Croson, 488 U.S.469, and Shelley v. Kraemer, 334 U.S.1: personal equality retained; the Court declines to equate territorial design alone with Croson’s racial allocation of opportunities.
- Anderson v. Martin, 375 U.S.399: official racial ballot labeling remains prohibited without proof that an election winner changed.
- Gomillion v. Lightfoot, 364 U.S.339: racial municipal disenfranchisement remains prohibited; Whittaker’s broader concurrence is not adopted as a rule covering every racial district adjustment.
- White v. Regester, 412 U.S.755, and McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents, 339 U.S.637: impaired participation may be unconstitutional despite formal access; no universal downstream-loss requirement.
- Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S.41; Leatherman, March 3, 1993: ordinary notice pleading retained, no demand for expert proof or a replacement map.
- Growe and Voinovich: statutory prerequisites, state primacy and unresolved coalition/influence questions remain distinct; no additional constitutional-injury gate imposed on § 2.

##### Federal preclearance dismissal remains separate

**Controlling proposition:** The district court outside the District of Columbia cannot use this action to review the Attorney General’s preclearance determination under the Voting Rights Act; § 14(b)’s designated channel and Morris’s restriction on review leave dismissal of the federal defendants undisturbed. That disposition does not immunize the State’s plan from an otherwise cognizable constitutional or statutory challenge.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court leaves the federal defendants’ dismissal undisturbed on grounds independent of the state claim. Section 14(b) channels actions restraining execution or enforcement of the Voting Rights Act to the District Court for the District of Columbia. Morris explains why the Attorney General’s preclearance determination is not an ordinary reviewable administrative adjudication and why substantive challenges to a state plan remain separate. The plaintiffs cannot obtain review of that determination in this North Carolina action by describing their objection as one to the resulting map. Conversely, preserving the federal dismissal says nothing about whether the State’s plan satisfies every independent constitutional or statutory command. Beer’s nonretrogression principle does not compel maximization of majority-minority districts, and preclearance is no universal certificate of legality. Because the designated channel and nonreviewability rules resolve this component, the Court does not decide an alternative merits defense for the federal officials or direct them to approve different boundaries.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morris v. Gressette, 432 U.S.491: restriction on reviewing the Attorney General’s preclearance determination applied; independent challenges to the state enactment remain available under their own rules.
- Beer v. United States, 425 U.S.130: nonretrogression distinguished from maximization or certification of compliance with every law.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 28, a five-Justice Court rejects this territorial racial-design-only constitutional theory. Independent exclusion, dilution, unequal participation, civic-status and statutory claims retain their own elements. No generally applicable replacement for Croson or Metro Broadcasting, predominance scrutiny rule, coalition/crossover entitlement, or requirement to prove federal compulsion enters. Federal-official dismissal rests on the separate channel.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone’s concurrence explains why equal political membership concerns actual personal treatment rather than aggregate racial officeholding. Constitutional amendments and lawful statutory and judicial changes have expanded participation without an uninterrupted progression or a guarantee of proportional officeholding. Harper corrects a wealth barrier; Reynolds protects equal voting power; the Reconstruction enforcement cases permit properly supported preventive legislation without a completed local constitutional violation in every covered application. Those authorities do not prove that North Carolina needed these lines. Stone preserves Shelley’s personal-rights principle for white and Black residents alike and rejects historical racial dominance as an answer to an actual deprivation. Croson presents a serious contrary analogy because classification can itself injure; his distinction concerns a common territorial electorate, not a supposed requirement of monetary or electoral loss. Wright left proven racial design unresolved, UJO is fractured, and Whittaker’s Gomillion concurrence advances a broader separation theory Stone declines to extend. Anderson’s ballot-label injury and McLaurin’s restrictions within shared facilities refute any universal claim that formal eligibility suffices. An operative racial designation or discriminatory denial of an otherwise available participation opportunity remains examinable; Knight supplies no general right to force officials to listen. Allen’s personal-treatment standing requirement and Heckler’s noneconomic equality injury remain distinct from success on the merits. Gingles’s behavioral evidence, independent § 2 requirements and Beer’s nonretrogression principle remain intact; neither coalition, crossover nor influence duties are newly decided. Congress’s supported preventive categories and a State’s independent authority remain different. Stone’s broader corrective-permission framework is not adopted as a replacement scrutiny tier, statutory duty or universal defense. Ordinary Rule 15/Foman amendment and preclusion rules remain; he directs no amendment and invents no additional facts.

White, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, finds UJO and the lack of an alleged participation impairment decisive, while recognizing that constitutional injury is personal. Their further account of districting practice is not an independent majority rule. Stevens separately distinguishes a State’s effort to recognize demonstrated racial voting conditions from a command that citizens vote or be represented according to race; his Karcher concern with arbitrary districting does not make shape alone dispositive. Souter distinguishes geographic constituency assignment from segregated access to public institutions and declines to infer that elected representatives will deny service to residents of another race. O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissents on the state claim. They would recognize the alleged separation itself as a personal constitutional injury and require a sufficiently supported compelling justification and narrow tailoring under Croson. They treat unusual shape as evidence of racial design, not as an aesthetic constitutional rule. They would reverse dismissal and allow examination of justification, not hold the map invalid on disputed facts or require it to be maximally compact.

### United States v. Dixon, No. 91-1231

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1992-12-02; decided 1993-06-28. On writ of certiorari to District of Columbia Court of Appeals, 598 A.2d 724 (1991).

**Question:** Whether the Double Jeopardy Clause bars substantive criminal prosecutions based on conduct already tried in nonsummary criminal-contempt proceedings, including the different counts against Dixon and Foster and the continued force of Grady v. Corbin.

**Material record:** Dixon’s release order prohibited criminal offenses. After an evidentiary hearing, he was convicted of contempt for possession of cocaine with intent to distribute and received 180 days; the later prosecution concerns that same drug offense. Foster’s protection order prohibited assault and threats against his estranged wife. Her counsel prosecuted a three-day contempt trial. Foster was convicted for two assaults and acquitted of the three threats relevant here. The later indictment charges simple assault for the first assault (I), those three threats (II, III, IV), and assault with intent to kill for the second assault (V). The Government did not prosecute the first Foster proceeding, but it was a court’s criminal-contempt prosecution with punitive sanctions.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Nonsummary punitive contempt triggers double-jeopardy protection

**Controlling proposition:** A completed nonsummary criminal-contempt prosecution places the accused in criminal jeopardy; private initiation and an order’s protective purpose do not categorically remove that protection. Whether a later prosecution is for the same offense remains a separate inquiry; coercive civil sanctions, summary courtroom contempt and separate sovereigns are not decided.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that punishment for violating a court order can implicate the protection against renewed criminal prosecution. Bloom recognizes criminal contempt as a crime for procedural protection, while Gompers distinguishes a fixed punitive sanction from a civil sanction avoidable through compliance. Foster underwent criminal adjudication, including acquittals and imprisonment, under the court’s authority. The fact that his wife’s counsel initiated enforcement does not make that completed punitive trial a private civil remedy. The Government’s institutional distinction identifies why an order matters, but cannot eliminate the constitutional character of the punishment already imposed. Young recognizes the judiciary’s authority to enforce its orders; that authority does not itself answer the separate identity-of-offenses question. The Court does not impair prospective enforcement of protective orders or decide every civil or summary contempt setting. The later counts must therefore be examined under the applicable double-jeopardy rules, with their different outcomes and rationales stated separately.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bloom v. Illinois, 391 U.S.194: criminal character of punitive contempt applied.
- Gompers v. Bucks Stove & Range Co., 221 U.S.418: punitive/coercive distinction applied.
- Young v. United States ex rel. Vuitton, 481 U.S.787: independent judicial enforcement authority retained, not a categorical exception from criminal protection.

##### Previously prosecuted necessary offenses cannot be tried again

**Controlling proposition:** When a criminal-contempt adjudication necessarily prosecutes the substantive offense forbidden by the order, the Government cannot prosecute that same substantive offense again. Dixon’s drug offense and Foster I’s November assault were necessarily prosecuted in the respective contempts and are barred; this shared proposition does not supply a common analysis of Foster II–V.

**Authority:** White’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Government cannot retry the substantive offenses already necessarily established in these contempts. Brown protects against successive prosecution for greater and included offenses, and Harris prevents an abstract description of a greater offense from concealing the substantive crime necessarily prosecuted within it. Dixon’s order required compliance with the criminal law, and his contempt adjudication established the same possession with intent to distribute later charged. Foster’s November assault similarly supplied the offense prosecuted and punished under his protective order. The existence and knowledge of the order do not erase the previously prosecuted substantive crime. The opposing abstract-elements view gives insufficient effect to the necessary content of these particular charges. The six Justices supporting this rule differ on the broader comparison for additional elements and on Grady’s application, but agree directly on this limited protection. No other contempt conviction is vacated, and the Court does not resolve the remaining counts by treating every act in a single episode as one offense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Brown v. Ohio, 432 U.S.161: successive greater/included-offense protection applied.
- Harris v. Oklahoma, 433 U.S.682: necessary substantive offense cannot be erased by an abstract description of the first charge.
- Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S.299: elements inquiry retained; disagreement over its broader application separately recorded.

##### Grady remains governing successive-prosecution law

**Controlling proposition:** Grady is not overruled: beyond Blockburger’s elements protection, a later prosecution is barred when establishing an essential element requires proof of conduct constituting an offense for which the defendant has already been prosecuted. Mere reuse of evidence or a common episode does not suffice, and Felix’s independently punishable conspiracy holding remains; no five-Justice rationale determines Grady’s application to Foster II–V in this fractured decision.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court retains the distinction between another prosecution of already prosecuted criminal conduct and the reuse of evidence about that conduct. Grady states that distinction, while Felix applies it to a separate conspiracy agreement and rejects a same-evidence rule. The Government’s concern about administering the conduct inquiry does not establish that this recent protection has become unworkable. Brown’s concern with finality after trial and the successive-prosecution line examined in Vitale support maintaining protection beyond a purely formal comparison of statutory language. The contrary proposal would discard Grady rather than merely clarify Felix; five Justices decline that change. Retention does not make all charges arising from one episode inseparable. Blackmun’s distinct view of contempt as vindication of judicial authority prevents a majority from applying this retained rule to bar Foster’s remaining counts, while four other Justices would overrule it. That fractured application must not be transformed into either an overruling or a new universally controlling contempt exception. Ashe remains separately available where an acquittal necessarily resolved an issue.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Grady v. Corbin, 495 U.S.508: expressly retained; no overruling by this decision.
- United States v. Felix, March 25, 1992: conspiracy and evidentiary-overlap distinctions preserved, not recast as an overruling.
- Illinois v. Vitale, 447 U.S.410: successive-prosecution concern beyond formal elements supports retention.
- Brown v. Ohio, 432 U.S.161: finality and greater/included-offense protection retained.
- Ashe v. Swenson, 397 U.S.436: independent issue-preclusion rule preserved for the remanded threat charges.

Foster II–V may proceed under a 5–4 judgment, subject to Ashe where applicable, but no majority adopts either the four-Justice Blockburger-only/overruling view or Blackmun’s special judicial-order distinction. ### Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, No. 92-102

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-03-30; decided 1993-06-28. On writ of certiorari to Ninth Circuit, 951 F.2d 1128 (1991).

**Question:** Whether the Federal Rules of Evidence displaced Frye’s general-acceptance prerequisite for scientific expert evidence and what admissibility inquiry governs the causation evidence offered in this federal diversity action.

**Material record:** Two children with limb-reduction birth defects and their parents allege that maternal Bendectin use caused the defects. The manufacturer relies on published epidemiological research showing no demonstrated association. Plaintiffs offer expert opinions drawing on animal studies, chemical-structure analysis, and reanalysis of epidemiological data. The courts below treated the lack of general acceptance and unpublished, unreviewed reanalyses as grounds for exclusion. No jury has resolved causation. The current record does not establish that every proposed method is reliable merely because the experts are qualified.

**Relief sought:** The children and their parents seek vacatur and reconsideration under the Federal Rules. Merrell Dow seeks affirmance because the proposed evidence lacks an adequate scientific foundation and cannot support causation.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

No finding of causation or direction to admit all experts. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The enacted Rules displace mandatory general acceptance

**Controlling proposition:** Frye’s mandatory general-acceptance prerequisite does not govern admission of scientific expert evidence in federal trials under the Federal Rules of Evidence. Acceptance remains potentially relevant under Rule 702, but its absence does not alone require exclusion and its presence does not dispense with the Rules’ other requirements.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the enacted Rules supply the federal admissibility standard. Rule 402 generally admits relevant evidence subject to applicable exceptions, and Rule 702 addresses qualified expert knowledge that assists the factfinder without imposing a consensus condition. Beech Aircraft requires interpretation of these Rules as enacted law, while Abel permits compatible common-law guidance rather than a competing prerequisite. Bourjaily illustrates that an incompatible earlier doctrine does not survive merely because it was familiar before the Rules. Frye’s rigid condition therefore cannot exclude a proffer simply because it is new, unpublished or disputed within the relevant community. The contrary assertion that the Rules silently preserved Frye finds no support in their text or the identified drafting history. This displacement does not require admission of unsupported expertise, resolve the children’s causation claims, or establish that every scientific disagreement belongs before a jury. The Court vacates the judgment based on the wrong screening rule and leaves the actual proffers for proper evaluation below.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Frye v. United States, 293 F.1013: displaced as a mandatory prerequisite in federal trials; not a Supreme Court Rule 702 holding being overruled.
- Beech Aircraft v. Rainey, 488 U.S.153: statutory interpretation of enacted Rules applied.
- United States v. Abel, 469 U.S.45: compatible common-law guidance retained, incompatible prerequisite rejected.
- Bourjaily v. United States, 483 U.S.171: Rules supersede incompatible common law.

##### Reliable scientific foundation and fit require flexible preliminary screening

**Controlling proposition:** Under original Rules 104(a) and 702, the proponent must establish by a preponderance the preliminary foundation for scientifically reliable knowledge that fits the disputed factual question and will assist the factfinder. The judge examines principles, methodology and valid application using relevant nonexclusive considerations—testability and testing, peer review/publication, meaningful error information and operational standards, and acceptance—without making any one consideration an invariant prerequisite; admissibility does not determine ultimate causation or sufficiency.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (8 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that scientific knowledge requires more than credentials or unsupported assertion. Rule 702’s knowledge and assistance requirements demand a defensible scientific foundation and a valid connection to the offered inference. Bourjaily supplies the preliminary preponderance burden under Rule 104(a), distinct from the substantive trial burden. Testing, peer scrutiny, error information, standards and acceptance can illuminate reliability, but publication is neither indispensable nor conclusive. A method valid for one purpose may not support another: the lower courts must examine the link between the offered studies or reanalysis and the human-causation proposition. The contrary concern that judges must become final arbiters of scientific truth mistakes preliminary admissibility for ultimate factfinding. Cross-examination and contrary evidence remain appropriate for shaky but admissible proof; they do not replace the judge’s screening duty. Rules 403, 703 and 706 and the ordinary judgment rules remain available. The Court neither decides which experts qualify here nor extends this scientific discussion to every technical specialty, legislative fact or appellate review question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Bourjaily v. United States, 483 U.S.171, 175–176: preponderance for preliminary admissibility applied, not a substantive causation burden.
- Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U.S.44: adversarial testing remains appropriate for admissible evidence; no excuse for unsupported foundation.
- United States v. Downing, 753 F.2d 1224: helpful fit and flexible reliability discussion informs interpretation, not a binding Supreme Court multifactor checklist.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 28, Frye’s federal consensus prerequisite is displaced 9–0; reliability/fit and flexible preliminary screening govern by eight direct joins. Rules 104(a)/702 burden is preponderance, not scientific certainty. No later Rule 702 text, universal technical expertise rule, appellate review standard or substantive civil rights evidence test enters.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens concurs in displacement and the vacatur but declines the further scientific-method discussion. He accepts some judicial screening responsibility yet considers abstract guidance insufficiently tied to a decision admitting or excluding a particular proffer; lower courts should develop the issue on concrete records. Stone concurs in the Court’s operative standard and emphasizes that an unpublished reanalysis must explain its data, choices and inference, while animal studies require a defensible bridge to the human proposition. Data quality is relevant rather than a universal extra element; litigation origin and scientific minority status do not establish unreliability. He distinguishes expert admissibility from legislative facts and substantive civil rights standards: no consensus prerequisite for constitutional enforcement, universal statistical significance level, mandatory regression model or retrospective change to earlier voting cases follows.

### Austin v. United States, No. 92-6073

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-20; decided 1993-06-28. On writ of certiorari to Eighth Circuit, 964 F.2d 814 (1992).

**Question:** Whether the Excessive Fines Clause applies to federal civil in rem forfeiture under 21 U.S.C. §§ 881(a)(4) and (a)(7), and the proper consequence if constitutional coverage exists.

**Material record:** Austin pleaded guilty to a South Dakota cocaine-possession-with-intent-to-distribute charge and received a state prison sentence. The United States separately sought the mobile home and auto-body shop under the federal forfeiture provisions. The account links a cocaine transaction to the two properties. The lower court did not undertake an Excessive Fines determination, and the Supreme Court record does not supply findings sufficient to fix their value, the constitutionally relevant proportionality, or the final forfeitable extent.

**Relief sought:** Austin seeks reversal of the ruling that the Clause does not reach the forfeitures and an opportunity to contest excessiveness. The United States seeks affirmance on the theory that these civil in rem forfeitures fall outside the Clause.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reversed and remanded, 9–0, for an Excessive Fines inquiry. No order returning property or fixing its value.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### These statutory forfeitures are covered fines

**Controlling proposition:** Forfeiture to the United States under 21 U.S.C.§§ 881(a)(4) and(a)(7) constitutes payment in kind to the sovereign at least partly as punishment and is subject to the Excessive Fines Clause. Its civil designation, proceeding against property, and concurrent remedial aims do not remove coverage; coverage does not establish excessiveness or import all criminal-procedure safeguards.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these statutory takings cannot be characterized as solely remedial. Browning-Ferris distinguishes private awards from governmental extraction; here the United States prosecutes the action and receives the property. The statutory connection to drug offenses and the separate owner defenses make culpability relevant, while deterrence and punishment are evident in the provisions’ operation. Halper recognizes that civil proceedings can serve punitive purposes, but its distinct double-jeopardy accounting analysis is not the test adopted here. The Government’s instrumentality argument does not make ordinary lawful property equivalent to contraband: Plymouth Sedan rejects that equivalence for a vehicle. Nor do widely varying property values function simply as calibrated reimbursement of enforcement expenses. The Court therefore rejects a categorical civil-forfeiture immunity from the Clause. It does not decide every historical forfeiture, a constitutional innocent-owner defense, taxation or compensatory recovery, state incorporation, or the ultimate amount that may lawfully be taken from Austin.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Browning-Ferris v. Kelco Disposal, 492 U.S.257: government prosecution/receipt distinguishes this fine from private punitive award.
- United States v. Halper, 490 U.S.435: partly punitive function recognized by analogy; separate Double Jeopardy analysis not imported.
- One 1958 Plymouth Sedan v. Pennsylvania, 380 U.S.693: lawful property is not contraband merely because used in crime.
- One Lot Emerald Cut Stones v. United States, 409 U.S.232, and United States v. One Assortment of 89 Firearms, 465 U.S.354: remedial customs/contraband characterizations distinguished, not abolished.
- Buena Vista, February 24, 1993: statutory owner defenses remain provision-specific; no § 881(a)(7) or constitutional innocent owner holding imported.

##### Historical in rem form does not negate punishment

**Controlling proposition:** The historical treatment of statutory in rem forfeiture includes punishment and does not establish categorical exemption from the Excessive Fines Clause for these forfeitures. The traditionally limited common-law innocent-owner defense does not prove that forfeiture was solely remedial; the constitutionality of forfeiting a truly innocent owner’s property remains unresolved.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that historical forfeiture practice supplies no categorical civil-property exception. Peisch described forfeiture as punishment of an owner and declined to treat unauthorized removal by strangers as the owner’s forfeiting act. The Palmyra allowed condemnation without a prior personal conviction, but did not thereby establish a purely remedial purpose. Goldsmith-Grant and Calero-Toledo connect traditional forfeiture to negligence, care in entrusting property, and deterrence, while reserving serious questions involving a truly innocent owner. These authorities answer the argument that the property’s formal status as defendant necessarily excludes punishment of its owner. The history permits concurrent remedial purposes; it does not require their absence before a sanction can be punitive in part. The actual drug forfeiture provisions reinforce that understanding through their offense predicates and express owner protections. The Court leaves the reserved constitutional innocence question unresolved and does not turn every historical statement into a rule that all governmental acquisition of property is a fine.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Peisch v. Ware, 4 Cranch 347: punitive understanding and stranger-removal qualification applied.
- The Palmyra, 12 Wheat 1: no prior personal conviction required, not an absence of punishment holding.
- J.W. Goldsmith, Jr.-Grant Co. v. United States, 254 U.S.505, and Calero-Toledo v. Pearson Yacht, 416 U.S.663: punitive/deterrent features recognized; truly innocent owner question reserved.

##### Remand leaves excessiveness distinct from statutory connection

**Controlling proposition:** The lower court must evaluate whether Austin’s actual forfeiture is excessive; statutory facilitation and coverage as punishment do not themselves decide that question. The Court adopts no comprehensive excessiveness test and does not limit the remand to instrumentality alone: the property’s connection to the offense may matter, and other relevant considerations may also be examined.

**Authority:** Blackmun’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter (6 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correcting the coverage error requires further consideration of the particular forfeiture. The court below expressed concern about the penalty but believed itself barred from applying the Excessive Fines Clause, so its comments are not a completed constitutional determination on developed findings. Browning-Ferris identifies governmental punishment as the coverage concern without supplying a comprehensive measure for this forfeiture. Calero-Toledo’s account of property connected to wrongdoing likewise does not decide the separate excessiveness inquiry. The proposed exclusive focus on instrumentality may identify a relevant consideration, but the Court leaves other considerations available rather than treating any sufficient statutory connection as conclusive. First-instance examination is appropriate because neither established values nor an applied constitutional standard are before this Court. No automatic return of the properties, numerical ratio, fixed monetary cap or ruling that the forfeiture necessarily survives follows. The Government retains its opportunity to establish lawful forfeiture within the statute and the constitutional limit.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Browning-Ferris, 492 U.S.257: fine coverage principle applied without inventing a comprehensive proportionality formula.
- Calero-Toledo, 416 U.S.663: statutory/historical connection not converted into a final excessiveness answer.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 28, § 881(a)(4)/(a)(7) forfeitures are covered fines unanimously. Five adopt the historical partly punitive explanation; six expressly leave other factors available beyond instrumentality. No comprehensive excessiveness formula, mandatory ratio, automatic return, state incorporation or Double Jeopardy holding enters. Stone’s factor proposal and Scalia’s exclusive test remain noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Scalia agrees that taking lawful property under these provisions is partly punitive and constitutes a fine. He rejects the need to establish owner blameworthiness across all historical forfeitures and would measure excessiveness through the property’s traditional closeness to the offense, treating value as irrelevant to that particular instrumentality inquiry. His exclusive test is not the Court’s rule. Kennedy, joined by Thomas, accepts the actual statutory conclusion but cautions against anachronistically imposing one personal-punishment theory on every historical forfeiture. He reserves whether such forfeitures invariably punish their owners and the constitutional question involving a truly innocent owner; he does not adopt Scalia’s exclusive remand test. Stone accepts coverage from the actual culpability-sensitive and deterrent statutory operation. He would examine the interest and supported value taken, seriousness and extent of wrongdoing, relevant owner culpability and the property’s actual role as nonexclusive considerations. He adopts no mandatory ratio, exhaustive checklist or later gross-disproportionality formula. The common remand leaves that choice of test open rather than converting Stone’s proposals into law.

### Hartford Fire Insurance v. California, No. 91-1111 and 91-1128

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-02-23; decided 1993-06-28. On writ of certiorari to Ninth Circuit, In re Insurance Antitrust Litigation, 938 F.2d 919 (1991).

**Question:** Whether domestic insurers lose McCarran-Ferguson antitrust protection by alleged agreement with foreign reinsurers; which pleaded conduct falls within the boycott exception; and whether international comity bars the Sherman Act claims against the London defendants. No. 91-1111 presents the two McCarran-Ferguson questions. No. 91-1128 presents extraterritorial application to the foreign conduct. These are distinct reviewed components of the same listed consolidated matter.

**Material record:** Nineteen States and private plaintiffs filed 36 complaints alleging concerted changes in commercial general liability insurance and reinsurance, including policy forms, pollution exclusions, and terms of coverage. The allegations include withholding broader reinsurance business to compel adoption of disfavored terms, as well as joint drafting without alleged refusal to deal. Other counts concern London reinsurers’ coordinated terms for American risks. These are allegations on motions to dismiss, not proven cartels. The London defendants invoke a British regulatory regime permitting their practices; they do not identify British law requiring conduct that American law forbids.

**Relief sought:** Domestic defendants seek reinstatement of dismissals under the insurance exemption. The London defendants seek dismissal of the foreign-conduct claims on comity grounds. State and private plaintiffs seek continued litigation of the challenged conspiracies.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Insurance exemption follows activity rather than association

**Controlling proposition:** Otherwise qualifying business of insurance regulated by state law does not lose McCarran-Ferguson § 2(b) antitrust protection merely because domestic insurers agree with foreign reinsurers. Whether the activity itself qualifies and is state-regulated remains essential; neither domestic nor foreign nationality establishes that condition categorically.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exemption’s object is the activity Congress described. Royal Drug distinguishes the business of insurance from all business done by insurance companies; its discussion of agreements with outsiders does not create an entity-based contamination rule. Pireno likewise examines risk transfer, the policy relationship and whether the practice remains within the insurance industry. The alleged agreements here concededly concern the business of insurance, and foreign reinsurers are not pharmacies or other parties wholly outside that industry. Even assuming their own activities lack state regulation, the domestic activity does not automatically lose otherwise established protection through association. The contrary analogy to exemptions for designated agricultural producers overlooks different statutory language. This ruling removes one appellate rationale without foreclosing the boycott exception or deciding the actual regulatory status of every reinsurer. Fabe’s separate first-clause protection of state insurance-regulatory law remains distinct. Qualifying activity, actual regulation and applicable statutory exceptions must still be evaluated.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Group Life & Health Insurance v. Royal Drug, 440 U.S.205: activity/entity distinction applied; loose outsider analogy confined to its context.
- Union Labor Life v. Pireno, 458 U.S.119: practice-specific criteria retained.
- SEC v. National Securities, 393 U.S.453: regulation of insurance business distinguished from regulation of companies.
- Case-Swayne v. Sunkist, 389 U.S.384, and United States v. Borden, 308 U.S.188: producer/entity-based exemptions distinguished.
- Fabe, June 11, 1993: separate first-clause holding not expanded into the antitrust proviso.

##### Collateral refusals distinguish boycott from agreement on terms

**Controlling proposition:** A concerted refusal of collateral or unrelated transactions to compel desired terms in a targeted transaction can constitute a McCarran-Ferguson § 3(b) boycott; a mere joint insistence on terms of the refused transaction does not, without more, do so. The pleaded broader withdrawals permit California I–IV/Connecticut I–II to proceed, subject to proof and whether other business genuinely affects the reinsured risk; California VII/Connecticut VI allege no refusal mechanism and cannot defeat domestic exemption on the reviewed grounds.

**Authority:** Scalia’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the boycott exception must leave room for the activity exemption Congress enacted. Barry involved withholding alternative insurance to force acceptance of another insurer’s terms, while Eastern States used unrelated dealings to compel a collateral trade restriction. Those examples distinguish coercive leverage from a collective demand about the very risk or transaction offered. Reinsurance terms depend on the underlying coverage, so refusing to reinsure an objectionable primary policy is not automatically collateral. The complaints nevertheless allege broader withdrawal even from otherwise acceptable business and linked demands that may cross that boundary. McLain and Conley require crediting that permissible pleaded account at this stage, not deciding the operational facts against the plaintiffs. Defendants may show that the other business genuinely affected the proposed risk. The model umbrella/excess counts contain no corresponding refusal allegation. The Court decides neither actual conspiracy nor antitrust injury, and supplies no categorical definition of every independent coercion or intimidation claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- St. Paul Fire & Marine v. Barry, 438 U.S.531: collateral-pressure application retained; not limited to boycotts of competitors.
- Eastern States Retail Lumber v. United States, 234 U.S.600: unrelated trade used as leverage illustrates the boundary.
- United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters, 322 U.S.533: coercive exclusion distinguished from mere agreement on terms; not abrogated.
- McLain v. Real Estate Board, 444 U.S.232, and Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S.41: then-operative pleading standard applied; no actual liability finding.

##### The asserted British permission does not bar these American-market claims

**Controlling proposition:** The pleaded London conduct intended to produce, and producing, substantial effects on American insurance coverage may be adjudicated under the Sherman Act; British permission without an incompatible duty or another established ground requiring nonapplication here does not warrant the asserted comity dismissal. The Court reserves a universal comity rule and §6a’s precise applicability, while preserving its qualifying-effects and claim-connection requirements wherever applicable.

**Authority:** Souter’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these allegations connect the challenged foreign conduct to the American market the statute protects. Alcoa and Matsushita recognize the Sherman Act’s reach to intended substantial domestic effects, and Continental Ore does not equate foreign legality with immunity from that law. The London defendants allegedly coordinated coverage restrictions aimed at American buyers; those alleged changes supply both the domestic effect and the asserted injury connection. Assuming §6a applies and adds requirements, the alleged direct, substantial and reasonably foreseeable domestic effect gives rise to these asserted claims. The Court does not decide the provision’s wider reach or every interaction with import commerce. British regulation and sovereign interests deserve consideration, but the defendants identify no British command inconsistent with American compliance. The contrary prescriptive-comity approach would displace established statutory reach on these allegations without a sufficient legal basis. The Court leaves broader comity questions open and requires proof below. Federal subject-matter power, substantive coverage and ultimate antitrust liability remain separate; none is established merely by labeling foreign practices objectionable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Aluminum Co. of America, 148 F.2d 416, and Matsushita, 475 U.S.574: intended substantial domestic effects standard applied.
- Continental Ore v. Union Carbide, 370 U.S.690: foreign permission does not itself immunize conduct.
- Société Nationale Industrielle Aérospatiale, 482 U.S.522: actual foreign/domestic conflict considered; Blackmun’s separate analysis identified as not the whole Court’s holding.
- Charming Betsy, 2 Cranch 64: interpretive canon retained; does not require the asserted dismissal here.
- Aramco, 499 U.S.244, and Sale, June 21, 1993: source-specific geographic scope preserved; different statutes do not erase established Sherman Act reach.
