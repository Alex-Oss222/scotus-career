### Lockhart v. Fretwell, No. 91-1393

Merits decision, January 25, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 3, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the Eighth Circuit, 946 F.2d 571, reviewing federal habeas relief from an Arkansas death sentence. The State seeks reversal of relief based on counsel's failure to object that the pecuniary-gain aggravating circumstance duplicated the robbery-murder element. Fretwell defends the direction to impose life without parole. Deficient performance is conceded. The District Court expected an objection under Collins v. Lockhart to succeed; the Court of Appeals treated circuit authority as binding on Arkansas. The jury rejected the separate arrest-avoidance aggravator and found no mitigating circumstance. The question is whether the omitted objection establishes prejudice under Strickland v. Washington after the substantive duplication rule was rejected.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Eighth Circuit's judgment is reversed, 6–3, on the Collins-based ineffective-assistance ground.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Collins-based ineffective-assistance relief | Reverse, 6–3 | White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas | Stone-Zsela would vacate and remand; Stevens and Blackmun would sustain relief | Reverse the Eighth Circuit judgment granting relief on this ground. This Court orders neither conditional resentencing nor a life sentence. |

Independent penalty-phase investigation and preparation claims not decided below remain unresolved. Guilt-stage claims are outside the State's writ. The Court enters no new execution date, stay ruling or final resolution of those other claims.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Loss of a displaced substantive rule

**Controlling proposition:** Counsel's failure to obtain a favorable application of a substantive rule subsequently rejected does not establish Strickland prejudice where the lost benefit is one the governing law does not recognize and the omission otherwise deprived the defendant of no fair or reliable sentencing protection. This limited rule does not replace ordinary reasonable-probability analysis, excuse deficient performance, or require an additional showing of unfairness in the usual ineffective-assistance case.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt the rule and its application to the Collins objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this omitted objection supplies no constitutional ground for setting aside the sentence. Strickland v. Washington measures prejudice through the fairness and reliability of the adversarial proceeding and excludes a favorable result attributable to an erroneous legal standard. Nix v. Whiteside demonstrates that a probable change in outcome is insufficient when the claimed benefit is not legally protected. Lowenfield v. Phelps permits the required narrowing of death eligibility at the guilt stage; the duplication objection accepted in Collins has since been rejected. Counsel's conceded deficiency therefore did not remove a substantive protection to which Fretwell is entitled. Kimmelman v. Morrison preserves ineffective assistance as a distinct claim even when direct habeas review of the underlying search claim is unavailable, but it still requires a meritorious underlying objection. That procedural restriction differs from the displaced substantive rule here. Ordinarily, a reasonable probability of a different result establishes prejudice. The Court does not reopen counsel's conceded deficiency or decide the separate, unresolved penalty-phase preparation claims.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), is applied and clarified for loss of a subsequently rejected substantive rule; ordinary reasonable-probability analysis remains the usual test.
- Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S. 157 (1986), is applied by analogy to distinguish a changed outcome from loss of a legally protected benefit; the Court makes no finding of perjury by Fretwell.
- Kimmelman v. Morrison, 477 U.S. 365 (1986), is distinguished: a procedural bar on direct habeas consideration does not eliminate a meritorious underlying claim, unlike the substantive premise here.
- Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231 (1988), supplies constitutionally sufficient narrowing at the guilt stage despite duplication at sentencing.
- Collins v. Lockhart, 754 F.2d 258 (8th Cir. 1985), supplies no cognizable benefit through its displaced double-counting rule. Perry v. Lockhart, 871 F.2d 1384 (8th Cir. 1989), rejected that rule, and the litigation accepts that premise. Whether Collins could independently be revived is not decided.

##### Teague and the timing of the prejudice inquiry

**Controlling proposition:** Teague's limitation on new rules supporting collateral relief does not require a federal habeas court to disregard a subsequent legal correction favorable to the State when deciding whether an omitted objection caused cognizable Strickland prejudice. Performance remains assessed from counsel's perspective at the time, while the prejudice inquiry here does not preserve an entitlement to the rejected Collins rule.

**Authority:** O'Connor's opinion of the Court, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six Justices adopt this answer to the temporal objection.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the different purposes of the two inquiries answer the objection based on hindsight. Strickland v. Washington protects counsel from an unreasonable retrospective assessment of professional performance. Deficient performance is conceded, so that protection is not in dispute. The remaining question concerns the constitutional consequence of the omission, not whether competent counsel should have recognized Collins. Teague v. Lane limits collateral disruption of state judgments through newly announced rules; its finality rationale does not give a prisoner a corresponding right to preserve a favorable but erroneous substantive interpretation. A State may therefore invoke the correction relevant to the claimed prejudice. The contrary approach would transform protection against new collateral obligations into an enduring right to a result that present governing law rejects. This conclusion does not eliminate ordinary prejudice analysis or resolve every retroactivity question. The Court has no need to adopt the Solicitor General's separate argument based on the present-custody wording of § 2254(a).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), is distinguished from an affirmative entitlement to an obsolete rule; its limitation protecting finality on collateral review does not operate reciprocally.
- Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), preserves the contemporaneous perspective for evaluating performance without mechanically fixing the prejudice inquiry to every favorable rule then available.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

Effective January 25, 1993, ordinary Strickland analysis remains governing law, subject to the limitation for a displaced substantive rule and the Teague distinction stated above. The Court adopts no actual-innocence prerequisite, general harmless-error rule or broad habeas deference standard. Sawyer's eligibility gateway, Wright's limited sufficiency result, and the invalid-weighing requirements of Stringer, Sochor and Espinosa remain distinct. The Court does not decide that Collins remains valid, the Solicitor General's alternative § 2254(a) theory or the independent assistance claims. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice Thomas concurs separately and joins the Court in full. A state court is not bound by a lower federal court's constitutional interpretation merely because that court sits in the same circuit. He rejects the Eighth Circuit's contrary premise about judicial authority. This clarification is an additional observation, not a separate holding joined by five Justices.

Justice Stevens dissents, joined by Justice Blackmun. They would sustain relief because counsel's deficient failure to invoke a professionally available argument had a reasonable probability of changing the original sentence. Strickland supplies that inquiry; Kimmelman treats adequate advocacy as an independent Sixth Amendment protection, and Nix's refusal to count perjured evidence does not equate lawful advocacy with wrongdoing. Later rejection of Collins does not repair the original adversarial failure. They object to allowing the State a retrospective advantage while Teague ordinarily denies a prisoner new favorable rules. Their position supports the judgment granting relief and does not join Chief Justice Stone-Zsela's conditional-remand disposition. Justice Stevens also considers, without making it necessary to his dissent, whether such profound failure on the central sentencing question can resemble the presumptively prejudicial breakdowns addressed by Cronic and Holloway.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents separately. He would vacate and remand for ordinary Strickland prejudice under the correct understanding of judicial authority. Collins was persuasive, not binding on Arkansas. The lower court must identify the professionally supportable objection, test it against then-controlling state and Supreme Court law, and reconsider whether the existing probability finding remains supported without assuming obedience to circuit precedent. He rejects a free-standing entitlement screen that automatically erases a lawful advocacy opportunity because its reasoning is later rejected. He equally excludes nullification, fabricated evidence, rulings forbidden by controlling authority and a bare numerical probability test. If prejudice is established, conditional relief must permit lawful resentencing under then-applicable law, subject to any independent restriction on renewed aggravating findings or punishment. He neither awards life without parole automatically nor revives Collins, authorizes a rejected aggravator without inquiry, supplies findings, or treats remand as permission to execute before the claim is resolved. No other Justice joins his writing.

### Bufferd v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue, No. 91-7804

Merits decision, January 25, 1993

Argued November 30, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the Second Circuit, 952 F.2d 675 (January 3, 1992), which affirmed the Tax Court. The question is whether the period for assessing an S corporation shareholder's individual income tax runs from the shareholder's return or the corporation's information return when the deficiency arises from pass-through items. Bufferd seeks reversal of the deficiency judgment as time barred.

The Court affirms, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, all of whom participated at argument and decision, reject the asserted bar arising solely from expiration of Compo's corporate-return period. Justice White delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas. All nine adopt the judgment, rule and explanation; no separate opinion is published.

For an assessment of an S corporation shareholder's individual income tax, the return that starts the ordinary § 6501(a) limitations period is the shareholder's return, including applicable extensions and statutory exceptions. Expiration of the period associated with the corporation's § 6037 information return does not bar correcting pass-through items in a timely shareholder assessment; the corporate return remains operative for corporate-level taxes to which it applies. This rule controls through the unanimous opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that the assessment period follows the taxpayer whose liability is being assessed. Section 6501(a) links the return to that liability, and § 6501(c)(4) permits the taxpayer and Commissioner to extend the period by agreement. Compo's erroneous loss and credit flowed to Bufferd; correcting those items did not assess a corporate tax. Automobile Club of Michigan explains why a filing lacking the information needed to compute the relevant tax does not start the assessment period. Germantown Trust likewise concerns an adequate return for the taxpayer assessed, not a substitute return for someone else. Section 6037 does not require a different answer: its limitations language retains work when the corporation owes tax, including when its S election is invalid. The burden of retaining corporate records cannot change the taxpayer Congress identified. Bufferd's individual extension therefore defeats this corporate-period defense. The Court does not decide the unpressed wording objection to that extension or the operation of special statutory audit regimes.

Automobile Club of Michigan v. Commissioner, 353 U.S. 180, 188 (1957), is applied by analogy: a filing insufficient to compute the relevant taxpayer's liability does not trigger that taxpayer's assessment period. Informational completeness alone does not transform one person's return into another's.

Germantown Trust Co. v. Commissioner, 309 U.S. 304 (1940), is distinguished in scope: a sufficient return for the same taxpayer may trigger limitations despite its form, but it supplies no corporate-return substitute for a shareholder's own return.

Holywell Corp. v. Smith, decided February 25, 1992, remains undisturbed: its distinct fiduciary and trust filing duties do not decide this limitations question. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The contrary corporate-period rule applied in Kelley v. Commissioner, 877 F.2d 756 (9th Cir. 1989), cannot govern this shareholder-assessment question. The Court decides no broader rule for C corporations.

Effective January 25, 1993, § 6501's taxpayer-specific return rule resolves which return and extension govern this pass-through adjustment. Section 6037 retains its corporate-level function. Special statutory audit and limitation regimes remain governed by their own terms; later unified audit provisions do not apply to this 1979 liability. The Court adopts no general presumption favoring the Commissioner, ruling on the disputed extension wording or expansion of assessment time beyond an applicable exception or agreement.

The identity, chronology and record are supported by [506 U.S. 523](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep506/usrep506523/usrep506523.pdf). The lower judgment and preservation of the issues are supported by [952 F.2d 675](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/952/952.F2d.675.91-4099.214.html) and the Government's May 1992 opposition in the [Internet Archive docket collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0317). The available opposition omits table-of-authorities page iii, without a material record gap. [Automobile Club, 353 U.S. 180](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep353/usrep353180/usrep353180.pdf), and [Germantown Trust, 309 U.S. 304](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep309/usrep309304/usrep309304.pdf), support the prior-law account. Sections 6501, 6503 and 6037 are supported by the judicial and contemporaneous primary materials. The preservation account rests on the lower opinion and certiorari brief. Source support does not extend to a complete merits-brief, appendix or transcript account. No verbatim opinion quotation is used.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. Bufferd's individual extension remains effective for the preserved dispute, and the Tax Court judgment sustained below stands. The Court calculates no new deficiency amount and orders no remand or additional accounting. Ordinary mandate practice applies, with no special mandate date, stay or retained proceeding established.

### United States v. Hill, No. 91-1421

Merits decision, January 25, 1993

Argued November 2, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the Federal Circuit, 945 F.2d 1529 (September 11, 1991), which adopted the Claims Court's refund judgment, 21 Cl. Ct. 713 (1990). The question is whether former 26 U.S.C. § 57(a)(8)'s adjusted basis in mineral property includes separately depreciable tangible drilling and development equipment. The United States seeks reversal of refunds for tax years 1981 and 1982.

The Court reverses and remands, 9–0. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, all of whom participated at argument and decision, vote to exclude the separately depreciable tangible improvements from the depletable mineral interests' adjusted bases for the 1981 and 1982 tax preference. Justice Souter delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas. All nine adopt the judgment and every controlling proposition; no separate opinion is published.

For tax years governed by former § 57(a)(8), the adjusted basis offset against percentage depletion is the adjusted basis of the taxpayer's § 614 interest in the mineral deposit, determined without that year's depletion deduction; it excludes separately depreciable tangible drilling and development improvements identified in Treasury Regulation § 1.612-4(c)(1). Lawfully capitalized intangible costs recoverable through depletion remain in depletable basis; this classification does not erase distinct statutory provisions governing other costs or later taxable years. The unanimous opinion of the Court supplies the rule's controlling authority.

The Court holds that the basis of a mineral deposit does not include the basis of its separately depreciable equipment. Section 57(a)(8) expressly incorporates § 614's definition of property as an interest in a mineral deposit. The established regulations distinguish minerals in place from the larger enterprise that also includes improvements. Section 1016 adjusts the basis of the property identified; it does not combine separate depreciation and depletion accounts, even when the assets are sold together. The taxpayers' commercial-integration argument therefore addresses a different unit from the one Congress selected. Nor does the heading of the cost-depletion regulation change that statutory definition. Permitting the same equipment balance repeatedly to shelter percentage depletion would further disconnect the preference from the mineral investment it measures. The regulations' treatment of capitalized intangible costs supplies no contrary rule: costs represented by physical improvements are recovered through depreciation, while qualifying other capital costs enter depletion. This decision preserves those distinctions and does not apply the later prospective depletion amendment to the years before the Court.

INDOPCO, Inc. v. Commissioner, decided February 26, 1992, remains undisturbed and is distinguished by issue: capitalization can be required without creating a separate asset; Hill concerns allocation among already distinct depreciation and depletion accounts. The Court adopts no restrictive separate-asset prerequisite. The controlling classification follows the coordinated statutory and regulatory text. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled or converted into a general rule against multiple tax benefits. Technical Advice Memorandum 8314011 supplies no precedent under then-§ 6110(j)(3); its § 616 deferred-development setting also contains an express basis direction different from the costs here. The memorandum is not a judicial holding.

Effective January 25, 1993, § 614's definition governs the former § 57(a)(8) adjusted-basis offset, excluding tangible depreciable improvements and preserving properly depletable capital costs. The decision resolves how existing separate statutory and regulatory categories enter the minimum-tax calculation for the years presented. It does not combine commercially integrated assets into a common basis account or forbid a tax benefit Congress authorized. Under the Energy Policy Act, amended § 57(a)(1) does not apply to § 613A(c) depletion deductions for taxable years beginning after December 31, 1992. The 1981–1982 holding remains operative for liabilities governed by the former provision. The Court decides no general question about every mineral-development expense.

The identity, chronology, record and statutory and regulatory text are supported by [506 U.S. 546](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep506/usrep506546/usrep506546.pdf). The judgment below, including the adopted Claims Court opinion and the Nies dissent, appears at [945 F.2d 1529](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/945/945.F2d.1529.91-5032.html). These primary judicial sources reproduce the operative 1981–1982 Code and regulations. The prospective amendment is supported by [Pub. L. 102-486, § 1915(a), (d), 106 Stat. 3023–3024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-106/pdf/STATUTE-106-Pg2776.pdf). Source support does not extend to a complete appendix or merits-brief account. No verbatim opinion quotation is used.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Federal Circuit's judgment is reversed, and the case returns through that court for proceedings consistent with the proper statutory calculation. The Hills' contrary refund judgment cannot stand. Any remaining accounting must use the stated categories and the existing supported record. The Court orders no unsupported reassignment of costs, fixes no new dollar amount and directs no new factual hearing. Ordinary mandate practice applies; no special mandate date, stay, retained jurisdiction or later filing is established.

### United States v. Nachtigal, No. 92-609

Certiorari grant and summary merits decision, February 22, 1993

Decided on the petition papers without oral argument. On petition for certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the Ninth Circuit's unpublished memorandum, 953 F.2d 1389 (January 27, 1992), which affirmed the District Court's reversal of a magistrate judge's conviction. The question is whether the federal national-park offense of driving under the influence, carrying at most six months' imprisonment, a $5,000 fine and alternative probation up to five years, carries a constitutional jury-trial right. The Government seeks review and summary reversal. Nachtigal seeks leave to proceed without paying filing fees, opposes summary reversal and requests plenary consideration if review is granted.

The Court grants Nachtigal's motion for leave to proceed in forma pauperis, grants certiorari and summarily reverses the Ninth Circuit's judgment. The summary merits judgment is unanimous: Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas agree that the authorized penalty package does not establish a constitutional jury entitlement. All nine participated at submission and decision and also agree to the ancillary filing disposition. The Court issues an unsigned, per curiam opinion joined in full by all nine Justices. Each joins the judgment and entire controlling rule; no separate writing is published.

An offense carrying no more than six months' imprisonment is presumptively petty for the constitutional jury-trial right, but the defendant may overcome that presumption by showing that the additional authorized penalties, considered with the maximum incarceration, are so severe that they clearly reflect a legislative judgment that the offense is serious. For this national-park offense, the six-month imprisonment ceiling, $5,000 fine and alternative probation of up to five years with its authorized conditions do not rebut the presumption; the defendant has no constitutional jury entitlement on that basis. The unanimous per curiam opinion supplies controlling authority for both the complete Blanton rule and its application here.

The Court applies Blanton's complete inquiry to the penalties the law authorizes. Blanton makes the maximum prison term the primary indication of seriousness while allowing unusually severe additional penalties to overcome the petty-offense presumption. Congress fixed a six-month ceiling for this offense even though the Secretary promulgated the regulation. The $5,000 fine and alternative probation do not establish the exceptional severity required. Probation may restrict work, residence and movement, require treatment or community service, and include community-facility residence or monitored home confinement; those restrictions differ from imprisonment. Any authorized intermittent custody remains limited by the offense's imprisonment ceiling. The Court considers those possible conditions, not merely Nachtigal's actual fine and one year of unsupervised probation. Other States' jury rules do not replace Congress's judgment about this federal offense. The Ninth Circuit's contrary distinction from Blanton therefore fails. This application does not declare that money penalties or probation can never make a six-month offense serious, and it decides no other challenge to the conviction.

Blanton v. North Las Vegas, 489 U.S. 538 (1989), is applied: the six-month presumption remains rebuttable after considering additional authorized penalties, and the governing legislature's choices supply the relevant judgment about seriousness rather than other jurisdictions' practices.

United States v. Craner, 652 F.2d 23 (9th Cir. 1981), is displaced to the extent that its jury-entitlement rule for this federal offense conflicts with Blanton as applied here. Delegated rulemaking does not remove Congress's enacted imprisonment ceiling from the analysis. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

Effective February 22, 1993, Blanton's existing rebuttable test governs this national-park penalty scheme, which does not overcome the presumption. The maximum legally authorized sanctions govern, rather than the punishment actually selected. Distinguishing five years of probation from five years of imprisonment does not remove the obligation to examine probation conditions. Nachtigal's papers invoked a conditional alternative fine based on gain or victim loss, but the record shows neither predicate. The Court neither declares that provision inapplicable in every prosecution for driving under the influence nor decides whether a differently triggered fine package would make the offense serious. The decision supplies no broader financial-sanction rule, proof burden beyond Blanton's showing or holding on another challenge to the conviction. The filing-fee disposition creates no new indigence doctrine.

The chronology, statutory penalties and disposition are supported by [507 U.S. 1](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep507/usrep507001/usrep507001.pdf). The lower memorandum appears at [953 F.2d 1389](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/953/953.F2d.1389.91-10212.html), and the governing prior opinion is [Blanton, 489 U.S. 538](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep489/usrep489538/usrep489538.pdf). Nachtigal's December 18, 1992 opposition and the Government's January 1993 reply in the [Internet Archive docket collection](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385013_0359) support the separate acquittal, actual sentence, asserted probation conditions and disputed conditional gain-or-loss fine. The primary materials reproduce 16 U.S.C. § 3; 18 U.S.C. §§ 3571(b)(6), (d), (e), 3581(b)(7), 3561 and 3563; and 36 C.F.R. §§ 1.3 and 4.23 (1992). The source account is a petition-stage summary disposition; it does not include a complete appendix or plenary merits-brief account. The source materials supply no verified individual certiorari vote. No verbatim opinion quotation is used.

The petition and summary merits proceedings are complete. The Ninth Circuit's judgment is reversed, and the case returns below for proceedings consistent with the absence of a jury-trial right under this penalty scheme. The decision rejects the jury ground on which the District Court set aside the conviction. It directs no new trial or judgment on an unreviewed objection and decides no other conviction challenge. The acquittal on the separate blood-alcohol count remains outside review and undisturbed. Ordinary mandate practice applies, with no special date, stay or retained Supreme Court proceeding established.

**End of entry: United States v. Nachtigal, certiorari grant and summary merits decision, February 22, 1993.**

### Growe v. Emison, No. 91-1420

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 2, 1992. On direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. 1253 from the three-judge District of Minnesota, 782 F. Supp. 427 (1992), which imposed congressional and legislative districts. Minnesota seeks reversal; appellees defend the federal plans. The questions are whether the federal panel should defer to timely state judicial redistricting and whether its minority district has the proof required by § 2 of the Voting Rights Act.

Parallel state and federal actions followed the 1990 census. The State's special judicial panel corrected legislative drafting defects and prepared congressional districts while the federal panel obstructed its work and adopted its own plans. The federal Minneapolis district combined several minority groups without local statistical or anecdotal proof of common political cohesion or majority bloc voting. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the federal redistricting judgment and remands with instructions to dismiss. The substantive reversal is unanimous; the mandatory-dismissal direction is 8–1.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Federal legislative and congressional plans and unsupported § 2 district | Reverse, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing Justice | Set aside federal displacement; no replacement federal map prescribed. |
| Mandatory termination of this federal action | Remand with instructions to dismiss, 8–1 | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela | District court must dismiss; Stone-Zsela would permit conditional retained jurisdiction and justified safeguards if a genuine election deadline or federal violation requires action. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court on state priority, the operative state judgment and § 2 | Scalia | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | All nine support reversal | All three holdings |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Timely state redistricting includes the State's courts

**Controlling proposition:** When a State's legislature or judiciary is timely undertaking redistricting, a federal court must defer its own redistricting action unless the record shows that the State will not produce a lawful plan in time for the election. The federal court may set a reasonable deadline and retain jurisdiction as a safeguard, but may neither obstruct timely state work nor displace it merely because it prefers another plan; completion of every state appeal before the election is not required.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion on state priority, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt this rule. The separate eight-Justice dismissal direction concerns this completed record, not the general availability of retained jurisdiction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Minnesota's own courts must receive the same opportunity to complete lawful redistricting as its legislature. Scott v. Germano expressly recognized state judicial responsibility and allowed a federal deadline and reserve jurisdiction; Reynolds v. Sims supplies the obligation to correct unequal districts while giving the State an initial opportunity. The federal panel instead directed its deadline solely to the legislature and treated the state court's provisional work as interference. It then relied on delay to which its own injunction had contributed. The state court had adopted its legislative plan and was prepared to act on congressional districts in time for the election. The presence of a federal statutory claim in only one complaint does not change the shared remedial task. Nor does the possibility of an unfinished state appeal justify taking that task away. Federal power remains available for a proved federal defect or an actual failure of timely state action; this decision establishes no universal rule of priority based on filing dates.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Scott v. Germano, 381 U.S. 407 (1965): applied to both legislative and congressional redistricting through state courts; its timely-action condition and reserve federal jurisdiction remain.
- Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533 (1964): applied for primary state responsibility and the obligation to remedy unconstitutional apportionment; population-equality requirements are unchanged.

##### A superseding state judgment must receive legal effect

**Controlling proposition:** Once a state court's final judgment replaces the challenged legislative plan, the federal court must give the replacement legal effect rather than treat it as one optional proposal. A challenge to the old plan becomes moot unless it also reaches the superseding plan; allegations against the old plan alone do not establish a violation in the replacement.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion on the live-plan issue, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the stated rule. No party-preclusion rule beyond that necessary legal effect is announced.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the federal court must address the plan that actually governs. The state court's final legislative judgment changed Minnesota law before the federal panel selected its own districts. Section 1738 and the principles explained in Atlantic Coast Line Railroad v. Locomotive Engineers require respect for a state judgment's legal effect; they do not permit federal judges to demote it to an optional map. The earlier allegations concerned the superseded 1983 districts. No party established that those allegations remained a live challenge to the replacement, and the federal panel neither held a hearing nor requested argument addressing a particular replacement plan's asserted statutory defect. Wise v. Lipscomb supplies the related requirement to examine a lawful replacement before imposing judicial relief. A general preference for a larger minority population in one district cannot supply the missing violation. The Court does not decide an unpleaded replacement-plan challenge or immunize the State's work from an appropriately presented federal claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co. v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 398 U.S. 281 (1970): applied for respect for state judgments and the federal-state judicial relationship; no enlarged preclusion rule adopted.
- Wise v. Lipscomb, 437 U.S. 535 (1978): applied for the need to evaluate the legally operative replacement before federal remedial displacement.

##### Fragmentation claims require proof of the causal voting conditions

**Controlling proposition:** A § 2 claim that single-member districts fragment a minority group's potential electoral majority requires proof that the group is sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a majority in a single-member district, is politically cohesive, and faces majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate. Without the required cohesion and majority-bloc evidence, the court may not order a minority district by adding distinct groups' populations; the Court assumes without deciding that a supported coalition claim is permissible and leaves influence-only claims and the population denominator unresolved.

**Authority:** Scalia's statutory opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the stated single-member extension and record application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a remedial district requires proof of dilution, not a demographic assumption. Thornburg v. Gingles identifies the conditions connecting an electoral structure to lost minority opportunity. Size and compactness establish a potential electoral majority; cohesion identifies a common electoral preference; majority bloc voting explains how the challenged arrangement ordinarily defeats that preference. Those reasons apply when single-member boundaries fragment voters just as they apply to their submergence in a multimember district. Gingles reserved that extension, and the Court now adopts it for this claim. Even assuming compactness and the permissibility of combining distinct minority groups, the record contains neither statistical nor anecdotal evidence of the necessary common cohesion or majority bloc voting in Minneapolis. National generalizations cannot replace local proof. The statute's totality inquiry does not dispense with those necessary conditions or guarantee proportional representation. No particular statistical technique is universally prescribed, and the Court does not decide every possible coalition or influence-district theory.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): extended from multimember dilution to the single-member fragmentation claim at issue; its causal prerequisites and totality inquiry remain distinct, and its influence-only reservation remains open.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

State judicial redistricting receives the same timely opportunity as state legislative work. Federal reserve jurisdiction remains available under the stated conditions, although this action must be dismissed. A superseding plan must be tested on a properly presented claim concerning that plan. Gingles's three causal prerequisites expressly apply to this fragmentation claim, with the totality-of-circumstances inquiry still required. Whether a coalition claim is legally available, influence-only claims and total-population versus voting-age-population measurement remain unresolved. Other asserted errors are not reached. No precedent is overruled, no priority rule based on filing dates is adopted, and state plans receive no general immunity from federal review.

#### Separate Position Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela agrees that the federal plans must be set aside and joins the Court's state-priority, live-plan and statutory holdings. He would allow the district court to defer to the state plans while retaining jurisdiction if a genuine election deadline or properly presented, proved federal defect justifies continued authority. Such retention is permitted, not required. Any implementation safeguard must respond to that actual need; federal preference cannot justify recreating the minority district without statutory proof. His disagreement concerns mandatory dismissal, not the validity of the federal maps or a categorical entitlement to federal supervision.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The district court must dismiss this action on remand. No replacement federal map is prescribed, and no Supreme Court proceeding is retained. Dismissal does not adjudicate an unpleaded future challenge to a state plan.

### Itel Containers International Corp. v. Huddleston, No. 91-321

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

Argued October 14, 1992. On certiorari under 28 U.S.C. 1257 to the Tennessee Supreme Court, 814 S.W.2d 29 (1991), which affirmed rejection of Itel's federal objections after the Chancery Court reduced the assessment under state law. Itel seeks a federal-law refund; Tennessee seeks affirmance. The questions concern the Container Conventions and federal customs rules, the Foreign Commerce Clause and the Import-Export Clause.

Itel is a domestic container lessor whose containers are leased exclusively for international use and delivered at its Memphis and designated third-party Tennessee terminals. The tax attaches to delivery transferring possession, rather than importation. The original $382,465 assessment of tax, penalties and interest for January 1983 through November 1986 was reduced to $158,012 under state law. That reduction is not reopened. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The Court affirms, 8–1. Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support affirmance and reject the treaty and regulatory challenges; Blackmun dissents and would invalidate the tax under the Conventions. On the Foreign Commerce challenge, Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas adopt the Court's rationale, Scalia agrees in the judgment, and Blackmun would invalidate the tax. On the Import-Export challenge, Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas adopt the Court's rationale and Scalia rejects the challenge on a separate ground. Blackmun's proposed reversal rests on other grounds without an independent Import-Export disposition. No additional federal-law refund is required on any of the presented grounds; the existing state-law reduction remains.

Kennedy delivers the opinion of the Court, joined throughout by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. Scalia joins Parts I–III and VI, including the treaty and regulatory reasoning and the conclusion, and concurs only in the judgment on Parts IV–V, addressing the constitutional grounds. Eight Justices therefore support the treaty and regulatory reasoning, and seven support the Foreign Commerce and Import-Export reasoning. Scalia writes separately, joined by no other Justice. Blackmun dissents alone on treaty construction and, independently, foreign-commerce uniformity. Itel accepts compliance with Complete Auto; those requirements are not independently relitigated, and the Court makes no new due-process holding.

The 1956 and 1972 Container Conventions prohibit direct or indirect taxes imposed by reason of, or in connection with, importation of qualifying temporarily admitted containers, rather than every tax involving those containers. Tennessee's nondiscriminatory tax on the transfer of possession under a local lease is not such an importation charge; the 1972 definition also excludes fees and charges limited to the approximate cost of services rendered.

Kennedy's treaty portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. These eight Justices adopt this construction and application.

The Court holds that the reason for imposing the tax controls, not the historical reason why the container is present in Tennessee. Air France v. Saks directs attention first to treaty text. Both Conventions connect the exemption to importation, and removing that connection would make their limiting words ineffective. Qualifying containers receive temporary admission subject to reexportation within three months unless customs authorities extend the period; the protection does not establish permanent tax immunity for their commercial use. Itel's lease proceeds are taxed because possession changes in Tennessee, without discrimination between domestic and foreign goods. Other nations' practices deserve attention under Factor v. Laubenheimer, but their use of different tax systems does not establish the asserted exemption. Direct and indirect taxes alike are prohibited when they attach to importation; neither form is prohibited merely because a container contributes to taxable commercial value. The Executive's supporting interpretation is persuasive support, not a substitute for construing the Conventions.

Air France v. Saks, 470 U.S. 392 (1985): applied for treaty interpretation beginning with text while considering relevant context.

Factor v. Laubenheimer, 290 U.S. 276 (1933): applied for consideration of treaty parties' practical construction; the practice asserted here does not displace the operative importation limitation.

The Container Conventions, 19 U.S.C. 1322 and their implementing regulations do not occupy all container taxation or preempt a nondiscriminatory local lease tax that neither taxes importation nor obstructs the federal temporary-admission policy. Tennessee's tax therefore survives both the asserted field-preemption and obstacle-preemption grounds; the distinct federal rules governing bonded warehouses do not confer the same exemption on these lease transactions.

Kennedy's regulatory-preemption portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas. These eight Justices adopt the stated rejection of both grounds.

The Court holds that the scope of the federal arrangement must be identified before state taxation can be displaced. Hines v. Davidowitz supplies the obstacle inquiry, but the pertinent federal objective is protected temporary admission of containers, not freedom from every ordinary local tax. McGoldrick and Xerox involved a different arrangement: goods held under continuous customs supervision for reexport, with duty treatment intended to encourage use of American facilities. Taxing those goods could defeat that particular benefit. The container rules instead concern admission, certification and specified customs treatment, and Tennessee's local lease tax leaves those protections intact. Nor does extensive federal regulation alone establish occupation of the entire field. R. J. Reynolds rejected that inference even for the more detailed warehouse regime. The Court rejects both the alleged obstacle and the asserted complete occupation without denying that another tax could conflict with federal law. Nondiscrimination is relevant here but does not by itself answer every preemption question.

Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941): applied for conflict with federal purposes; no such obstacle established here.

McGoldrick v. Gulf Oil Corp., 309 U.S. 414 (1940): distinguished because the bonded-warehouse benefit and supervision differ from container leasing.

Xerox Corp. v. County of Harris, 459 U.S. 145 (1982): distinguished on the same specific federal warehouse purpose; its protection remains.

R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Durham County, 479 U.S. 130 (1986): applied against inferring complete field occupation from detailed customs regulation.

In addition to Complete Auto's substantial nexus, fair apportionment, nondiscrimination and fair relation to state services, a tax affecting foreign commerce must avoid a substantial risk of international multiple taxation and interference with the Nation's ability to speak with one voice. With Complete Auto compliance conceded here, Tennessee's discrete local transaction and credit for tax properly paid to another domestic or foreign jurisdiction on that same transaction, together with the demonstrated federal arrangement, satisfy those additional requirements; Executive support informs but does not decide their application.

Kennedy's foreign-commerce portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. These seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia agrees only in the judgment on this issue; Blackmun dissents.

The Court holds that potential foreign taxing authority alone does not make a local transaction constitutionally untaxable. Japan Line supplies separate multiple-taxation and national-uniformity inquiries; domestic ownership does not eliminate them. Container Corp. distinguishes unavoidable overlapping taxation from the mere possibility that another sovereign could tax, while Wardair recognizes the significance of a discrete local transaction. Tennessee taxes delivery under the lease and credits tax properly paid elsewhere on the same event, reducing the relevant duplication risk. Itel accepts the lower court's Complete Auto determination, so the Court does not independently reconsider those four requirements. The tax also fits the actual treaty, statutory and regulatory arrangement and does not conflict with a demonstrated international practice requiring exemption of this transaction. The United States' submission supports that conclusion but cannot authorize an otherwise unconstitutional tax. The absence of a demonstrated foreign conflict supports this application; it does not reduce the one-voice inquiry to express statutory preemption or dispense with apportionment in another case.

Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady, 430 U.S. 274 (1977): its four requirements remain mandatory; their satisfaction is conceded in this case.

Japan Line, Ltd. v. County of Los Angeles, 441 U.S. 434 (1979): retained and distinguished; foreign-owned property taxed fully abroad presented a different multiple-taxation and uniformity problem.

Container Corp. of America v. Franchise Tax Board, 463 U.S. 159 (1983): applied to distinguish inevitable duplication from potential foreign taxation and to assess foreign-policy evidence.

Wardair Canada Inc. v. Florida Department of Revenue, 477 U.S. 1 (1986): applied for the significance of a discrete local transaction and the actual federal arrangement.

Under Michelin, this nondiscriminatory local lease tax does not offend national commercial uniformity, divert federal import revenue or disrupt interstate harmony, and therefore does not violate the Import-Export Clause. The tax attaches to temporary possession transferred in Tennessee, rather than to the containers or their cargo as imports or exports in transit; the Court leaves unresolved whether Michelin otherwise altered Richfield's rule for direct taxation of goods in transit.

Kennedy's Import-Export portion is joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter and Thomas. These seven Justices adopt this rule and application. Scalia concurs only in this component's judgment on a distinct export-status ground; Blackmun does not supply a separate Import-Export disposition.

The Court holds that the Import-Export Clause requires its own analysis even when the Commerce Clause challenge fails. Michelin identifies protection of national commercial policy, federal import revenue and harmony among States. The national-policy inquiry is satisfied for the reasons already stated, and Washington Stevedoring explains why a properly connected, apportioned and evenhanded charge for local commercial activity respects interstate harmony. The revenue concern also fails here because Tennessee collects revenue from a local transfer of possession, not from importing goods. Richfield involved a direct tax upon the export transaction in goods already committed to export. This lease tax falls on a distinct service and its proceeds. The fact that a container will carry goods abroad does not transform every earlier local commercial event into an import duty or export impost. The Court need not decide how Michelin affects other direct taxes on goods in transit, and it neither overrules Richfield nor grants a general exemption from constitutional review.

Michelin Tire Corp. v. Wages, 423 U.S. 276 (1976): applied through its three constitutional concerns.

Department of Revenue v. Association of Washington Stevedoring Cos., 435 U.S. 734 (1978): applied for distinct local services and the interstate-harmony inquiry.

Richfield Oil Corp. v. State Board of Equalization, 329 U.S. 69 (1946): distinguished; its direct-export rule is not overruled or finally reconciled with every implication of Michelin.

These holdings govern this transaction effective February 23, 1993. The preexisting general rules and limited temporary-admission regime now receive a controlling construction and application to a nondiscriminatory domestic container-lease transaction. Complete Auto remains mandatory; Japan Line's two additional inquiries remain independent; federal preemption remains distinct from constitutional review; and Michelin's three concerns govern the Import-Export holding. Neither domestic ownership nor Executive support eliminates the foreign-commerce inquiry. No general local-tax immunity, apportionment exemption, express-conflict-only rule or overruling of Richfield follows. No precedent is overruled.

Scalia joins the treaty and regulatory holdings but agrees only in the judgment on the constitutional grounds. He would enforce a negative Commerce Clause, as a matter of precedent, against facial discrimination and laws indistinguishable from a type previously invalidated, without extending open-ended balancing tests. This tax fits neither prohibition. His Import-Export analysis preserves Richfield's textual protection but treats these empty containers as not yet exports when delivered for use in domestic loading; he therefore does not reach Michelin's broader test. His criticism of allowing an Executive litigation position to determine state taxing authority does not alter the Court's independent constitutional holding.

Blackmun would construe the Conventions in light of their purpose and the signatories' practice to protect these recurring transfers as integral to international container movement. He distinguishes a tax on container leases from a value-added tax on imported goods and regards the local-transaction distinction as insufficient. Independently, he would find that the state tax threatens the uniform treatment protected by Japan Line. The Executive's brief cannot itself authorize a tax, and congressional silence does not supply unmistakably clear authorization to override Commerce constraints. Those treaty and foreign-commerce positions support his proposed reversal; they are not Court law.

Scalia's prospective doctrine is limited by his assumption, rather than decision, that precedent's facial-discrimination rule applies equally to foreign commerce. His writing retains the separate textual Import-Export inquiry and does not reduce it to nondiscrimination. Blackmun's treaty-purpose construction is independently sufficient for his proposed reversal, and his foreign-commerce ground remains additional. All these propositions are noncontrolling.

Supreme Court review is complete. Tennessee's judgment and the state-law reduction stand, and no additional federal-law refund is required. The Court orders no remand or new tax computation, makes no penalty ruling, and retains no proceeding. Future assessments remain subject to the stated constitutional and federal-law requirements.

### Fex v. Michigan, No. 91-7873

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued December 8, 1992. On certiorari to the Michigan Supreme Court, 439 Mich. 117, 479 N.W.2d 625 (January 9, 1992), which reversed the intermediate appellate court and reinstated convictions for armed robbery and possession of a firearm during a felony. The Court reviews when Article III(a) of the Interstate Agreement on Detainers starts the 180-day trial period. A separately granted question concerns alleged delayed notice under Article III(c).

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The judgment of the Michigan Supreme Court is affirmed on the Article III(a) receipt question. The writ is dismissed as improvidently granted as to the separate Article III(c) notice question.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Article III(a) delivery trigger | Affirm, 6–3 | White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens | Trial began within 180 days of receipt by prosecutor and court; the reinstated convictions stand against this challenge. No dismissal or factfinding remand. |
| Allegedly delayed Article III(c) notice | Dismiss the writ as improvidently granted on this question; all nine join | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing Justice | No decision that the prompt-notice duty was satisfied or violated, and no prescribed remedy for a violation. Fex delivered his request to the Indiana custodial official on September 7, 1988. It was mailed on September 22 and received by both the Michigan prosecutor and court on September 26. Trial commenced on March 22, 1989, 196 days after custodial submission and 177 days after receipt by the designated recipients. Stone-Zsela would reverse and remand for application of the earlier date and adjudication of actual statutory continuances or exclusions, with dismissal with prejudice if the lawful period had expired. Blackmun and Stevens likewise favor the custodial trigger and a remand applying the compact's actual exceptions.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing or action | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |

No Justice joins a dissent on the separate notice-question disposition. Neither dissent supplies controlling law.

#### Controlling Proposition and Authority

##### Article III(a): actual receipt starts the trial period

**Controlling proposition:** When a prisoner invokes Article III while serving a term of imprisonment and an untried charge in another participating State is the subject of a lodged detainer, the 180-day trial period begins only when the required written notice and request for final disposition have actually reached both the prosecuting officer and the appropriate court in that State. Delivery to the custodian alone does not start it; a necessary or reasonable continuance may be allowed for good cause shown in open court with the prisoner or counsel present, Article VI(a) tolls the period while the prisoner is unable to stand trial as determined by the court having jurisdiction, and Article V(c) requires dismissal with prejudice when the applicable period is violated.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion, joined by White, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, supplies six votes for this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the compact starts the prosecution period on receipt by the officials who must act on the request. Article III(a) names the prosecutor and court as the recipients; Article III(b) separately makes the custodian responsible for forwarding the papers and accompanying certificate. Requiring a return receipt reinforces a definite, verifiable starting point. Carchman v. Nash supplies the treatment of this congressionally approved compact as federal law and requires attention to its operative terms. Houston v. Lack protects a prisoner's own appeal filing upon delivery to prison authorities; it does not determine when another jurisdiction's period for bringing a prosecution begins. The contrary interpretation addresses a serious risk of official delay, but it could consume the receiving State's trial period before that State receives the request. Article IX's liberal-construction direction does not eliminate the named recipients. Because trial began 177 days after both recipients received Fex's papers, Article III(a) does not require dismissal. The Court leaves any separate notice violation, independent constitutional remedy, and the availability of a continuance first sought after expiration unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carchman v. Nash, 473 U.S. 716 (1985): applied for the compact’s federal-law character and attention to operative language; its exclusion of probation-violation detainers remains intact.
- Houston v. Lack, 487 U.S. 266 (1988): distinguished because a prisoner’s own appeal-filing deadline differs from commencement of the receiving State’s prosecution period; its mailbox rule remains in force within its setting.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 23, 1993, Article III(a)'s 180-day period starts on actual receipt by both prosecutor and court. The custodian's prompt-forwarding duty and the Article III(c) prompt-notice duty remain obligations; a delay in performing either is not converted into compliance by this trigger holding. No automatic grace period, bad-faith prerequisite, constitutional speedy-trial rule, damages remedy, or remedial rule for Article III(c) is adopted. Article III(a)'s good-cause continuance and Article VI(a)'s judicially determined inability-to-stand-trial exclusion retain their separate terms. No earlier Supreme Court decision had resolved this exact receipt trigger, and none is overruled.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Blackmun, joined by Stevens, dissenting on Article III(a).** The proper custodial submission completes the act the compact requires from a prisoner. Article III(b) channels the request through officials whom the prisoner cannot supervise; reading Article III(a) together with that prescribed channel and Article IX places transmission risk on the participating governments. Houston v. Lack supplies the analogous protection for a prisoner who has surrendered the paper to the mandatory intermediary. Blackmun's Carchman opinion recognizes the serious burdens associated with unresolved detainers, which explain why prompt disposition matters independently of ultimate conviction. ### United States v. Dunnigan, No. 91-1300

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

Argued December 2, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) to the Fourth Circuit, 944 F.2d 178 (August 30, 1991, amended September 12, 1991). The court below affirmed the cocaine-conspiracy conviction but vacated the sentence because it considered a mandatory Guidelines increase for a defendant’s trial perjury unconstitutional. The United States seeks reversal of that sentencing ruling. The questions are whether U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 constitutionally applies to the defendant’s own trial perjury, what findings it requires, and whether the findings here suffice.

The Court reverses the sentencing judgment, 9–0, and remands for proceedings consistent with the opinion. Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision. All nine reject a categorical constitutional bar to the perjury adjustment and agree that the district court’s findings encompass the required elements here. Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Souter, and Thomas. No separate writing is published.

A sentence increase under U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 for independently established trial perjury—willfully false testimony under oath or affirmation about a material matter—does not violate the defendant’s right to testify. Once the court properly determines that the defendant committed such perjury, the applicable Guideline requires its two-level obstruction adjustment; neither exercising the trial right nor a guilty verdict alone supplies that predicate.

Kennedy’s unanimous opinion directly controls this rule, its explanation, and its application to this record.

The Court holds that the right to testify does not protect deliberate material falsehood under oath. Nix v. Whiteside supplies that boundary: a defendant’s opportunity to present a defense does not include a right to perjury. United States v. Grayson permits sentencing courts to consider a defendant’s willful false testimony while warning against a reflex increase whenever a testifying defendant is convicted. Section 3C1.1 respects that distinction when the court independently finds perjury. The Fourth Circuit’s reliance on the Guidelines’ reduced emphasis on rehabilitation does not justify a categorical exemption, because intentional corruption of the trial process also bears on retribution and incapacitation. Nor does the mandatory consequence erase the factual prerequisites that make the increase lawful. A defendant may testify inaccurately through confusion or faulty memory, or truthfully advance a defense the jury rejects, without committing perjury. The Court rejects the constitutional prohibition here while preserving those limits; it does not require a separate perjury prosecution or decide a different sentencing framework.

Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S. 157 (1986), applied to the right’s boundary: the right to present testimony includes no entitlement to perjury; the decision does not authorize assuming falsehood from an unsuccessful defense.

United States v. Grayson, 438 U.S. 41 (1978), applied: actual willful trial falsehood may inform sentencing; its rehabilitation discussion is not an exclusive justification and its warning against reflex increases remains operative.

When a defendant objects to a § 3C1.1 increase based on trial testimony, the court must review the evidence and independently find willfully false sworn testimony concerning a material matter, excluding confusion, mistake and faulty memory. Separate clear findings for each element are preferable, but a single determination encompassing all the factual predicates is sufficient; the district court’s findings here meet that standard.

The same nine Justices join Kennedy’s perjury-definition and findings holding, including the sufficiency of the existing finding on this record; that holding controls directly.

The Court requires a finding of perjury, not merely a finding that the defendant lost at trial. Section 1621 supplies the established elements of sworn, willfully false testimony about a material matter. Grayson’s caution against reflex sentencing requires the judge to examine the evidence rather than treat the guilty verdict as the missing proof of those elements. Separate findings on falsity, materiality and intent are preferable because they make the basis for review clear. A combined finding is nevertheless sufficient when it actually encompasses each predicate. Here the district court reviewed the evidence, identified Dunnigan’s denials of participation, and found untruthful testimony on material matters designed substantially to affect the outcome. That determination encompasses intentional material falsehood; its reference to the verdict does not reduce it to the verdict alone. Respondent’s contrary reading overlooks the judge’s own assessment and the purpose attributed to the testimony. The Court requires no special verbal formula and leaves confusion, mistake and faulty memory outside perjury.

United States v. Grayson, 438 U.S. 41 (1978), applied with its safeguard intact: a sentencing response requires an actual assessment of deliberate false testimony, not an automatic consequence of conviction.

Nix v. Whiteside, 475 U.S. 157 (1986), left intact in its factual boundary: absence of a protected right to perjury does not establish that disputed testimony was perjurious.

Williams v. United States, decided March 9, 1992, is distinguished by the question presented and left undisturbed: its rules for departures based on invalid factors do not create a categorical constitutional bar to this adjustment or dispense with independent perjury findings. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. Summary contempt concerns a different power to punish immediate obstruction; its requirements do not add a separate actual-obstruction element to trial perjury under this Guideline. The Court selects no new general standard of proof and announces no general rule governing the binding force of all Guidelines commentary.

Effective February 23, 1993, the constitutional and findings holdings govern § 3C1.1 adjustments for trial perjury. They apply Grayson’s allowance for considering actual perjury and Nix’s boundary on the right to testify to the mandatory Guidelines, subject to independent findings of every perjury element. The Court does not order automatic punishment of every convicted defendant who testified. It requires no separate perjury prosecution and does not address sentencing or jury-finding questions beyond the perjury adjustment presented.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The case returns through the Fourth Circuit for sentencing proceedings consistent with the rejection of its categorical bar and the sufficiency of the existing findings. The two-level adjustment is required when the properly found perjury predicate is satisfied. The Court neither requires a new evidentiary hearing nor requires adequate combined findings to be repeated in separate sentences, and it does not itself select a new sentence. The conviction remains affirmed; collateral indictment, evidentiary, and disclosure questions are not reopened.

### Negonsott v. Samuels, No. 91-5397

Merits decision, February 24, 1993

Argued January 11, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the Tenth Circuit, 933 F.2d 818 (May 8, 1991), affirming denial of federal habeas relief, 696 F. Supp. 561 (D. Kan. 1988). The question is whether the Kansas Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3243, permits Kansas to prosecute a state-law offense by an Indian against another Indian on a reservation when the conduct is also covered by the Indian Major Crimes Act.

Negonsott, a Kickapoo Indian living on the Kickapoo Reservation in Brown County, Kansas, was convicted of aggravated battery after shooting another Indian on that reservation in March 1985. The Kansas Supreme Court reinstated the conviction in State v. Nioce, 239 Kan. 127, 716 P.2d 585 (1986), after the trial court initially set it aside for lack of jurisdiction. The later sentence was three to ten years.

The Court affirms the Tenth Circuit’s judgment and the denial of the jurisdiction-based habeas claim, 9–0. Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision and support the judgment. White delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. No separate writing issues. The opinion rests on the text and structure of the Kansas-specific enactment; legislative history is not a necessary ground or an independent controlling alternative.

Under 18 U.S.C. § 3243, Kansas may prosecute offenses committed by or against Indians on Indian reservations, including trust or restricted allotments, within Kansas to the same extent its courts may prosecute offenses elsewhere in the State under state law, even when the conduct also violates the Indian Major Crimes Act. Federal courts retain their jurisdiction over federally defined offenses; this concurrent authority over conduct does not authorize Kansas courts to adjudicate federal criminal charges.

White’s unanimous opinion controls the jurisdictional grant, the saving clause, and the disposition.

The Court holds that the Kansas Act gives effect to both state and federal authority. Its first sentence grants Kansas jurisdiction over offenses involving Indians on reservations without excluding the major offenses. Its second sentence preserves the jurisdiction of federal courts over offenses defined by federal law. A single act may violate both bodies of law without allowing a state court to try a federal charge. Reading the second sentence to preserve exclusive federal authority over the conduct would withdraw a substantial part of the first sentence's unqualified grant. The otherwise exclusive operation of the Indian Major Crimes Act therefore does not defeat this specific congressional authorization. County of Yakima illustrates the need to identify the precise state power Congress authorized; it supplies no general state power over Indian country. Here Congress supplied the necessary authorization expressly. Because the text resolves the allocation, the canon favoring Indians in cases of statutory ambiguity does not change the result. Kansas could prosecute Negonsott for aggravated battery. The Court decides neither the independent scope of tribal adjudicatory authority nor any question of successive prosecutions.

County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation (January 14, 1992): its requirement of specific congressional authorization is applied at that level; its land-tax holding is neither converted into criminal jurisdiction nor enlarged.

No earlier decision is overruled. The ordinary federal allocation under the Indian Major Crimes Act remains subject to this express Kansas-specific enactment.

Effective February 24, 1993, the Kansas Act authorizes state-law prosecutions within its Indian-reservation and allotment coverage even when the conduct also constitutes a listed federal major crime. The grant derives from this enactment, not Kansas’s general police power or a general theory of diminished tribal sovereignty. It grants no corresponding power to another State, adjudicates no extinction of tribal authority, and resolves no double-jeopardy or successive-prosecution question.

The denial of federal habeas relief on the asserted lack of Kansas jurisdiction stands. Ordinary mandate procedures follow; no Supreme Court remand or further factfinding is required. The Court orders neither a new trial nor a jurisdictional dismissal. Unrelated conviction claims and the separate extent of tribal jurisdiction remain unadjudicated.

### United States v. A Parcel of Land Known as 92 Buena Vista Avenue, No. 91-781

Merits decision, February 24, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 13, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Third Circuit, 937 F.2d 98 (June 17, 1991; rehearing denied August 13, 1991), following an interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b), which permitted review before the case’s final judgment. The questions are whether a person receiving property as a gift may invoke 21 U.S.C. § 881(a)(6)’s innocent-owner defense and whether § 881(h)’s relation-back rule defeats that defense before adjudication. Relation back gives a completed forfeiture retrospective effect from the act giving rise to it.

Beth Ann Goodwin bought and held title to the residence with money given to her by Joseph Brenna in 1982. The Government alleges that the gift derived from drug transactions. The forfeiture proceedings begun in 1989 reached a probable-cause and legal-eligibility stage; Goodwin’s asserted innocence is not an adjudicated fact. The lower court’s other certified questions concerning seizure, immunity, and limitations or delay are outside the questions decided here and are not reopened.

Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the judgment allowing Goodwin to invoke the innocent-owner defense and remands for further proceedings. It neither decrees title nor adjudges her innocent.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The innocent-owner defense is available to an owner who received a gift

**Controlling proposition:** An actual owner is not excluded from § 881(a)(6)'s innocent-owner defense merely because the owner acquired the interest by gift rather than purchase for value. To prevent forfeiture to the extent of that interest, the owner must establish that the relevant act or omission occurred without the owner's knowledge or consent as the statute requires; eligibility does not establish that showing or decide its precise temporal measure.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter, supplies five votes for this proposition; Scalia and Thomas also accept donee eligibility in their judgment concurrence. The Court opinion independently controls.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the statutory word owner includes a person who receives property as a gift. Congress protected an owner's interest without limiting the protection to purchasers for value. Russello v. United States instructs courts to respect differences in language Congress chose for forfeiture provisions; a restriction stated in one provision cannot simply be inserted into another. The criminal-forfeiture provision considered in Caplin & Drysdale contains a distinct scheme for third-party transferees, including an express purchaser qualification, and does not supply that missing term here. Goodwin holds title to the residence, but title and the alleged source of the purchase money do not establish innocence. She must prove the absence of knowledge or consent required by § 881(a)(6). The Government's relation-back argument presents a separate question about when its title becomes effective; it does not rewrite the defense as a purchaser-only protection. The Court therefore affirms eligibility and leaves the claimant's proof, the precise temporal knowledge rule, and any unpresented constitutional protection unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16 (1983): applied to respect Congress’s different property-forfeiture language; it does not itself decide the claimant’s knowledge or establish an innocent-owner finding.
- Caplin & Drysdale, Chartered v. United States, 491 U.S. 617 (1989): distinguished because its criminal-forfeiture and third-party-transfer provisions differ; its attorney-fee and criminal-forfeiture holdings remain intact.

##### Relation back operates after the court determines that forfeiture is authorized

**Controlling proposition:** Section 881(h) is not a self-executing adjudication that eliminates an asserted § 881(a)(6) innocent-owner defense before forfeiture is determined. The claimant may establish that defense first; if the property or the relevant interest is lawfully forfeitable, the resulting governmental title relates back to the act giving rise to forfeiture, subject to the statutory protection of any interest for which the defense succeeds.

**Authority:** Stevens's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, O’Connor and Souter, controls with five votes. Scalia and Thomas concur in this practical result on their separately stated relation-back analysis; no narrower-ground inference is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the relation-back provision must operate with the defense Congress placed in the same forfeiture scheme. Section 881(h) refers to property described in subsection (a), and subsection (a)(6) excludes an owner's protected interest when that owner establishes the statutory defense. The Government cannot establish that an interest is forfeitable by assuming away the defense that determines whether it may be forfeited. United States v. Stowell explains the traditional operation of relation back: condemnation perfects a title that then reaches back to the forfeiture-producing act. That mechanism does not dispense with the determination authorizing forfeiture. Caplin & Drysdale enforces relation back within a different criminal-forfeiture scheme; it does not erase this civil provision's express owner protection. Goodwin therefore may attempt to prove her defense before governmental title is perfected against her interest. If the defense fails and forfeiture is authorized, relation back remains effective. The Court leaves knowledge timing, any properly raised equitable bar, and the factual entitlement to forfeiture for appropriate further proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Stowell, 133 U.S. 1 (1890): applied for the requirement of condemnation to perfect forfeiture title and its retrospective effect; relation back survives but does not bypass the statutory defense.
- Caplin & Drysdale, Chartered v. United States, 491 U.S. 617 (1989): distinguished as a different statutory scheme; no weakening of valid relation back after authorized forfeiture follows.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

Effective February 24, 1993, a donee with an ownership interest may invoke § 881(a)(6), and § 881(h) does not defeat that defense before the court determines forfeiture. Both rules rest on a five-Justice Opinion of the Court, with two additional Justices concurring in the judgment. The claimant bears the statutory showing as to absence of knowledge or consent. The Court does not resolve the precise temporal knowledge standard, choose a general construction of knowledge versus consent, decide source tracing or actual innocence, resolve an unpresented estoppel issue, or announce a constitutional innocent-owner guarantee. Section 881(a)(7)’s separate real-property defense is not construed. Section 881(h) was enacted in 1984, after Goodwin’s 1982 acquisition, but traditional relation back also affords an opportunity to present the defense; no retroactivity holding is necessary.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Scalia, joined by Thomas, concurring in the judgment.** They agree that Goodwin may assert the defense and that gift status alone is not disqualifying. Their analysis starts with the conventional operation of forfeiture, reflected in Stowell: the Government must complete the forfeiture process, after which its title reaches back to the offense. Section 881(d)'s incorporation of customs procedures confirms that Congress retained an adjudicative process and did not make § 881(h) an instantly completed transfer immune to the other statutory conditions. A claimant may invoke the conditions that prevent forfeiture during that process. The concurrence therefore rejects the premise that the Government has already conclusively eliminated Goodwin's ownership before her defense can be considered. It does not accept the Court's full account that the subsection (a)(6) exception, by defining the property described in subsection (a), alone resolves what subsection (h) covers; ordinary relation back and the statutory procedures supply the necessary explanation. Nor would these Justices turn statements in Goodwin's submissions into an unlitigated estoppel or a binding election of the date at which knowledge must be measured. The Court's express reservation of that date is consistent with their conclusion, but does not remove their distinct structural objection. Their concurrence preserves the Government's retrospective title once forfeiture is validly completed. It establishes no immunity for a donee who cannot prove the statutory defense, no exemption for fictitious ownership, and no rule under the separate real-property provision.

**Kennedy, joined by White, dissenting.** They would give greater effect to the Government's offense-date claim to forfeitable property. A recipient ordinarily cannot receive a better title by gift than the donor possessed. In their view the donor's title was already subject to the Government's superior forfeiture claim, and the gift did not displace that claim merely because the recipient lacked knowledge. Stowell's avoidance of intermediate transfers and Caplin & Drysdale's application of offense-date governmental title support that conclusion. This is a property-title argument, not an insertion of the words purchaser for value into § 881(a)(6) as an independent defense requirement. They distinguish an innocent purchaser who gives value, and a person succeeding to a genuinely protected title, from a donee taking directly from the holder of forfeitable property. They would reverse the categorical defense-eligibility ruling and leave room below to examine governing property law and any legally sufficient consideration or other protected title Goodwin could establish. They do not adjudge those possible factual predicates on this record. They reject the Court's sequencing because they regard the Government's superior claim as limiting the ownership that can support the defense, even though formal forfeiture proceedings remain necessary. These views are noncontrolling.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The Court affirms the defense-eligibility ruling and remands for further proceedings consistent with the two statutory holdings. Goodwin must establish the statutory defense; the Government may pursue forfeiture and unresolved factual or legal questions properly within the remand. The judgment supplies neither a title decree nor unconditional immunity from forfeiture. No Supreme Court matter remains pending; lower-court proceedings remain open.

### Voinovich v. Quilter, No. 91-1618

Merits decision, March 2, 1993

Argued December 8, 1992. On direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1253 from the three-judge District Court for the Northern District of Ohio. The District Court invalidated Ohio’s legislative apportionment on Section 2, Fifteenth Amendment purpose, and Fourteenth Amendment population grounds. Ohio officials seek reversal; challengers seek to retain invalidation and corrective districting. The questions are whether the State must demonstrate remedial necessity before creating majority-minority districts, whether the asserted influence-dilution and purpose claims succeed on this record, and whether departures above ten percent foreclose a county-boundary justification. Ohio’s 1991 plan was revised in 1992.

The Court reverses, 9–0, and remands only for further proceedings on the population-equality claim. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision. All nine reject the Section 2 prohibition and the initial burden imposed on Ohio, reject the presented Section 2 influence-dilution claim, reverse the finding of discriminatory purpose under the Fifteenth Amendment, and vote to reverse and remand on the population claim under the Fourteenth Amendment. No particular racial configuration is ordered. O’Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas: Part I addresses posture, Part II the statutory holdings, Part III the purpose finding, and Part IV the population standard and limited remand. No separate writing is published.

All three presented grounds are reached. The Court reviews the legal standards independently and the finding of discriminatory purpose for clear error. It assumes only for deciding this case that an influence-dilution claim is cognizable and that the Fifteenth Amendment covers vote dilution; neither assumption establishes a rule. The distinct Fourteenth Amendment challenge to racial classification was presented neither below nor here.

Section 2 does not prohibit a State from choosing majority-minority districts merely because no federal violation compels their creation; challengers bear at least the initial burden of showing, under the totality of circumstances, unequal electoral opportunity caused by the challenged arrangement. A federal court may order such districts only when their creation is necessary to remedy an established federal-law violation, but that limit does not become a categorical restriction on state redistricting authority; independently applicable federal constitutional and statutory requirements remain enforceable.

Part II.B of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion directly controls the rejection of the statutory prohibition and the lower court’s allocation of the burden; all nine Justices adopt the complete rule.

Section 2 protects equal electoral opportunity rather than a preferred distribution of minority voters among districts. Gingles requires challengers to demonstrate unequal access under the totality of circumstances; it does not declare a particular district form inherently unlawful. Concentrating minority voters can improve their ability to elect preferred candidates, diminish that ability, or leave it unchanged. The consequences require proof. The District Court instead required Ohio to demonstrate a prior statutory violation before creating majority-minority districts. That reverses the statute's initial allocation of the burden and mistakes an absence of federal compulsion for a federal prohibition. Growe recognizes the State's primary responsibility for apportionment and limits federal judicial intervention to an established federal defect. A State derives its districting authority independently of a federal court's remedial power. Its choice remains subject to federal requirements, but Section 2 supplies no categorical ban here. The Court does not decide the distinct constitutional challenge to conscious racial classification, which was neither presented below nor raised in this appeal.

Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): followed for the challengers' burden and the totality-of-circumstances inquiry; no district form is automatically unlawful under Section 2.

Growe v. Emison, decision of February 23, 1993: applied to distinguish the State's primary districting authority from federal judicial remedial authority; its actual majority holdings remain controlling.

Assuming that Section 2 reaches the asserted loss of minority influence districts, the claim fails because challengers did not establish sufficiently cohesive white majority voting usually to defeat the minority's preferred candidates. The Gingles prerequisites apply with attention to the claim's nature: an influence claim would require modification or elimination of the first prerequisite that the minority could constitute a district majority, but the Court need not select that adaptation or decide whether influence claims are cognizable because the independently necessary bloc-voting showing is absent.

Parts II.A and II.C of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion control this independently dispositive rejection of the presented Section 2 claim and each stated reservation.

Packing can impair electoral opportunity, just as dispersing a cohesive minority can. Gingles recognizes both possible mechanisms, and Growe applies its threshold framework to challenges involving single-member districts. Here, however, challengers seek additional districts in which black voters would be an influential minority relying on crossover votes, rather than districts in which they could themselves form a majority. The Court assumes this theory is cognizable and does not mechanically demand satisfaction of a majority-size prerequisite inconsistent with that assumption. Whether that first prerequisite should be modified or eliminated can remain open. The third prerequisite independently defeats the claim: the District Court found an absence of racially polarized voting, and challengers accept that finding. Without sufficiently strong white bloc voting to frustrate the minority's preferred candidates, this record does not establish the unequal opportunity asserted. The lower court cannot replace that missing causal proof with the bare observation that creating majority-minority districts removes black voters from other districts. Neither all influence claims nor all packing claims are rejected.

Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): applied to the necessity of majority bloc voting and the causal mechanism of dilution; its reservations about influence claims remain unresolved.

Growe v. Emison, decision of February 23, 1993: followed for application of the threshold framework to single-member district challenges, with its influence-claim reservation preserved rather than silently decided.

Assuming that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted vote-dilution claim, the finding that Ohio intentionally injured black voters cannot stand on this record: attention to perceived federal-law requirements and possession of an opponent's speculative documents, without evidence of reliance, do not establish the prohibited purpose the District Court inferred. The Court reverses that finding as clearly erroneous without deciding the Amendment's coverage of dilution or its relationship to race-conscious districting generally.

Part III of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion controls this disposition confined to the record. All nine Justices adopt it; none adopts a general immunity for political objectives or racial awareness.

The Court assumes that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted dilution claim. Beer v. United States leaves that coverage question unresolved, and this case does not require an answer. The asserted violation depends on the intentional injury to black voters that the District Court found. Neither cited piece of evidence supports that inference. Tilling's decision to honor federal requirements when he perceived a conflict with state law demonstrates attention to the Supremacy Clause, not a purpose to abridge minority voting strength. The documents he possessed were an opponent's speculation about possible partisan strategies; they were not his adopted instructions, and no evidence shows that he relied on them. The affirmative record instead shows participation by minority organizations and incorporation of substantial features of the Ohio NAACP's proposal. Possible political objectives do not eliminate the need for evidence connecting those objectives to prohibited racial injury. On this record the finding is clearly erroneous. The Court expresses no broader view on race-conscious districting or the unpresented racial-classification claim.

Beer v. United States, 425 U.S. 130 (1976): followed in leaving unresolved whether the Fifteenth Amendment reaches vote-dilution claims; the assumption made here creates no new coverage holding.

Mobile v. Bolden, 446 U.S. 55 (1980): its plurality and separate writings are background to the alleged intentional-discrimination claim, not treated as a single majority rationale or a new categorical rule governing every race-conscious map.

A state legislative plan whose maximum total population deviation exceeds ten percent makes the initial showing that requires the State to justify the disparity; crossing that threshold does not make preservation of county boundaries legally unavailable as a justification. The District Court must determine whether the actual plan reasonably advances that rational state policy, including its substantiated connection to the deviations and consistency of implementation, and whether the resulting disparities nevertheless exceed constitutional limits; the Court does not decide that Ohio has met its burden.

Part IV of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion controls this standard and the population-only remand. All nine Justices adopt both; the Court approves neither the State’s justification nor a particular remedial map.

Equal protection requires substantially equal population in state legislative districts, but it does not demand mathematical identity in every district. Brown v. Thomson explains that deviations below ten percent are generally minor, while larger disparities make the initial showing requiring state justification. Ohio's plan crosses that threshold, so its officials must substantiate their explanation. The District Court nevertheless erred by treating county preservation as legally incapable of justifying deviations above ten percent. Mahan v. Howell permits a rational policy preserving political subdivisions to justify some disparities; Brown preserves both the need for a reasonable connection between plan and policy and an independent constitutional limit on the resulting inequality. Invoking county lines by name does not establish that connection. The inquiry concerns the actual plan, the consistency of the policy's application, and the magnitude of the departures it produces. The record has not received that analysis under the proper standard. The Court therefore remands this issue alone, without approving the deviations or ordering any particular racial arrangement.

Brown v. Thomson, 462 U.S. 835 (1983): followed for the threshold requiring a state justification, the required connection to a rational state policy, and the separate constitutional limit; departures below ten percent remain generally minor, not categorically immune.

Mahan v. Howell, 410 U.S. 315 (1973): followed in allowing a substantiated political-subdivision policy to justify departures above ten percent; it does not automatically validate Ohio's particular departures.

Effective March 2, 1993, these four holdings apply and clarify the existing rules. Section 2 requires proof of the challenged arrangement’s unequal electoral consequences; it does not require a State first to demonstrate a federal violation before choosing majority-minority districts. The assumed influence claim fails for absent majority bloc voting, while the availability of that claim and adaptation of Gingles’s first prerequisite remain open. The purpose ruling is confined to the deficient evidence here. Population departures above ten percent require substantiated state justification under Brown and Mahan; neither automatic invalidity nor automatic approval follows. Growe’s holdings remain intact. Its dismissal mandate addressed its exhausted record and does not dismiss Ohio’s independently live population claim. No precedent is overruled, and the Court decides no distinct racial-classification claim, Fifteenth Amendment dilution-coverage question, or general rule about race-conscious districting.

Supreme Court merits review ends with reversal and a remand limited to the population-equality claim. The District Court must determine, with the burden of justification on Ohio, whether the actual plan reasonably advances its county-preservation policy and whether the resulting disparities exceed constitutional limits. The statutory and purpose grounds do not remain open for retrial. The Court does not determine that Ohio has carried its population burden, set a new election date, or order a particular remedial district map. The lower-court population proceeding remains open; no return to this Court is automatically calendared.
