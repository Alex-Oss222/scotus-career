#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### No. 92-5584: an arguable current petition is not defeated by filing history

**Controlling proposition:** Rule 39.8 permits denial of leave to file without prepayment only upon a finding that the particular petition is frivolous or malicious; earlier unsuccessful filings cannot substitute for that finding. No. 92-5584's distinct general-rule and prospective statutory arguments clear the nonfrivolousness threshold on this record, so the Court grants leave without deciding that the arguments warrant certiorari or would succeed on the merits.

**Authority:** Part I of the per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine participating Justices adopt this filing-specific application and its limits.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Martin's history does not dispose of the paper now before it. Zatko permits discretionary fee denial for identified frivolous submissions in an extreme pattern of abuse; it does not make previous denials conclusive. Feldman distinguishes a challenge to a general bar rule from review of an individual admission judgment. Martin invokes that distinction and asserts prospective statutory grounds. His personal grievance, extensive collateral litigation and uncertain statutory theories present substantial obstacles, but those obstacles do not establish that every distinct question is frivolous. Denton preserves the difference between an arguable claim and a clearly baseless allegation; its statutory screening rule does not mechanically govern every feature of this Court's filing practice. The Court neither credits the disputed medical allegations nor finds that the asserted general classification actually exists. It decides no question of statutory coverage, retroactivity, preservation, immunity or ultimate jurisdiction. The motion is granted; the petition remains subject to ordinary discretionary review.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Zatko v. California, 502 U.S. 16 (November 4, 1991): applied as the existing discretionary, particular-submission rule; its extreme-abuse finding does not establish the frivolousness of a different current petition.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 460 U.S. 462 (1983): its general-rule/individual-judgment distinction supplies an arguable legal question here; the Court does not decide which side of that distinction the complete complaint occupies.
- Denton v. Hernandez, 504 U.S. 25 (May 4, 1992): its distinction between arguability and clearly baseless factual allegations informs this limited assessment; its statutory review and remedial rules remain unchanged and are not imported wholesale into Rule 39.

##### No. 92-5618: particular frivolousness and the available paid route

**Controlling proposition:** Where the current recusal petition identifies no concrete constitutionally disqualifying interest and develops no federal challenge to the independent state jurisdictional ground that defeats the action, those defects together establish frivolousness for this Rule 39.8 motion. In light of Martin's established extreme repetitive filing abuse, the Court exercises its discretion to deny this motion only, allowing payment of the Rule 38 fee and submission in Rule 33 form through November 23, 1992; the petition itself is not denied by this order.

**Authority:** Part II of the per curiam opinion, joined by White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; six of nine control the motion, its stated ground and the compliance opportunity. Stone-Zsela, Blackmun and Stevens dissent from this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this motion must be judged from the McDermott petition itself. Zatko permits a targeted fee denial when a present frivolous submission accompanies established extreme abuse. The Delaware judgment rests on the state rule that an appeal from the Justice of the Peace court retain the same action and parties. Martin's petition develops no federal objection to that jurisdictional ground. It instead attributes unconstitutional bias to professional ties, appearances and related allegations without identifying a concrete disqualifying interest comparable to the personal financial stake in Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Lavoie. A genuine constitutional defect in the decisionmaker cannot be dismissed merely by calling another ground jurisdictional. Here, however, the undeveloped constitutional premise and the unaddressed jurisdictional obstacle operate together. Neither filing volume, the state court's characterization, nor an affiliation alone supplies the finding. Denying this fee privilege leaves paid, compliant filing available. The Court decides no general rule insulating judicial affiliations from scrutiny and enters no merits judgment on the recusal claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Zatko v. California, 502 U.S. 16 (November 4, 1991): applied to this distinct submission; fee denial remains discretionary and separate from disposition of certiorari.
- Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Lavoie, 475 U.S. 813 (1986): distinguished because this petition establishes no comparable concrete personal financial stake; other constitutionally disqualifying interests are not categorically excluded.

##### No prospective filing restriction on this record

**Controlling proposition:** The Court declines to impose a prospective fee-and-form condition on Martin's unidentified future civil-certiorari petitions because the established record does not show that the available treatment of particular filings is inadequate. This case-specific refusal decides neither that a prior warning is invariably required nor that prospective restrictions are categorically unlawful, and imposes no condition on future civil or criminal filings.

**Authority:** Part III of the per curiam opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the sufficiency of this narrower, record-specific ground without resolving the Court's power on a different record.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy should remain confined to the filings before it. McDonald and Sindram permit measures directed at concrete abuse of extraordinary writs; Sindram also identifies the special risk created by petitions that have no filing deadline. Those decisions do not compel a restriction on all future ordinary civil petitions. Zatko denied the identified motions and preserved a paid route. This record establishes no subsequent warning, breach of that order or sequence of intervening adjudications demonstrating that its narrower response failed. Moreover, one present petition raises distinct arguments sufficient to permit filing. The established repetitive history supports close examination and, in the companion matter, fee denial, but does not demonstrate a need to prejudge unknown papers. The Court therefore gives the Clerk no prospective exclusion instruction. Its conclusion rests on this record and available remedies, without deciding a universal notice requirement, a numerical threshold, or the legality of every possible future restriction.

**Precedent treatment:**

- In re McDonald, 489 U.S. 180 (1989): preserved as authority for targeted protection against extraordinary-writ abuse; no extension to all future civil-certiorari petitions is made here.
- In re Sindram, 498 U.S. 177 (1991): its extraordinary-writ setting and preservation of other filing categories are distinguished; its existing force is unchanged.
- Zatko v. California, 502 U.S. 16 (November 4, 1991): its limited remedy is preserved; no warning, compliance default or further adjudication is inferred from that order.

Effective November 2, 1992, the Court applies the existing discretionary, filing-specific Rule 39.8 rule of *Zatko*, which permits targeted fee denial for a particular frivolous submission in the established abuse setting. No. 92-5584 clears the low filing threshold; the distinct defects in No. 92-5618 support denial of its fee motion. No replacement rule, numerical test, categorical ban, mandatory warning prerequisite, or constitutional right to unrestricted fee waiver is created. The refusal of prospective relief is confined to this record. *Denton*’s §1915(d) rules for screening factual allegations and appellate review remain unchanged. No precedent is overruled. Neither underlying judgment is adopted as this Court’s merits holding, and denial of certiorari establishes no bar-admission, statutory, immunity, or recusal merits precedent.

### Church of Scientology of California v. United States, No. 91-946

Merits decision, November 16, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 6, 1992; decided November 16, 1992. On writ of certiorari, granted March 2, 1992, to the Ninth Circuit’s September 10, 1991 unpublished order in No. 91-55506 dismissing the Church’s appeal from IRS summons enforcement as moot.

The IRS summoned recordings held by the clerk of the Los Angeles County Superior Court during litigation concerning their possession. After the federal district court’s April 15, 1991 enforcement order and unsuccessful requests for a stay, copies reached the IRS. The Ninth Circuit dismissed the appeal because compliance had occurred. The question is whether return or destruction of retained copies could still supply effective relief. This review does not decide the underlying attorney-client privilege or crime-fraud controversy.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participate.

#### Judgment

The Court vacates the judgment dismissing the appeal as moot and remands for further proceedings.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Legal ground and remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mootness of summons-enforcement appeal | Vacate and remand, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | Possible meaningful relief concerning retained copies preserves a live controversy; the Ninth Circuit must resume appellate review. |
| Privilege, crime-fraud, validity of enforcement, and appropriate eventual relief | Not decided | No merits determination by any Justice in this disposition | No merits determination by any Justice in this disposition | No return, destruction, or suppression is commanded, and no unlawful summons is found. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Stevens | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Vacatur and remand | Entire opinion; nine votes, controlling. |

#### Controlling Proposition and Authority

**Controlling proposition:** Compliance with an IRS summons-enforcement order does not moot a timely appeal when, if enforcement proves unlawful, a court can afford meaningful partial relief by ordering return or destruction of copies the Government retains. Complete restoration of secrecy is unnecessary; this ruling establishes the availability of review, not entitlement to that remedy.

**Authority:** Stevens’s opinion for the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas: nine votes for this rule and its application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an appeal remains a live controversy when judicial relief can still protect a concrete interest. Mills v. Green supplies the requirement of possible effectual relief; it does not demand that a court undo every consequence of the challenged order. The IRS has learned the contents of these recordings, but it also retains copies. An order requiring their return or destruction could address the Church’s continuing interest in possession and privacy. The Government’s argument confuses irreversible disclosure with the absence of every remedy. Reisman v. Caplin establishes that IRS enforcement orders are appealable. Sections 7402(b) and 7604(a), which authorize judicial enforcement, do not withdraw that appellate review after compliance. The Court need not determine the whole extent of a district court’s power to condition enforcement. Nor does recognizing possible relief decide whether these recordings are privileged, whether the crime-fraud exception applies, or whether the Government may later use information derived from them. Those questions do not prevent review of the order.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), is applied: some effectual relief suffices.
- Reisman v. Caplin, 375 U.S. 440, 449 (1964), is applied: summons-enforcement orders remain judicially reviewable.
- United States v. Zolin, 491 U.S. 554 (1989), is left undisturbed: its privilege ruling and divided disposition of enforcement-condition jurisdiction are not reopened.

#### Limits, Precedent Treatment, and Current-Law Effect

The Ninth Circuit’s Kersting-based rule that completed production alone moots the appeal cannot stand where retained copies allow effectual relief. United States v. Ryan, 402 U.S. 530 (1971), and Perlman v. United States, 247 U.S. 7 (1918), concern finality and immediate appeal, not an automatic mootness rule; this decision establishes no general third-party exception. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled.

From November 16, 1992, the mootness inquiry in this setting asks whether a court can still grant meaningful relief concerning retained materials. Possible partial relief by return or destruction suffices despite irreversible acquisition of knowledge. Existing appellate review of IRS enforcement orders remains available. The merits, scope of district-court enforcement conditions, future-use restrictions, claimed preclusion, and proposed third-party exception remain reserved. No privilege, tax-exemption, damages, or suppression doctrine changes. There is no separate writing.

#### Source Notes

The [petition and judicial appendices](https://archive.org/download/micro_IA40385013_0224/micro_IA40385013_0224%202.%20Petition%20for%20Writ%20of%20Certiorari.pdf) supply the lower record: Appendix A, 1a–2a, contains the Ninth Circuit dismissal; B–D, 3a–11a, contain district orders; and 12a–49a contain prior judicial proceedings. The [Official OT1991 Journal](https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/journals/scannedjournals/1991_journal.pdf), p. 510, supports the March 2 grant. [United States Reports, 506 U.S. 9–18](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep506/usrep506009/usrep506009.pdf) supports the event, chronology, and prior statutory and precedential text. These sources do not establish the Church’s ultimate entitlement to relief.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Review of the mootness dismissal is complete. The Ninth Circuit must proceed with the appeal. Whether the summons was improperly issued or enforced and, if so, what lawful relief should issue remain for further proceedings. The Court enters no return or destruction order.

**End of entry: Church of Scientology of California v. United States, merits decision, November 16, 1992.**

### Hadley v. United States, No. 91-6646

Dismissal of certiorari as improvidently granted, November 16, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1992; decided November 16, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 918 F.2d 848 (November 14, 1990), rehearing denied September 9, 1991. The March 2, 1992 grant was unqualified. The petition’s four questions concern admission of other-acts evidence to prove intent, the offer to stipulate concerning intent, Rule 403 prejudice balancing, and the claimed circuit disagreement. Separate expert-testimony, evidentiary-sufficiency, and sentencing issues considered below were not questions in the petition.

Hadley was tried on severed federal sexual-abuse counts and convicted on counts VII–IX concerning one complainant, with acquittals on X–XI. He disputed the acts and offered to stipulate concerning intent if the acts were established. That offer was not accepted. Evidence of earlier acts against other children was admitted. The Ninth Circuit sustained its relevance for a purpose other than showing a propensity to commit the charged acts and its admissibility under Rule 403. The grant brought the identified other-acts issues before this Court.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participate.

#### Public Action and Judgment

The Court dismisses the writ of certiorari as improvidently granted by per curiam order. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents. No merits vote count is published; his dissent is the only publicly identified separate position.

#### Scope and Current-Law Effect

The dismissal supplies no merits holding. It leaves the lower judgment intact without adopting the Ninth Circuit’s view of other-acts relevance, approving its Rule 403 balance, deciding harmless error, or creating a rule for uncontested intent. The Court neither holds that a concession automatically excludes other-acts evidence nor approves ignoring such a concession in Rule 403.

No precedent is overruled, limited, extended, or newly adopted. The Federal Rules, Huddleston, and the existing Estelle holding remain as they stood. The Ninth Circuit judgment survives as the judgment below, not as Supreme Court law. The dissent’s proposed remand and explanation supply no controlling proposition.

#### Separate Position Relevant to the Decision

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents from the dismissal and would vacate and remand on the preserved Rules 404(b) and 403 issue. He does not treat the defense’s offer concerning intent as an accepted stipulation or as removal of an element. The prosecution must still prove intent, but its actual need for remote other-victim testimony bears on probative value. Huddleston v. United States identifies proper purpose, relevance, balancing, and limiting instructions as separate protections; it does not itself command reversal. These authorities support his noncontrolling position.

For attempted abuse, the question after independent proof of preliminary conduct is whether the conduct was intended to culminate in penetration. Stone-Zsela regards the charged incident involving the same complainant approximately one month earlier as a closer source of that inference. For the completed act and contact counts, a court must identify the remaining uncertainty about mental state and explain how the other testimony addresses it without relying on propensity. The Ninth Circuit performed a Rule 403 analysis; his objection concerns its adequacy. Similarity does not by itself explain the independent value of inflammatory accounts of remote abuse. The limiting instruction matters, but does not settle whether unfair prejudice substantially outweighs probative value. He would require reconsideration under ordinary abuse-of-discretion review and harmless-error review if admission proves erroneous. He would neither order an automatic acquittal nor decide expert testimony, sufficiency, sentencing, or any question under later sex-offense evidence legislation. No other Justice joins this proposed application.

### Parke v. Raley, No. 91-719

Merits decision, December 1, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 5, 1992; decided December 1, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Sixth Circuit, 945 F.2d 137 (September 19, 1991), which granted conditional habeas relief concerning one prior plea used in Kentucky persistent-felony-offender sentencing.

Raley entered a conditional plea to second-degree robbery and first-degree persistent-felony-offender status after a hearing rejected his challenge to prior counseled burglary convictions. His five-year robbery sentence was enhanced to ten years. The prior proceedings are conventionally called the 1979 and 1981 pleas; the first form was signed November 29, 1979, and the state appellate account dates formal acceptance to January 1980. The second was October 8, 1981. No complete plea transcript survives. The Sixth Circuit sustained the 1979 determination but required a new hearing on the 1981 plea under a rule placing the whole validity burden on the Commonwealth by clear and convincing evidence. The questions concern that allocation, that measure of proof, and whether this record supports the 1981 plea.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the Sixth Circuit judgment granting conditional habeas relief regarding Raley’s 1981 plea. Justice Blackmun concurs in the judgment.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kentucky initial production allocation | Upheld, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposed | A proved final counseled judgment may carry a rebuttable presumption of regularity; Kentucky’s responsive persuasion obligation is preserved. |
| Sixth Circuit clear-and-convincing requirement | Rejected, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposed | That heightened measure of proof is not constitutionally compelled for this collateral sentencing inquiry. |
| 1981 plea and conditional writ | Reversed, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No Justice opposed | Supported findings and combined affirmative evidence establish a knowing, voluntary plea; conditional new-hearing relief is set aside. |

The 1979 plea remains undisturbed; its validity is no longer challenged here.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Eight Justices support all three holdings and reversal | Entire opinion |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Blackmun | No other Justice joined | Agrees with the judgment on a ground limited to sentencing | Separate concurrence; does not join the Court’s opinion |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Holding 1 — initial production burden

**Controlling proposition:** In Kentucky’s collateral challenge to a final counseled conviction used for sentence enhancement, due process permits the Commonwealth to prove the judgment and require the defendant to produce evidence of infringement or procedural irregularity. If the defendant refutes regularity, the Commonwealth must affirmatively establish constitutional validity under Kentucky’s procedure; a missing old transcript alone, without alleged official misconduct, does not establish invalidity.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; eight votes at this level. Blackmun agrees with the result in a separate writing limited to sentencing.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State may begin with the regularity of a proved final judgment while allowing a genuine challenge to its validity. Boykin v. Alabama requires a knowing and voluntary guilty plea and an affirmative record on direct review. It does not allocate every burden when a final judgment is later challenged in a separate sentencing proceeding. Johnson v. Zerbst recognizes that collateral constitutional claims may carry a challenger’s evidentiary burden. Kentucky allows rebuttal, provides a hearing when needed, and requires affirmative state proof once regularity is refuted. The defendant participated in the earlier proceeding, while the State may face comparable difficulty reconstructing it. A missing transcript therefore does not itself demonstrate fundamental unfairness. Burgett v. Texas preserves the prohibition against use of an invalid uncounseled conviction; it does not make every unavailable plea record proof of a constitutional defect. This decision neither transfers the burden of proving an offense nor permits courts to ignore evidence that a particular plea was involuntary.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), is distinguished as direct review without weakening its valid-waiver requirement.
- *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 468–469 (1938), applies to the presumption of regularity on collateral review.
- *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), remains controlling for constitutionally defective uncounseled convictions.
- *Medina v. California* (June 22, 1992) is distinguished: its narrow competence-burden holding does not establish a controlling categorical ban on practical due-process analysis.

##### Holding 2 — measure of proof

**Controlling proposition:** Due process does not require the clear-and-convincing extrarecord proof demanded by the Sixth Circuit to establish validity of an old guilty plea in this collateral sentencing proceeding. Kentucky’s retention of the ultimate persuasion burden does not entail that heightened constitutional quantum; the fact of prior conviction and the validity inquiry remain distinct.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice Court opinion; Blackmun independently agrees on his sentencing ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Sixth Circuit demanded more than the Constitution requires when it prescribed clear and convincing extrarecord evidence. Boykin did not decide the measure of proof in a collateral proceeding, and its direct-review record requirement does not supply that missing rule. Johnson v. Zerbst permits ordinary collateral proof of constitutional invalidity; it does not require every uncertainty from an aged record to be resolved by a heightened state burden. Reconstruction can be difficult for both parties. Kentucky responds by recognizing an initial presumption, allowing contrary evidence and requiring a responsive state showing when the presumption is refuted. Neither the constitutional importance of a voluntary plea nor that responsive burden establishes the particular quantum imposed below. The Court rejects that mandatory quantum without announcing one evidentiary measure for every state system. Kentucky’s separate requirement to prove the fact of prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt remains unaffected, as do the prosecution’s burdens concerning the present offense.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Boykin* is not extended into a clear-and-convincing collateral proof requirement.
- *Zerbst* supports the distinction between constitutional validity and a universally heightened burden.
- The Sixth Circuit’s *Dunn v. Simmons*, 877 F.2d 1275 (1989), cannot govern insofar as it demands that constitutional measure of proof and shifts the entire production burden solely because no transcript survives.

##### Holding 3 — the 1981 plea

**Controlling proposition:** Under then-applicable 28 U.S.C. §2254(d), a competent state court’s factual determination, evidenced by written findings, an opinion or other reliable and adequate written indicia after a merits hearing involving the applicant and the State receives a presumption of correctness subject to the conditions and exceptions below; the ultimate federal question whether a plea was knowing and voluntary remains independently determined. On the established earlier warnings, contemporaneous 1981 charge form and jury warning, representation, education, unimpaired condition and testimony here, Raley’s 1981 plea was knowing and voluntary and the Sixth Circuit’s conditional new-hearing remedy is unwarranted.

**Statutory qualifications:** The presumption does not govern if the applicant establishes, it otherwise appears, or the State admits that (1) the state hearing did not resolve the merits of the factual dispute; (2) its factfinding procedure was inadequate to afford a full and fair hearing; (3) material facts were not adequately developed; (4) the state court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction or jurisdiction over the applicant; (5) an indigent applicant was denied appointed counsel in deprivation of his constitutional right; (6) the applicant did not receive a full, fair and adequate hearing; or (7) the applicant was otherwise denied due process in that proceeding. Under paragraph (8), the presumption also does not govern when the pertinent state record is produced and the federal court, considering that record as a whole, concludes that the factual determination lacks fair record support. At a federal evidentiary hearing, once the state factual determination is duly proved, and absent those circumstances, the applicant must establish its error by convincing evidence. That federal factual-review burden is distinct from Kentucky’s allocation and measure of proof of a prior plea’s constitutional validity. Inferences receive deference only when fairly supported by the facts; no presumption attaches to the ultimate federal legal conclusion.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice Court opinion; Blackmun concurs in reversal without a separate contrary factual conclusion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the record supports the Kentucky courts’ inference of continued understanding. Marshall v. Lonberger distinguishes historical findings, which receive statutory deference when fairly supported, from the ultimate federal validity of the plea. Raley’s earlier advice is established by a rights-and-waiver form and counsel’s confirmation, not by the bare existence of a conviction. In 1981 he again had counsel, understood the charge form and acknowledged a jury-trial warning. His education, lack of impairment and testimony about sentencing and bargaining support the inference that he understood the choice he made. His inability to recall additional warnings does not compel the contrary inference. The state hearing resolved the factual dispute through adequately developed evidence and fair procedures; the whole pertinent record fairly supports its findings. No jurisdictional, counsel or other due-process defect in that hearing is shown. Applying Boykin and North Carolina v. Alford independently to those facts, the plea was knowing and voluntary. The Court does not presume understanding from any prior conviction or make every experienced defendant’s plea valid. It reverses the conditional writ on this particular showing; the unchallenged 1979 determination remains intact.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Marshall v. Lonberger*, 459 U.S. 422, 431–438 (1983), applies to supported historical inferences and independent legal review.
- *Boykin* applies to the requirement of an informed, voluntary choice.
- *North Carolina v. Alford*, 400 U.S. 25, 31 (1970), applies to the requirement of an informed, voluntary choice.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The Sixth Circuit’s Dunn v. Simmons allocation and compulsory proof quantum are rejected; no Supreme Court precedent is overruled. Kentucky’s Dunn v. Commonwealth, 703 S.W.2d 874 (1985), supplies the actual state procedure, not an independently created federal rule. The statutory sentencing provisions and Kentucky’s Gadd construction remain distinct from the federal due-process permission.

Kentucky may apply its rebuttable production allocation in this collateral challenge; it must follow its own responsive affirmative-proof rule after rebuttal. The Sixth Circuit cannot require the State to bear the entire burden by clear and convincing evidence solely because old transcripts are unavailable. The Court does not establish a universal collateral proof quantum or a categorical no-right-to-collateral-review rule. Then-§2254(d) governs historical facts subject to the hearing, factual-development, jurisdiction, counsel, due-process and whole-record-support exceptions set out in the third holding above; federal validity remains independently determined. The 1981 conditional writ is reversed on the actual evidentiary showing. Direct-review Boykin, the exclusion of invalid uncounseled convictions, criminal-element proof, and the Medina fracture remain undisturbed.

#### Separate Position

Justice Blackmun concurs in the judgment. He accepts Kentucky’s allocation and rejects the Sixth Circuit’s required clear-and-convincing proof because this proceeding concerns sentence enhancement after conviction, not adjudication of a separate offense. A proved final judgment may carry a rebuttable presumption of regularity in that setting. The defendant may introduce evidence of irregularity; the Commonwealth must then make the affirmative showing Kentucky requires. This conclusion does not excuse an involuntary plea or an invalid uncounseled conviction, nor remove the State’s burden to prove the fact of prior conviction as state law requires.

Blackmun reserves a different case in which the State defines repeat-offender status as a separate crime: he would not permit a silent record and an evidentiary shift to substitute for the State’s affirmative proof of the constitutional validity required for that offense. His distinction between guilt and punishment is consistent with his own and Stevens’s discussions in Marshall v. Lonberger. He does not join a general rule that every recidivist provision is merely sentencing, and he does not challenge the judgment concerning the evidence supporting Raley’s 1981 plea. His concurrence is not a controlling rule for a separate-crime statute; that issue is outside this case.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The judgment does not authorize reliance on an actually invalid or uncounseled conviction. Whether the Constitution requires other state systems to permit collateral attacks was not raised below or in the certiorari petition and is not decided. The Commonwealth did not invoke a defense under *Teague*; the Court does not address one.

It supports Kentucky’s burden sequence, both plea hearings and the conditional remedy. The state appellate opinion’s second-degree-robbery account controls over the federal lower opinions’ inconsistent description of the current offense as burglary. [Official Marshall v. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The judgment neither vacates the robbery conviction nor directs a new hearing; no special new-hearing condition is imposed. The existing determination concerning the 1979 plea remains undisturbed.

### Richmond v. Lewis, No. 91-7094

Merits decision, December 1, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 13, 1992; decided December 1, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Ninth Circuit, 948 F.2d 1473, whose amended opinion and denial of rehearing en banc were issued December 18, 1991, affirming denial of federal habeas relief. Certiorari was granted March 30, 1992.

Richmond was convicted of robbery and murder. After an earlier sentence was vacated for improperly limited mitigation, he was resentenced to death in 1980. The judge found prior serious-murder and violent-kidnapping aggravators and the then-vague especially-heinous/cruel/depraved factor. Arizona’s 1983 review produced a two-Justice principal opinion retaining a narrowed factor, a two-Justice concurrence rejecting it but affirming death, and a dissent favoring life. The federal question is whether that adjudication corrected the weighing error. The Court does not review guilt on this question.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the denial of habeas relief as to the death sentence and remands for conditional relief. Justice Thomas concurs; Justice Scalia dissents.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weighing error and failure of appellate cure | Reversed, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Scalia would affirm | The vague factor entered the death-selection balance; no sufficient lawful appellate cure occurred. |
| Conditional sentence relief | Ordered, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Scalia | Return through the Ninth Circuit to the district court for a writ unless Arizona corrects the constitutional error within a reasonable period or vacates death and imposes a lawful lesser sentence. |

The conviction and other claims remain undisturbed. The decision grants no guilt relief, automatic life sentence, or federal resentencing, and does not resolve unused alternative claims.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O’Connor | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Eight Justices support reversal and conditional relief | Both holdings and the remedy |
| Concurrence | Thomas | No other Justice joined | Also joins the Court in full | Separate concurrence |
| Dissent | Scalia | No other Justice joined | Would affirm | Rejects the Court’s weighing-error rule and conditional remedy |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Holding 1 — an actual cure is necessary

**Controlling proposition:** When a death sentence in a State that weighs aggravation against mitigation rests on an unconstitutionally vague aggravating factor, remaining valid factors do not alone cure the error. The sentence may stand only after an authorized sentencer actually makes a constitutionally valid new sentencing determination, including lawful appellate reweighing, or the State establishes constitutional harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s opinion for the Court, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas: eight votes. Thomas also writes separately; Scalia dissents.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an invalid factor cannot remain uncorrected in the process choosing death. Arizona’s statute directs consideration of aggravating and mitigating circumstances and permits death when an aggravator exists and mitigation is not sufficiently substantial to call for leniency. Its courts construe that direction as weighing. The survival of two valid aggravators therefore establishes eligibility without establishing a lawful selection decision. Clemons v. Mississippi permits an authorized state appellate court to correct the error through actual reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review. The operative Stringer, Sochor and Espinosa decisions require the substance of that correction, not an assumption that the same outcome would follow. No particular formula of words is indispensable, but a general obligation of independent review is not performance of a new sentencing calculus. The federal habeas court cannot supply Arizona’s missing weighing itself. This rule leaves the State free to choose an authorized constitutional cure and does not require an automatic life sentence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Clemons v. Mississippi*, 494 U.S. 738 (1990), permits lawful state appellate cure, but not automatic affirmance based on the number of remaining factors.
- *Stringer v. Black* (March 9, 1992) is applied at its controlling level: actual weighing error requires actual constitutional correction.
- *Sochor v. Florida* (June 8, 1992) is applied at its controlling level: actual weighing error requires actual constitutional correction.
- *Espinosa v. Florida* (June 29, 1992) is applied at its controlling level: actual weighing error requires actual constitutional correction.
- *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), preserves the State’s obligation to establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.

##### Holding 2 — Arizona’s adjudication did not cure this sentence

**Controlling proposition:** On the parties’ premise that a majority of the Arizona Supreme Court had to cast valid curative votes, this sentence cannot stand: even assuming the two Justices retaining the narrowed aggravator lawfully reweighed, the two other affirming Justices relied conclusively on the remaining criminal history without a new balance of mitigation. The denial of sentence relief is reversed for a conditional writ allowing a reasonable period for constitutional correction or vacation of the death sentence and imposition of a lawful lesser sentence.

**Authority:** The same eight-Justice Court opinion. No rule demanding identical reasoning from every member of a state appellate majority is adopted.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the state judgment lacks enough votes for a lawful cure on the premise the parties accept. Chief Justice Holohan and Justice Hays retained the heinous-or-depraved factor and balanced mitigation against three aggravators. The Court assumes, without deciding, that their application of the later narrowing construction and their reweighing were valid. Justices Cameron and Gordon rejected that factor, but their separate explanation treated Richmond’s murder and kidnapping history as conclusive justification for death. They did not perform a new balance against the mitigation, which included evidence of rehabilitation. Their general agreement with an independent-review obligation cannot overcome that specific explanation. Justice Feldman would have imposed life. Thus at most two valid votes supported affirmance, and one more was necessary. The Court need not decide whether Richmond drove over the victim, whether the principal opinion permissibly applied the narrowed factor, or whether separately valid rationales could otherwise combine. The conviction remains untouched; only the uncorrected death sentence requires relief.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), is preserved: narrowing constructions may be valid, but do not supply a curative vote absent here.
- *Lewis v. Jeffers*, 497 U.S. 764 (1990), is preserved: appropriately reviewed state applications may be valid, but do not supply a curative vote absent here.
- *State v. Gretzler*, 135 Ariz. 42, 659 P.2d 1 (1983), remains the state narrowing source; its proper application by Holohan and Hays is not decided.
- *Clemons* requires an actual lawful cure and supports the stated remedy.
- *Stringer* requires actual constitutional correction and supports the stated remedy.
- *Sochor* requires actual constitutional correction and supports the stated remedy.
- *Espinosa* requires actual constitutional correction and supports the stated remedy.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Walton/Jeffers permit properly narrowed aggravators and appropriate habeas review; they do not authorize an automatic affirmance with remaining factors. The Ninth Circuit’s alternative treatment of Arizona as outside weighing is rejected under the statutory direction and authoritative Arizona construction. The Court assumes rather than resolves the validity of Holohan-Hays’s Gretzler application. No Supreme Court precedent is overruled, and no rule requiring a single common state appellate rationale is established.

Arizona is a weighing State for this sentencing rule. Remaining valid aggravators do not cure a vague factor’s effect on the selection process. An actual lawful state cure or constitutional harmlessness is necessary, and the federal habeas court cannot replace that cure with its own sentencing balance. On the parties’ majority-vote premise, the 1983 adjudication lacks a sufficient curative affirmance. This application strengthens no general rule about fractured state judgments beyond the accepted premise. Existing weighing doctrine remains operative; Scalia’s eligibility-only theory and Thomas’s preserved retroactivity objection remain noncontrolling.

#### Separate Positions

Justice Thomas concurs in full and separately explains why adherence to existing Stringer law requires the result. His earlier dissent challenged the conclusion that the no-automatic-affirmance principle was already dictated for Teague purposes. He maintains that objection to Stringer, but treats the decision as binding. The State asserts no Teague defense here, and under the operative rule the Cameron-Gordon opinion supplies no actual reweighing. His disagreement with Stringer’s retroactivity reasoning therefore supplies no basis to deny this petitioner relief. The concurrence neither reopens Teague nor changes the Court’s rule.

Justice Scalia dissents and would affirm. Relying on the position he stated in Walton v. Arizona and maintained in Sochor, he regards the existence of constitutionally valid narrowing factors as the Eighth Amendment requirement. The uncontested prior-murder and violent-kidnapping factors satisfy that eligibility restriction. He rejects the further constitutional requirement that mitigation be weighed in selecting the sentence. On that understanding, failure to reweigh after removing another aggravator raises no independent federal constitutional defect, and any dispute over the principal opinion’s use of that factor cannot invalidate this sentence. He does not join the Court’s weighing-error rule or conditional remedy. His position has one vote and does not alter the governing Stringer/Sochor/Espinosa rule.

The Court’s Stringer, Sochor and Espinosa decisions supply the operative rules and the separate positions discussed here. #### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The case is remanded to the Ninth Circuit with instructions to return it to the federal district court. The district court must condition relief on Arizona, within a reasonable period that the district court fixes, either correcting the constitutional sentencing error or vacating the death sentence and imposing a lawful lesser sentence. Authorized appellate reweighing remains available. Constitutional harmless-error review is not categorically barred, although none occurred here and the State did not assert that the trial error was harmless. The conviction remains intact. The decision neither directs an automatic life sentence nor resolves unused alternative claims. No federal court itself reweighs the aggravation and mitigation for Arizona.

### Soldal v. Cook County, No. 91-6516

Merits decision, December 8, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 5, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Seventh Circuit, 942 F.2d 1073 (1991), reviewing the adverse Fourth Amendment ruling in its en banc disposition. The en banc judgment formally affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded by reinstating the panel outside its Fourth Amendment discussion. The question is whether government-attributable removal of a home is a seizure without an accompanying privacy or liberty invasion. The Soldals seek restoration of their seizure claim under §1983; respondents defend the judgment, alternatively for want of state action. Reporter locator: 506 U.S. 56.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses and remands, 9–0.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fourth Amendment coverage and adverse summary judgment | Reverse and remand, 9–0 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | Restore the seizure claim; no damages or final unreasonableness determination. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Reversal and remand | Entire opinion. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A seizure of a home protects possession independently of privacy

**Controlling proposition:** Meaningful government-attributable interference with possession of a home is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment even without a search, invasion of privacy or liberty, or criminal investigation. The family’s alleged forcible dispossession meets that threshold, but coverage alone does not establish unreasonableness or every element of §1983 liability.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; nine Justices adopt the same rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that taking a home implicates the Fourth Amendment even when officials do not search it. Jacobsen distinguishes a search’s invasion of privacy from a seizure’s interference with possession; Place likewise examines the detention of property independently of a search. Hicks confirms that the absence of a new privacy intrusion does not remove a seizure from constitutional scrutiny. The alleged removal here deprived the family of its home while deputies prevented resistance. Adickes permits the joint-action inference accepted below at summary judgment, and respondents’ alternative ground does not require disturbing that determination. The concern that this rule federalizes every repossession confuses coverage with liability. Private conduct alone is insufficient, reasonableness remains to be adjudicated, and municipal policy, causation, immunity and relief retain their separate requirements. The Court does not decide those matters or extend the Amendment to every category of property. A home is expressly protected by its text.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Jacobsen, 466 U.S. 109, is applied: possession and privacy remain independently protected interests.
- United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696, is applied: seizure scrutiny does not require an accompanying search.
- Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321, is applied: a seizure of property in plain view remains subject to the Fourth Amendment.
- Adickes v. S. H. Kress & Co., 398 U.S. 144, is applied to the summary-judgment inference of joint action, without a final conspiracy finding.
- Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658, is preserved: seizure coverage does not establish municipal liability.
- Wyatt v. Cole, May 18, 1992, is preserved: attribution does not settle immunity or a private merits defense.

##### A due-process characterization does not displace the specific seizure claim

**Controlling proposition:** A court may not reject an otherwise applicable Fourth Amendment claim merely because the same conduct also concerns a deprivation of property under the Due Process Clause. Each applicable constitutional protection must be examined on its own terms; this disposition does not adjudicate the family’s separate substantive-due-process claim.

**Authority:** White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; this holding is unanimous.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a general description of the controversy cannot erase a specific constitutional protection. Graham directs courts to use the explicit constitutional source governing the challenged conduct instead of replacing it with generalized substantive due process. It does not authorize treating the dominant character of a dispute as a reason to disregard another applicable constitutional command. Here, the seizure of a home falls within the Fourth Amendment’s own terms. The landlord-tenant setting and the availability of state remedies therefore cannot end that inquiry. Parratt addresses the adequacy of process for a property deprivation; it does not decide whether a government-attributable seizure is reasonable. Hudson’s prison-specific treatment of possession does not establish a general exclusion for homes outside prison. The family may pursue the restored seizure claim without obtaining a ruling here on its separate substantive-due-process contention. The Court leaves that question, and the remaining liability and remedial issues, unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386, is applied: the specific Fourth Amendment source governs its covered conduct and is not displaced by a theory based on the dispute’s dominant character.
- Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527, is distinguished: procedural remedies after a deprivation do not answer the distinct seizure claim.
- Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517, is confined to its prison setting for the relevant coverage proposition; no rule excluding possession of a home follows.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. Effective December 8, 1992, Fourth Amendment seizure coverage reaches meaningful government-attributable interference with possession of a home independently of privacy, liberty, a search or criminal investigative purpose. A competing due-process characterization does not displace the specific seizure claim. The general §1983 and immunity frameworks remain unchanged; ultimate reasonableness and separate liability and remedy requirements remain open.

### Farrar v. Hobby, No. 91-990

Merits decision, December 14, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 7, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, Estate of Farrar v. Cain, 941 F.2d 1311 (1991), which reversed the fee award for lack of prevailing-party status. The petition, granted without an express limitation, presents whether an enforceable nominal-damages judgment establishes eligibility for attorney’s fees under 42 U.S.C. §1988. The parties dispute whether this Court should also decide the reasonable amount. Only Joseph Farrar’s estate coadministrators petitioned; Dale Farrar’s individual fee claim is outside review. Reporter locator: 506 U.S. 103.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court unanimously recognizes eligibility under §1988 and vacates and remands the fee judgment, 5–4.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Enforceable nominal relief establishes eligibility

**Controlling proposition:** A civil-rights plaintiff who obtains an enforceable nominal-damages judgment against the defendant from whom fees are sought is a prevailing party under §1988. The judgment must directly benefit the plaintiff by changing the defendant’s legal obligation; its nominal amount does not defeat eligibility.

**Authority:** Part I of White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine Justices support this controlling proposition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that an enforceable dollar judgment is actual relief. Carey recognizes nominal damages for a proved denial of procedural due process when compensatory injury cannot be shown. That judgment obliges Hobby to pay money he otherwise had no duty to pay. Hewitt denied prevailing-party status where favorable judicial language produced no relief, and Rhodes did so where a declaration could benefit neither plaintiff. Those limitations do not describe this enforceable award. Garland makes a material alteration in the parties’ legal relationship the statutory threshold and assigns the extent of success to the amount inquiry. Its discussion of an insignificant technical victory does not convert an enforceable monetary judgment into no judgment at all. The estate therefore qualifies as a prevailing party. This conclusion neither revisits the jury’s liability or causation findings nor guarantees any positive fee; §1988 still requires that any amount awarded be reasonable.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, is applied: nominal relief vindicates the established procedural violation without presumed compensatory injury.
- Hewitt v. Helms, 482 U.S. 755, is distinguished: favorable language without enforceable relief differs from a payment judgment.
- Rhodes v. Stewart, 488 U.S. 1, is distinguished: ineffective declaratory relief differs from a judgment directly benefiting the estate.
- Texas State Teachers Assn. v. Garland Independent School District, 489 U.S. 782, is applied and clarified: the magnitude of relief affects amount, not eligibility where nominal relief is enforceable.

##### Reasonable amount requires reconsideration on this review

**Controlling proposition:** When the court of appeals denies fees solely because a nominal-damages plaintiff is ineligible, correction of that error does not itself determine a reasonable amount. On this record, the fee judgment is vacated and remanded for preserved reasonableness objections and an explained Hensley assessment of actual success, allowing reduction to zero without automatically reinstating the prior award or requiring proportionality to damages.

**Authority:** Part II of White’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and Souter; five Justices control the remedy and stated fee principles.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the amount should be reconsidered under the correct eligibility rule. Hensley makes the degree of success central, requires exclusion of unrelated unsuccessful work, and calls for an explanation of the fee awarded. The failed demand for $17 million plainly bears on that assessment. Hobby expressly argues for zero fees or a substantial reduction. Petitioners object that the Fifth Circuit never decided amount and seek remand of that question. The Court chooses to leave the preserved alternative to lower-court review of the discretionary award; it does not treat that argument as waived or unbriefed. Rivera forecloses a mechanical ratio between damages and fees; it does not justify compensating unsuccessful work or inventing a public benefit. Blanchard likewise commits the reasonable amount to the circumstances rather than a rigid measure. The remand permits a substantially reduced award or zero after the required analysis and does not restore the $280,000 award automatically. The Court does not categorically forbid affirmance on a sufficient alternative fee ground in another properly presented case.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, is applied: actual success, unrelated unsuccessful work and a reasoned discretionary assessment govern amount.
- City of Riverside v. Rivera, 477 U.S. 561, is applied within its authority: no mechanical proportionality requirement follows, and fees for nominal relief are not guaranteed.
- Blanchard v. Bergeron, 489 U.S. 87, is applied: the court must determine a reasonable statutory fee in the circumstances.
- City of Burlington v. Dague, June 24, 1992, is distinguished: contingency enhancement under the two environmental statutes does not decide this eligibility or remand question.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. Effective December 14, 1992, an enforceable nominal judgment establishes §1988 prevailing-party status. Actual success governs the reasonable amount, including possible zero; no mandatory proportion between damages and fees or presumption of substantial fees follows. This case requires remand rather than immediate appellate fixation at zero. Dague’s separate contingency rule remains unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Thomas, joined by O’Connor, Scalia and Kennedy, agrees that the enforceable dollar establishes eligibility but would affirm denial of all fees. He applies Hensley’s degree-of-success requirement to the $17 million demand and absence of proven compensatory injury. In his view, the existing record permits zero without a mechanical lodestar calculation. He does not assert that every nominal judgment necessarily warrants zero.

O’Connor separately agrees with that result and examines the extent of relief, significance of the legal issue and demonstrated public purpose. She finds this result too limited to justify fees while recognizing that nominal relief can represent meaningful vindication in another case. Those immediate-zero conclusions and O’Connor’s explanatory framework are noncontrolling.

### District of Columbia v. Greater Washington Board of Trade, No. 91-1326

Merits decision, December 14, 1992

Argued November 3, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the D.C. Circuit, 948 F.2d 1317 (1991), which reversed dismissal of the employer’s preemption suit. The question is whether a workers’ compensation continuation obligation measured by an existing ERISA-covered health plan is preempted even if separately administered through an exempt arrangement. The District seeks dismissal; the Board seeks protection against enforcement. Reporter locator: 506 U.S. 125. All nine Justices participated at argument and decision.

The challenged D.C. Code §36-307(a-1) applies when an employer already provides health insurance and the employee receives or is eligible to receive workers’ compensation. Eligibility means either absence from work because of a job-related injury for which the employee has filed a compensation claim, or the employer’s knowledge of a job-related injury causing the employee’s absence and pursuant to which compensation may become due under §36-315. Coverage must equal the existing insurance, at the benefit level when receipt or eligibility began, for no more than 52 weeks. The employer must pay the entire cost, including the contribution the employee otherwise would have made. The duty lasts only while receipt or eligibility continues, within that cap.

Paragraph (5) directs reimbursement to the employer from the special fund established by Act §41, D.C. Code §36-340. When the employer fails to provide coverage and the employee procures it and is reimbursed by the employer under subsection (d), fund reimbursement is limited to what the employer would have paid had it supplied coverage. Under subsection (d), the employee’s insurance-reimbursement remedy requires a request and employer refusal or neglect. Its distinct alternative concerning urgently needed treatment or service does not dispense with that insurance request. The Mayor must order suspension of compensation, medical payments and health coverage during an employee’s unreasonable refusal of medical or surgical treatment, an examination by the employer’s chosen physician, or vocational rehabilitation, unless circumstances justify the refusal. Neither the fund mechanism nor these duration and enforcement qualifications removes the binding use of the covered plan’s benefit content.

The Court affirms the judgment in the covered-plan application, 8–1. Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support the judgment; Stevens dissents and would reverse. Thomas delivers the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. Their agreement covers the bounded holding concerning covered plans, with the statutory exceptions and qualification for exempt plans preserved. Stevens writes a dissent with no recorded join.

The Court holds that ERISA §514(a) preempts this continuation obligation insofar as it incorporates an ERISA-covered health plan’s benefit content as the legal measure of mandatory replacement coverage, even when an employer may administer the replacement benefits separately through an exempt workers’ compensation arrangement. No §514(b) savings claim is decided, and this ruling does not preempt requirements measured solely by an ERISA-exempt governmental or church plan. Thomas’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter, controls because those eight Justices adopt the same operative rule.

The Court holds that separate administration does not remove the obligation’s legal dependence on a covered plan. Shaw supplies the connection-or-reference construction of §514(a), while preserving separately administered plans maintained solely to meet workers’ compensation, unemployment-compensation or disability-insurance requirements. Here the District’s command fixes replacement coverage by the employee’s existing covered benefits, for the statutory continuation period. Mackey and Ingersoll-Rand establish that such operative reference can require preemption even when a law addresses another subject. Alessi likewise prevents a workers’ compensation label from insulating interference with a covered plan. Section 4(b)(3) excludes the separately maintained arrangement from ERISA coverage; it does not transform the referenced covered plan into an exempt one. Fort Halifax’s one-time-payment rule does not apply to this conceded health plan. The Court leaves unpresented §514(b) savings questions and applications measured solely by exempt plans outside this holding. It decides no general rule that incidental economic influence or every mention of benefits suffices.

Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., 463 U.S. 85, is applied: its permission to maintain a separate unit concerns independently fixed exempt benefits and does not eliminate operative reference to a different covered plan. Mackey v. Lanier Collection Agency & Service, Inc., 486 U.S. 825, is applied: specific legal reference is distinguished from ordinary general garnishment. Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon, 498 U.S. 133, is applied: dependence on a covered plan can trigger preemption despite the nominal subject of the state rule. Alessi v. Raybestos-Manhattan, Inc., 451 U.S. 504, is applied: regulation through workers’ compensation does not avoid ERISA’s restriction concerning covered plans.

Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1, is distinguished: a one-time payment without ongoing administration differs from this health plan. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Massachusetts, 471 U.S. 724, is preserved: its holding saving insurance regulation from preemption supplies no additional §4(b) escape from a covered-plan reference. Morales v. Trans World Airlines, June 1, 1992, supplies only a consistent analogy based on similar language; its aviation holding does not replace ERISA’s own coverage and saving provisions. No precedent is overruled. Existing law concerning connection with or reference to a covered plan applies to this mandatory benchmark; separate exempt-plan administration and §514(b) saving provisions retain their distinct operation.

Effective December 14, 1992, mandatory replacement health coverage legally measured by an existing covered plan is preempted in this application notwithstanding separate exempt administration. The 52-week statutory cap describes the invalidated obligation’s scope, not a federal safe harbor. Measures based solely on exempt plans and unpresented §514(b) savings questions remain outside this application. Ordinary indirect economic effects remain unresolved here.

Stevens would treat the independently administered workers’ compensation obligation as regulation of an expressly exempt program. In his view, using the worker’s entire compensation, including health benefits, as a replacement measure does not regulate the covered plan or require its administrator to change operations. He reads Shaw in the context of the exemption and would require a more direct effect on covered-plan regulation than reference alone. The Court rejects that construction because the covered plan’s content is the obligation’s binding legal benchmark; Stevens’s proposed limitation is not controlling.

The [D.C. Circuit opinion, 948 F.2d 1317](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/948/948.F2d.1317.91-7061.html), supplies the command, 52-week limit and lower-court treatment. ### Mississippi v. Louisiana, No. 91-1158

Merits decision, December 14, 1992

The Court unanimously reverses the interstate adjudication, directs dismissal of Louisiana’s complaint against Mississippi for want of jurisdiction, and remands the remaining title dispute. Argued November 9, 1992, the case comes on writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit in *Houston v. Thomas*, 937 F.2d 247 (1991). The questions encompass the location of the river boundary, acquiescence, and whether the District Court properly took jurisdiction of Louisiana’s third-party complaint against Mississippi. The threshold statutory jurisdiction question disposes of the interstate claim; the boundary and acquiescence merits are not reached. The reporter citation is 506 U.S. 73.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision. Stone-Zsela delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas. Those same nine Justices support both the reversal concerning Louisiana’s complaint against Mississippi and the remand concerning the remaining title dispute and Louisiana’s intervention, each by a vote of 9–0. No Justice opposes either component.

The Court holds that §1251(a) denies a federal district court jurisdiction to grant relief on an actual controversy between two states, even when the complaint is joined to private litigation and this Court previously declined an original action. The appellate judgment is reversed insofar as it adjudicates Louisiana’s complaint against Mississippi, with instructions to direct dismissal of that complaint for want of jurisdiction. This is the unanimous holding of Stone-Zsela’s opinion, joined by all eight Associate Justices.

The Court holds that exclusive jurisdiction cannot be shared by implication. Section 1251(a) reserves controversies between states to this Court. *California v. Arizona* recognizes that a district court cannot hear one state’s claim against another under that allocation. Louisiana’s third-party complaint expressly seeks relief against Mississippi; its origin in a private title suit does not change those parties. The earlier refusal to entertain an original action therefore supplies no affirmative district-court power. *Illinois v. Milwaukee* and *Ohio v. Wyandotte* address the circumstances warranting an original forum, and *Texas v. New Mexico* recognizes discretion in its exercise. Those authorities do not authorize transferring a statutorily exclusive controversy to another federal court. The general federal-question statute cannot overcome the specific restriction. Mississippi’s own argument for district jurisdiction, and the convenience of deciding all questions together, cannot cure the defect. The Court accordingly removes the interstate adjudication without determining the boundary or the merits of acquiescence.

*California v. Arizona*, 440 U.S. 59, is applied: this Court is the exclusive federal forum for a state’s claim against another state. *Illinois v. City of Milwaukee*, 406 U.S. 91, is distinguished as to different defendants and applied to preserve discretion over the exercise of original jurisdiction. *Ohio v. Wyandotte Chemicals Corp.*, 401 U.S. 493, is preserved: that discretion does not grant another court exclusive interstate jurisdiction. *Texas v. New Mexico*, 462 U.S. 554, is preserved because case-specific discretion does not negate §1251(a). *Wyoming v. Oklahoma*, decided January 22, 1992, is distinguished: its exercise of original jurisdiction and separate objections supply no district-court authority here.

The Court also holds that §1251(a) does not bar an otherwise lawful private-title action merely because deciding title requires locating a state boundary; such adjudication does not bind the states on their boundary. Louisiana’s intervention that seeks no relief against Mississippi is unaffected, and the remaining title claims return for a determination whether the existing record permits fair resolution or additional proceedings are necessary. This holding and remand limitation likewise command the unanimous support of Stone-Zsela and all eight Associate Justices in the opinion of the Court.

The Court holds that jurisdiction follows the parties to the controversy, not every geographic issue a court may need to consider. The District Court’s authority over the private plaintiffs’ claims against the private defendants is conceded. *Durfee* explains that a boundary determination in private litigation does not bind the states themselves. A court may therefore decide private title within its lawful jurisdiction without issuing an authoritative interstate boundary decree. Louisiana’s intervention in that title dispute does not itself seek relief against Mississippi and survives the statutory ruling. But the courts below intermingled private ownership with the purported interstate adjudication. This Court cannot assume that excising the latter leaves a complete and fair basis for the former. The Court of Appeals must examine that question and require additional District Court proceedings if needed. No private claimant’s title is determined here, no state is bound by a private boundary finding, and no new original action is commenced.

*Durfee v. Duke*, 375 U.S. 106, is applied: adjudication between private parties does not bind the states on their boundary. *California v. Arizona*, 440 U.S. 59, applies to the actual interstate controversy without withdrawing otherwise lawful private-title jurisdiction. No precedent is overruled. Statutory exclusivity, discretion over original jurisdiction, and private boundary-related adjudication remain distinct. The Court decides no rule of accretion, avulsion, or sovereign acquiescence and adopts no replacement original-jurisdiction or boundary test. Effective December 14, 1992, a state-against-state complaint remains excluded from district-court adjudication notwithstanding joinder to private litigation or an earlier denial of leave to bring an original action; lawful private-title litigation, including Louisiana’s nonadverse intervention, remains possible but cannot bind the states on their boundary.

**Source Notes.** The [official U.S. Reports, 506 U.S. 73–79](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep506/usrep506073/usrep506073.pdf), support the dates, grant scope, Mississippi’s jurisdictional position, distinct pleadings, and remedial structure. The [Fifth Circuit opinion, 937 F.2d 247](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/937/937.F2d.247.90-1031.html), supplies its boundary and acquiescence judgment. [*California v. Arizona*, 440 U.S. 59](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep440/usrep440059/usrep440059.pdf), supports the exclusive-forum premise. The geographic merits and joint appendix have not been independently re-adjudicated.

The Fifth Circuit judgment is reversed insofar as it adjudicates Louisiana’s complaint against Mississippi, and the Fifth Circuit must require dismissal of that complaint for want of jurisdiction. The balance is remanded for inquiry whether the title claims may fairly be decided on the existing record or need further District Court proceedings. Louisiana’s intervention in the title dispute remains unaffected. No boundary decree or private title judgment is entered here.

**End of entry: Mississippi v. Louisiana, merits decision, December 14, 1992.**

### Republic National Bank of Miami v. United States, No. 91-767

Merits decision, December 14, 1992

Argued October 5, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit, 932 F.2d 1433 (1991), which dismissed the bank’s appeal from civil forfeiture after the Government transferred the substituted sale proceeds out of the District. #### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Removal does not itself terminate perfected forfeiture jurisdiction

**Controlling proposition:** Once valid attachment has established in rem jurisdiction—jurisdiction over the property—removal of the property or substituted proceeds does not by itself defeat appellate jurisdiction. The bank’s failure to obtain a stay or bond therefore does not foreclose this appeal where a favorable judgment could still provide lawful effectual relief; an entirely useless judgment presents a distinct limit.

**Authority:** Parts I, II, and IV of Blackmun’s opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, and Souter; eight Justices adopt this rule. Thomas supports the judgment on a different statutory ground.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that initial control of the property and continuing custody are different requirements. *The Rio Grande* rejects the proposition that removal automatically destroys jurisdiction already acquired, and *The Little Charles* identifies practical inability to effectuate a decree as a different concern. *The Brig Ann* addresses abandonment before proceedings began; *Taylor v. Carryl* concerns the original seizure needed to obtain jurisdiction. Neither establishes the Government’s proposed perpetual-custody rule. Here the parties substituted identifiable sale proceeds for property validly attached, and the Government then placed those proceeds in its own Treasury account. The bank’s failure to secure a stay did not undo the original attachment. *Church of Scientology* likewise confirms that possible effectual relief can preserve review despite an intervening event. The statutory-payment holding supplies that possibility here. The Court reserves a case where a judgment would be entirely useless, the amendment’s temporal application, and every question about whether this bank should prevail on the forfeiture merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *The Rio Grande*, 23 Wall. 458, is applied: removal alone does not terminate jurisdiction properly acquired.
- *The Little Charles*, 26 F. Cas. 979, is applied: the ability to effectuate relief is distinct from uninterrupted custody.
- *The Brig Ann*, 9 Cranch 289, is distinguished: abandonment before institution differs from removal after valid attachment.
- *Taylor v. Carryl*, 20 How. 583, is distinguished: initial jurisdiction does not establish perpetual custody as a condition.
- *Church of Scientology of California v. United States*, decided November 16, 1992, is applied by analogy on effectual relief; its IRS-copy rule supplies no payment authority.

##### The return statute and judgment appropriation permit effective relief upon success

**Controlling proposition:** If the bank prevails in the underlying forfeiture and obtains the requisite judgment, 28 U.S.C. §2465 supplies a substantive right to return of the substituted proceeds; 31 U.S.C. §1304 and 28 U.S.C. §2414 supply the judgment-payment route when payment is not otherwise provided, the Comptroller General certifies payment, and the obligation satisfies the statutory final-judgment and payable-judgment requirements. That conditional lawful route defeats the Government’s claim that this appeal can produce no effective relief, without ordering payment now or recognizing judicial spending power apart from an applicable appropriation.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s statutory-payment opinion, joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas, supplies six express joins and independently controls. Stevens expressly accepts the same sufficient statutory proposition in his own writing, making seven supporters without a formal join in Stone-Zsela’s opinion.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Congress has supplied a lawful means of effectuating a favorable forfeiture judgment. Section 2465 commands return when the claimant prevails, and sale of the attached property does not eliminate that right; the proceeds stand in its place. Section 1304 appropriates sums for qualifying final judgments when payment is not otherwise provided and the certification and statutory-payment conditions are met. Section 2414 supplies the relevant final district-court judgment route. *Richmond* cautions that the Judgment Fund is not an all-purpose judicial account: a substantive statutory right must support payment. Section 2465 supplies that right here if the bank succeeds. The Government’s Treasury transfer therefore does not make relief legally impossible. *Knote*’s restriction on payment without legislative authority need not be qualified because the necessary statutory route exists. The Court does not decide whether disputed funds may ever be returned without an appropriation, whether another payment source applies, or whether the bank is entitled to prevail. No money is awarded today.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Office of Personnel Management v. Richmond*, 496 U.S. 414, is applied: a substantive statutory entitlement and applicable appropriation, not equity alone, are required for this payment route.
- *Knote v. United States*, 95 U.S. 149, is preserved: the statutory resolution leaves the broader constitutional question about contested forfeiture proceeds undecided.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No Supreme Court precedent is overruled. The traditional attachment authorities are applied within their distinction between initial control and continuing custody. *Richmond*’s requirement of a substantive statutory basis is satisfied conditionally through §2465 and the judgment appropriation. The Eleventh Circuit’s continuous-custody rule cannot sustain this dismissal.

Effective December 14, 1992, perfected civil-forfeiture jurisdiction is not lost solely by transfer of the property or proceeds where lawful effectual relief remains possible. In this setting §2465 reaches substituted proceeds, and §§1304 and 2414 permit payment upon the required favorable judgment and their statutory conditions. The bank must still establish its entitlement. The majority adopts no constitutional doctrine allowing return without an appropriation and leaves §1355(c)’s application to pre-enactment events unresolved. The statute’s already-effective text remains in force independently of this decision.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun’s Part III, joined by Stevens and O’Connor, treats the disputed proceeds as property whose ownership remains unresolved, not finally vested public money. In their view, returning it after the bank succeeds would not require a new appropriation. They distinguish *Knote*’s completed forfeiture and question whether the Judgment Fund is the natural vehicle for a forfeiture judgment that determines ownership. That constitutional route has three votes and does not control.

White joins both controlling grounds but would ordinarily defer the payment question until a claimant actually prevails. Once the Court addresses it, he accepts the enacted statutory route and rejects use of the Government’s own transfer to defeat review.

Stevens joins Blackmun’s full writing and separately accepts the statutory-payment proposition as a sufficient alternative. That agreement is additional support for the proposition, not a formal join in Stone-Zsela’s writing.

Thomas would apply the newly enacted §1355(c) to this pending jurisdictional question under *Bruner*, *United States v. Alabama*, and *Andrus*: jurisdictional legislation generally governs pending proceedings without deciding substantive entitlement. He sees no need to resolve the pre-amendment admiralty rule, refuses that portion, and joins the statutory-payment holding. His temporal construction receives no other join and does not become a controlling interpretation of §1355(c).
