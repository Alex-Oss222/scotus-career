### Alexander v. United States, No. 91-1526

Merits decision, June 28, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Alexander v. United States, No.91-1526, October Term 1992. Merits decision June 28, 1993; argued January 12, 1993. On writ of certiorari to the Eighth Circuit, 943 F.2d825. The questions are whether the postconviction RICO forfeiture violates the First Amendment and whether its extent requires Excessive Fines review. The lower court sustained the forfeiture; Alexander seeks relief from it. The jury found four magazines and three videotapes obscene as predicates for the RICO convictions. The forfeiture reached enterprise assets and almost $9 million; the Government subsequently destroyed expressive inventory. The convictions and tax issues are not reopened.

At argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. All nine participate; White remains seated through this decision. Quorum six; judgment majority five.

#### Judgment

The Court vacates the challenged forfeiture approval and remands for independent First Amendment and Excessive Fines examinations, leaving the convictions undisturbed.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. This postconviction forfeiture is not a traditional prior restraint

**Controlling proposition:** A postconviction RICO forfeiture of identified assets linked to adjudicated racketeering does not constitute a traditional prior restraint merely because it impairs the owner's future ability to publish, where it neither forbids future publication nor requires permission to publish. That classification does not dispose of an independent First Amendment challenge to removal of protected works.

**Authority:** Souter's Part I, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas; six Justices adopt this proposition. Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy do not join the classification. The independent First Amendment question has a different coalition.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the forfeiture's financial consequences do not turn it into an order forbidding future speech. Near invalidated a restraint on future publication; Vance likewise involved a continuing restraint on films not yet adjudged obscene. Alexander faces no comparable command and no contempt sanction merely for returning to publishing with lawfully available assets. Fort Wayne Books required adjudication before the removal of presumptively protected materials on the record there; here the obscenity and racketeering charges have been tried. That difference defeats the categorical contention that any resulting loss of publishing assets is necessarily a prior restraint. It does not establish that every part of the forfeiture is constitutional. Arcara distinguishes the incidental effect of a neutral sanction from governmental regulation implicating expressive activity. The Court preserves that distinction and the separate inquiry into the treatment of lawful works. Neither the criminal judgment nor this classification licenses a future-publication ban or a seizure of protected stock on probable cause alone.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S.697, and Vance v. Universal Amusement Co., 445 U.S.308: distinguished as restraints on future expression; their protection remains in force.
- Fort Wayne Books, Inc. v. Indiana, 489 U.S.46: its pretrial protection is preserved; the present adjudicated RICO case differs, and its reserved postconviction question is not treated as settled.
- Arcara v. Cloud Books, Inc., 478 U.S.697: applied to distinguish an ordinary sanction's incidental consequences from regulation implicating expression; no universal asset exemption follows.

##### 2. The forfeiture approval requires an independent First Amendment examination

**Controlling proposition:** Where obscenity predicates produce forfeiture of an expressive enterprise and removal of lawful inventory, the judgment sustaining that forfeiture cannot rest solely on the existence of valid convictions and the absence of a traditional prior restraint. The judgment must be vacated for an independent First Amendment examination of the challenged expressive burden, distinguishing the judicial forfeiture from subsequent administrative destruction and confining correction to a reviewable injury and available relief.

**Authority:** Souter's Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Kennedy; five Justices expressly adopt this limited rule and remand. Their separate positions do not establish a majority rule either categorically immunizing expressive inventory or adopting a comprehensive scrutiny framework for every postconviction forfeiture.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that punishment after conviction remains subject to the First Amendment. Arcara sustained a sanction for nonexpressive misconduct and distinguished sanctions triggered by expressive conduct or singling out expression. Here obscenity offenses trigger the sanction, and lawful books and films are removed with the enterprise's other assets. Fort Wayne Books did not resolve that postconviction question. Marcus and Quantity of Books explain why suppressing protected inventory presents a concern distinct from collecting an ordinary monetary sanction. The Government correctly observes that a publisher cannot shelter criminal assets merely by investing in books; Simon & Schuster preserves neutral forfeiture while forbidding selective burdens on protected expression. But statutory coverage alone does not answer whether this sanction impermissibly suppresses lawful works. The lower court must separate the interests forfeited, the judicial injury and the Government's later destruction, and determine what correction this proceeding can supply. The Court neither orders return of destroyed stock nor creates a damages action, and it leaves the ultimate constitutional treatment of the distinct assets unresolved.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Arcara, 478 U.S.697: its nonexpressive-conduct rationale does not dispense with examination of this expression-triggered sanction; ordinary neutral sanctions remain enforceable.
- Fort Wayne Books, 489 U.S.46: the reservation concerning postconviction forfeiture is respected; its pretrial safeguard remains unchanged.
- Marcus v. Search Warrant, 367 U.S.717, and A Quantity of Copies of Books v. Kansas, 378 U.S.205: their protection against indiscriminate removal of expressive works supplies the need for distinct examination, without mechanically converting their pretrial remedies into this mandate.
- Simon & Schuster, Inc. v. Members of the New York State Crime Victims Board, December 10, 1991, decision: content-selective financial burdens remain subject to its rule; lawful publishing does not immunize assets from neutral forfeiture.

##### 3. Punitive criminal forfeiture requires Excessive Fines review

**Controlling proposition:** A criminal RICO forfeiture extracting property as punishment is subject to the Excessive Fines Clause independently of the validity of the convictions or the permissibility of the prison term. The lower court must examine the extent of the exaction in relation to the offenses supporting it; it may not reject that inquiry simply because the sentence is less than life imprisonment without parole.

**Authority:** Souter's Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, O'Connor, Scalia, Thomas; six Justices adopt coverage and remand. Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy accept the alternative excessiveness remand without joining Part III or adopting a proportionality formula.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a punitive transfer of property to the Government is within the constitutional protection against excessive fines. Browning-Ferris distinguishes private civil damages from governmental extraction as punishment; this criminal forfeiture falls on the governmental side of that distinction. The court below treated the monetary objection as though a limitation on prison-sentence review resolved it. That approach fails to give the separate clause effect. The existence of valid RICO convictions establishes a basis for punishment, not the permissible extent of every forfeiture. Review must account for the forfeited interests and the offenses that support them. The inquiry is not reduced to counting the seven titles adjudged obscene, and it may not presume that every asset or dollar of lawful business is itself criminal activity. The Court leaves the actual excessiveness determination to the lower court, including appropriate record development. It establishes neither a numerical cap nor a complete proportionality formula, and it leaves the independently sustained convictions undisturbed.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Browning-Ferris Industries v. Kelco Disposal, 492 U.S.257: its distinction between private awards and governmental punishment supports coverage of this criminal forfeiture; its private-award holding remains intact.
- Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S.277: no rule about imprisonment displaces the separate Excessive Fines inquiry; the Court adopts no general prison-sentence holding here.
- Austin v. United States, June 28, 1993, is a same-day decision and supplies no earlier authority for this holding. This holding does not adopt its separate writings.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. The Court does not adopt a content-selection rationale from R.A.V. or enlarge its overbreadth rule. Wisconsin v. Mitchell preserves punishment of independently criminal conduct and relevant motive; its battery enhancement does not settle removal of protected inventory here.

Effective June 28, 1993: traditional-prior-restraint classification, independent First Amendment review of this forfeiture, and punitive criminal forfeiture coverage under the Excessive Fines Clause are controlling at their stated levels. No majority adopts an ultimate inventory-immunity rule, Stone-Zsela’s full scrutiny framework, his list of excessiveness considerations or a numerical proportionality formula. The First and Eighth Amendment inquiries are independent. Each of the three holdings commands a direct majority; none rests on combining incompatible separate grounds under Marks. Austin operates prospectively alongside this decision within its own civil-forfeiture scope.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

**Kennedy, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, and by Souter as to the protected-inventory discussion, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.** All four join Part II's corrective remand. They would go further and hold unconstitutional forfeiture of expressive inventory not adjudged obscene or otherwise unprotected when imposed here as punishment for obscenity. Marcus and Quantity of Books protect each work rather than permit one unlawful title to taint neighboring lawful works. Arcara involved temporary closure for prostitution while the books remained available elsewhere; it did not authorize destruction of the inventory. Kennedy, Blackmun and Stevens also would invalidate the speech-enterprise and influence-property forfeiture under the principles underlying Near and Vance. Their concern is suppression of future expression, not merely the amount of punishment. Souter does not join that broader enterprise rationale or the rejection of Part I's classification; he joins Parts I–III of the principal opinion. The three broader writers do not join Parts I or III, but accept the Excessive Fines remand as alternative relief. Their preferred categorical inventory rule has four votes, and their broader enterprise rule three. The common remand does not adopt either as controlling law.

**Stone-Zsela, concurring.** He joins Parts I–III and explains his proposed method for the unresolved First Amendment inquiry. Ordinary sanctions for nonexpressive misconduct do not require special scrutiny merely because a publisher loses assets. Where expressive predicates draw the sanction and lawful inventory is removed, a genuinely content-neutral incidental restriction must satisfy O'Brien: constitutional authority, an important or substantial interest unrelated to suppressing expression, and a restriction no greater than essential to that interest. Suppression because of protected content requires compelling justification and narrow tailoring. He distinguishes transfer of forfeitable economic interests from gratuitous elimination of lawful works and would examine whether lawful realization of the assets can accomplish the authorized punishment. There is no universal least-restrictive-means requirement or categorical inventory immunity. The judicial order, administrative destruction, causation, reviewability and available correction remain distinct; destroyed stock cannot be returned by this appeal and no damages action is created. On excessiveness, the interests and supported value taken, seriousness and extent of wrongdoing, culpability and property's role are nonexclusive proposed considerations, not a binding numerical formula. These additional specifications are his alone, not added to the Court's holdings.

**White, joined by O'Connor, Scalia and Thomas, concurring in part and dissenting in part.** They join Parts I and III but oppose Part II and would reject the First Amendment challenge. In their view the adjudicated RICO violation and established nexus permit a generally applicable forfeiture without converting the assets' expressive character into constitutional immunity. Fort Wayne Books accepts substantial punishment for obscenity, and Arcara does not require special scrutiny of every sanction affecting a bookseller. They regard the statutory forfeiture and its deterrent consequences as punishment rather than a governmental system controlling future publication. They preserve objection to targeted censorship or a future-publication prohibition, but see no basis for the additional judicial-forfeiture remand on this record. They agree that excessiveness is independently reviewable under the Eighth Amendment. Their First Amendment approval has four votes and does not control.

### DeBoer v. DeBoer, No. A-64

DeBoer v. DeBoer, No.A-64, October Term 1992; application for stay, July 26, 1993. Applicant is Jessica DeBoer, also called Baby Girl Clausen, by next friend Peter Darrow. The application seeks a stay of Michigan's July 2, 1993 custody-enforcement order following the Iowa determination that the biological father's rights had not been lawfully terminated. The child was born February 8, 1991; the Iowa Supreme Court acted September 23, 1992. The question is interim protection pending proposed federal review, not a new adoption or custody adjudication.
