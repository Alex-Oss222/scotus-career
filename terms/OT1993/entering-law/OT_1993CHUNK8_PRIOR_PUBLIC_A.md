### United States v. Salerno et al., No. 91-872

Merits decision, June 19, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued April 20, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, reviewing [937 F.2d 797](https://static.case.law/f2d/937/html/0797-01.html), amended on rehearing at [952 F.2d 623](https://static.case.law/f2d/952/html/0623-01.html). This case concerns the admission of former testimony, with case reference [505 U.S. 317](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-505/pdf/USREPORTS-505-317.pdf). Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

The District Court excluded grand-jury testimony offered by the defense and entered convictions. The court of appeals reversed the appealing defendants’ convictions, treating similar motive as dispensable and finding spillover from the evidentiary ruling. The seven respondents before this Court are Anthony Salerno, Vincent DiNapoli, Louis DiNapoli, Nicholas Auletta, Edward Halloran, Alvin O. Chattin and Aniello Migliore. Ianniello’s distinct appellate relief is outside this writ.

The question is whether unavailable witnesses’ grand-jury testimony may be admitted for the defense under Federal Rule of Evidence 804(b)(1) without showing that the Government had a similar motive to develop it, including the asserted fairness and forfeiture grounds. The United States seeks reversal; the respondents defend admission and their appellate relief.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses and remands the Second Circuit’s judgment insofar as it dispenses with the similar-motive requirement, by a vote of 8–1. Stevens dissents.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justice | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dispensation with similar motive for the seven respondents | Reversed and remanded, 8–1 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stevens | Consider actual similar motive and the resulting admissibility and judgment consequences, preserving independent appellate grounds. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Thomas | White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter | Reversal and remand | Full opinion; both controlling propositions. |
| Concurrence | Blackmun | No other Justice | Joins the Court in full | Similar motive is a contextual factual inquiry; it requires neither an identical motive nor an always-or-never rule. |
| Concurrence in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Reversal and remand | Every Rule condition, actual comparable motive, no forfeiture from independent contradiction, and preservation of independent appellate grounds; no formal join in the Court’s opinion. |
| Dissent | Stevens | No other Justice | Would affirm | The existing record on the central issue establishes actual opportunity and similar motive; tactical restraint is not absence of motive. |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Every condition of the former-testimony exception remains necessary

**Controlling proposition:** A party offering an unavailable witness’s former testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 804(b)(1) must establish that the opposing party had both an opportunity and a similar motive to develop that testimony; the Government’s power to immunize the witness at trial and an appeal to adversarial fairness do not dispense with either requirement. Similar motive must be considered on the actual proceedings and arguments; this decision neither finds that condition satisfied here nor establishes a categorical exclusion of grand-jury testimony.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined in full by White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. These seven of the nine participating Justices adopt this rule and explanation. Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment on his separately stated compatible rule and qualifications, without formally joining the opinion. Stevens dissents from the remand on the application of actual motive, not on an exemption from the Rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the former-testimony exception requires the showing Congress specified. Rule 804(b)(1) joins opportunity and similar motive as separate conditions, and neither favorable testimony nor the opposing party’s power to obtain live testimony removes those words. Green v. Bock Laundry Machine Co. supplies the obligation to respect enacted evidentiary restrictions rather than revise them for a preferred result. Dennis v. United States permits access to grand-jury materials under the applicable disclosure rule; it does not make those materials admissible for their truth. Here the court of appeals made similar motive unnecessary because the Government could immunize the witnesses. It therefore did not fully consider the parties’ opposing accounts of the actual examination and investigative purposes. The Government’s assertion that the trial judge made a conclusive factual finding does not require this Court to decide that unresolved dispute first. The case returns for consideration under the correct Rule. Compelled immunity, other exceptions and an independently preserved constitutional ground remain outside this holding.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Green v. Bock Laundry Machine Co., 490 U.S. 504 (1989): applied for enforcing the enacted evidentiary rule rather than altering it for a preferred litigation result; Green’s separate impeachment rule is unchanged.
- Dennis v. United States, 384 U.S. 855 (1966): distinguished as a disclosure decision under Rule 6(e); its protection of access to relevant grand-jury material remains fully operative without creating automatic trial admissibility.
- United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 (1984): its respect for the enacted evidentiary scheme remains consistent with this application; its distinct bias-impeachment rule is unchanged.

##### Contradictory independent evidence does not itself forfeit the objection

**Controlling proposition:** When the Government offers independent evidence contradicting unavailable witnesses’ grand-jury accounts, but does not present any version of those accounts to the trial jury, that act alone does not forfeit its Rule 804(b)(1) objection. Even assuming a privilege-forfeiture analogy is available, mere contradiction is not disclosure of the protected communication; this decision does not establish that every hearsay objection is a testimonial privilege.

**Authority:** Thomas’s Opinion of the Court, joined by White, Blackmun, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy and Souter. These seven Justices expressly reject the asserted forfeiture ground at this limited level of generality. This is a distinct controlling answer to the defendants’ alternative theory of admissibility, not a finding of ultimate inadmissibility.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the defendants’ forfeiture argument fails on its own terms. They rely on the principle that a party cannot disclose its version of a privileged communication and then prevent use of the communication needed to test that version. United States v. Miller, the cited Fifth Circuit decision, concerns that form of selective disclosure; it does not treat every contradictory litigation position as a disclosure. Here the Government used contractors, recordings and documents to establish Cedar Park’s participation. It did not tell the jury what DeMatteis or Bruno had said to the grand jury. The contrary theory would replace a showing of disclosure with the ordinary fact that opposing evidence conflicts. The Court does not decide that the hearsay rule is generally equivalent to a testimonial privilege, and it does not decide the consequences of an actual selective presentation of former testimony. The defendants remain free on remand to establish the actual similar motive that the enacted exception requires.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Miller, 600 F.2d 498 (5th Cir. 1979): distinguished on the premise of selective disclosure; the Court rejects the proposed analogy without adopting a general rule equating hearsay with testimonial privilege. Miller is a Fifth Circuit decision, not a prior holding of this Court.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

The parties’ actual-motive arguments and the resulting admissibility and judgment consequences return for consideration. The Court makes no finding of actual similar motive, perjury, ultimate guilt, entitlement to immunity or constitutional admissibility. Other preserved appellate grounds remain. Ianniello’s separate relief is outside the writ. The controlling rationale is stated in the Opinion of the Court and requires no analysis under Marks.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 19, Rule 804(b)(1)’s requirements of opportunity and similar motive apply without the court of appeals’ immunity-based fairness exception. Contradiction through independent evidence alone does not forfeit the objection. These precise trial-admissibility questions were previously unresolved.

White v. Illinois, decided January 15, retains its Confrontation Clause rule keyed to the applicable hearsay exception. United States v. Williams, decided May 4, retains its confined grand-jury disclosure duty and qualified dismissal remedy. Neither decision supplies automatic trial admission; neither is overruled or narrowed. No precedent is overruled. Actual motive, other preserved evidentiary and constitutional grounds, and their consequences for the judgments remain unresolved.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun joins the Court and explains that similar motive does not mean identical motive. The actual issues and context of questioning govern, with neither a categorical exemption for the prosecution nor automatic admission. He favors remand because the appropriate inquiry was not completed below.

Stone-Zsela concurs in the judgment. He requires the defense to establish the evidentiary conditions without shifting the prosecution’s burden to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. The prosecutor may be investigating the same alleged conspiracy or different issues; actual motive governs. Contradictory independent evidence is not disclosure or adoption of the prior account, and access to material does not make it admissible. He reserves properly preserved independent constitutional objections, other exceptions and the factual motive determination, and preserves independent appellate grounds.

Stevens would affirm. In his view, the same crucial question—Cedar Park’s participation in the alleged Club—gave the Government a similar motive to test the witnesses’ denials. A tactical choice not to use the full examination opportunity does not eliminate that motive. He agrees that genuinely different issues can defeat similarity; his conclusion that similarity is established here does not control.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court review of this merits event is complete. On the ordinary mandate, the Second Circuit must consider the actual-motive arguments under the proper Rule and determine their effect together with the remaining appellate grounds. The Court orders neither categorical exclusion nor unconditional reinstatement of every conviction. It directs no special mandate date, stay, immediate retrial or finding about the truth of either witness’s account. No Supreme Court jurisdiction is retained, and no later filing or result is established.

**End of entry: United States v. Salerno et al., merits decision, June 19, 1992.**

### White v. Illinois, No. 90-6113

Merits decision, January 15, 1992

*White v. Illinois*, 502 U.S. 346 (1992), No. 90-6113. Argued November 5, 1991; decided January 15, 1992. The Court granted review of the judgment of the Illinois Appellate Court, Fourth District, 198 Ill. App. 3d 641, 555 N.E.2d 1241, after the Illinois Supreme Court denied review. Review is limited to whether the Confrontation Clause requires production or an unavailability showing before admission of statements accepted as spontaneous declarations and statements for medical treatment. Petitioner seeks reversal of his convictions on that ground.

All nine Justices participated at argument and at decision.

The Illinois Appellate Court judgment is affirmed 9-0 on the limited Confrontation Clause claim. Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas support affirmance.

Stone delivers the opinion of the Court. White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter join in full. Scalia and Thomas join Part I, the exception and unavailability analysis, but not Part II, rejecting the government's proposed limitation of confrontation to formal accusations. Thomas concurs in part and in the judgment, joined by Scalia.

Where the granted review assumes that statements were properly admitted under the firmly rooted spontaneous-declaration and medical-treatment exceptions, the Confrontation Clause does not require the prosecution first to produce the declarant or establish unavailability. The accepted exception foundations remain necessary; the ruling does not validate a disputed foundation or every police interview.

Part I of Stone's opinion controls this rule. White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas join it; all nine Justices endorse the rule.

The Court holds that absence alone does not impose a production requirement on these accepted exceptions. Inadi explains why trial testimony cannot necessarily replace a statement whose evidentiary value derives from the circumstances in which it was made. A spontaneous response and a statement made for treatment carry features that later testimony cannot recreate. Roberts does not establish a universal preliminary demand for unavailability; its former-testimony setting is different. Under the received reliability framework, the firmly rooted exceptions at issue supply the requisite assurance when their foundations are satisfied. Wright preserves the separate inquiry applicable outside such exceptions, and an exception label cannot settle disputed facts. Here state-law membership in the exceptions is the premise of the limited review. Coy and Craig concern safeguards when a trial witness is presented through an altered procedure; they do not require the extra evidentiary gateway sought here. No question of harmless error arises on the claim resolved.

The Court extends *United States v. Inadi*, 475 U.S. 387, from coconspirator declarations to the two accepted exceptions because later testimony does not replace their contextual value.

*Ohio v. Roberts*, 448 U.S. 56, remains part of the governing confrontation framework; its unavailability analysis for former testimony does not apply universally.

*Idaho v. Wright*, 497 U.S. 805, preserves constitutional reliability requirements outside firmly rooted exceptions; unsupported judicial assurances cannot substitute for those requirements.

*Bourjaily v. United States*, 483 U.S. 171, preserves the established reliability significance of firmly rooted exceptions.

*Coy v. Iowa*, 487 U.S. 1012, and *Maryland v. Craig*, 497 U.S. 836, concern the presentation of trial witnesses and are distinguished on that basis; their safeguards remain intact.

The Confrontation Clause is not categorically confined to formal affidavits or equivalent trial substitutes, so that limiting definition does not supply an alternative basis for admitting these statements. The Court resolves the accepted exceptions under existing confrontation law and leaves any broader reconstruction of that law open.

Part II of Stone's opinion controls this rejection of the proposed categorical narrowing. White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter join it, forming a seven-Justice majority with Stone. Scalia and Thomas do not join this part.

The Court declines to remove this case from confrontation law by defining its protection to cover only formal accusations. The decisions governing hearsay and confrontation address more than affidavits or their formal equivalents. Mattox recognizes the constitutional problem created by absent testimony while permitting an established exception, and the later reliability cases continue to examine the substance and setting of out-of-court statements. Inadi itself supplies an exception-specific answer rather than declaring that informal statements can never implicate confrontation. The government's proposed categorical boundary would discard that framework without a need to do so here. The accepted spontaneous and treatment exceptions resolve the actual production question. This conclusion does not establish that every hearsay statement violates the Constitution, nor does it decide the constitutional treatment of a formal investigative accusation on a disputed record. Those questions require their own factual premises and candid consideration of the governing precedents.

*Mattox v. United States*, 156 U.S. 237, remains evidence of confrontation's substantive protection and its coexistence with established exceptions.

*California v. Green*, 399 U.S. 149, and *Dutton v. Evans*, 400 U.S. 74, remain in force; their treatment of out-of-court statements is not displaced by a definition limited to formal affidavits.

*Inadi* and *Roberts* apply as confrontation decisions sensitive to the particular exception, without replacement by a new categorical testimonial rule.

The two accepted firmly rooted exceptions require no separate production or unavailability showing. Roberts, Inadi and Wright remain the governing framework, with the foundations for each exception preserved. The Court adopts no new categorical testimonial test, blanket approval of police questioning, automatic child-necessity rule, or harmless-error holding.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, agrees with the exception-based judgment and its analysis but declines to reject the government's proposed definition of a witness. He would leave open reconsideration of whether confrontation particularly addresses formalized testimonial materials; that suggested reconstruction is not adopted law.

The affirmance ends Supreme Court review of the granted claim. No new trial or evidentiary hearing is required on the unavailability contention. State-law admissibility outside the granted premise and any other claims receive no new adjudication.

**End of entry: White v. Illinois, merits decision, January 15, 1992.**

### Foucha v. Louisiana, No. 90-5844

Merits decision, May 18, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 4, 1991; decided May 18, 1992. On writ of certiorari under 28 U.S.C. §1257 to the Louisiana Supreme Court, 563 So.2d 1138 (June 14, 1990), which affirmed continued psychiatric confinement after an insanity acquittal. The question is whether the State may continue that confinement on dangerousness and the acquittee's failure to prove safety after disclaiming present mental illness.

Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the judgment sustaining Foucha's continued psychiatric confinement and remands for implementation.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Continued custody under Louisiana's challenged insanity-acquittee provision | Reversed, 6–3 | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | End confinement on that basis; remand for implementation. No indefinite continuation while seeking another rationale. Independently lawful custody requires its own authority and procedures. |

The equal-protection theory supplies no separate claim for additional relief and no majority holding. The Court does not find Foucha harmless or prescribe conditions for every possible alternative preventive statute.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | White | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Reversal and implementation remand | Parts I (record and posture), II-A (ended predicate), II-C (alternative dangerousness regime), IV (mandate); six Justices support each part |
| Opinion of the Court | White | Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Souter | Independent due-process ground for reversal | Part II-B (distinct psychiatric grounds and procedures); five Justices support this part |
| Plurality opinion | White | Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Supports reversal; no controlling equal-protection holding | Part III (equal protection); four Justices support this part |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | O'Connor | No other Justice | Joins White's Parts I–II and IV and the judgment | Emphasizes reservation of narrower preventive schemes |
| Concurrence in part and in the judgment | Stone-Zsela | No other Justice | Joins White's Parts I, II-A, II-C and IV and the judgment | States the ground concerning the particular authority for custody and the immediate implementation limit |
| Dissent | Kennedy | No other Justice | Would affirm | Distinct authority arising from the acquittee's criminal adjudication |
| Dissent | Thomas | Scalia | Would affirm | Acquittee distinction and adequacy of review procedures |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### 1. The insanity judgment no longer authorizes this psychiatric confinement

**Controlling proposition:** When the State concedes that an insanity acquittee is no longer mentally ill, it may not continue his psychiatric confinement under the existing insanity-acquittee authority solely because he cannot prove that he will be harmless. The original acquittal supports neither a suspended criminal sentence nor indefinite psychiatric custody after its illness predicate disappears.

**Authority:** White's Part II-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this proposition and application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Louisiana must identify a present lawful basis for the restraint it imposes. Jones v. United States permits initial commitment because an insanity verdict supports an inference of illness and dangerousness; its rejection of a hypothetical criminal-sentence ceiling rests on those continuing conditions. O'Connor v. Donaldson establishes that a permissible commitment cannot continue after its supporting basis disappears. Jackson v. Indiana requires the nature and duration of custody to remain reasonably related to its purpose. Louisiana no longer claims that Foucha is mentally ill. His antisocial personality and the doctor's unwillingness to guarantee safety do not restore that conceded absence. The State's substantial interest in protecting the public therefore cannot perpetuate this particular psychiatric commitment. The original criminal conduct does not make the acquittal a conviction authorizing punishment. The Court neither finds Foucha harmless nor sets the maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment. Any separate lawful restraint must rest on its own established authority and procedures.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the continuing illness-and-danger basis of insanity commitment; initial verdict-based commitment and rejection of an automatic hypothetical-sentence ceiling remain intact.
- O'Connor v. Donaldson, 422 U.S. 563 (1975): applied to the requirement that confinement end when its authorizing basis disappears; the Court does not equate a conditional-release recommendation with a finding of harmlessness.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the required relationship between confinement's nature, duration and purpose.

##### 2. Psychiatric custody requires appropriate grounds and procedures

**Controlling proposition:** Once the insanity-acquittal basis for holding Foucha as a mental patient has ended, Louisiana cannot preserve that psychiatric custody without constitutionally adequate proceedings establishing the grounds required for it. If the State invokes ordinary civil commitment, it must establish current mental illness and dangerousness by clear and convincing evidence; additional procedure cannot supply an absent substantive ground.

**Authority:** White's Part II-B, joined by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: five of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground. Stone-Zsela does not join this section's separate Vitek-based procedural characterization.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a hearing about dangerousness alone cannot authorize confinement as a mental patient after the State disclaims mental illness. Vitek v. Jones recognizes that placement in a psychiatric institution imposes a distinct deprivation requiring appropriate justification and procedure, even for someone already lawfully confined. Jackson v. Indiana requires a new lawful basis and the protections belonging to it when the original commitment purpose no longer supports custody. Addington v. Texas supplies the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment. Louisiana instead requires Foucha to disprove danger, although its conceded facts cannot presently establish the illness required for that civil route. The State's reliance on a counseled release hearing does not answer this defect: fairness in deciding the wrong statutory condition does not establish the missing condition. Baxstrom v. Herold reinforces that an expired original basis does not remove the safeguards of the new proceeding. The Court does not prescribe a universal burden for every possible preventive regime or disturb the distinct rules governing initial insanity commitment.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Vitek v. Jones, 445 U.S. 480 (1980): applied to psychiatric institutionalization's distinct liberty deprivation and need for appropriate grounds and procedures.
- Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972): applied to the need for an independently authorized commitment proceeding after the initial basis expires.
- Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418 (1979): applied unchanged to the clear-and-convincing burden for ordinary civil commitment; that burden is not made universal for every preventive custody channel.
- Baxstrom v. Herold, 383 U.S. 107 (1966): relied on for the ordinary protections attending a new civil commitment after the prior detention basis ends; no separate majority equal-protection holding is created here.

##### 3. Public safety does not sustain this unbounded dangerousness regime

**Controlling proposition:** Louisiana's scheme permitting indefinite psychiatric confinement of a no-longer-mentally-ill acquittee because he has not proved nondangerousness violates due process. A legitimate preventive purpose and periodic review do not cure the absence of a carefully limited substantive authority; the Court leaves materially narrower preventive regimes undecided.

**Authority:** White's Part II-C, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Souter: six of nine participating Justices adopt this independently sufficient rejection of Louisiana's asserted alternative preventive justification. The holding adopts no new general scrutiny tier or universal dangerousness proof standard.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the exceptional power to prevent danger cannot be detached from the limits that justify the particular restraint. United States v. Salerno sustained a narrowly focused pretrial scheme with an identified threat, government proof, an adversarial hearing, review and strict temporal limits. Louisiana's rule instead permits confinement without an endpoint while requiring the acquittee to prove safety. The doctor's reluctance to certify Foucha as harmless is enough under that rule, although the State concedes that the psychiatric illness has ended. A fair procedure for predicting danger does not supply substantive authority for an otherwise unauthorized restraint. The original verdict supplies no punitive authority under Jones. If later conduct constitutes a crime, ordinary criminal proceedings remain available; the Court makes no new finding that a particular assault occurred or warrants conviction. A better hearing alone cannot cure this regime's substantive defect. The Court reserves narrowly drawn alternatives rather than converting today's holding into a universal prohibition of preventive detention.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739 (1987): distinguished because its confined pretrial detention authority and safeguards do not authorize Louisiana's indefinite post-acquittal psychiatric restraint.
- Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983): applied to the absence of authority to punish an insanity acquittee as a convicted offender.

#### Current-Law Effect and Limits

Effective May 18, 1992, Jones's distinct initial-commitment rule remains in force, but Louisiana cannot continue this psychiatric confinement after conceding the end of illness on the acquittee's failure to prove safety. Five Justices additionally require appropriate psychiatric grounds and procedures; ordinary civil commitment retains Addington's clear-and-convincing burden. Six reject the indefinite dangerousness alternative under this scheme. These are applied and clarified channel limits, not a new universal scrutiny tier or dangerousness standard. No majority adopts the plurality's equal-protection rule. Maximum duration of an otherwise valid commitment, materially narrower preventive regimes, and the scope of state insanity defenses remain undecided. No Marks inference is needed because each controlling due-process proposition has at least five direct joins.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and Souter, concludes in Part III that Louisiana also denies equal protection by withholding ordinary commitment safeguards from a sane acquittee while not permitting comparable indefinite dangerousness confinement for other persons whose original custody basis has ended. The four would require a particularly convincing justification for that disparity; this is a plurality position, not law.

O'Connor emphasizes that the holding concerns this indefinite psychiatric regime. She leaves open detention carefully limited in nature and duration to substantial safety concerns and questions extended confinement beyond what a conviction could have permitted, without deciding that question. No constitutional requirement that a State provide an insanity defense is adopted.

Stone-Zsela agrees that the psychiatric channel's ended predicate requires relief. He reserves independent equal protection, a new general scrutiny tier, universal proof rules and narrowly defined alternative regimes. Implementation cannot allow indefinite continuation while the State looks for a new justification; separate custody requires its own established authority and procedures.

Kennedy would affirm because the insanity adjudication follows proof of criminal conduct and belongs to a distinct legal channel. He views state definitions of criminal insanity and incapacitation as constitutionally relevant and rejects automatic transfer of ordinary civil-commitment rules to this setting.

Thomas, joined by Scalia, would affirm because acquittees differ from ordinary civil committees, the State may attach continued safety-based custody to that status, and periodic counseled review is adequate here. They reject an expansive new substantive right against this confinement and distinguish its lack of a fixed endpoint from a refusal ever to provide release review. Neither dissent controls.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The Louisiana courts must implement reversal by ending custody under the challenged insanity-acquittee basis. The mandate permits no indefinite continuation of that custody while the State searches for a substitute rationale. Any independently lawful custody requires its own established authority and procedures; the Court orders neither automatic release from a separate lawful restraint nor a new civil commitment. No future hearing date, factual diagnosis, new charge, compliance finding or retained Supreme Court proceeding is created.

**End of entry: Foucha v. Louisiana, merits decision, May 18, 1992.**

### Herrera v. Collins, No. 91-7328

Merits decision, January 25, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 7, 1992; on certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 954 F.2d 1029, which vacated a district-court execution stay in a second federal habeas proceeding. The question concerns a claim of innocence based on later affidavits after a constitutionally valid capital conviction, and the requested judicial proceedings or stay. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument and decision. This case and *Graham v. Collins* are decided on the same day; neither decision supplies a premise for the other.

#### Judgment

The Court affirms the Fifth Circuit’s judgment, 5–4. The requested federal relief is denied on this submission. The Court neither decides that Herrera has proved innocence nor announces a common general test for an innocence claim independent of another constitutional violation.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reviewed judgment vacating the innocence-based federal stay and rejecting the requested further relief | Affirmed, 5–4 | White, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | No federal hearing, writ or renewed stay is ordered on this submission; no immediate execution order or new date is issued |

Stone-Zsela would vacate the categorical exclusion and remand subject to separate procedural gates. Blackmun, Stevens and Souter would reverse and remand for district-court consideration under their proposed claim and burden. The separately rejected *Brady* claim and claims 2–5 are not reopened.

#### Opinion Topology

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plurality opinion | O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Four support affirmance | Ordinary habeas discussion, assumed exceptional claim and insufficiency of this submission |
| Concurrence | O’Connor | Kennedy | Both also join the plurality | Executing an established innocent person would be constitutionally intolerable, but the freestanding-remedy question is reserved |
| Concurrence in the judgment | White | Author alone | Supports affirmance | Expressly agrees with the record-insufficiency reasons; assumes a claim and states a rational-factfinder minimum; does not join the broader plurality reasoning |
| Concurrence | Scalia | Thomas | Both also join the plurality | Rejects a constitutional right to judicial consideration of new innocence evidence after a fair trial |

| Dissent | Stone-Zsela | Author alone | Would vacate the categorical exclusion and remand | Clear-and-convincing affirmative innocence rule, retaining procedural gates and express stay findings |

No complete opinion has five joins. Five Justices expressly support the limited conclusion that this submission is insufficient; their agreement does not establish a unified general burden.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### This record does not establish entitlement to federal innocence relief

**Controlling proposition:** Herrera’s delayed and materially inconsistent affidavits, considered with the identification, physical and documentary evidence supporting his convictions, do not establish entitlement to the requested federal habeas relief or justify the district court’s innocence-based stay, even assuming an exceptionally persuasive post-trial showing could constitutionally bar execution. This record-specific rejection establishes no uniform freestanding-innocence burden and does not decide whether such a constitutional claim exists.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s plurality opinion, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, and White’s express agreement with its reasons for finding this submission insufficient in his concurrence in the judgment: five Justices adopt this limited record conclusion. White does not join the plurality’s broader discussion or an unspecified extraordinarily high threshold. No whole opinion commands five votes; no synthesis under *Marks*, the rule for identifying controlling reasoning in a fractured decision, establishes a common general test.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that these papers do not warrant the federal relief requested. *Jackson* distinguishes constitutional sufficiency of the trial record from a fresh determination of guilt; Herrera does not show that the original conviction failed that requirement. His later affidavits must nevertheless be assessed together with the existing evidence for purposes of the assumed claim. The identifications, connection to the automobile, physical evidence and incriminating letter remain substantial. The delayed accounts attributing both murders to his deceased brother conflict over important circumstances and leave the guilty plea to Rucker’s murder unexplained. Their possible probative value does not make this submission a persuasive affirmative demonstration of innocence. The District Court made no credibility finding or innocence-hearing determination to which this Court could defer. *Barefoot* and *Delo* require substantial grounds for relief before a stay on a successive petition; that requirement is unmet here. The Court does not adjudicate the affidavits false in every respect, define a general innocence standard, or authorize execution of a person whose factual innocence has been established.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307: Trial-record sufficiency remains distinct. White uses its rational-factfinder formulation only as a minimum for an assumed new-evidence claim, without five-Justice adoption of that general burden.
- *Barefoot v. Estelle*, 463 U.S. 880: Applied to require a substantial legal basis for an execution stay.
- *Delo v. Stokes*, 495 U.S. 320: Applied to the substantial-ground requirement on a second or successive federal petition.
- *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, and *Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes*, May 4, 1992: Preserved. Necessary factual development of a properly available claim remains distinct from legal sufficiency, whether the claim is legally available, exhaustion and abuse of the writ.
- *Sawyer v. Whitley*, June 22, 1992: Distinguished. Its gateway concerning eligibility for capital punishment does not decide freestanding innocence of the crime or supply that claim’s merits burden.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

Effective January 25, 1993, this submission does not justify the requested federal relief even upon the assumption of an exceptional constitutional innocence claim. The existence and precise burden of such a claim remain unresolved by a controlling rationale. No logical subset is established between an undefined extraordinary threshold and White’s rational-factfinder minimum, so *Marks* supplies no general standard. White’s distinct minimum burden is noncontrolling.

The Court does not overrule *Townsend*, recognize a general new claim, replace *Jackson* or import *Sawyer*’s eligibility gateway as a freestanding guilt-innocence standard. The plurality’s broader discussion of whether the claim is legally available, state deadlines and clemency has four votes. The case-specific determination that the showing is insufficient, together with the ordinary requirement of substantial grounds for a stay, supports affirmance. *Townsend* and *Keeney* hearings on properly available, materially undeveloped claims, statutory exhaustion, *McCleskey* abuse-of-the-writ rules, *Teague* and the distinct *Sawyer* gateway retain their own operation.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Justice O’Connor, joined by Justice Kennedy, emphasizes in a separate concurrence that execution of a person established to be factually innocent would be constitutionally intolerable, but that the difficult question of a freestanding federal remedy need not be decided on this submission. The plurality’s account of ordinary habeas practice and Texas’s late-new-trial and clemency procedures does not attract White’s join and supplies no new five-Justice holding that such claims are categorically unavailable or that clemency is adequate.

Justice White concurs in the judgment alone. Assuming that a persuasive showing of innocence could bar execution despite an expired new-evidence deadline, he would require at least a showing that no rational trier could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt upon the old and new evidence together. He expressly agrees that Herrera’s proof falls short for the reasons in the plurality. This does not adopt an established freestanding claim or a definitive burden.

Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Thomas, also joins the plurality and separately rejects a constitutional entitlement to reopen a lawfully completed conviction on newly discovered innocence evidence alone. *Townsend*, historical procedural practice and finality support his position; deciding the case on an assumed claim does not cause him to accept a new constitutional right. His categorical position has two votes.

Justice Blackmun dissents, joined by Justices Stevens and Souter in the legal discussion and proposed remand. He would recognize an independent Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment claim because execution of a person shown to be innocent is itself a constitutional injury. *Ford* and *Johnson* show that a valid judgment does not resolve every later objection to execution; *Beck* connects capital punishment to reliable guilt determinations. He would require the prisoner to show probable actual innocence on the whole evidence, exceeding a mere likelihood that a juror would entertain reasonable doubt. He rejects leaving that constitutional claim conclusively to clemency and would remand for district-court consideration of threshold factual and procedural requirements, without declaring Herrera innocent. Blackmun alone adds his broader criticism of restrictions on capital review; Stevens and Souter do not join that concluding commentary.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela dissents separately. He would vacate only the categorical exclusion of a properly presented capital-innocence claim, preserving exhaustion and then-governing abuse-of-the-writ rules. His proposed merits burden is clear and convincing affirmative proof, on the relevant old and new evidence together, that the prisoner did not commit the crime for which execution is sought. *Ford* and *Beck* support extending substantive protection; *Penry*’s exception for substantive limits on capital punishment would extend to that protected class. This is a proposed extension, not a claim that those cases already decided freestanding innocence. Clemency is additional protection, not a conclusive answer to an established constitutional prohibition. Allegations provisionally credited must be capable of satisfying that burden before factual development becomes material. *Townsend* and *Keeney* then retain deliberate bypass, while ordinary counsel negligence alone does not bar a necessary hearing. Any temporary stay requires an express *Barefoot*/*Delo* showing; no indefinite stay follows. If innocence is proved, execution for that crime is barred and another sentencing hearing cannot cure the defect. His proposal neither orders acquittal nor disturbs independent lawful custody, revives the *Brady* claim, changes the trial burden or resolves all noncapital claims.

### United States v. Olano, No. 91-1306

Merits decision, 1993-04-26

#### Chronology and Posture

United States v. Olano; No. 91-1306; October Term 1992. Argued: 1992-12-09; decided 1993-04-26. Source locator: 507 U.S. 725 (1993). Ninth Circuit, 934 F.2d 1425 (1991). On writ of certiorari to that court.

Question: Whether the unobjected-to presence of instructed alternate jurors during deliberations permits reversal under Rule 52(b), and how error, plainness, substantial rights and remedial discretion differ.

Two alternates remained during deliberations after a three-month joint trial; the court instructed them not to participate. One later left at his request. Counsel did not make a timely objection. The Government concedes error and plainness on the assumption that Rule 24(c) was not waivable; the Court does not decide that assumption. No participation or chilling effect was shown, and respondents did not request a hearing. The Ninth Circuit separately reversed convictions for insufficiency, then vacated the remaining convictions under a per se alternate-presence rule and did not reach other appellate issues.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participate at argument (1992-12-09) and decision (1993-04-26). No recusal or other nonparticipation is established.

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Forfeiture, waiver and the limits on plain-error authority

**Controlling proposition:** Rule 52(b) permits correction of a forfeited legal error only if it is plain and affects substantial rights; valid intentional relinquishment of a known right extinguishes error, while mere failure timely to assert a right does not. Plain means clear or obvious under current law, and the defendant ordinarily must show prejudice affecting the outcome; errors first clarified on appeal, special outcome-independent categories and justified prejudice presumptions are reserved.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that failure to object changes the conditions of appellate relief without making a violated rule disappear. Johnson v. Zerbst supplies the distinction between intentional surrender and omission; whether a right can be waived and what participation or procedures waiver requires depend on the particular right. Young reads Rule 52(b) as a limited power, not two disjunctive routes based on either plainness or substantial rights. Its prejudice inquiry ordinarily examines the record, as harmless-error review does, but places persuasion on the defendant who forfeited the objection. This does not decide that every error demands a specific outcome showing. Fulminante preserves the distinction between trial errors and defects affecting the tribunal’s basic structure. Here the Government’s concessions permit the Court to assume error and plainness without resolving whether Rule 24(c) can be waived. They do not concede prejudice. The preserved constitutional error in Brecht arose under a different review posture and does not supply the burden here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458: applies intentional relinquishment; right-specific waiver requirements remain.
- United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1: applies limited plain-error authority and ordinary prejudice requirement.
- Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279: preserves structural/trial-error distinction without deciding every Rule 52(b) category.
- Brecht, April 21, 1993: distinguished; preserved Doyle habeas review does not control forfeited Rule 24(c) error.

##### Correction remains discretionary

**Controlling proposition:** Even when plain forfeited error affects substantial rights, correction under Rule 52(b) is permitted, not automatic, and should occur only when the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Actual innocence is not a necessary condition, but satisfaction of substantial rights alone does not satisfy this additional inquiry.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the word “may” leaves a separate remedial judgment to the reviewing court. Atkinson supplies its governing measure: the effect on the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Young carries that standard into Rule 52(b). Treating every prejudicial plain error as requiring correction would erase the discretion the Rule deliberately retains. The contrary argument that relief must always depend on actual innocence also fails. Innocence can make correction compelling, but serious injury to the administration of justice can exist independently of guilt. The collateral gateway addressed in Sawyer concerns a different procedural obstacle and does not rewrite this appellate rule. These distinctions prevent either an automatic reversal rule or an innocence-only restriction. Because respondents have not satisfied the preceding substantial-rights requirement, the Court does not decide whether this particular intrusion, had it been prejudicial, would warrant the exercise of remedial discretion.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157: applies the independent fairness, integrity and public-reputation standard.
- United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1: applies discretionary, circumscribed correction.
- Sawyer, June 22, 1992: distinguished; capital actual-innocence gateway is not a universal limit on appellate correction.

##### Instructed alternates do not establish prejudice by presence alone

**Controlling proposition:** For these alternates, instructed not to participate, mere presence during deliberations neither establishes prejudice nor warrants a presumption of prejudice under Rule 52(b). Without a specific showing of participation or a chilling effect, respondents have not shown an effect on substantial rights; the per se new-trial ruling must be reversed.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court; Stone-Zsela, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). This proposition commands a majority of the Court.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that deliberative privacy protects against improper influence, and the inquiry must attend to that purpose. Parker and Remmer examine intrusions for prejudice; Smith v. Phillips rejects a new trial merely because a juror encountered a potentially compromising situation. Those authorities do not make this Rule 24(c) violation harmless by definition, but they defeat treating every intrusion as automatically reversible. The alternates heard the same evidence, took the ordinary oath, and received an express direction not to participate. Richardson supports the ordinary assumption that jurors follow instructions. Respondents showed neither actual participation, including nonverbal influence, nor restraint of the regular jurors’ discussion, and the circumstances do not justify presuming either. The Court does not decide the Government’s burden had an objection been preserved. It also reserves what Rule 606(b) would permit at a hearing and whether an appellate court may order such a hearing on plain-error review; none was requested here.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Parker v. Gladden, 385 U.S. 363, and Remmer v. United States, 347 U.S. 227: apply the focus on prejudicial influence, preserving appropriate presumptions in other circumstances.
- Smith v. Phillips, 455 U.S. 209: applies rejection of automatic retrial for every compromising contact.
- Richardson v. Marsh, 481 U.S. 200: applies the ordinary instruction-following presumption, not an irrebuttable cure.
- Zafiro, January 25, 1993: consistent record-specific instruction analysis; no blanket rule that instructions cure intrusions.

#### Current-Law Effect

Before: Rule 52(b) and Atkinson/Young supplied discretionary correction without this complete articulation. After: four separate requirements govern forfeited-error review; ordinary defendant prejudice burden and the no-presumed-prejudice result for instructed alternates apply. Valid waiver, new clarity on appeal, structural categories and Rule 606(b)/hearing authority remain reserved. Brecht’s Chapman rule for preserved Doyle habeas error and Zafiro’s record-specific instruction analysis remain unchanged.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy joins the Court. He emphasizes that the defendant’s burden is the usual framework, not necessarily the formulation for an error noticed by the court on its own initiative. Rule 24(c) remains independently obligatory; trial judges should not put counsel in the position of resisting a proposed departure from the prescribed jury structure. A preserved objection would place the burden of showing harmlessness on the Government, which could face substantial difficulty. None of those observations permits withholding an objection and demanding automatic reversal later.

Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, would affirm. Drawing on Vasquez v. Hillery and Gray v. Mississippi, he regards the privacy of deliberations as protecting the tribunal’s integrity independently of measurable verdict prejudice. He nonetheless distinguishes satisfaction of substantial rights from discretionary relief. A reasonable judge could decline reversal after considering the nature of the violation, counsel’s conduct and likely effect; his conclusion is that the Ninth Circuit did not abuse its broad supervisory discretion by granting relief here.
