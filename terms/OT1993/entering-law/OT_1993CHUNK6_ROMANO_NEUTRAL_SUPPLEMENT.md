## Broader path: exact reach and qualification

At 1144–1146 and 1146 n.2, Blackmun concludes that the existing death-penalty system cannot deliver constitutionally required fairness, consistency and reliability; on that sufficient ground he does not address Callins's individual errors. At 1147–1150 he recognizes that constitutional text appears to permit death while arguing that actual administration must nevertheless satisfy the Eighth Amendment. Thus the objection is not merely a personal moral preference, a finding of innocence, or an objection confined to one evidentiary error.

His principal structural argument is that the discretion needed for individualized consideration and mercy permits the arbitrary and discriminatory selection that guided sentencing must prevent. At 1151–1153 he considers, then rejects as insufficient, the proposed reconciliation of objective eligibility narrowing with discretionary selection: reducing the eligible group does not eliminate arbitrariness in choosing who actually dies. At 1153–1155 he adds the problem of racial discrimination. At 1156–1159 he separately criticizes diminished constitutional supervision and habeas review. These are arguments in his writing, not adjudicated findings about Romano's jury, counsel, race, guilt or Oklahoma's entire system.

The conclusion concerns capital punishment **as then administered** (1158–1159). The final section leaves open the theoretical prospect of procedures actually supplying consistency, fairness and reliability, while expressing doubt that they can be devised. Preserve that qualification; do not rewrite the source as a textual proposition that death is forbidden under every imaginable future system.

If independently adopted for Romano, this ground would address the continued death sentence without depending on whether disclosure of the other sentence violated Caldwell or due process. A defect inherent in imposing death would not be cured merely by removing that disclosure and holding another capital hearing. It would call for relief directed to death and a lawful noncapital disposition through appropriate state proceedings, not acquittal, a finding of innocence, or relief in the separate Thompson case. Those are conditional remedial implications of the proposed ground, not a selected disposition or mandate.

His broader concluding criticism of capital-review restrictions is his alone and expressly supplies no categorical death-penalty holding. It supports continuity of concern, not an already-adopted general prohibition. The actual Court's record-insufficiency holding leaves the existence and general burden of a freestanding claim unresolved.

Callins's criticism of the historical Johnson result is displaced in this timeline.

Victor/Sandoval (March 22) adjudicates the instruction questions and expressly leaves unrelated sentencing issues outside review. Neither supplies a categorical capital-punishment position. These differences require evaluation, not a mechanical conclusion. Graham and Johnson provide concrete counterevidence to Callins's historical claim that this Court abandoned effective mitigation. Conversely, Callins's structural objection expressly addresses even an eligibility/selection system that permits mitigation; it is not logically exhausted by criticism of those two historical results. ## Narrower paths and remaining scope uncertainty

The existing packet supplies an independently available case-specific path: examine whether disclosure of another jury's death judgment displaced this jury's responsibility under Caldwell, or rendered the whole penalty proceeding fundamentally unfair. Blackmun's antecedent join in Caldwell's responsibility reasoning, identified at 472 U.S. 328–334 in the validated packet, supplies personal support for considering that narrower route. The competing affirmance path preserves Caldwell's limits, accurate responsibility instructions, appeal information, and the distinction between constitutional unfairness and state evidentiary irrelevance. A case-specific violation may permit an adequate new capital proceeding; that differs materially from the broader objection's remedial premise.

The strongest objection to reaching the broader ground here is the actual limited grant: it asks about another death sentence's effect on the sentencing jury, not the constitutional validity of capital punishment generally. Neither the permitted packet nor this supplement establishes that a categorical claim was raised below, preserved, briefed, or fairly included. The petition, actual grant terms, merits briefs, and relevant lower-record presentation would be needed before representing it as a party-presented claim or a proper new Court-wide holding. An individual Justice's broader objection to leaving death in place must be identified as that distinct rationale, with its reach explained, rather than silently expanding the question or attributing the position to Romano. No new factual finding or additional Court event fills this gap.
