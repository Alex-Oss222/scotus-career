### Espinosa v. Florida, No. 91-7390

Limited grant and summary merits decision, June 29, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

On petition for certiorari to the Supreme Court of Florida, 589 So.2d 887 (1991), which affirmed convictions and a death sentence. There was no oral argument. The Court takes the preserved question whether a vague heinous, atrocious or cruel aggravator can infect a sentence through a jury recommendation entitled to great weight despite the judge's direct use of valid aggravators. Although the petition seeks relief from conviction and sentence on additional grounds, this grant and contemporaneous summary decision form a single action limited to the sentencing issue. All nine Justices participate: Chief Justice Alex-Lamar Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas.

#### Public Action and Judgment

The Court grants leave to proceed without prepayment of fees and certiorari limited to the preserved aggravating-instruction question, reverses the rejection of the sentencing claim, and remands.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preserved death-sentencing claim | Summary reversal and remand, 7–2 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | White opposes summary disposition and would grant review and set argument; Scalia dissents and would deny review | Lawful reweighing or Chapman harmless-error review must address the recommendation's influence; a new sentencing proceeding is required if no lawful cure is available. |

White supplies no present merits vote. The guilt judgment remains outside this disposition, and no automatic life sentence follows.

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Per curiam | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Supports summary reversal and remand | Sentencing defect and cure instructions |
| Noted procedural dissent | White | No additional joiners | Would grant review and hear oral argument | Opposition to summary decision; no present merits position |
| Dissent | Scalia | No additional joiners | Would deny review | Rejects the constitutional weighing premise |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### A legally influential recommendation cannot rest on an inadequately narrowed aggravator

**Controlling proposition:** When a weighing State requires its sentencing judge to give great weight to a jury's recommendation, an inadequately narrowed aggravating instruction to that jury can infect the final sentence even if the judge directly finds only valid aggravators. The especially wicked, evil, atrocious or cruel instruction given here lacks the guidance the Eighth Amendment requires; the judge's separate findings do not by themselves cure the recommendation's legal influence.

**Authority:** The per curiam Opinion of the Court is joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas, seven Justices, for this rule and its application. White's preference for argument is not a present merits vote. Scalia rejects the weighing premise in dissent. The express seven-Justice majority supplies the controlling authority.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that constitutional guidance must follow the actual allocation of sentencing responsibility. Godfrey, Maynard and Shell require an aggravating category to distinguish the cases to which it applies; the ordinary words given this jury supply no adequate boundary. Stringer and Sochor distinguish eligibility supported by valid factors from the later weighing that selects death. Florida law makes the jury's general recommendation consequential by requiring the judge to give it great weight, whether it recommends life or death. Mills does not permit an assumption that jurors ignored an authorized invalid category, and Walton's presumption that a judge follows the law means the recommendation received the weight state law required. The judge thus cannot be treated as wholly independent of the defective recommendation. Baldwin permits a different allocation of sentencing roles; it does not eliminate an actual duty of deference. The Court does not find that particular jurors selected particular aggravators. It decides the legal effect of the unguided recommendation and preserves the State's ability to structure a constitutionally guided sentencing process.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420 (1980), Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356 (1988), and Shell v. Mississippi, 498 U.S. 1 (1990): apply their requirement of meaningful guidance for a capital aggravating category; the instruction here is insufficiently narrowed.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992: applies the distinction between valid eligibility and constitutionally defective weighing; surviving valid aggravators alone do not remove the error.
- Sochor v. Florida, June 8, 1992: applies its weighing-error rule while distinguishing its independent state preservation bar to a different jury claim and its separate factual-invalidity issue.
- Mills v. Maryland, 486 U.S. 367 (1988): applies the refusal to presume that jurors disregard an instruction's constitutionally defective route; the Court attributes no factor-specific verdict to the jury.
- Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 (1990): preserves genuine judicial sentencing and the presumption that judges apply legal narrowing; distinguishes a system in which a separate unguided recommendation carries required weight.
- Baldwin v. Alabama, 472 U.S. 372 (1985), and Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447 (1984): preserve States' latitude in allocating sentencing responsibility; that latitude does not validate influential unguided weighing.
- Tedder v. State, 322 So.2d 908 (Fla. 1975), Smith v. State, 515 So.2d 182 (Fla. 1987), and Grossman v. State, 525 So.2d 833 (Fla. 1988): supply the state-law requirement to give great weight to jury recommendations of life or death.
- Smalley v. State, 546 So.2d 720 (Fla. 1989): rejects its premise that the jury's advisory designation alone makes this vague instruction constitutionally immaterial.

##### Correction must address the recommendation's influence on the death sentence

**Controlling proposition:** Reversal of the rejection of this sentencing claim requires further proceedings that remove the influence of the invalid jury recommendation through lawful reweighing or harmless-error review satisfying Chapman beyond a reasonable doubt. If no lawful cure is available, a new sentencing proceeding is required; valid remaining aggravators alone do not establish a cure, and neither an automatic life sentence nor disturbance of the guilt judgment follows.

**Authority:** The same seven Justices join the per curiam opinion's remedy instruction. They expressly apply the existing Stringer, Sochor, Clemons and Chapman correction requirements to the two-stage defect found here. No agreement is attributed to White on this unargued merits question or to Scalia on the need for correction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that correction must reach the error actually found. Clemons permits an authorized reviewing court to reweigh valid aggravation against mitigation or determine that an invalid factor was harmless; it does not treat a surviving majority of factors as an answer. Chapman supplies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt harmlessness standard. Stringer and Sochor require an actual lawful cure of invalid weighing, and Parker requires attention to the sentencing record that genuinely existed. Here the jury's general recommendation carried legally required weight into the judge's decision. The judge's four valid aggravators and two mitigators therefore do not establish that this indirect error had no effect. Further review must account for that channel of influence, rather than repeat the premise that the judge alone imposed sentence. The Court does not undertake its own balancing, choose a sentence, or decide that Florida law authorizes every proposed appellate cure. It leaves those proceedings to the state court under the stated constitutional limits. The conviction and unrelated petition questions are outside this disposition.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738 (1990): applies lawful appellate reweighing or harmless-error correction; merely counting valid aggravators does not cure the error.
- Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967): applies harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt; no lesser standard or burden on the defendant is created.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992, and Sochor v. Florida, June 8, 1992: apply actual cure requirements to the recommendation's transmitted influence; no unreasoned harmlessness label suffices.
- Parker v. Dugger, 498 U.S. 308 (1991): applies review grounded in the real sentencing record and mitigation, not an assumed alternative record.

#### Limits and Questions Not Reached

No automatic reduction to life, retrial of guilt, adjudication of unrelated aggravators, factor-by-factor jury finding, or collateral-review gateway rule follows. The petition's severance and mitigation/time/funding/proportionality questions are not granted or adjudicated in this limited summary action; this action does not separately deny them. No prior Supreme Court decision is overruled.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

Effective June 29, 1992, a jury recommendation that receives great legal weight is part of the constitutionally relevant weighing process. Its unguided aggravating category can contaminate the final sentence through that required influence even when the judge directly uses valid factors. The Court applies the anti-vagueness and weighing rules described above, distinguishes genuine independent judicial sentencing, and requires correction of this indirect influence. Stringer and Sochor supply the actual-cure requirement; Chapman supplies review for harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. Lawful reweighing or that review must remove the defect, with a new proceeding required if no lawful cure exists. States retain latitude in assigning sentencing roles. The decision creates no automatic life sentence, collateral-review rule or presumption of factor-specific jury findings, and overrules no Supreme Court precedent.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White would obtain oral argument before deciding the merits. His procedural dissent expresses no merits position on the preserved claim. Scalia would deny review because the valid aggravators already satisfy the constitutionally required narrowing of the death-eligible class. Following his positions in Walton and Sochor, which reject a separate constitutional requirement for the later weighing stage, he rejects the premise that the Eighth Amendment requires removing an invalid factor from that stage. His one-Justice view does not control.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

The limited petition and summary merits action end in remand to the Supreme Court of Florida. That court must supply lawful reweighing or harmless-error review under Chapman's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard addressing the recommendation's influence. If no lawful cure is available, a new sentencing proceeding is required. The convictions and unrelated petition questions receive no new Supreme Court merits adjudication. This disposition determines no later sentence or state finding of harmlessness.

**End of entry: Espinosa v. Florida, limited grant and summary merits decision, June 29, 1992.**

### Presley v. Etowah County Commission; Mack v. Russell County Commission, Nos. 90-711 and 90-712

Merits decision, January 27, 1992

#### Chronology and Posture

*Presley v. Etowah County Commission; Mack v. Russell County Commission*, 502 U.S. 491 (1992), Nos. 90-711 and 90-712. Argued November 12, 1991; decided January 27, 1992. Direct appeals from a three-judge District Court for the Middle District of Alabama concerning §5 of the Voting Rights Act. The appealed questions are whether Etowah County's Common Fund Resolution and Russell County's Unit System require preclearance, meaning advance approval under §5. The distinct Road Supervision ruling is unappealed. Other §2, Title VI and constitutional claims are outside these appeals.

All nine Justices participated at argument and at decision.

#### Judgment

The noncoverage judgments in both appeals are affirmed.

| Judgment component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy or remand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No. 90-711 — Common Fund Resolution | Noncoverage affirmed, 5-4 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens would reverse and remand for preclearance | No §5 preclearance required on this record |
| No. 90-712 — Unit System | Noncoverage affirmed, 5-4 | O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone, White, Blackmun, Stevens would reverse and remand for preclearance | No §5 preclearance required on this record |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Joined by | Relationship to judgment | Scope joined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | Kennedy | O'Connor, Scalia, Souter, Thomas | Affirms both noncoverage judgments | Full opinion |
| Dissent | Stevens | White, Blackmun | Would reverse both judgments and require preclearance | Dissenting opinion |
| Separate dissent | Stone | Stone writes separately | Would reverse both judgments and require preclearance | Joins no other writing |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The two resolutions do not change a voting practice

**Controlling proposition:** Section 5 does not require preclearance of these reallocations of governing authority because they do not alter voting procedures, candidacy, the electorate, or the method of selecting the relevant offices and have no direct relation to voting. This ruling does not resolve whether an extreme transfer could effectively replace an elective office with an appointive one, nor does it exempt a change merely because it occurs after an election.

**Authority:** Kennedy's opinion, joined by O'Connor, Scalia, Souter and Thomas; five Justices control both noncoverage judgments.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that §5 addresses changes with a direct relation to voting, not every redistribution of power exercised by elected officials. Allen's broad reading reaches changes that alter electoral choice, including replacement of elective selection with appointment. Perkins and Dougherty County likewise concern the electorate or the process by which votes select officials. The Common Fund Resolution changes how an elected commission allocates road money, and the Unit System changes administration while leaving substantial authority with the elected commission. Neither changes the relevant selection mechanism or electorate. The dissent's concern that removing functions can diminish a district's influence is substantial, but influence over a particular governmental function is not itself the statutory voting practice. The familiar categories are illustrative, not exhaustive. The Court therefore leaves open a transfer so complete that an elective office is effectively replaced by an appointed one; the retained powers in this record do not present that question.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *Allen v. State Board of Elections*, 393 U.S. 544 — broad coverage of changes affecting electoral choice remains; elective-to-appointive selection is distinguished from these retained elected offices.
- *Perkins v. Matthews*, 400 U.S. 379 — changes to the electorate and voting arrangements remain covered; coverage remains distinct from discrimination merits.
- *Dougherty County Board of Education v. White*, 439 U.S. 32 — candidacy restrictions directly related to electoral choice remain covered.
- *McCain v. Lybrand*, 465 U.S. 236, and *City of Lockhart v. United States*, 460 U.S. 125 — changes in electoral arrangements remain within §5; neither decides ordinary internal governing duties.
- *NAACP v. Hampton County Election Commission*, 470 U.S. 166 — practical changes to election timing or procedure require proper review; postelection timing alone supplies no exemption here.

##### Administrative interpretation does not eliminate the voting boundary

**Controlling proposition:** The Attorney General's interpretation of §5 is entitled to substantial consideration but cannot extend preclearance to governing changes that lack the statutory connection to voting. The proposed significant-transfer/different-constituencies test cannot substitute for that connection in these appeals.

**Authority:** The same five-Justice Kennedy opinion controls this statutory boundary and the rejection of the broader administrative and lower-court formulations.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that administrative expertise cannot remove the limit Congress expressed by linking §5 to voting. Sheffield recognizes the Attorney General's important interpretive role in applying the preclearance scheme. Chevron preserves that role when the agency resolves a statutory gap, but does not authorize an interpretation that exceeds the statute's coverage. Here the proposed focus on substantial transfers between officials serving different constituencies can sweep in decisions about governmental responsibility without identifying a changed voting practice. Section 5's demanding preclearance process does not make all such governing decisions electoral rules. The Court rejects that extension while preserving broad coverage when a measure actually changes election procedures, candidacy, the electorate or office selection. Beer also separates coverage from the later question of discriminatory effect. Accordingly, the Court makes no finding about the resolutions' racial purpose or consequences and leaves the other statutory and constitutional claims to their proper proceedings.

**Precedent treatment:**

- *United States v. Board of Commissioners of Sheffield*, 435 U.S. 110 — administrative interpretation retains substantial weight within the statutory voting boundary.
- *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 — preserved; deference does not authorize exceeding the statute's scope.
- *Beer v. United States*, 425 U.S. 130 — coverage and discriminatory-effect review remain separate inquiries.
- *South Carolina v. Katzenbach*, 383 U.S. 301 — §5's remedial function is preserved; it does not by itself establish coverage of every governing change.

#### Current-Law Effect

The two challenged allocations of governing power are outside §5 because they lack a direct relation to voting; other claims remain available. The familiar electoral categories are not exhaustive, and effective replacement of an elective office by an appointed one remains open. Administrative interpretation cannot erase the voting boundary. No racial-purpose or effect finding is made.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, would require preclearance of both resolutions. Allen's functional protection should reach substantial removal of the authority voters select an official to exercise; Etowah's change following the remedial electoral arrangement supplies an additional reason for coverage, without deciding discriminatory intent. Stone separately would cover substantial structural removal of a core, independently exercisable district-office function into appointment or materially different collective control. He distinguishes these changes from routine appropriations, individual hiring, or losses in ordinary policy votes, and would leave preclearance merits to the designated statutory channels. All four would reverse both appealed noncoverage judgments.

#### Mandate, Remedy, and Stage

Both appealed noncoverage judgments are affirmed. The Common Fund Resolution and Unit System are not enjoined under §5 on these grounds. The unappealed Road Supervision ruling is undisturbed, and the remaining §2, Title VI and constitutional claims continue in the district court on their own terms. The judgment neither approves discriminatory intent or effect nor exempts every later allocation of official power. Supreme Court review of these coverage rulings ends without retained supervision.

**End of entry: Presley v. Etowah County Commission; Mack v. Russell County Commission, merits decision, January 27, 1992.**

### Stringer v. Black, No. 90-6616

Merits decision, March 9, 1992

Argued December 9, 1991. [On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit, 909 F.2d 111](https://static.case.law/f2d/909/html/0111-01.html).

Question presented: Whether Teague bars consideration of the vague-aggravator claim because Maynard or the requested application to Mississippi’s weighing system announced a new rule after Stringer’s sentence became final.

Stringer’s death sentence became final on February 19, 1985. The jury found three aggravating circumstances, including an undefined especially heinous, atrocious or cruel factor, in Mississippi’s system requiring aggravators to be weighed against mitigation. The state appellate court upheld the sentence. The federal district court denied habeas relief both on procedural-default and merits grounds. Following an earlier remand for consideration in light of Clemons, the Fifth Circuit again denied relief on Teague new-rule grounds without deciding the procedural bar. Stringer seeks reversal of that retroactivity ruling. The State invokes finality and the asserted distinction between weighing and nonweighing systems. Whether adequate state correction actually occurred and whether procedural default prevents relief remain for further proceedings. [Record materials, 503 U.S. 222](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-503/pdf/USREPORTS-503-222.pdf).

The Court reverses the Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remands, 6–3. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor and Kennedy support reversal; Justices Scalia, Souter and Thomas dissent. Justice Kennedy delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor. That opinion controls both the determination that the claim invokes an existing rule and the requirement of genuine reweighing or constitutional harmless-error review in this weighing system.

For a sentence final on February 19, 1985, Godfrey already prohibited the unguided heinousness aggravation later addressed in Maynard, and applying that protection to Mississippi’s weighing system does not create a new rule. Teague therefore does not bar consideration of this claim on the ground that Maynard or the corrective principles discussed in Clemons postdated finality.

Justice Kennedy’s opinion, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor, controls this determination.

The Court holds that Teague protects reasonable reliance on existing law, not a refusal to recognize what that law already required. Butler requires attention both to the rule announced by a later decision and to whether its proposed application extends the precedent in a new way. Godfrey had already rejected an aggravating phrase that gave the sentencer no meaningful guidance. Maynard applied that same prohibition to closely equivalent language. Mississippi’s formal weighing of aggravation against mitigation makes the defect consequential to sentence selection; it does not exempt the system from the preexisting constitutional protection. Zant expressly distinguished its nonweighing arrangement and supplies no affirmative permission to weigh an invalid factor. The State’s finality argument therefore does not make this particular application novel. Clemons identifies lawful methods of correcting the error rather than first creating the right to challenge it. The Court does not hold that every later capital decision applies retroactively, and it decides no Teague exception or unresolved procedural default.

The Court applies the existing rule of Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288: the claim seeks an old rule at the relevant finality date. The Court applies Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407: both novelty of the later rule and novelty of its application must be considered. The Court applies Godfrey v. Georgia, 446 U.S. 420: meaningful guidance was already required for the heinousness aggravator. Maynard v. Cartwright, 486 U.S. 356, applies Godfrey’s existing rule to equivalent language. The Court applies Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738, for its permissible methods of correction; Clemons did not create a new protection in this setting.

When a capital sentencer weighs a constitutionally invalid aggravating factor against mitigation, remaining valid aggravators do not by themselves cure the error. The state judicial system must supply genuine reweighing without the invalid factor, where authorized, or constitutional harmless-error review establishing that the invalid factor did not affect the sentence; the adequacy of any cure here remains for further proceedings.

Justice Kennedy’s six-Justice opinion also controls the weighing and correction rule. Its application and any eventual conditional remedy remain reserved.

The Court holds that an invalid factor placed on the statutory scale cannot be ignored simply by counting the valid factors that remain. Zant sustained a system in which the invalid factor did not do the formal weighing work present here. Barclay also involved actual review of the sentencing choice and a different kind of invalidity; it does not permit automatic affirmance of this constitutional weighing error. Clemons recognizes that a properly authorized state appellate court can itself weigh valid aggravation against mitigation, or can determine under constitutional harmless-error principles that the error did not affect the sentence. Chapman places the harmlessness burden on the State beyond a reasonable doubt. Bare proportionality review or an assertion that other aggravators exist does not establish either correction. The Court leaves the actual state review and the unresolved procedural-default defense to the lower courts. If habeas relief becomes warranted after those matters are resolved, a conditional remedy may permit lawful state correction; death eligibility is not automatically negated.

The Court distinguishes Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862: its nonweighing holding does not automatically cure a weighing-system error. The Court distinguishes Barclay v. Florida, 463 U.S. 939: its actual review and state-law invalidity do not justify uncorrected constitutional aggravation. The Court applies Clemons v. Mississippi, 494 U.S. 738: genuine appellate reweighing or constitutional harmlessness can correct a weighing error. The Court applies Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, to the harmless-error route: the State must establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court distinguishes Lowenfield v. Phelps, 484 U.S. 231: duplication in narrowing does not answer vague-factor weighting and cannot supply a prefinality reliance premise.

Effective March 9, the Court applies Teague to this specific Mississippi claim and makes the weighing/nonweighing remedial distinction controlling at the stated scope. No universal retroactivity exception or automatic cure from surviving aggravators is added. Genuine reweighing and constitutional harmlessness remain different routes. Actual cure, procedural default and any eventual conditional-writ terms are unresolved.

Souter, joined by Scalia and Thomas, dissents because Zant reserved the weighing question and Barclay did not clearly require the precise constitutional correction now demanded. In his view, reasonable jurists at finality could distinguish constitutionally protected conduct from a vague aggravator and regard the Mississippi application as unsettled. The majority holds that the statutory weighing role strengthens the old guidance requirement rather than making it new.

Supreme Court review ends with remand to the Fifth Circuit. The preserved procedural-default defense remains undecided; the lower courts must consider it and any adequate state correction. If relief is otherwise warranted, a conditional writ may permit resentencing or authorized appellate cure. No unconditional writ, immediate release, automatic life sentence or finding that a particular state cure failed is made.

**End of entry: Stringer v. Black, Merits decision, March 9, 1992.**

### Shaw v. Reno, No. 92-357

Merits disposition, 1993-06-28

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-20; decided 1993-06-28. On direct appeal from three-judge District Court for the Eastern District of North Carolina, 808 F. Supp.461 (1992).

**Question:** Whether the complaint states an Equal Protection claim against North Carolina officials for allegedly arbitrary racial construction of congressional districts, notwithstanding the asserted Voting Rights Act purpose; separately, whether the claims against federal preclearance officials may proceed in this district.

**Material record:** After the 1990 census, North Carolina received a twelfth congressional seat. The Attorney General objected to its initial one-majority-Black-district plan under § 5; the State then adopted a two-majority-Black-district plan. The Attorney General’s objection identified a possible additional district in the southeast; the revised second district instead followed roughly 160 miles of the Interstate 85 corridor farther north. Forty counties were covered by § 5, and the State did not pursue the alternative declaratory-judgment preclearance route. The complaint alleges arbitrary concentration by race rather than ordinary geographic criteria. Its five Durham County voters include two assigned to District 12 and three to adjacent District 2. Racial purpose, a legally sufficient remedial predicate, and the actual relationship between the challenged boundaries and voting-rights compliance have not been resolved at trial.

**Relief sought:** The voters seek reversal of dismissal, declaratory relief, and an injunction against the challenged plan. State officials seek affirmance; federal officials defend the jurisdictional and preclearance-review bars.

All nine Justices—Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas—participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Affirmed: dismissal of the racial-design-only equal-protection claim against the state defendants, 5–4; federal-official dismissal left undisturbed, 9–0. No injunction or redistricting remand.

| Component | Disposition | Supporting Justices | Opposing or other positions | Remedy / scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| State defendants: racial-design-only equal-protection claim | Affirm dismissal (5–4) | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | No injunction, new map or trial on this rejected theory; other properly presented claims and any properly sought amendment remain governed by ordinary procedural and preclusion rules. |
| Federal defendants: challenge to preclearance determination | Leave dismissal undisturbed (9–0) | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing vote | The separate statutory review channel and nonreviewability ground remain; no federal merits injunction. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing / author | Joined by | Exact scope | Judgment relationship |
|---|---|---|---|

| O’Connor | Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Dissent as to state claim: pleaded racial separation itself warrants strict scrutiny, with actual justification and narrow tailoring examined on remand. | Reverse state dismissal; no finding that the map is already invalid |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Territorial racial design alone does not state the asserted deprivation

**Controlling proposition:** Alleged deliberate or predominant consideration of race in drawing territorial constituencies, even with departures from ordinary districting criteria, does not by itself state this equal-protection claim where no racial exclusion, intentional dilution, materially unequal participation, legally inferior civic membership or other independently cognizable constitutional deprivation is alleged. This is a substantive disposition of the design-only theory, not heightened pleading, a universal requirement of additional downstream loss, or an added constitutional-injury prerequisite for an independent statutory claim.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter (5 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the alleged design alone does not deny these voters equal membership in their territorial constituency. UJO supports the limited distinction between racial awareness and electoral discrimination, although its separate rationales establish no comprehensive test. Croson protects each person against racial allocation of competitive opportunities; statewide racial strength therefore cannot answer a personal deprivation. Here the alleged demographic design does not itself prescribe racial eligibility or limit the representative’s constituency to one race. Anderson forbids official racial ballot cues, and Gomillion forbids boundaries used to expel a racial population from municipal voting; both remain enforceable without this theory. The contrary view reasonably identifies a danger of racial stereotyping, but that danger does not make every deliberate demographic boundary an official rule of unequal membership. The Court accepts the allegations rather than inventing benign geography. Conley supplies ordinary pleading rules, while the asserted substantive theory fails. No finding of federal compulsion, final compliance with other law, or right to a particular racial composition follows.

**Precedent treatment:**

- United Jewish Organizations v. Carey, 430 U.S.144: its fractured reasoning supports a limited distinction, not a comprehensive controlling test or a statewide-white-representation defense.
- Wright v. Rockefeller, 376 U.S.52: distinguished; racial purpose was not established there, so it did not decide the present assumed-purpose question.
- City of Richmond v. Croson, 488 U.S.469, and Shelley v. Kraemer, 334 U.S.1: personal equality retained; the Court declines to equate territorial design alone with Croson’s racial allocation of opportunities.
- Anderson v. Martin, 375 U.S.399: official racial ballot labeling remains prohibited without proof that an election winner changed.
- Gomillion v. Lightfoot, 364 U.S.339: racial municipal disenfranchisement remains prohibited; Whittaker’s broader concurrence is not adopted as a rule covering every racial district adjustment.
- White v. Regester, 412 U.S.755, and McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents, 339 U.S.637: impaired participation may be unconstitutional despite formal access; no universal downstream-loss requirement.
- Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S.41; Leatherman, March 3, 1993: ordinary notice pleading retained, no demand for expert proof or a replacement map.
- Growe and Voinovich: statutory prerequisites, state primacy and unresolved coalition/influence questions remain distinct; no additional constitutional-injury gate imposed on § 2.

##### Federal preclearance dismissal remains separate

**Controlling proposition:** The district court outside the District of Columbia cannot use this action to review the Attorney General’s preclearance determination under the Voting Rights Act; § 14(b)’s designated channel and Morris’s restriction on review leave dismissal of the federal defendants undisturbed. That disposition does not immunize the State’s plan from an otherwise cognizable constitutional or statutory challenge.

**Authority:** Stone-Zsela’s Opinion of the Court, this proposition; Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9 Justices). Direct majority support at the stated level of generality; no Marks aggregation.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court leaves the federal defendants’ dismissal undisturbed on grounds independent of the state claim. Section 14(b) channels actions restraining execution or enforcement of the Voting Rights Act to the District Court for the District of Columbia. Morris explains why the Attorney General’s preclearance determination is not an ordinary reviewable administrative adjudication and why substantive challenges to a state plan remain separate. The plaintiffs cannot obtain review of that determination in this North Carolina action by describing their objection as one to the resulting map. Conversely, preserving the federal dismissal says nothing about whether the State’s plan satisfies every independent constitutional or statutory command. Beer’s nonretrogression principle does not compel maximization of majority-minority districts, and preclearance is no universal certificate of legality. Because the designated channel and nonreviewability rules resolve this component, the Court does not decide an alternative merits defense for the federal officials or direct them to approve different boundaries.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Morris v. Gressette, 432 U.S.491: restriction on reviewing the Attorney General’s preclearance determination applied; independent challenges to the state enactment remain available under their own rules.
- Beer v. United States, 425 U.S.130: nonretrogression distinguished from maximization or certification of compliance with every law.

#### Current-Law Effect

Effective June 28, a five-Justice Court rejects this territorial racial-design-only constitutional theory. Independent exclusion, dilution, unequal participation, civic-status and statutory claims retain their own elements. No generally applicable replacement for Croson or Metro Broadcasting, predominance scrutiny rule, coalition/crossover entitlement, or requirement to prove federal compulsion enters. Federal-official dismissal rests on the separate channel.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Stone’s concurrence explains why equal political membership concerns actual personal treatment rather than aggregate racial officeholding. Constitutional amendments and lawful statutory and judicial changes have expanded participation without an uninterrupted progression or a guarantee of proportional officeholding. Harper corrects a wealth barrier; Reynolds protects equal voting power; the Reconstruction enforcement cases permit properly supported preventive legislation without a completed local constitutional violation in every covered application. Those authorities do not prove that North Carolina needed these lines. Stone preserves Shelley’s personal-rights principle for white and Black residents alike and rejects historical racial dominance as an answer to an actual deprivation. Croson presents a serious contrary analogy because classification can itself injure; his distinction concerns a common territorial electorate, not a supposed requirement of monetary or electoral loss. Wright left proven racial design unresolved, UJO is fractured, and Whittaker’s Gomillion concurrence advances a broader separation theory Stone declines to extend. Anderson’s ballot-label injury and McLaurin’s restrictions within shared facilities refute any universal claim that formal eligibility suffices. An operative racial designation or discriminatory denial of an otherwise available participation opportunity remains examinable; Knight supplies no general right to force officials to listen. Allen’s personal-treatment standing requirement and Heckler’s noneconomic equality injury remain distinct from success on the merits. Gingles’s behavioral evidence, independent § 2 requirements and Beer’s nonretrogression principle remain intact; neither coalition, crossover nor influence duties are newly decided. Congress’s supported preventive categories and a State’s independent authority remain different. Stone’s broader corrective-permission framework is not adopted as a replacement scrutiny tier, statutory duty or universal defense. Ordinary Rule 15/Foman amendment and preclusion rules remain; he directs no amendment and invents no additional facts.

White, joined by Blackmun and Stevens, finds UJO and the lack of an alleged participation impairment decisive, while recognizing that constitutional injury is personal. Their further account of districting practice is not an independent majority rule. Stevens separately distinguishes a State’s effort to recognize demonstrated racial voting conditions from a command that citizens vote or be represented according to race; his Karcher concern with arbitrary districting does not make shape alone dispositive. Souter distinguishes geographic constituency assignment from segregated access to public institutions and declines to infer that elected representatives will deny service to residents of another race. O’Connor, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, dissents on the state claim. They would recognize the alleged separation itself as a personal constitutional injury and require a sufficiently supported compelling justification and narrow tailoring under Croson. They treat unusual shape as evidence of racial design, not as an aesthetic constitutional rule. They would reverse dismissal and allow examination of justification, not hold the map invalid on disputed facts or require it to be maximally compact.

### Graham v. Collins, No. 91-7580

Merits decision, January 25, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued October 14, 1992. On writ of certiorari to the Fifth Circuit sitting en banc, 950 F.2d 1009, affirming denial of federal habeas relief. The questions are whether Teague bars Graham’s claim and whether Texas’s three submitted special issues permitted effective use of youth, background and positive-character mitigation. This decision and Herrera v. Collins are issued on the same day, without a sequence between them.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses denial of sentencing relief on the youth and background grounds and remands for a conditional writ directed to the death sentence. The murder conviction remains undisturbed.

| Component | Disposition and vote | Supporting Justices | Opposing Justices | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Teague and the youth/background sentencing claim | Reverse denial of relief, 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Conditional writ: Texas must provide an adequate new sentencing proceeding within a reasonable district-court period or replace the death sentence with a lawful noncapital sentence |
| Positive character as a separately sufficient ground for relief | No independent relief on this component, nine supporting disposition on different grounds | Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | No opposing votes | Youth/background relief remains; no separate additional remedy. Stone-Zsela supports this component in the judgment only |

The new sentencer must have an effective route for all relevant mitigation, but no particular weight or favorable sentence is dictated. No guilt retrial or release from independent lawful custody is ordered.

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### The youth and background claims apply law settled before finality

**Controlling proposition:** Teague does not bar Graham's claim that Texas's submitted sentencing questions prevented effective use of his youth and unstable background as material mitigation independent of deliberateness or future danger. Jurek's conditional approval, Lockett and Eddings dictated that functional requirement before his conviction became final on September 10, 1984; no new procedural-rule exception is created.

**Authority:** Parts I–III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this prefinality-law holding, its application to youth and background, and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that applying an existing protection to these facts does not announce a new rule. Teague and Butler protect reasonable reliance on law at finality; they do not require an earlier case with identical evidence. Jurek approved the Texas scheme on the understanding that relevant mitigation could receive effective consideration. Lockett rejected legal barriers to mitigating aspects of character, record and offense, and Eddings expressly protected youth and disturbed background. Those decisions preceded Graham's final judgment. Penry later applied the same duty when the special issues failed to express a material mitigating judgment; it did not create that duty. The State's reliance on Jurek is substantial, but facial approval subject to effective mitigation cannot authorize its material exclusion in a particular application. Franklin's treatment of prison conduct and Saffle's rejection of unrestricted sympathy do not negate the preexisting requirement. Stringer confirms that a later application need not create new law merely because judges disagree. The Court reaches these claims without invoking either Teague exception.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, and Butler v. McKellar, 494 U.S. 407 — applied; the relevant result must be dictated at finality, not merely supported by a later plausible extension.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — preserved as qualified approval; it does not immunize every application of the special issues.
- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, and Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied as the pre-1984 effective-mitigation rule.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied as recognition of that existing rule, not as law inserted retroactively into 1984.
- Stringer v. Black, March 9, 1992 — applied at its existing Teague scope; disagreement alone does not establish novelty.
- Franklin v. Lynaugh, 487 U.S. 164, and Saffle v. Parks, 494 U.S. 484 — distinguished; evidence whose mitigating use fits the questions, and unrestricted sympathy, differ from material culpability mitigation excluded by them.

##### Youth requires an effective route for its independent mitigating force

**Controlling proposition:** When the record supports a material mitigating judgment about culpability that the authorized sentencing answers cannot express, a reasonable likelihood that the submitted mechanism prevents that use requires an effective corrective instruction or procedure. Graham's youth at seventeen has that independent significance notwithstanding deliberate conduct or predicted future danger, and the three submitted special issues provide no adequate route for it here.

**Authority:** Parts II and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this independently sufficient ground for sentencing relief and its conditional remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a prediction of future violence does not answer every question about a young offender's culpability. Eddings recognizes immaturity, inexperience and undeveloped judgment as material mitigation. A juror could accept those considerations while finding that Graham deliberately killed and might remain dangerous. Jurek's approval therefore does not end the inquiry: the permitted answers must actually allow the material mitigating judgment. The provocation question supplies no route for youth's independent significance either. Texas correctly observes that youth may predict rehabilitation, and the jury could consider that prediction. But permitting one material use does not authorize exclusion of another. Penry confirms this distinction between admitting evidence and allowing its mitigating effect. Boyde's reasonable-likelihood formulation assesses the submitted mechanism; the substantive duty remains the prefinality rule. The Court requires an effective sentencing vehicle, not favorable weight, acquittal or an unrestricted mercy question. It announces no categorical prohibition on executing all seventeen-year-old offenders and does not decide the constitutionality of Texas's later statute.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied to youth's independent culpability significance; jurors may assign weight but cannot be legally prevented from giving it effect.
- Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 — its conditional framework remains; this defective application does not establish facial invalidity.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied beyond a permanent-handicap limitation; a mitigating trait need not have only aggravating relevance to future danger to require an effective vehicle.
- Boyde v. California, 494 U.S. 370 — applied as the reasonable-likelihood method of assessing instructional operation, not as the source of a new substantive rule at 1984 finality.

##### The submitted background evidence also requires effective consideration

**Controlling proposition:** A capital sentencing system may not prevent a jury from acting on the material culpability significance it finds in a defendant's disturbed upbringing merely because that evidence also bears on future dangerousness or lacks a uniquely severe permanent handicap. The evidence of Graham's mother's recurring mental illness and hospitalizations and his shifting family care supports such a mitigating judgment, which the submitted special issues cannot adequately express; this independently requires the same sentencing remedy.

**Authority:** Parts III and V of Souter's opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor; five Justices adopt this independent background-evidence ground and the conditional sentencing remedy.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the sentencing jury must have a lawful way to act on the material mitigating significance of this upbringing. Eddings protects consideration of disturbed family background, particularly for a young defendant; it does not demand proof of a uniquely severe permanent handicap. Graham's grandmother described his mother's repeated hospitalization and his movement between family caregivers. The jury could find that history reduced his culpability without denying that the killing was deliberate or that future violence was probable. The provocation question does not express that judgment. The State argues that the favorable testimony about his behavior shows no lasting harm and that the evidence can support rehabilitation. Those arguments properly address weight, but cannot establish that every material mitigating use fits the authorized answers. Penry requires an effective means of expressing the supported culpability judgment; admission alone is insufficient. The Court neither finds a psychiatric disability nor requires jurors to infer a particular causal effect. It requires a lawful opportunity for sentencing assessment and leaves the weight to the new sentencer.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 — applied to relevant background proffered as a basis for less than death.
- Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 — applied; disturbed upbringing cannot be excluded from mitigating effect as a matter of law.
- Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 — applied; neither a uniquely severe permanent handicap nor complete absence of relevance to the special issues is an exclusive trigger.

##### Positive character as an independent ground

**Judgment without controlling rationale:** The separate contention that Graham's household assistance, religious observance and financial support of his children independently require additional sentencing relief is rejected by all nine Justices, on different grounds. Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor and Souter regard its asserted independent culpability significance as insubstantial on this record and its future-behavior use as available under the second issue; White, Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas would bar the requested mitigation relief under Teague. Stone-Zsela joins the denial of this independent component in the judgment only. These grounds do not yield a common general rule or a Marks subset; the youth and background holdings independently require sentencing relief.

**Limits:** No universal rule about positive character evidence or its value apart from the asserted culpability and future-danger uses is decided. The jury remains free to hear constitutionally relevant evidence at the new sentencing proceeding. Neither denial of this independent ground nor the sentencing remedy disturbs the murder conviction.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

No precedent is overruled. Jurek's qualified approval and the prefinality Lockett/Eddings rule are applied. Penry is not confined to uniquely severe permanent disabilities or evidence wholly lacking mitigating relevance to the special issues. Franklin's prison-conduct example and Saffle's unrestricted-sympathy claim remain distinct. Teague retains its dictated-by-prefinality-law requirement and exceptions; neither exception supplies this judgment. Sawyer's gateway and Stringer's separate weighing-cure law are unchanged.

Effective January 25, 1993, Texas's former special-issue mechanism is unconstitutional as applied to the material culpability significance of Graham's youth and disturbed background, and relief on those grounds is not new law under Teague. Lower courts must ask whether supported material mitigating use can actually receive effect, using the reasonable-likelihood instruction inquiry; mere admission or some relevance to future danger is insufficient. This is no categorical fourth-question requirement, entitlement to favorable weight, facial invalidation, or juvenile-execution prohibition. The positive-character component yields no new general controlling rule.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

White, joined by Scalia, Kennedy and Thomas, would affirm because reasonable jurists in 1984 could read Jurek, Lockett and Eddings as allowing Texas to channel this mitigation through deliberateness and future dangerousness. In their view a new instruction requirement is not dictated by prefinality law; it neither removes a class of conduct or persons from punishment nor supplies a watershed accuracy procedure, so neither Teague exception saves it. They distinguish Penry's disabling evidence from Graham's evidence supporting rehabilitation and do not adopt the Court's as-applied merits rule.

Thomas also writes separately. He regards Penry's expansive account of mitigating effect as inconsistent with Furman's requirement that capital discretion be structured against arbitrariness and discrimination. He would permit reasonable state definitions of relevance and sentencing criteria while allowing defendants to present constitutionally relevant information. He would overrule Penry to that extent; the Court does not do so. His view is separate from White's sufficient Teague ground.

Stevens concurs while joining the controlling opinion. He regards narrowing eligibility, avoiding vague aggravators, proportionality limits and individualized mitigation as complementary protections. Requiring a sentencer to act on relevant individual circumstances reduces resort to irrelevant considerations such as race; it does not demand an unreasoned preference. His discussion responds to Thomas without creating an additional test or changing the conditional remedy.

Souter's Part IV, joined by Blackmun, Stevens and O’Connor, explains the limited character-evidence disposition. Ordinary chores, religious observance and support for children can inform future dangerousness through the second special issue. On the asserted theory here, their separate bearing on culpability for this offense is insubstantial. The four do not decide the value of such traits apart from the submitted culpability/future-danger arguments. Stone-Zsela joins only the denial of independent relief on that component.

### Growe v. Emison, No. 91-1420

Merits decision, February 23, 1993

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued November 2, 1992. On direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. 1253 from the three-judge District of Minnesota, 782 F. Supp. 427 (1992), which imposed congressional and legislative districts. Minnesota seeks reversal; appellees defend the federal plans. The questions are whether the federal panel should defer to timely state judicial redistricting and whether its minority district has the proof required by § 2 of the Voting Rights Act.

Parallel state and federal actions followed the 1990 census. The State's special judicial panel corrected legislative drafting defects and prepared congressional districts while the federal panel obstructed its work and adopted its own plans. The federal Minneapolis district combined several minority groups without local statistical or anecdotal proof of common political cohesion or majority bloc voting. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated at argument and decision.

#### Judgment

The Court reverses the federal redistricting judgment and remands with instructions to dismiss. The substantive reversal is unanimous; the mandatory-dismissal direction is 8–1.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Mandatory termination of this federal action | Remand with instructions to dismiss, 8–1 | White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas | Stone-Zsela | District court must dismiss; Stone-Zsela would permit conditional retained jurisdiction and justified safeguards if a genuine election deadline or federal violation requires action. |

#### Opinion Topology

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Timely state redistricting includes the State's courts

**Controlling proposition:** When a State's legislature or judiciary is timely undertaking redistricting, a federal court must defer its own redistricting action unless the record shows that the State will not produce a lawful plan in time for the election. The federal court may set a reasonable deadline and retain jurisdiction as a safeguard, but may neither obstruct timely state work nor displace it merely because it prefers another plan; completion of every state appeal before the election is not required.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion on state priority, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt this rule. The separate eight-Justice dismissal direction concerns this completed record, not the general availability of retained jurisdiction.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that Minnesota's own courts must receive the same opportunity to complete lawful redistricting as its legislature. Scott v. Germano expressly recognized state judicial responsibility and allowed a federal deadline and reserve jurisdiction; Reynolds v. Sims supplies the obligation to correct unequal districts while giving the State an initial opportunity. The federal panel instead directed its deadline solely to the legislature and treated the state court's provisional work as interference. It then relied on delay to which its own injunction had contributed. The state court had adopted its legislative plan and was prepared to act on congressional districts in time for the election. The presence of a federal statutory claim in only one complaint does not change the shared remedial task. Nor does the possibility of an unfinished state appeal justify taking that task away. Federal power remains available for a proved federal defect or an actual failure of timely state action; this decision establishes no universal rule of priority based on filing dates.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Scott v. Germano, 381 U.S. 407 (1965): applied to both legislative and congressional redistricting through state courts; its timely-action condition and reserve federal jurisdiction remain.
- Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533 (1964): applied for primary state responsibility and the obligation to remedy unconstitutional apportionment; population-equality requirements are unchanged.

##### A superseding state judgment must receive legal effect

**Controlling proposition:** Once a state court's final judgment replaces the challenged legislative plan, the federal court must give the replacement legal effect rather than treat it as one optional proposal. A challenge to the old plan becomes moot unless it also reaches the superseding plan; allegations against the old plan alone do not establish a violation in the replacement.

**Authority:** Scalia's opinion on the live-plan issue, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the stated rule. No party-preclusion rule beyond that necessary legal effect is announced.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the federal court must address the plan that actually governs. The state court's final legislative judgment changed Minnesota law before the federal panel selected its own districts. Section 1738 and the principles explained in Atlantic Coast Line Railroad v. Locomotive Engineers require respect for a state judgment's legal effect; they do not permit federal judges to demote it to an optional map. The earlier allegations concerned the superseded 1983 districts. No party established that those allegations remained a live challenge to the replacement, and the federal panel neither held a hearing nor requested argument addressing a particular replacement plan's asserted statutory defect. Wise v. Lipscomb supplies the related requirement to examine a lawful replacement before imposing judicial relief. A general preference for a larger minority population in one district cannot supply the missing violation. The Court does not decide an unpleaded replacement-plan challenge or immunize the State's work from an appropriately presented federal claim.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co. v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 398 U.S. 281 (1970): applied for respect for state judgments and the federal-state judicial relationship; no enlarged preclusion rule adopted.
- Wise v. Lipscomb, 437 U.S. 535 (1978): applied for the need to evaluate the legally operative replacement before federal remedial displacement.

##### Fragmentation claims require proof of the causal voting conditions

**Controlling proposition:** A § 2 claim that single-member districts fragment a minority group's potential electoral majority requires proof that the group is sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a majority in a single-member district, is politically cohesive, and faces majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate. Without the required cohesion and majority-bloc evidence, the court may not order a minority district by adding distinct groups' populations; the Court assumes without deciding that a supported coalition claim is permissible and leaves influence-only claims and the population denominator unresolved.

**Authority:** Scalia's statutory opinion, joined by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas; all nine adopt the stated single-member extension and record application.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a remedial district requires proof of dilution, not a demographic assumption. Thornburg v. Gingles identifies the conditions connecting an electoral structure to lost minority opportunity. Size and compactness establish a potential electoral majority; cohesion identifies a common electoral preference; majority bloc voting explains how the challenged arrangement ordinarily defeats that preference. Those reasons apply when single-member boundaries fragment voters just as they apply to their submergence in a multimember district. Gingles reserved that extension, and the Court now adopts it for this claim. Even assuming compactness and the permissibility of combining distinct minority groups, the record contains neither statistical nor anecdotal evidence of the necessary common cohesion or majority bloc voting in Minneapolis. National generalizations cannot replace local proof. The statute's totality inquiry does not dispense with those necessary conditions or guarantee proportional representation. No particular statistical technique is universally prescribed, and the Court does not decide every possible coalition or influence-district theory.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): extended from multimember dilution to the single-member fragmentation claim at issue; its causal prerequisites and totality inquiry remain distinct, and its influence-only reservation remains open.

#### Limits and Current-Law Effect

State judicial redistricting receives the same timely opportunity as state legislative work. Federal reserve jurisdiction remains available under the stated conditions, although this action must be dismissed. A superseding plan must be tested on a properly presented claim concerning that plan. Gingles's three causal prerequisites expressly apply to this fragmentation claim, with the totality-of-circumstances inquiry still required. Whether a coalition claim is legally available, influence-only claims and total-population versus voting-age-population measurement remain unresolved. Other asserted errors are not reached. No precedent is overruled, no priority rule based on filing dates is adopted, and state plans receive no general immunity from federal review.

#### Separate Position Relevant to the Decision

Stone-Zsela agrees that the federal plans must be set aside and joins the Court's state-priority, live-plan and statutory holdings. He would allow the district court to defer to the state plans while retaining jurisdiction if a genuine election deadline or properly presented, proved federal defect justifies continued authority. Such retention is permitted, not required. Any implementation safeguard must respond to that actual need; federal preference cannot justify recreating the minority district without statutory proof. His disagreement concerns mandatory dismissal, not the validity of the federal maps or a categorical entitlement to federal supervision.

Supreme Court merits review is complete. The district court must dismiss this action on remand. No replacement federal map is prescribed, and no Supreme Court proceeding is retained. Dismissal does not adjudicate an unpleaded future challenge to a state plan.

### Voinovich v. Quilter, No. 91-1618

Merits decision, March 2, 1993

Argued December 8, 1992. On direct appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1253 from the three-judge District Court for the Northern District of Ohio. The District Court invalidated Ohio’s legislative apportionment on Section 2, Fifteenth Amendment purpose, and Fourteenth Amendment population grounds. Ohio officials seek reversal; challengers seek to retain invalidation and corrective districting. The questions are whether the State must demonstrate remedial necessity before creating majority-minority districts, whether the asserted influence-dilution and purpose claims succeed on this record, and whether departures above ten percent foreclose a county-boundary justification. Ohio’s 1991 plan was revised in 1992.

The Court reverses, 9–0, and remands only for further proceedings on the population-equality claim. Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas participated at argument and decision. All nine reject the Section 2 prohibition and the initial burden imposed on Ohio, reject the presented Section 2 influence-dilution claim, reverse the finding of discriminatory purpose under the Fifteenth Amendment, and vote to reverse and remand on the population claim under the Fourteenth Amendment. No particular racial configuration is ordered. O’Connor delivers the Opinion of the Court, joined in full by Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas: Part I addresses posture, Part II the statutory holdings, Part III the purpose finding, and Part IV the population standard and limited remand. No separate writing is published.

All three presented grounds are reached. The Court reviews the legal standards independently and the finding of discriminatory purpose for clear error. It assumes only for deciding this case that an influence-dilution claim is cognizable and that the Fifteenth Amendment covers vote dilution; neither assumption establishes a rule. The distinct Fourteenth Amendment challenge to racial classification was presented neither below nor here.

Section 2 does not prohibit a State from choosing majority-minority districts merely because no federal violation compels their creation; challengers bear at least the initial burden of showing, under the totality of circumstances, unequal electoral opportunity caused by the challenged arrangement. A federal court may order such districts only when their creation is necessary to remedy an established federal-law violation, but that limit does not become a categorical restriction on state redistricting authority; independently applicable federal constitutional and statutory requirements remain enforceable.

Part II.B of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion directly controls the rejection of the statutory prohibition and the lower court’s allocation of the burden; all nine Justices adopt the complete rule.

Section 2 protects equal electoral opportunity rather than a preferred distribution of minority voters among districts. Gingles requires challengers to demonstrate unequal access under the totality of circumstances; it does not declare a particular district form inherently unlawful. Concentrating minority voters can improve their ability to elect preferred candidates, diminish that ability, or leave it unchanged. The consequences require proof. The District Court instead required Ohio to demonstrate a prior statutory violation before creating majority-minority districts. That reverses the statute's initial allocation of the burden and mistakes an absence of federal compulsion for a federal prohibition. Growe recognizes the State's primary responsibility for apportionment and limits federal judicial intervention to an established federal defect. A State derives its districting authority independently of a federal court's remedial power. Its choice remains subject to federal requirements, but Section 2 supplies no categorical ban here. The Court does not decide the distinct constitutional challenge to conscious racial classification, which was neither presented below nor raised in this appeal.

Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): followed for the challengers' burden and the totality-of-circumstances inquiry; no district form is automatically unlawful under Section 2.

Growe v. Emison, decision of February 23, 1993: applied to distinguish the State's primary districting authority from federal judicial remedial authority; its actual majority holdings remain controlling.

Assuming that Section 2 reaches the asserted loss of minority influence districts, the claim fails because challengers did not establish sufficiently cohesive white majority voting usually to defeat the minority's preferred candidates. The Gingles prerequisites apply with attention to the claim's nature: an influence claim would require modification or elimination of the first prerequisite that the minority could constitute a district majority, but the Court need not select that adaptation or decide whether influence claims are cognizable because the independently necessary bloc-voting showing is absent.

Parts II.A and II.C of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion control this independently dispositive rejection of the presented Section 2 claim and each stated reservation.

Packing can impair electoral opportunity, just as dispersing a cohesive minority can. Gingles recognizes both possible mechanisms, and Growe applies its threshold framework to challenges involving single-member districts. Here, however, challengers seek additional districts in which black voters would be an influential minority relying on crossover votes, rather than districts in which they could themselves form a majority. The Court assumes this theory is cognizable and does not mechanically demand satisfaction of a majority-size prerequisite inconsistent with that assumption. Whether that first prerequisite should be modified or eliminated can remain open. The third prerequisite independently defeats the claim: the District Court found an absence of racially polarized voting, and challengers accept that finding. Without sufficiently strong white bloc voting to frustrate the minority's preferred candidates, this record does not establish the unequal opportunity asserted. The lower court cannot replace that missing causal proof with the bare observation that creating majority-minority districts removes black voters from other districts. Neither all influence claims nor all packing claims are rejected.

Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30 (1986): applied to the necessity of majority bloc voting and the causal mechanism of dilution; its reservations about influence claims remain unresolved.

Growe v. Emison, decision of February 23, 1993: followed for application of the threshold framework to single-member district challenges, with its influence-claim reservation preserved rather than silently decided.

Assuming that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted vote-dilution claim, the finding that Ohio intentionally injured black voters cannot stand on this record: attention to perceived federal-law requirements and possession of an opponent's speculative documents, without evidence of reliance, do not establish the prohibited purpose the District Court inferred. The Court reverses that finding as clearly erroneous without deciding the Amendment's coverage of dilution or its relationship to race-conscious districting generally.

Part III of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion controls this disposition confined to the record. All nine Justices adopt it; none adopts a general immunity for political objectives or racial awareness.

The Court assumes that the Fifteenth Amendment reaches the asserted dilution claim. Beer v. United States leaves that coverage question unresolved, and this case does not require an answer. The asserted violation depends on the intentional injury to black voters that the District Court found. Neither cited piece of evidence supports that inference. Tilling's decision to honor federal requirements when he perceived a conflict with state law demonstrates attention to the Supremacy Clause, not a purpose to abridge minority voting strength. The documents he possessed were an opponent's speculation about possible partisan strategies; they were not his adopted instructions, and no evidence shows that he relied on them. The affirmative record instead shows participation by minority organizations and incorporation of substantial features of the Ohio NAACP's proposal. Possible political objectives do not eliminate the need for evidence connecting those objectives to prohibited racial injury. On this record the finding is clearly erroneous. The Court expresses no broader view on race-conscious districting or the unpresented racial-classification claim.

Beer v. United States, 425 U.S. 130 (1976): followed in leaving unresolved whether the Fifteenth Amendment reaches vote-dilution claims; the assumption made here creates no new coverage holding.

Mobile v. Bolden, 446 U.S. 55 (1980): its plurality and separate writings are background to the alleged intentional-discrimination claim, not treated as a single majority rationale or a new categorical rule governing every race-conscious map.

A state legislative plan whose maximum total population deviation exceeds ten percent makes the initial showing that requires the State to justify the disparity; crossing that threshold does not make preservation of county boundaries legally unavailable as a justification. The District Court must determine whether the actual plan reasonably advances that rational state policy, including its substantiated connection to the deviations and consistency of implementation, and whether the resulting disparities nevertheless exceed constitutional limits; the Court does not decide that Ohio has met its burden.

Part IV of O’Connor’s unanimous opinion controls this standard and the population-only remand. All nine Justices adopt both; the Court approves neither the State’s justification nor a particular remedial map.

Equal protection requires substantially equal population in state legislative districts, but it does not demand mathematical identity in every district. Brown v. Thomson explains that deviations below ten percent are generally minor, while larger disparities make the initial showing requiring state justification. Ohio's plan crosses that threshold, so its officials must substantiate their explanation. The District Court nevertheless erred by treating county preservation as legally incapable of justifying deviations above ten percent. Mahan v. Howell permits a rational policy preserving political subdivisions to justify some disparities; Brown preserves both the need for a reasonable connection between plan and policy and an independent constitutional limit on the resulting inequality. Invoking county lines by name does not establish that connection. The inquiry concerns the actual plan, the consistency of the policy's application, and the magnitude of the departures it produces. The record has not received that analysis under the proper standard. The Court therefore remands this issue alone, without approving the deviations or ordering any particular racial arrangement.

Brown v. Thomson, 462 U.S. 835 (1983): followed for the threshold requiring a state justification, the required connection to a rational state policy, and the separate constitutional limit; departures below ten percent remain generally minor, not categorically immune.

Mahan v. Howell, 410 U.S. 315 (1973): followed in allowing a substantiated political-subdivision policy to justify departures above ten percent; it does not automatically validate Ohio's particular departures.

Effective March 2, 1993, these four holdings apply and clarify the existing rules. Section 2 requires proof of the challenged arrangement’s unequal electoral consequences; it does not require a State first to demonstrate a federal violation before choosing majority-minority districts. The assumed influence claim fails for absent majority bloc voting, while the availability of that claim and adaptation of Gingles’s first prerequisite remain open. The purpose ruling is confined to the deficient evidence here. Population departures above ten percent require substantiated state justification under Brown and Mahan; neither automatic invalidity nor automatic approval follows. Growe’s holdings remain intact. Its dismissal mandate addressed its exhausted record and does not dismiss Ohio’s independently live population claim. No precedent is overruled, and the Court decides no distinct racial-classification claim, Fifteenth Amendment dilution-coverage question, or general rule about race-conscious districting.

Supreme Court merits review ends with reversal and a remand limited to the population-equality claim. The District Court must determine, with the burden of justification on Ohio, whether the actual plan reasonably advances its county-preservation policy and whether the resulting disparities exceed constitutional limits. The statutory and purpose grounds do not remain open for retrial. The Court does not determine that Ohio has carried its population burden, set a new election date, or order a particular remedial district map. The lower-court population proceeding remains open; no return to this Court is automatically calendared.

### Arave v. Creech, No. 91-1160

Reversal in part and remand, 1993-03-30

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1992-11-10; decided 1993-03-30. Reported source: 507 U.S. 463 (1993). Ninth Circuit, 947 F.2d 873. The case is before the Court on writ of certiorari to that court.

Question: Whether Idaho’s utter-disregard aggravator, as narrowed in State v. Osborn, is facially too vague to guide capital sentencing.

Creech pleaded guilty to murdering fellow prisoner Jensen. The sentencing judge found an initially unprovoked attack and justified self-defense, followed by fatal violence after Jensen was helpless; the judge also described excessive violent rage. Those findings are not replaced by Creech’s inconsistent accounts suggesting a planned killing. The Ninth Circuit invalidated the aggravator and independently required an opportunity for new mitigation on resentencing; it also found missing state-law intent findings for two other aggravators. Review is limited to the facial Osborn question, not those independent grounds or guilt.

Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter and Thomas participated in argument and decision.

#### Judgment

Reverse facial invalidity in part, 6–3; independent resentencing grounds undisturbed.

| Component | Judgment and named votes | Remedy and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Facial validity of the Osborn construction | Reverse in part: White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6); Affirm facial invalidity: Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens (3) | Reverse only the facial invalidity ruling and remand. |
| Independent mitigation and missing intent-finding grounds; conviction | Leave undisturbed: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (9) | Resentencing remains required, including the opportunity to present new mitigation. The conviction is not disturbed; no reinstatement of death sentence or as-applied adjudication. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing | Author | Parts and exact joins | Judgment relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion of the Court | O'Connor | I–II: facial validity; III: limited remedy. Joined by White, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas. | Reverse in part and remand |

| Dissent | Stone-Zsela | Entire. No other join. | Would affirm facial invalidity on the stated construction ground |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Facial validity of Idaho’s narrowed aggravator

**Controlling proposition:** Idaho’s utter-disregard aggravator, as authoritatively limited to the cold-blooded, pitiless slayer—one who kills without feeling or sympathy—provides adequate guidance and genuinely narrows its broad first-degree-murder class. The holding does not approve the unqualified statutory words alone or establish that Creech satisfies the aggravator.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, I–II; White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). The named majority adopts this proposition as the Court’s controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the State may distinguish a killer’s attitude toward the killing and the victim from the intent necessary to commit murder. Walton permits a judicial narrowing construction that supplies meaningful guidance even when the factual inquiry lacks mathematical precision. Osborn’s construction identifies absence of feeling or sympathy, rather than merely attaching another condemnatory adjective to the crime. A mental state can be inferred from circumstances and is not constitutionally subjective simply because proof is difficult. Zant also requires real narrowing. Idaho’s first-degree category includes killings attended by anger, jealousy or other emotion, so this construction does not encompass every member of that class. Pitilessness alone would not suffice. Godfrey and Maynard therefore remain applicable to unbounded descriptions of a murder’s horror. The disputed finding of violent rage raises an application question, which the Court does not decide. Its resolution cannot eliminate the independent resentencing relief already granted.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Walton, 497 U.S. 639 — applied: a sufficiently definite state judicial construction may guide a judge’s capital sentencing.
- Zant, 462 U.S. 862 — applied: the eligibility class must genuinely narrow.
- Godfrey, 446 U.S. 420, and Maynard, 486 U.S. 356 — distinguished on the adopted state-of-mind construction; their prohibition of unguided aggravation remains.

##### Formulation, application and the limited remand

**Controlling proposition:** A facial challenge may test whether a State has maintained a consistent, constitutionally sufficient formulation; alleged inconsistency in applying a valid formulation is a separate inquiry. Under Jeffers, constitutional review of its application asks whether no reasonable sentencer could find the aggravator; that question and the unreviewed grounds for resentencing remain open or intact here.

**Authority:** O'Connor’s Opinion of the Court, II–III; White, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas (6 Justices). The named majority adopts this proposition as the Court’s controlling rule.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court distinguishes the words that define eligibility from their application in a particular case. Jeffers supplies the separate constitutional boundary for applying an otherwise valid aggravator. A facial court may examine other state decisions to learn what construction the State actually uses; it does not turn every disputed later application into a defect in the formulation itself. The State repeatedly reaffirmed Osborn, and the later examples offered against it did not influence Creech’s original sentencing and appellate review. That does not establish that the factor was rationally applied to Creech. His finding of violent rage and the relevance of other killings remain undecided under the granted facial question. The Ninth Circuit also ordered new mitigation proceedings and identified missing findings under state law for other aggravators. Those grounds were not brought within this review. Reversal of one facial holding consequently neither reinstates the sentence nor authorizes Idaho to disregard the surviving resentencing directions.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Jeffers, 497 U.S. 764 — applied: facial validity and rational application are distinct.
- Sochor, June 8, 1992 — consistent formulation may be examined; actual correction of invalid weighing remains required when that separate problem arises.
- Stringer, Espinosa and Richmond — preserved within their weighing-error and cure scopes; no substitute cure or harmlessness finding made here.

#### Current-Law Effect

Facial validity of this particular construction established March 30; no change to actual weighing-error correction, mitigation, intent-finding requirements or the as-applied Jeffers question.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Blackmun, joined by Stevens, would affirm because the overlapping descriptions in Osborn do not provide a coherent boundary that a reviewing court can enforce. Godfrey, Maynard and Blackmun’s Walton dissent demand a State’s actual limiting principle, not a federal reconstruction of rhetoric. The violent-rage finding illustrates the instability but is not converted into an independent as-applied holding. Stone-Zsela separately would affirm on the same limited facial question: objective proof of mental state is possible, but that possibility does not define the line among emotional detachment, lack of sympathy, calculated intent and furious violence. Pitilessness does not itself narrow. His dissent preserves judicial narrowing as a permissible method and valid mental-state aggravators as a category. Both dissents leave the conviction and independent resentencing relief undisturbed. None controls.

### Johnson v. Texas, No. 92-5653

Merits disposition, 1993-06-24

#### Chronology and Posture

Argued 1993-04-26; decided 1993-06-24. On writ of certiorari to Texas Court of Criminal Appeals, 773 S.W.2d 322: capital conviction and death sentence affirmed; mitigation claim addressed on rehearing.

The question presented is whether the former special issues permitted meaningful mitigating effect for Johnson’s youth-related reduced culpability, not merely its predictive relevance to future danger.

Johnson was nineteen at the time of the murder. The penalty jury answered whether his conduct was deliberate with a reasonable expectation that death would result and whether he probably would commit future criminal violence. Jurors were told to consider all aggravating and mitigating evidence in answering those issues. His father described his troubled adolescence, substance use, and family losses. The questions did not separately ask whether his youth reduced culpability sufficiently to warrant a sentence less than death even if both prescribed answers were yes.

All nine Justices participated at argument and decision: Stone-Zsela, White, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, and Thomas.

#### Judgment

Reversed as to the death sentence and remanded, 5–4; guilt determination untouched.

| Component | Disposition | Supporting Justices | Other Justices / position | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Death sentence and youth mitigation | Reversed and remanded; 5–4 | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter | White, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas | Provide a constitutionally adequate capital sentencing proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence. Preserve conviction; no automatic acquittal or judicially prescribed wording. |

#### Opinion Topology

| Writing / author | Joined by | Scope | Judgment relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| O’Connor | Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, Souter | Opinion of the Court | Supports sentence reversal |
| Kennedy | White, Scalia, Thomas | Dissent on adequacy of special issues and Graham’s rule | Would affirm sentence |
| Scalia | Thomas | Separate dissent on the Penry requirement | Would reject the expanded mitigation-vehicle rule |

#### Controlling Propositions and Authority

##### Youth’s independent culpability significance needs an effective sentencing route

**Controlling proposition:** Considering the charge, evidence and trial as a whole, there is a reasonable likelihood that the special issues prevented Johnson’s jury from giving effect to his youth-related reduced culpability when it truthfully found deliberateness and future danger. Under Graham, Eddings, Penry and Boyde, admission of the evidence and partial relevance to future danger do not supply the missing route; mere possibility of a restrictive reading would be insufficient.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, supported on this holding by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, controls by a majority of 5 Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that its decision in Graham governs this application. Eddings recognizes that immaturity can reduce culpability independently of a prediction about later conduct. Johnson was nineteen, and his father’s account of troubled adolescence, substance use and family losses supplies a concrete mitigating argument. Texas correctly observes that youth can be transient and can reduce a prediction of future violence. But the general instruction to consider mitigating evidence remained tied to answering deliberateness and future-danger questions; it did not authorize a lesser sentence when both answers remained yes. Penry distinguishes admitting evidence from allowing its mitigating force, and Boyde requires a reasonable likelihood of preclusion in the charge and trial as a whole. That standard is satisfied, rather than merely conceivably implicated, on this record. Jurek’s conditional general approval remains, but cannot displace Graham’s effective-consideration rule. No categorical age prohibition, favorable evidentiary weight or unrestricted mercy instruction is required.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Graham v. Collins, January 25, 1993 — independent youth-culpability holding applied as binding merits law.
- Eddings, 455 U.S.104 — immaturity’s independent culpability significance applied.
- Lockett, 438 U.S.586 — material mitigation cannot be legally excluded from effect.
- Penry, 492 U.S.302 — admission distinguished from meaningful sentencing effect.
- Boyde, 494 U.S.370 — reasonable likelihood, not mere possibility, applied.
- Jurek, 428 U.S.262 — conditional facial approval preserved; this application fails.

##### Direct review permits the necessary sentence correction

**Controlling proposition:** Because this is direct review of the preserved mitigation claim, Teague does not bar relief. The death sentence must be reversed for an effective sentencing vehicle permitting the material youth mitigation to affect punishment without a false answer to a factual special issue; Texas may provide adequate capital resentencing or a lawful noncapital sentence, while the guilt judgment remains intact.

**Authority:** O’Connor’s Opinion of the Court, supported on this holding by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O’Connor, Souter, controls by a majority of 5 Justices.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the remedy must correct the defective sentencing mechanism without adjudicating guilt anew. Teague governs collateral use of new procedural rules, not a case still on direct review. Moreover, Graham held that the relevant effective-mitigation duty was established before its own finality date; that settled duty supplies the governing rule here. The defect here concerns what the jury was legally able to do with supported mitigation, not a judicial determination that youth necessarily outweighs aggravation. A lawful instruction or special issue can permit the relevant judgment without requiring jurors to deny deliberateness or predicted danger they actually find. The State retains authority to structure its sentencing process consistently with that duty. The Court neither prescribes exact future wording nor automatically selects life imprisonment regardless of state law. It reverses only the judgment sustaining death and remands for a constitutionally adequate capital proceeding or a lawful noncapital sentence.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Teague, 489 U.S.288 — collateral-review restriction inapplicable to this direct appeal.
- Graham, January 25, 1993 — merits and remedy principles followed; collateral-review limitations do not bar this direct appeal.
- Jurek, 428 U.S.262 — authority to structure sentencing retained subject to effective mitigation.

#### Precedent Treatment and Current-Law Effect

The decision applies and confirms Graham’s effective-mitigation rule on direct review of a nineteen-year-old’s concrete youth evidence. No categorical age bar, facial invalidation or right to favorable mitigation weight. The conviction survives.

#### Separate Positions Relevant to the Decision

Kennedy, joined by White, Scalia and Thomas, dissents. Youth’s transience permits mitigating effect through future dangerousness, and the instruction to consider all evidence gives an adequate route in their view. They acknowledge that the Court’s Graham holding rejects the premise that partial relevance always suffices; they would restrict or reconsider that holding while acknowledging its present binding force. They distinguish Penry’s disabling evidence and object to requiring an additional route whenever mitigation has independent culpability significance. They do not invoke Teague to bar a direct appeal.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, separately dissents. Their objection concerns the coherence of Penry’s mitigation requirement with Furman’s demand for structured capital discretion. Thomas’s published Graham position supplies the same objection. They would reconsider that expansion, but neither their view nor the four-Justice principal dissent changes the governing rule.
