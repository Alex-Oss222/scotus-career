## 65. City of Ladue v. Gilleo

The existing packet's identity, date, amended-complaint posture, lower judgment, and description of the window sign are corroborated. The following details make its reference to ten exemptions concrete.

Gilleo initially put a 24-by-36-inch sign on her lawn on December 8, 1990, asking opposition to war in the Persian Gulf and contact with Congress. After interference with the signs, the police told her the existing ordinance prohibited them; the city council denied a variance. After a preliminary injunction she placed an 8.5-by-11-inch peace sign in a second-story window. Ladue repealed the prior ordinance and enacted a replacement. The replacement removed the variance provision and grandfathered signs already lawfully in place. The amended complaint challenges that replacement. These are successive ordinances, not a still-available discretionary variance under the challenged text. Source: 512 U.S. 45–47 and nn. 3–6.

Section 35-2 prohibits erecting or maintaining a sign except in conformity with the ordinance; §35-3 authorizes removal of nonconforming signs. Section 35-1 defines a sign as a name, word, letter, writing, identification, description, or illustration on a building, structure, or land that publicizes an object, product, place, activity, opinion, person, institution, organization, or place of business, or advertises or promotes any person's interests. It expressly includes banners, pennants, insignia, bulletin boards, ground signs, billboards, poster billboards, illuminated signs, projecting signs, temporary signs, marquees, roof signs, yard signs, electric signs, wall signs, and window signs, placed outdoors in public view or indoors as a window sign. This is a description of the enacted definition, not a finding about every possible application.

The exemption catalog, with special size limitations, is: municipal signs; subdivision and residence identification signs; road and driveway signs for danger, direction, or identification; health-inspection signs; signs for churches, religious institutions, and schools subject to §35-5; identification signs for other nonprofit organizations; signs identifying public-transportation stops; ground signs advertising real-property sale or rental; commercial signs in commercial or industrial districts subject to the other ordinance restrictions; and signs identifying safety hazards. Residence identification is limited to one square foot. The real-property provision also addresses exchange, identifies the owner or agent, and prohibits attachment to a tree, fence, or utility pole; its permitted content is the proposed sale or rental and the seller's or agent's name and address or telephone number. On-site filling-station signs have §35-6 restrictions. The report does not reproduce every numerical size or number restriction for every category; no missing limit is inferred. Source: 512 U.S. 46–47 and n.6, reproducing ordinance §§35-4 to 35-6 and 35-10.

The city identifies aesthetics, traffic and pedestrian safety, property values, privacy, and avoidance of proliferation as purposes. It argues that the exempt categories are naturally limited in number or serve special public needs. Its proposed alternative channels include hand-held signs, letters, handbills, flyers, telephone calls, newspaper advertisements, bumper stickers, speeches, meetings, and rectangular flags; its counsel represented that flags bearing messages were not restricted although banners and pennants were. Source: 512 U.S. 47, 52, 56–57 n.16 (reported submissions only).

The Eighth Circuit's February 22, 1993 judgment, 986 F.2d 1180, independently confirms the permanent injunction and its content-discrimination rationale. It also rejected the city's proposed six-square-foot backup provision because neither side argued its constitutionality to the district court. The appeal separately reduced the fee award by removing a contingency enhancement. Those fee proceedings are background; the supplied Supreme Court merits question concerns the sign ordinance, and this addendum does not expand it. The lower report gives final-injunction citation 774 F.Supp. 1564; 774 F.Supp. 1559 concerns the preliminary proceedings. The differing citations should not erase the successive stages.

Sources: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512043/usrep512043.pdf), [complete judgment below](https://static.case.law/f2d/986/html/1180-01.html). The ordinance appendix itself was not separately obtained. If a proposed disposition depends on an omitted category's precise size limit, the actual ordinance appendix is needed.

## 66. O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC

The existing packet correctly distinguishes FSLIC's conservatorship in February 1986, receivership in June 1988, and FDIC substitution under FIRREA in 1989. The official report uses simplified receiver/FDIC terminology in its narrative but supplies those exact distinctions in 512 U.S. 82 n.1. No chronology correction to the packet is required.

ADSB's ownership was 96% Sahni and 4% Day; they were chairman/CEO and president. O'Melveny worked on the Wells Park and Gateway Center syndications, which closed December 31, 1985. Professional negligence and fiduciary-duty claims are California claims. The May 15, 1990 district-court judgment gave no substantive explanation beyond entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. The Ninth Circuit judgment is June 29, 1992, 969 F.2d 744. Source: 512 U.S. 81–83; 969 F.2d 746–748.

The lower opinion identifies material issues that the compressed packet does not spell out. The parties disputed the precise scope of the firm's engagement as tax or securities counsel and the extent to which the offering memoranda represented ADSB's financial and regulatory soundness. Stipulated or uncontroverted misconduct by insiders is not a finding that every professional-negligence element was proved. The firm's additional defenses included extinguishment of assigned investor claims by repayment and the California statute of frauds. The lower opinion discusses a duty-of-care question, corporate imputation, receiver-specific equitable defenses, and potential damages separately. Source: 969 F.2d 746–752 and nn.1–3.

The lower opinion expressly says the FDIC was **not seeking reimbursement of the rescission payments to investors**, which an ADSB subsidiary underwrote. It describes the requested client-loss measure as properly attributable out-of-pocket costs, including fee relief, settlement costs, brokers' commissions, and documented losses on property purchased through the offerings. This is the lower court's description of the action and potential damages, not an award and not a Supreme Court remedy. The parties also disagree over California's substantive imputation rules. Source: 969 F.2d 752; 512 U.S. 89.

The quoted succession clause, 12 U.S.C. §1821(d)(2)(A)(i), states that the FDIC succeeds by operation of law to the insured institution's rights, titles, powers, and privileges. FIRREA contains separate provisions governing the limitations period, certain repudiated contracts, officer/director gross negligence, and specified agreements. They are distinct statutory subjects, not interchangeable exceptions to the imputation issue. The FDIC's brief was willing to assume FIRREA applied in time; the 1986/1988 history does not itself establish that assumption. The full statutory text and a retroactivity determination are not supplied by this fact addendum. Source: 512 U.S. 85–87.

Sources: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512079/usrep512079.pdf), [complete judgment below](https://static.case.law/f2d/969/html/0744-01.html). Lower-report caption spelling is “Meyers”; the official Supreme Court caption is “Myers.” No actual finding of professional liability, damages, or California adverse-interest exception follows from this source review.

## 67. Romano v. Oklahoma

The supplied limited question is reproduced in the official report, 512 U.S. 6. It concerns disclosure of an existing death sentence during the Sarfaty penalty proceeding. It does not reopen the separate Thompson case or every issue resolved in the much longer Oklahoma appeal.

At the Sarfaty sentencing, the State offered four aggravators: prior conviction of a felony involving use or threat of violence; especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel murder; murder to avoid or prevent lawful arrest or prosecution; and probability of future violent criminal acts constituting a continuing threat to society. The jury found all four. The instructions listed 17 proposed mitigating circumstances and required unanimity on at least one aggravator proved beyond a reasonable doubt and on aggravation outweighing mitigation. The lower court distinguished proof beyond a reasonable doubt of an aggravating fact from the weighing process; the latter was not assigned that proof standard. Source: 512 U.S. 3–5; 847 P.2d 386, 391–392.

Defense counsel offered to stipulate to the Thompson conviction while objecting to revealing the death sentence. The document disclosed both the sentence and an intention to appeal; the trial court told the jury the conviction was on appeal and not final. The offered form also commanded execution. The record therefore contains both the sentence disclosure and the appeal information; neither should be omitted. Source: 512 U.S. 4–5, 17, 24 n.6.

The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals' January 13, 1993 opinion, rehearing denied March 17, 1993, is 847 P.2d 368. It held the death sentence evidence irrelevant, rejected the responsibility/due-process objection in light of the charge, and separately dealt with the Thompson conviction's intervening reversal. It removed the prior-violent-felony aggravator and reweighed the remaining three against mitigation before sustaining the Sarfaty death sentence. It continued to regard underlying Thompson conduct as relevant to future danger because the reversal was not for evidentiary insufficiency. These are discrete lower-court acts and grounds. The present Supreme Court inquiry cannot be described as reviewing an unreduced four-aggravator judgment. Source: 847 P.2d 389–394; 512 U.S. 11.

The earlier Thompson conviction was reversed because Romano should have been tried separately from his codefendant. His subsequent reconviction and death sentence were reported in his brief; the State did not contend those subsequent events changed the limited question. They were not information known by the original Sarfaty jury. Source: 512 U.S. 5 n.2, 18 n.3.

Sources: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512001/usrep512001.pdf), [complete judgment below](https://static.case.law/p2d/847/html/0368-01.html). The full lower opinion, including matters beyond the limited question, was inspected for posture and alternative grounds. It is not authority to expand the review. The original trial transcript and actual instructions were not independently obtained; the report reproductions support the foregoing particulars.

## 68. Ibanez v. Florida Board of Accountancy

The active Florida CPA license, authorized private CFP credential, unchanged credential status, communications, argument date, and jurisdictional concession are corroborated. The advertising placed “CPA CFP” next to Ibanez's name without identifying the private certifying organization. The directory listing was under attorneys; stationery described law offices. The Board initiated investigation after an anonymous mailing of the listing, not a demonstrated complaint from a deceived customer. Its three witnesses were current or former departmental employees. Source: 512 U.S. 138–145 and n.10, 150.

The administrative chronology matters: an unlicensed-firm charge was withdrawn before the hearing closed; the hearing officer recommended dismissal for lack of proof on January 15, 1992; the Board rejected that recommendation and reprimanded Ibanez on May 12, 1992. The First District affirmed without opinion, reported at 621 So.2d 435 (1993); that form left no appeal of right to the Florida Supreme Court. A reasoned First District opinion is not missing from an otherwise available published rationale. Source: 512 U.S. 140–142.

Three distinct Board restrictions appear in the sources:

- Rule 61H1-24.001(1)(g), as quoted in 512 U.S. 140 n.5, treats advertising as fraudulent, false, deceptive, or misleading if it states or implies formal recognition as a public-accountancy specialist unless the recognizing agency is Board-approved. The CFP Board of Standards was not Board-approved. This was the cited specialty-recognition charge.
- Rule 61H1-24.001(1)(i), quoted at 147 n.11 and 151, prohibits a form of recognition by an entity other than the Board using “certified.” This is a distinct prohibition; it does not supply an exception merely because a disclaimer is furnished under paragraph (j).
- Rule 61H1-24.001(1)(j), quoted at 146 and 152, addresses a statement or implication of formal specialty recognition and requires a disclaimer saying the recognizing agency is not affiliated with or sanctioned by state or federal government, plus the agency's recognition requirements, including but not limited to education, experience, and testing. These statements must appear in immediate proximity to the specialty statement. A short generic disclaimer cannot be substituted for the actual requirement.

The Board's CPA theory depended on a claimed representation of regulatory submission; its CFP theory concerned private certification and suggested government recognition. Florida's Title Act allows non-CPAs to perform the underlying nonattest services, while restricting the CPA title and attest function to licensees; licensed CPA practice also carries firm-ownership and remuneration restrictions. Ibanez said she performed no attest function. Withdrawal of the unlicensed-firm count must not be represented as a finding of misconduct under that count. Source: 512 U.S. 139–144 and nn.2–8.

The CFP amicus described educational, examination, experience, ethics, and continuing-education standards. These are reported amicus representations, not a separately inspected certification record. The Florida Bar supported use of the credentials in the law practice. Source: 512 U.S. 147–148.

Source: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512136/usrep512136.pdf). The full Board order, original rule publication/effective dates, and appellate judgment sheet were not separately recovered. The source quotes the rules in the 1994 codification and describes the actual charges; a proposed decision relying on a disputed amendment/effective-date issue would require those underlying materials.

## 69. Livadas v. Bradshaw

The existing packet's dates and undisputed wage amount are corroborated. Livadas was discharged January 2, 1990, received the full wage check January 5, filed with DLSE January 9, and received the refusal letter dated February 7. She challenged the termination separately through the contractual grievance procedure and obtained reinstatement with backpay. The parties disputed that recovery's effect on the state penalty. Neither party initiated a grievance over the penalty itself. Source: 512 U.S. 110–115 and n.8.

Keep these distinct statutory terms together:

- Labor Code §201: “If an employer discharges an employee, the wages earned and unpaid at the time of discharge are due and payable immediately.”
- Section 203 applies when an employer **willfully fails** to pay the required wages; wages continue as a penalty at the same rate from the due date **until paid or an action is commenced, but not beyond 30 days**. Three days were requested here. A penalty is not automatic from any delay regardless of willfulness.
- Section 219 forbids private written, oral, or implied agreements contravening or setting aside these wage-payment protections. Employers remain free to pay more frequently, in larger amounts, or earlier. The separate §204.2 provision permits designated executive, administrative, and professional employees to bargain for different pay periods; the separate §227.3 vacation-pay provision allows different terms by collective agreement. Those are not exceptions silently inserted into Livadas's own §201/203 claim.
- Section 229: “Actions to enforce the provisions of this article [Labor Code §§200–243] for the collection of due and unpaid wages claimed by an individual may be maintained without regard to the existence of any private agreement to arbitrate. This section shall not apply to claims involving any dispute concerning the interpretation or application of any collective bargaining agreement containing such an arbitration agreement.” This is statutory text; the broader automatic refusal based on consulting the wage rate was the Commissioner's interpretation.

Sources for these terms: 512 U.S. 111 nn.2–3, 112 n.4, 113 n.6, 128; 987 F.2d 554, 557. Section 203 is paraphrased only to combine the quoted operative conditions; the unrelated provisions are identified separately.

The contract's §18.8 independently permits either party to submit a direct wage claim not involving interpretation of the agreement either to its grievance procedure or to another authorized tribunal or agency. The general arbitration clause covered interpretation/application disputes, including wrongful discharge. The wage-rate calculation and the separate discharge grievance must not be conflated.

The Commissioner can bring court proceedings under §98.3(a) or use administrative hearings under §§98(a), 98.1. Section 98.3(a) addresses claimants whom the Commissioner judges financially unable to employ counsel and claims judged valid and enforceable. The challenged policy operated across both enforcement routes; it does not prove every individual claimant is otherwise eligible. Section 218 preserves individual suit, and §218.5 permits prevailing-party fees. Source: 512 U.S. 112 n.5; 987 F.2d 554.

The complaint named Aubry **in official capacity**; Bradshaw succeeded him. Livadas requested a declaration, prospective injunction, and processing of her claim or damages if state time limits prevented processing. In the Court of Appeals she acknowledged likely Eleventh Amendment difficulty with the monetary relief awarded against the official. That official-capacity constraint is separate from any hypothetical individual immunity analysis. Source: 987 F.2d 554–555; 512 U.S. 110 n.1, 114–115 n.9.

The Ninth Circuit opinion was initially filed September 11, 1991 and amended on denial of rehearing March 8, 1993; 987 F.2d 552 is the amended text. The majority recognized a §1983 channel for an actual NLRA-right deprivation but treated this as erroneous state-law eligibility interpretation; Judge Kozinski dissented on the discrimination and actual preemption questions. Both the majority and dissent are independently verified. The original decision date matters if a historical chronology is stated; “1993 judgment” alone suppresses that history.

Sources: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512107/usrep512107.pdf), [complete amended judgment below](https://static.case.law/f2d/987/html/0552-01.html). No employer-liability or damages judgment follows from this extraction.

## 70. United States v. Carlton

The transaction dates and rounded figures in the packet are corroborated. Day died September 29, 1985; her extended return deadline was December 29, 1986. Carlton purchased 1.5 million MCI shares December 10 for $11,206,000 and sold them December 12 for $10,575,000, losing $631,000. The MCI ESOP had expressed interest but had no binding purchase agreement when the estate bought the stock; the estate bore interim market risk. The Government stipulated to specific reliance and that the estate qualified under the original text. The parties narrowed the refund action so the constitutionality of applying the ownership amendment resolved entitlement. Contract Clause and Takings theories rejected below were not pursued in the Ninth Circuit appeal. Source: 972 F.2d 1053–1055.

The original §2057 deducted 50% of qualified proceeds from a qualifying executor's sale of employer securities to a qualifying ESOP or eligible worker-owned cooperative. Proceeds had to be received before the estate-tax return deadline including extensions. The original text excluded securities received by the decedent in a qualifying tax-exempt-plan distribution or under specified stock-option/right provisions. It required a verified statement from the covered employer or authorized cooperative officer consenting to §4979A's application, used §409(l)'s employer-securities definition, and stated a December 31, 1991 sale cutoff. These conditions remained distinct from the ownership amendment. Source: 972 F.2d 1062 n.1, which reproduces the statute but elides some ESOP language; no claim is made that this addendum reproduces the full original Act.

The material new §2057(d), added by Pub. L. 100-203 §10411(a), requires both that **the decedent directly owned the securities immediately before death** and that after sale the securities be allocated to participants or held for future allocation in connection with an exempt loan under §4975 or a transfer of assets under §4980(c)(3). For the allocation condition, substitution for other securities already allocated or held for future allocation does not count, **except in a bona fide business transaction, such as substitution in a merger of employers**. Section 10411(b) expressly makes the amendment effective as if included in §1172 of the Tax Reform Act of 1986. Source: 972 F.2d 1062 n.2, reproducing the complete amending provision; 512 U.S. 29 and n.3. Allocation was not a disputed qualification here; the estate conceded it failed the newly imposed ownership requirement.

The IRS advance notice was January 5, 1987, formally published January 26; corrective bills were introduced February 26, 1987; enactment was December 22. The original deduction became law October 22, 1986, after the 99th Congress's October 18 adjournment. The parties stipulated no ownership-condition bill or resolution was introduced between passage of the 1986 Act and adjournment. Later legislative explanations characterize original intent; they are not earlier enacted ownership language. Source: 972 F.2d 1053–1055; 512 U.S. 28–29.

The Ninth Circuit's August 10, 1992 decision, 972 F.2d 1051, reversed and directed judgment for the executor, emphasizing notice and detrimental reliance; Judge Norris dissented, emphasizing deferential retroactivity review, the nature of the tax change, and the limited interval. These lower grounds and the dispute over legislative purpose are independently checked.

The official report gives a claimed deduction of $5,287,000 at 512 U.S. 28; the lower opinion states $5,287,500 at 972 F.2d 1054, which equals one-half of the undisputed sale proceeds. The packet uses rounded reduction figures, so this discrepancy does not contradict it. If an exact deduction is printed later, identify the lower stipulated figure and do not silently attribute it to the inconsistent official-report number. The tax reduction attributable to the claimed deduction is $2,501,161 in both sources.

Sources: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512026/usrep512026.pdf), [complete judgment below and statutory reproductions](https://static.case.law/f2d/972/html/1051-01.html). No source in this addendum determines the constitutional result.

## 71. Department of Taxation and Finance v. Milhelm Attea

The packet's facial posture and regulatory outline are corroborated. New York Tax Law §471(1) taxes cigarettes possessed in the State except those the State is without power to tax; §471(2) places ultimate incidence and liability on the consumer. Licensed agents buy and affix stamps before the first sale. The report's then-current rate is 56 cents per pack. The audit affidavit's ratios were 20 times statewide per-capita consumption in 1987–1988 and 32 times in 1988–1989 if all untaxed deliveries were consumed by exempt Indians. They are the State's evidence supporting the asserted collection problem, not individualized adjudications against all purchasers. Source: 512 U.S. 64–65.

The 1992 regulatory text reproduced in the report uses 20 N.Y.C.R.R. §§336.6–336.7. A qualified Indian consumer is an enrolled member of an exempt New York nation or tribe buying within a qualified reservation for that individual's own use or consumption **within that reservation**, not resale. Untaxed wholesale deliveries require a valid exemption certificate, intention to distribute to exempt consumers, and delivery on the reservation. A buyer displays its certificate on first purchase and signs subsequent invoices; a consumer supplies individual-exemption certification at first purchase and identity evidence later. These are different obligations for different stages. Source: 512 U.S. 65–67 and nn.5–6.

A tribe may enter a regulatory agreement under §336.7(c)(1), with tribal approval for deliveries. Without such an agreement, probable demand is calculated by the Department. If the tribe regulates, licenses, or controls cigarette distribution, the Department uses submitted demand evidence; otherwise it multiplies New York per-capita consumption by enrollment. Trade territory is determined in consultation with a regulating tribe, otherwise from the Department's available information. Purchase approval depends on valid orders and quantities reasonably related to exempt demand; monthly coupons implement the allotment. The Department may withhold delivery approval from past or current violators and cancel noncompliers' certificates. Wholesalers must be state-licensed agents, precollect taxes on nonexempt sales, keep buyer-specific untaxed-sale records, and report monthly. The scheme places no numerical quota on stamped-cigarette sales. Source: 512 U.S. 65–67 and nn.3–6.

The principally invoked federal text is 25 U.S.C. §261: “The Commissioner of Indian Affairs shall have the sole power and authority to appoint traders to the Indian tribes and to make such rules and regulations as he may deem just and proper specifying the kind and quantity of goods and the prices at which such goods shall be sold to the Indians.” Sections 262–264 concern trading fitness/permission, presidential exclusion/revocation authority, and penalties. Source: 512 U.S. 70 and n.7. The latter provisions' complete text is not reproduced here; a theory depending on one of their particular qualifications needs that text.

The June 10, 1993 New York Court of Appeals judgment, 81 N.Y.2d 417, both read federal trader law as preventing additional state burdens on wholesale trade to Indians and alternatively found these burdens significant. It reversed the Appellate Division, granted the plaintiffs summary judgment, and denied the State's cross-motions. That alternative ground must be preserved. The lower opinion refers to an earlier historical Supreme Court remand; the source verifies lower-court procedural history but does not itself authorize importing that Court action into the simulated timeline.

The wholesalers contested authority to impose probable-demand limits rather than the accuracy of the initial calculations. The United States raised state certification, trade-territory, and allocation concerns. A Seneca Nation amicus raised treaty objections different from the wholesalers' position; the New York Court of Appeals did not adjudicate those treaty objections. Sources: 512 U.S. 69, 77 n.11. An actual inadequate quota or withheld delivery is not established by the unimplemented facial record.

Sources: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512061/usrep512061.pdf), [complete lower opinion at Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/nyctap/081_0417.htm). CAP attempts did not retrieve this lower opinion; Cornell's text is labeled uncorrected. Its regulatory references use §335 while the official Supreme Court report uses §336; the described mechanisms agree, but the original codification was not independently recovered. Preserve the official report's identified edition and flag any provision-specific effective-date dispute rather than silently harmonizing numbering.

## 72. Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co.

The packet's argument date, vessel-negligence channel, limited claim, and summary-judgment posture are corroborated. The Third Circuit's 998 F.2d 1003 entry is an affirmance **without opinion**. The review record therefore contains the district court's reasons, not an undiscovered reasoned appellate explanation. Source: 512 U.S. 94–96.

Howlett was unloading bags of cocoa beans from the MV Presidente Ibanez at Philadelphia for Northern Shipping. Removal of a draft exposed an eight-square-foot tween-deck area. He jumped approximately three feet from surrounding bags and slipped on clear plastic. He claimed dirt and debris concealed it. The parties agreed paper and plywood were customary condensation protection and that plastic was improper for that purpose because it aggravated condensation. Evidence indicated the independent Guayaquil loading stevedore placed it; the vessel supplied plastic along with paper, plywood, and dunnage. Crew presence on the upper deck during loading was part of the record. Source: 512 U.S. 94–95.

The district court required actual vessel knowledge and a nonobvious hazard, declined to infer knowledge from supplying materials or crew presence, and alternatively reasoned that a condition observable to the crew in loading would be open and obvious. The different loading and discharge settings are record facts, not interchangeable viewpoints. Howlett's own witnesses also described visibility of the plastic during discharge, including from the upper deck. The packet's statement that perception is disputed should be read with those adverse submissions, not as an uncontested concealment finding. Source: 512 U.S. 95, 105–106 (lower reasons and testimony only).

Howlett confined the claim to the turnover duty to warn. He advanced Restatement (Second) of Torts §412 as support for reasonable, not continuous, inspections during and after an independent contractor's work. The competing circuit approaches were Derr v. Kawasaki Kisen K.K., 835 F.2d 490 (3d Cir. 1987), and Turner v. Japan Lines, Ltd., 651 F.2d 1300 (9th Cir. 1981). These identify the predecision dispute; this addendum does not choose between them. Source: 512 U.S. 95–96, 99–100.

The material §905(b) text authorizes an injured covered person's negligence action against the vessel as a third party, bars direct or indirect employer liability to the vessel for those damages and contrary agreements or warranties, and states that vessel liability under the subsection shall not be based on the warranty of seaworthiness or its breach. This quoted portion is not the full statute or a statement that no other statutory qualification exists. Here the alleged independent stevedore negligence and the vessel's alleged warning omission must remain distinct. Source: 512 U.S. 96–97. The separately supplied Scindia source is needed for its actual duty formulation and contract, positive-law, and custom qualifications; no same-matter Howlett formulation is imported as entering law.

Source: [official report](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep512/usrep512092/usrep512092.pdf). A guessed CAP URL did not retrieve the appellate table entry; its opinionless form is independently established by the official report. The original district-court order and depositions were not separately recovered. If a proposed disposition resolves actual knowledge or discharge visibility as an undisputed matter, the full summary-judgment record remains necessary; the reported competing submissions cannot establish such a finding.
