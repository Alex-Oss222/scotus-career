## Farmer v. Brennan — No. 92-7247

**Source:** [Official report, 511 U.S. 825](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511825/usrep511825.pdf), factual and procedural account at 829–832 and reported record/party submissions at 848–851. Oral argument January 12, 1994; the reviewed Seventh Circuit action was a summary affirmance without an opinion. The official report identifies no case-specific nonparticipation; use the simulated OT1993 roster.

- Farmer sought compensatory and punitive damages and an injunction against future confinement in any penitentiary. An additional request for a co-correctional facility was apparently abandoned after the Bureau ceased operating such facilities. Do not restore that request without a source.
- The USP–Terre Haute warden and the Bureau of Prisons Director were sued **only in official capacities**. The FCI–Oxford warden and case manager and the North Central Regional Director and regional official were sued in official and personal capacities. The report's grouping matters to the separate damages and prospective channels.
- The March 9, 1989 transfer was for disciplinary reasons. Segregation in Farmer's history included both disciplinary segregation and at least one safety-based placement. The record did not establish the exact 1989 security designations of Oxford and Terre Haute. Farmer's precise appearance in prison was unclear, although the parties agreed that Farmer projected feminine characteristics. Alleged assault and each defendant's knowledge remain allegations or disputed matters, not completed findings.
- Officials moved for summary judgment and invoked qualified immunity; Farmer submitted an opposing affidavit, a cross-motion, and an event-date Rule 56(f) request for documents. Officials also sought a protective order staying discovery. The district court denied Farmer's discovery request and entered merits summary judgment without ruling on that protective-order request. It cited the absence of an express safety complaint as the basis for finding no knowledge. A quotation of that rationale describes the lower ruling rather than establishing the truth of the asserted lack of knowledge.
- The requested discovery concerned the institution's history of sexual assault and violence and individual officials' knowledge. An affidavit reported later segregation directed by the regional office at Oxford staff's request. Those statements are evidence in the record, not a determination that every transferring official controlled every placement decision.
- The officials' initial prospective-relief argument relied on administrative detention. At argument the Government reported that Farmer was then in the general population of a medium-security prison. Its argument about no present transfer threat depends on the current-risk facts; continued administrative segregation is not an established present fact.
- Scope verification: the report describes the parties as accepting deliberate indifference as the governing inquiry and contains no party request to overrule *Wilson* or *Estelle*. The cited earlier foundations remain *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97 (1976), *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294 (1991), and *Hudson v. Palmer*, 468 U.S. 517 (1984), subject to the effective simulated *Helling* and *Hudson* records. The alternative formulations of recklessness remain questions, not supplied answers.

**Source limit:** The lower merits judgment is summarized in the official report; no separate reasoned Seventh Circuit opinion exists in that account. The precise current risk, necessary discovery, defendant-specific knowledge, immunity, and appropriate relief have not been found by this source supplement.

## Department of Revenue of Montana v. Kurth Ranch — No. 93-144

**Sources:** [Official report, 511 U.S. 767](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511767/usrep511767.pdf), 770–776 for statute and proceedings; [Ninth Circuit, 986 F.2d 1308](https://static.case.law/f2d/986/html/1308-01.html), February 26, 1993; [bankruptcy judgment, 145 B.R. 61](https://static.case.law/br/145/html/0061-01.html), May 8, 1990, especially 64–68, 72, and 74–76. Oral argument January 19, 1994. The official report identifies no case-specific nonparticipation.

- The assessed law was the **1987** Montana Dangerous Drug Tax Act, effective October 1, 1987. Amendments in 1989 and 1991 do not substitute their text for this assessment. The raid and arrest were October 18, 1987; criminal sentences were entered July 18, 1988; Chapter 11 proceedings began in September 1988. The law was already effective at the raid. Administrative rules were promulgated in November 1987; the cited printed rule version is 1988.
- Section 15-25-111(1) imposes a tax on possession and storage of dangerous drugs and makes each person possessing or storing them liable. Under subsection (2), the marijuana amount is the **greater of** 10% of assessed market value, as determined by the Department, or $100 per ounce of marijuana or its derivatives determined by aggregate weight seized. Hashish and its derivatives carry a different $250-per-ounce alternative. These rates are not interchangeable; the state-law rejection of the other drug assessments is outside the remaining federal dispute.
- Section 15-25-111(3) says the tax is to be “collected only after any state or federal fines or forfeitures have been satisfied.” Collection priority is distinct from the earlier initial assessment. Do not describe every assessment step as occurring only after the criminal sentences.
- Rule 42.34.102(1) requires a return within 72 hours of arrest. Under subsection (3), officers complete the Department's dangerous-drug information report at arrest and afford the taxpayer an opportunity to sign. If the taxpayer refuses, the officer files within 72 hours. The officer-report mechanism and taxpayer return obligation should not be collapsed into a finding that the taxpayer must personally supply the information in every case.
- Six family members entered pleas. Richard pleaded to substantive drug offenses; the other five pleaded to conspiracy. The State's contention that only Richard could raise a same-offense objection was not raised below; the Kurths answered that withdrawn possession charges and the plea agreements mattered. The source does not resolve that later contention or make conspiracy identical to possession.
- The bankruptcy judgment rejected the plant, oil, and tar calculations under state law. The surviving harvested-marijuana component is approximately $181,000 in principal. The bankruptcy table at 145 B.R. 68 gives **$181,100 for 1,811 ounces**; the official Supreme Court report repeatedly summarizes that as $181,000. The Ninth Circuit calls the surviving levy $208,105, while the bankruptcy discussion at 75 describes it as exceeding $208,150 without interest. Do not conflate principal with a total including administrative charges or penalties, or resolve the $45 discrepancy by arithmetic assumptions. An exact dollar command would require the assessment/accounting record; adjudication of the assessment's constitutional character need not fix a new dollar total.
- The original amended proof of claim is $864,940.99 after credits in the bankruptcy account; the official report gives approximately $894,940.99 when including roughly $30,000 previously collected. Neither is the narrow surviving principal component. The bankruptcy judgment also ordered remittance of sums already recovered to the trustee. That is part of the lower judgment whose treatment must be stated, not a new award by this supplement.
- The drugs were confiscated. The official report describes their subsequent destruction as presumed, not as a separately authenticated destruction date or trial finding. A precise date or method is not supplied. The $100-per-ounce assessment on the low-value shake was eight times the recorded $200-per-pound market value; this ratio does not describe every seized product.
- The Ninth Circuit's judgment was expressly as applied, with the State's refusal to provide a cost accounting central to its reasoning. The State sought judicial notice of general drug-control costs. The bankruptcy court had denied summary judgment so evidence could be presented; it found that no such cost evidence was produced at trial. A national expenditure figure is not an established case-specific cost finding.

**Date-eligible law:** *United States v. Halper*, 490 U.S. 435 (1989), including its actual-loss/remedial inquiry and Kennedy's concurrence, *Helvering v. Mitchell*, 303 U.S. 391 (1938), *United States v. Ward*, 448 U.S. 242 (1980), *United States v. Sanchez*, 340 U.S. 42 (1950), and *Marchetti v. United States*, 390 U.S. 39 (1968), are distinct earlier sources; no own-case resolution of their relationship is supplied here. The effective simulated *Dixon*, *Austin*, and *Alexander* govern their own propositions.

## Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc. — No. 93-405

**Sources:** [Official report, 511 U.S. 863](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511863/usrep511863.pdf), 865–867 for record; [Tenth Circuit, 993 F.2d 755](https://static.case.law/f2d/993/html/0755-01.html), May 17, 1993. Oral argument February 22, 1994. The official report identifies no case-specific nonparticipation.

- The confidential agreement was reached March 25, 1992. Digital agreed to pay for the trade name and trademark, waiver of damages, and dismissal; Desktop filed its dismissal that day. No exact settlement dollar amounts are supplied.
- The district order permitting withdrawal of dismissal and rescinding the settlement was entered **January 5, 1993**. Its stated finding was that a factfinder **could** find nondisclosure of material facts sufficient to cause rejection of the settlement. It did not state that a completed trial had found fraudulent inducement. The lower appellate court regarded the order as conclusive despite its cryptic wording and found conceptual separateness; those are lower-court judgments, not stipulations binding this Court's jurisdictional examination.
- The Tenth Circuit dismissed the appeal for want of jurisdiction and denied the stay request; it did not affirm settlement rescission on its merits. Digital's contention that the district court applied the wrong rescission standard is a contention, not a verified legal error. Desktop did not seriously press failure of conclusiveness or separateness as alternative grounds in the Supreme Court argument described by the report.
- Section 1291 governs appeals from final district-court decisions. Section 1292(b) is a separate route requiring a district judge's written certification of a controlling legal question, substantial ground for disagreement, and possible material advancement of termination; timely application and appellate permission remain necessary. The source does not show that such a certificate or permission had issued here. An asserted alternative route is not an existing appeal.
- Eligible earlier sources include *Cohen*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949), *Coopers & Lybrand*, 437 U.S. 463 (1978), *Lauro Lines*, 490 U.S. 495 (1989), *Van Cauwenberghe*, 486 U.S. 517 (1988), *Abney*, 431 U.S. 651 (1977), and *Mitchell v. Forsyth*, 472 U.S. 511 (1985). *Lauro Lines*' majority analysis and Scalia's separate explanation are distinct sources; no later Digital resolution is imported. The effective simulated *Puerto Rico Aqueduct* and *Kokkonen* must be used instead of their external historical texts.

## Key Tronic Corp. v. United States — No. 93-376

**Sources:** [Official report, 511 U.S. 809](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep511/usrep511809/usrep511809.pdf), 811–814 for record and quoted provisions; [Ninth Circuit, 984 F.2d 1025](https://static.case.law/f2d/984/html/1025-01.html), January 28, 1993; [district judgment, 766 F. Supp. 865](https://static.case.law/f-supp/766/html/0865-01.html), for the lower findings where identified. Oral argument March 29, 1994. The official report identifies no case-specific nonparticipation.

- Key Tronic agreed to pay $4.2 million to EPA; the Air Force separately agreed to $1.45 million and obtained §122(g)(5) contribution protection. Key Tronic's contribution claim for the $4.2 million consent obligation was dismissed after its concession that the protection barred that claim. It does not reenter through review of the fees question.
- A distinct claim concerned about $1.2 million in pre-settlement response expenses, outside the matters covered by the Air Force settlement as the district court understood it. Later settlement resolved the response-cost issues apart from attorney fees and prejudgment interest. The district fee award was $155,500 covering the three disputed work categories. The Air Force **did not appeal prejudgment interest**; the source does not reopen that award or other payments not in dispute.
- Keep the three categories distinct: identifying other responsible parties; preparing and negotiating Key Tronic's EPA agreement; and prosecuting this recovery litigation. The district court found that the search uncovered Air Force disposal activity and prompted EPA enforcement. Whether and to what extent a payment belongs to a category must follow the work actually performed, rather than the professional title alone.
- Section 107(a)(4)(B), 42 U.S.C. §9607(a)(4)(B), supplies: “any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person consistent with the national contingency plan.” The relevant contrast in subsection (A) is government, State, or Indian-tribe costs **not inconsistent** with that plan. Do not substitute subsection (A)'s phrase for subsection (B)'s requirement.
- Section 101(25), 42 U.S.C. §9601(25), reads: “The terms ‘respond’ or ‘response’ means remove, removal, remedy, and remedial action; all such terms (including the terms ‘removal’ and ‘remedial action’) include enforcement activities related thereto.” SARA added the enforcement language in 1986. The meaning of that language for this private fee claim remains disputed.
- Section 122(g)(5), as quoted in the lower opinion, protects a party who resolved its United States liability under that subsection from “claims for contribution regarding matters addressed in the settlement.” This is not a universal release of every claim concerning the site. Section 113(f)'s express contribution route, §107's costs provision, and the federal-facility provisions remain distinct.
- Congress expressly addressed fees in other CERCLA settings, including §310(f), 42 U.S.C. §9659(f), citizen suits; §106(b)(2)(E), reimbursement following an erroneous order under the stated conditions; and §110(c), 42 U.S.C. §9610(c), covered employee-discrimination proceedings. Their presence is statutory context, not a determination of the disputed implication for §107. *Alyeska*, 421 U.S. 240 (1975), and *Runyon*, 427 U.S. 160 (1976), are eligible earlier fee authorities. Whether §107's own wording supplies adequate authorization is open.
