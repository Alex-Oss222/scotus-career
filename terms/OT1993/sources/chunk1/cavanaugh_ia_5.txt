STATEMENT OF THE GROUNDS ON WHICH THE 
JURISDICTION OF THE UNITED STATES 
SUPREME COURT IS INVOLVED 
The petitioner has been granted review of the Janu- 
ary 15, 1993, opinion and judgment of the United States 
Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. The petitioner 
filed a petition for writ of certiorari with the United 
States Supreme Court on March 8, 1993, and it was 
granted on May 24, 1993. 
Jurisdiction is conferred on the United States 
Supreme Court by 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) and 28 U.S.C. 
§ 1257(8), as characterized in Supreme Court Rule 10.1(a). 
The former statute allows this Court to review, upon 
petition for writ of certiorari, cases of the courts of 
appeals, before or after judgment has been rendered. The 
latter statute allows the Court to review, also upon peti- 
tion for writ of certiorari, final judgments rendered by the 
highest court of a state in which a decision could be had, 
where the validity of a state statute is being drawn in 
question on the ground of its being repugnant to the 
Constitution. In this matter, South Carolina Code Sections 
16-1-60, 24-21-645, 24-21-650 are being drawn in question 
on the ground that their application to the Respondent 
violates the Ex Post Facto Clause. Further, this is a matter 
in which a United States court of appeals has decided a 
federal question in a way that conflicts with a state court 
of last resort. The Fourth Circuit’s decision squarely con- 
flicts with the South Carolina Supreme Court’s decision 
in Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989), on 
the ex post facto question; and on the threshold question of 
federal jurisdiction under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, it conflicts 
with this Court’s decision in Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 
475 (1973), and its line.

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The respondent, Gary Lee Roller, was convicted of 
voluntary manslaughter and grand larceny in the Chester 
County Court of General Sessions for the State of South 
Carolina. Both crimes were committed on December 13, 
1982. Roller was sentenced on March 25, 1983. (J.A. 6) On 
October 27, 1990, Roller became eligible for parole under 
the parole eligibility statute in force on December 13, 
1982, the date he committed his crimes. (J.A. 37) That 
parole eligibility date has not changed. 
When Roller committed his crimes, the procedural 
law governing the rescheduling of parole hearings fol- 
lowing a rejection set the time at one year. S.C. Code Ann. 
§ 24-21-620 (1976), as amended. (J.A. 39) Effective June 3, 
1986, the law controlling the rescheduling procedure was 
changed to require a two-year period between parole 
hearings for violent offenders. S.C. Code Ann. 
§§ 24-21-645, 24-21-650. (J.A. 40) At the same time, the 
South Carolina General Assembly enacted S.C. Code Ann. 
§ 16-1-60, which enumerated those crimes deemed to be 
violent and included in that enumeration the crime of 
voluntary manslaughter. (J.A. 41) The punishment for 
voluntary manslaughter, including parole eligibility, how- 
ever, remained exactly what it had been when Roller 
committed that crime in 1982. The South Carolina 
Supreme Court ruled in Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 113, 378 
S.E.2d 443 (1989), that the decrease in the frequency of 
parole hearings for violent offenders was a permissible 
procedural change and did not violate the Ex Post Facto 
Clause of the Constitution. 
SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 
This case raises two questions. The first question 
invites the Court to revisit Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 
475 (1973), and the problem of defining the line between 
federal jurisdiction under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 of the Civil 
Rights Act and federal Habeas Corpus. As presented for 
the Court’s review, the question is whether federal juris- 
diction for the Respondent’s challenge of a South Caro- 
lina law that decreases the frequency of his parole 
reconsideration hearings from one year to two years is 
properly founded on Section 1983; or, whether the 
Respondent’s challenge reaches the very “core of habeas 
corpus” and, as such, should require him to exhaust the 
remedies available to him in the state courts of South 
Carolina. The Fourth Circuit, adhering to the interpreta- 
tion it gave Preiser in the case of Strader v. Troy, 571 F.2d 
1263 (4th Cir. 1978), held that federal jurisdiction to hear 
the prisoner’s petition was properly founded on § 1983. 
We think the Fourth Circuit erred and should be 
reversed. The Strader interpretation of this Court’s deci- 
sion in Preiser expands federal jurisdiction under Section 
1983 in these prisoner petition cases far beyond the hold- 
ing in Preiser. In Preiser, this Court held that “when a state 
prisoner is challenging the very fact or duration of his 
physical imprisonment, and the relief he seeks is a deter- 
mination that he is entitled to immediate release or a 
speedier release from that imprisonment, his sole federal 
remedy is a writ of habeas corpus.” And a speedier 
release, we think, is just what the Respondent is seeking 
when he demands a more frequent parole review- 
whether he says it in so many words, or not. That, after 


PDF PAGE 7
all, is what his ex post facto challenge, at its core, seeks to 
achieve. 
We must look to the core of the Respondent's claim, 
not merely to the language in which he couches that 
claim. The Fourth Circuit, relying on Strader, ignores the 
core of the Respondent’s claim and sees only the lan- 
guage. In this view, the Fourth Circuit’s analysis might 
fairly be summarized as follows: The Respondent never 
comes out and says he is seeking a speedier release; 
therefore, that’s not what he’s seeking. Preiser looks 
deeper, to the bedrock of the claim. As demonstrated by 
the Fourth Circuit’s decision below, and indeed by the 
wide difference of opinion expressed by federal courts in 
every circuit when confronted with a Preiser puzzle, this 
Court, we suggest, needs once again to take the measure 
of Preiser’s depth to some fathom of greater precision. At 
bottom, Preiser was deeply concerned with the larger 
principles of federalism and comity between the federal 
government and the governments of the several states. 
The Fourth Circuit’s decision, we believe, effectively dis- 
torts these principles, under which, when viewed in 
accord with the teachings of Preiser, this case should 
never have gained entry in the federal courts. 
The second question goes to the merits of the 
Respondent’s claim: Whether or not a 1986 statutory 
amenament to the South Carolina Code, which increases 
the time between parole reconsideration hearings from 
ong to two years for offenders convicted of violent crime, 
is an insubstantial procedural change outside the pur- 
view of the Ex Post Facto Clause. The South Carolina 
Supreme Court, in Gunter v. State, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989), a 
case directly on point, held that the change presented no 
ex post facto violation. The Fourth Circuit disagreed, effec- 
tively overruling South Carolina’s highest court. 
We believe that the Fourth Circuit’s decision on the 
merits was error. The Fourth Circuit oversimplified by 
equating reconsideration for parole with eligibility for 
parole. The two are not the same, and the difference is 
absolutely crucial to a correct analysis of the ex post facto 
problem presented here. When reconsideration for parole 
is equated with parole eligibility the analysis comes to an 
end, because parole eligibility attaches to the punish- 
ment. A change in parole eligibility, applied retroactively 
to the disadvantage of the prisoner, violates the constitu- 
tional prohibition. 
But the point is irrelevant, because parole eligibility 
was never at issue here. Respondent’s parole eligibility 
has remained unchanged from the date of his conviction 
and sentencing. If we fix our attention on the relevant- 
the scheduling of parole reconsideration hearings-we 
arrive at this Court’s ex post facto analysis in Collins v. 
Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990). In Collins, the Court pur- 
posefully reached back to the “original u’  erstanding” of 
the Ex Post Facto Clause, expressed in Calder v. Bull, 3 
Dall. 386 (1798), to reject the increasingly wide-spread 
notion that any change which alters the situation of a 
party to his disadvantage violates the Clause. In place of 
this mistaken idea, the Court substituted the notion that 
the Ex Post Facto Clause “was intended to secure substan- 
tial personal rights against arbitrary and oppressive legis- 
lation.” This idea allows for a flexible case-by-case 
approach. When this approach is applied to the particular 
facts presented in this case, we believe that the change, 
though retroactive, is not one of so substantial a moment 


PDF PAGE 8
as to violate any personal rights of the Respondent 
intended to be secure against arbitrary and oppressive 
legislation. The Fourth Circuit simply avoids the real 
question altogether. 
Should the matter go to the merits, we would ask the 
Court to reverse the Fourth Circuit on the ex post facto 
question and correct this error. Should it not go to the 
merits because the Court finds jurisdiction improperly 
founded on Section 1983, we would ask for an order 
vacating the Fourth Circuit’s opinion and judgment, to 
ensure that it will have no binding effect. 
I. 
THE PRISONER’S CHALLENGE OF SOUTH CARO- 
LINA’S PAROLE RELEASE SYSTEM REACHES THE 
VERY “CORE OF HABEAS CORPUS” AND, BECAUSE 
IT DOES, FEDERAL JURISDICTION IS NOT PROPER 
UNDER 42 U.S.C. § 1983. 
Section 1983 is not available to test the application of 
the challenged parole law. The action is cognizable only 
in habeas corpus. Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475 (1973), 
states the rule that controls here: 
[W]e hold today that when a state prisoner is 
challenging the very fact or duration of his 
physical imprisonment, and the relief he seeks is 
a determination that he is entitled to immediate 
release or a speedier release from that imprison- 
ment, his sole federal remedy is a writ of habeas 
*corpus. [emphasis added] 
Id. This holding forecloses Respondent’s § 1983 action 
here. Preiser also teaches that “a § 1983 is a proper rem- 
edy for a state prisoner who is making a constitutional 
challenge to the conditions of his prison life, but not to 
the fact or length of his custody.” Id. at 499. The District 
Court relied on Strader v. Troy, 571 F.2d 1263 (4th Cir. 
1978), which incorrectly interprets Preiser. The Fourth 
Circuit held in Strader that Preiser stands for the proposi- 
tion that “§ 1983 may be used to challenge parole pro- 
cedures” only when the prisoner seeks “consideration for 
parole.” Relying thus on Strader, the District Court was 
then able to conclude that Roller’s use of § 1983 was 
proper because he “does not say he is entitled to parole.” 
But in fact that is precisely his claim-that he is being 
unlawfully restrained or confined by being denied an 
annual parole consideration hearing. He is seeking to 
shorten his confinement. Historically, this issue has 
always been addressed by habeas corpus, not § 1983. In 
order to get into the federal courts on § 1983, Roller 
strenuously denies that what he seeks is his release from 
prison. However, his claim that South Carolina has vio- 
lated the Ex Post Facto Clause of the Constitution appears 
to be predicated on either of two premises: that annual 
parole consideration hearings are a part of his punish- 
ment and that by extending the interval between hear- 
ings, his punishment has been increased; or that the 
retroactive denial of an annual parole hearing has sub- 
stantially disadvantaged him. Either premise asks the 
Court to focus on the notion that the relief he seeks in his 
complaint controls whether the claim itself lies in habeas 
or § 1983. He seeks thereby to divert attention from the 
core of his complaint, which is nothing other than a 
challenge to the duration of his physical imprisonment, a 
challenge which only habeas can address. See Del Raine v. 


PDF PAGE 9
10 
Carlson, 826 F.2d 698, 702 (7th Cir. 1987); Wilson v. Lock- 
hart, 949 F.2d 1051 (8th Cir. 1991); Alexander v. Johnson, 742 
F.2d 117 (4th Cir. 1984). 
Roller cannot have it both ways. Either he is chal- 
lenging the increase in the time between his parole hear- 
ings as constituting an ex post facto violation when 
applied to him, in which case the action lies solely in 
habeas corpus (Preiser, 411 U.S. at 500) and must be 
played out in the South Carolina state courts. Or he is not 
challenging that increase, in which case it is a mystery 
just what he might be challenging as the retroactive dis- 
advantage supposed to be substantial enough to violate 
the Ex Post Facto Clause. It is clear that the core of his ex 
post facto challenge, which attacks the retroactively 
applied decrease in the frequency of his parole consider- 
ation hearings, is a challenge of his future restraint, in 
order to obtain for himself an earlier release. To call it 
anything else is semantic subterfuge. The Fourth Circuit 
itself has said as much in cases coming after Strader and 
interpreting Preiser. Leonard v. Hammond, 804 F.2d 838 (4th 
Cir. 1986); Todd v. Baskerville, 712 F.2d 70 (4th Cir. 1983); 
Alexander v. Johnson, 742 F.2d 117 (4th Cir. 1984). The 
Eighth Circuit has well observed that t'e chance for an 
earlier parole release, or the chance that a sentence might 
be commuted to a term of years, falls within habeas 
corpus. The Eighth Circuit justifies this extension of Pre- 
iser on the grounds that a state court system should have 
the first opportunity to decide the merits of the underly- 
ing constitutional issue. Wilson v. Lockhart, 949 F.2d 1051 
(8th Cir. 1991). 
11 
Insofar as Strader ignores all this, it simply gets Pre- 
iser wrong. And in so doing, Strader needlessly com- 
pounds the problem, already “difficult enough,” Hamlin 
v. Warren, 664 F.2d 29, 33 (4th Cir. 1981), cert. denied, 455 
U.S. 911 (1982), confronting state and federal courts alike: 
How to sort through the frustrating conflict between the 
civil rights and habeas corpus remedies for state pris- 
oners. The heart of this conflict and, more important, how 
we finally resolve the conflict, implicates our deepest 
concern for and understanding of certain fundamental 
notions of federalism and all that that bedrock principle 
entails in the way of comity between the federal govern- 
ment and the several states. To rely on Strader, we 
believe, undermines federalism and upsets the delicate 
balance which comity helps to keep. Braden v. 30th Judicial 
Circuit of Kentucky, 410 U.S. 484 (1973) (exhaustion of 
state-court remedies ensures comity between federal and 
state courts). For, what Strader suggests is that South 
Carolina’s right to exercise concurrent jurisdiction with 
the federal courts in deciding questions of federal consti- 
tutional law need not be accorded the full respect that 
federalism says is its due, and may in fact be ignored 
altogether whenever a state prisoner, crafty enough to 
capitalize on the confusion now troubling the federal 
courts, renames his habeas corpus petition a § 1983 claim 
for relief. 
The Court should also recognize that there is a fur- 
ther obstacle in the limitations on suits against the states 
imposed by the Eleventh Amendment. As this Court 
stated in the leading case of Will v. Michigan Dept. of State 
Police, 491 U.S. 58, 65 (1989), “Section 1983 provides a 


PDF PAGE 10
12 
federal forum to remedy many deprivations of civil liber- 
ties, but it does not provide a federal forum for litigants 
who seek a remedy against a State for alleged depriva- 
tions of civil liberties.” The Eleventh Amendment, as the 
Court put it, “bars such suits unless the State has waived 
its immunity.” Id. And South Carolina, we would assure 
the Court, has never waived its immunity here. Further, 
although Will suggests that an action against a state offi- 
cial in his official capacity for prospective injunctive relief 
under § 1983 is proper because such an action would not 
be treated as an action against the state, the suggestion 
doesn’t fit the case here. Id. at 2311, n.10. What Roller is 
challenging is not the official capacity action of a state 
official; what he is challenging is the system South Caro- 
lina has set up for reviewing paroles after a rejection. The 
difficulty in relying on Strader is thus complicated. And 
the only way out for the Court, we would suggest, is for 
the Court to overrule the Fourth Circuit’s reliance on 
Strader in favor of the lucid reasoning expressed in Preiser 
and well followed by the Fourth Circuit in Todd and 
Leonard. 
The analysis by which cases are included within or 
excluded from § 1983 is difficult at best. Focusing on the 
distinction between the nature of the complaint and the 
nature of the relief is essential. This focus does not, 
however, always yield a bright-line test. A finer focus 
may be necessary. If the state has provided a specific legal 
remedy to address problems arising out of specific issues 
such as parole, then exhaustion should be required in all 
cases where the inmate is seeking release or earlier 
release from confinement. If a state has not provided a 
13 
remedy, the debate goes on. This Court has often empha- 
sized that the states should be allowed to experiment and 
develop their own procedures. See Gerstein v. Pugh, 420 
U.S. 103, 123 (1987); Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 
(1974); Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correc- 
tional Complex, 442 U.S. 1 (1979). Finally, it is crucial for 
the Court to remember that South Carolina has in place 
adequate state-law remedies-the Uniform Post-Convic- 
tion Procedure Act, S.C. Code Ann. § 17-27-10 et seq. and 
the Tort Claims Act, S.C. Code Ann. § 15-78-10 et seq.—that 
have always been available to Roller. He need only have 
availed himself of those remedies, which he has chosen 
not to do. Instead, Roller resorts to the Civil Rights stat- 
ute in order to circumvent the exhaustion requirements of 
the habeas corpus statute. For this Court to countenance 
such highjinks even for a moment would return us to the 
state of affairs that existed in 1971, before Preiser dealt 
with the problem. It was a state of affairs that led one 
judge of the Second Circuit to lament, “[s]tate prisoners 
are increasingly resorting to the Civil Rights statutes in 
order to circumvent the [exhaustion] requirements of [the 
habeas corpus statute].” Rodriguez v. McGinnis, 451 F.2d 
730 (2d Cir. 1971). The situation, of course, was later 
corrected when the Court reversed in Preiser. The reason- 
ing that should guide the Court’s deliberations here is 
first stated in Preiser. Certainly the Fourth Circuit deci- 
sion does serious harm to federal-state comity. South 
Carolina therefore requests that this Court reverse the 
Fourth Circuit and rule that a challenge to the denial of 
annual parole consideration hearings can only be brought 
under the habeas corpus statutes after exhaustion of state 
remedies.

14 
AN INCREASE IN THE TIME BETWEEN PAROLE 
CONSIDERATION HEARINGS FROM ONE TO TWO 
YEARS, APPLIED RETROACTIVELY, IS AN INSUB- 
STANTIAL PROCEDURAL CHANGE OUTSIDE THE 
PURVIEW OF THE EX POST FACTO CLAUSE. 
The ex post facto question isn’t as simple as Roller and 
the Fourth Circuit have made it out to be. The Fourth 
Circuit wrongly equates parole eligibility in South Caro- 
lina with parole reconsideration. Once this equation is 
made, it is but a short step to the conclusion that the 
statutory change in question violates the ex post facto 
prohibition. It is a short step because the equation, albeit 
an erroneous one, puts the question squarely under the 
well-established rule that parole eligibility is a substan- 
tive matter and attaches to the punishment. Change 
parole eligibility retroactively and to the prisoner’s dis- 
advantage, and the prohibition against ex post facto laws 
is violated. 
True enough. But the point is wholly irrelevant to the 
question here. The change in the procedure setting the 
time between parole consideration hearings is a matter of 
great importance. But it is qualitatively and quan- 
titatively different from a change in parole eligibility. In 
South Carolina, once a prisoner becomes eligible for 
parole on his sentence the eligibility vests; thereafter, he 
is reconsidered for parole every year. In South Carolina, a 
prisoner becomes eligible for parole on his sentence only 
once; thereafter, he is reconsidered for parole every year 
(in the case of non-violent offenders) or every two years 
(in the case of violent offenders). See S.C. Code Ann. 
§§ 24-21-645 and 24-21-650 (1976). (J.A. 40) Roller’s date 
15 
of eligibility for parole has not changed; that date was 
fixed from the start. See S.C. Code Ann. § 24-21-610 (1976), 
as amended. (J.A. 37) What has changed is the procedure 
governing the interval between parole hearings after a 
rejection. For Roller that interval used to be one year (see 
§ 24-21-620); now it is two years (see § 24-21-645). (J.A. 
39, 40 respectively) This is all that has been changed. No 
punishment has been added to Roller’s original sentence 
after the fact. His sentence has not been lengthened. His 
parole eligibility has not been revoked or altered in any 
way. No “gain time” credits have been lost retroactively. 
As the Supreme Court of South Carolina found in Gunter 
v. State, 298 S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989), “the standards 
governing petitioner’s parole eligibility have not been 
changed. Instead, only the frequency with which peti- 
tioner can be reconsidered for parole has been altered.” 
The only change in Roller’s situation is just this and 
nothing more-an insubstantial procedural change deter- 
mining how frequently he will be reconsidered for parole. 
South Carolina concedes that this change does oper- 
ate to the prisoner’s disadvantage, but it most certainly 
does not offend the Ex Post Facto Clause. Not all retroac- 
tive changes offend, a point well-established in the prece- 
dent of this Court: 
[T]he prohibition of ex post facto laws does not 
extend to every change of law that “may work 
to the disadvantage of a defendant.” It is 
intended to secure “substantial personal rights” 
from retroactive deprivation and does not “limit 
the legislative control of remedies and modes of 
procedure which do not affect matters of sub- 
stance.” 


PDF PAGE 12
16 
Portley v. Grossman, 444 U.S. 1311, 1312 (1980), quoting 
Dobbert v. Florida, 432 U.S. 282, 293 (1977). See also, Miller 
v. Florida, 482 U.S. 423 (1987); Beazell v. Ohio, 269 U.S. 167, 
(1925); Hopt v. Utah, 110 U.S. 574, 590 (1884). 
This Court reminds us in Collins v. Youngblood, 497 
U.S. 37, (1990), that we must go back tb the beginnings of 
ex post facto analysis, to clear the books of much of the 
confusion that has arisen in recent years. In Beazell v. 
Ohio, 269 U.S. 167, 171 (1925), this Court confidently 
summarized the general rule of ex post facto analysis, 
which had its beginning in Calder v. Bull, 3 Dall. 386 
(1798): 
It is settled, by decisions of this Court so well 
known that their citation may be dispensed 
with, that any statute which punishes as a crime 
an act previously committed, which was inno- 
cent when done; which makes more burden- 
some the punishment for a crime, after its 
commission, or which deprives one charged 
with crime of any defense available according to 
law at the time when the act was committed, is 
prohibited as ex post facto [citations omitted]. 
Collins, which goes back to Calder via Beazell, reminds us 
of the Court’s own “original understanding” of the Ex 
Post Facto Clause. Id. at 42. That original understanding 
rejects the notion—now increasingly current in some Cir- 
cuits, and among them, the Fourth Circuit, in particular- 
that any change which alters the situation of a party to 
his disadvantage effectively violates the Ex Post Facto 
Clause. Id. at 49. Collins emphasizes, in both its majority 
and concurring opinions, that the Ex Post Facto Clause 
“was intended to secure substantial personal rights 
17 
against arbitrary and oppressive legislation.” Id. at 46 and 
57, quoting Malloy v. South Carolina, 237 U.S. 180, 183 
(1915). Just what alterations of procedure will be held to 
be of sufficient moment to transgress the constitutional 
prohibition “cannot be embraced within a formula or 
stated in a general proposition. The distinction is one of 
degree.” Collins at 57, quoting Malloy. 
The Fourth Circuit’s reasoning leaves no room for 
this tried and true distinction. It leaves no room for case- 
by-case analysis of ex post facto problems based on the 
countless variations of fact these cases present. It also 
leaves no room for case-by-case analysis based on the 
numerous variations of law within the several states. The 
Fourth Circuit’s analysis reduces all levels of complexity 
to the lowest common denominator of simplicity: all 
changes in the law which operate retroactively to the 
disadvantage of the convicted criminal, no matter how 
slight, violate the prohibition against ex post facto laws. 
Furthermore, the Fourth Circuit’s simplistic analysis 
effectively homogenizes all state laws in such a way as to 
obliterate their distinctive differences. This reduction of 
differences for the sake of easy analysis ignores all state 
lines and state laws. Ultimately, it discourages states from 
once-permissible experimentation. 
Such a reduction occurs when the Fourth Circuit 
likens the case of Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d 1558 (11th Cir. 
1991), to this case. Whether the Fourth Circuit sees it or 
not, this case is distinct from Akins, and the distinction is 
based on an important matter of substance and degree, 
one which this Court has recognized under Collins v. 
Youngblood. As a matter of law and fact, the situation 
presented in Akins is vastly different from the situation 


PDF PAGE 13
18 
here. In Akins, the interval between parole reconsidera- 
tion hearings after a denial was increased from one year 
to eight years. This was even longer than it took to 
become eligible for parole in the first instance, which was 
seven years in Akins’ case. The Eleventh Circuit equated 
the period between parole hearings with parole eligibility 
itself. It did so on the basis of its finding that the Georgia 
legislature “considers a parole reconsideration hearing an 
essential and important part of parole eligibility.” 922 
F.2d at 1562. It was then easy for the Akins Court to 
conclude that the change increased the punishment retro- 
actively and therefore violated the prohibition against ex 
post facto laws. It is important to emphasize, once again, 
that in South Carolina parole eligibility and parole recon- 
sideration are statutorily two different things, separate 
and distinct. 
The Fourth Circuit’s decision, however, ignores this 
crucial distinction between parole eligibility and the 
scheduling of parole consideration hearings. In doing so, 
the Fourth Circuit, without expressly saying so, con- 
cludes that a change in the scheduling of parole consider- 
ation hearings is the same thing as a change in the 
inmate’s parole eligibility (as the Akins Court held) and 
therefore is a violation of the Ex Post Facto Clause. The 
sweeping import of the Fourth Circuit’s analysis is that 
no change in a state’s parole procedure which disadvan- 
tages an inmate can be made. This result conflicts with 
the holding in Collins, as well as the holdings in Dobbert 
ahd Portley. Supra. 
South Carolina would not deny that even under its © 
own parole system such a change as was at issue in Akins 
would violate the Ex Post Facto Clause. Clearly, any 
19 
increase of seven years, from one to eight years between 
parole reconsideration hearings, operates as a substantial 
disadvantage by denying the inmate a meaningful oppor- 
tunity for a parole hearing. See Rodriguez v. U.S. Parole 
Commission, 594 F.2d 170, 174-175 (7th Cir. 1979). Such a 
degree of change is of sufficient moment to transgress the 
constitutional prohibition. Indeed, a change in parole 
review potentially far more severe than the one before the 
Court here-a denial of annual parole review where the 
law had previously provided for such review-was 
recently upheld by the Eighth Circuit, reviewing a change 
in Minnesota’s parole release mechanism, as presenting 
no ex post facto violation. See Bailey v. Gardebring, 940 F.2d 
1150 (8th Cir. 1991), cert. denied, Bailey v. Noot, 112 S.Ct. 
1516 (1992). In Bailey, the prisoner lost not only his annual 
parole review but any scheduled date of review. The 
Eighth Circuit went so far as to hold that Minnesota’s 
parole regulations were not laws for the purpose of the 
Ex Post Facto Clause. Id. South Carolina’s parole law does 
not go anywhere near this extreme. This is how Collins 
would approach the Akins problem. Looking at the facts 
of the case at bar, Collins would say that nothing of the 
kind has occurred here. Roller has had, and still enjoys, a 
meaningful opportunity for parole under South Caro- 
lina’s system of paroles. 
South Carolina has more freedom than Georgia to 
experiment with changes in parole reconsideration, because 
South Carolina’s parole laws do not make parole recon- 
sideration an integral or essential part of parole eligi- 
bility, as do Georgia’s parole laws. South Carolina should 
not lose its legitimate freedom to experiment with its 
parole system in the way that it has distinguished 


PDF PAGE 14
20 
between parole reconsideration hearings for violent and 
non-violent offenders. South Carolina will lose a large 
part of that legitimate freedom to experiment if the 
Fourth Circuit’s holding is affirmed. This Court has well 
recognized that the several states should be encouraged 
to develop their own parole systems and be allowed to 
experiment with ways of improving those parole systems. 
Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional 
Complex, 442 U.S. 1 (1979); Gerstein v. Pugh, 420 U.S. 103 
(1975). Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974). The Fourth 
Circuit’s approach to ex post facto analysis, as evinced 
here, undermines this Court’s wise concern for the right 
of every state to fashion its own parole system, leaving to 
each state as much freedom as possible under the federal 
constitution. The Court should find, therefore, that this 
case presents no violation of the Ex Post Facto Clause and 
reverse the Fourth Circuit’s decision.

CONCLUSION 
Based upon the foregoing reasons, the judgment of 
the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals should be reversed. 
This Court should hold that 42 U.S.C. § 1983 does not 
afford a jurisdictional avenue to challenge South Caro- 
lina’s parole system, and it should further hold that South 
Carolina’s retroactive application of a two-year wait 
between parole consideration hearings does not violate 
the Ex Post Facto Clause.
