STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The Respondent, Gary Lee Roller, was 
convicted in the Chester County Court of 
General Sessions for the crimes of 
voluntary manslaughter and grand larceny. 
Both crimes were committed on December 
13, 1982. Roller was sentenced on March 
25, 1983, to serve thirty years for the 
voluntary manslaughter and a consecutive 
sentence of five years for the grand 
larceny. On October 27, 1990, Roller 
became eligible for parole under the 
parole eligibility statute in force on 
December 13, 1982, the date he committed 
his crimes. That parole eligibility date 
has not changed. Roller was heard for 
parole on October 31, 1990, and parole 
was denied. According to the procedures 
set up by the Parole Board, Roller was 
duly informed in writing of his rejection 
20 
and the reasons for it. He was also 
notified that his next parole hearing 
would be scheduled on or about October 
31, 1992, two years following his re- 
jection, pursuant to the law in effect at 
the time of his initial parole hearing. 
S.C. Code Ann. §§24-21-645 and ~-650 
(1976) as amended. 
When Roller committed his crimes, the 
procedural law governing the rescheduling 
of parole hearings following a rejection 
set the time at one year. S.C. Code Ann. 
§§24-21-620, (1976), as amended. 
Effective June 9. 1986, the law 
controlling the rehearing procedure was 
changed to require a two-year period 
between parole hearings for violent 
offenders. At the same time, the South 
Carolina General Assembly enacted §16-1- 
60, which enumerated those crimes deemed 
to be violent and included in that 
21 


PDF PAGE 13
enumeration the crime of voluntary 
manslaughter. The punishment for 
voluntary manslaughter, however, remained 
exactly what it had been when Roller 
committed that crime in 1982. The South 
Carolina Supreme Court ruled in Gunter v. 
State, 298 S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 
(1989), that the decrease in the frequen- 
cy of parole hearings for violent 
offenders was a permissible procedural 
change and did not violate the ex post 
facto clause of the Constitution.

District for the District of South Caro-

10, 1991, the United States Magistrate 
issued his Report and Recommendation on 
the parties motions for Summary Judgment. 
In an Order dated November 1, 1991, the

the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals 
reversed the District Court. In February 
1993, the Fourth Circuit granted the

seeks a Writ of Certiorari to review the 
Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals' decision 
of January 15, 1993, which conflicts with

Court's decision in Preiser v. Rodriguez,

federal jurisdiction under 42 U.S.C.

§1983. 
I. 
FEDERAL JURISDICTION FOR ROLLER'S 
CHALLENGE OF SOUTH CAROLINA'S PAROLE 
RELEASE SYSTEM WAS NOT PROPER UNDER 42 
U.S.C. §1983, AND THE FOURTH CIRCUIT 
ERRED IN HOLDING IT WAS. THE PRISONER'S 
PETITION REACHES THE VERY “CORE OF HABEAS 
CORPUS" AND, BECAUSE IT DOES, IT REQUIRES 
THE PETITONER TO EXHAUST HIS REMEDIES IN 
THE STATE COURTS OF SOUTH CAROLINA, 
BEFORE PROCEEDING TO THE FEDERAL COURTS. 
Section 1983 is not available to test 
the application of the challenged parole 
law. The action is cognizable only in 
habeas corpus. Preiser v. Rodriquez, 411 
U.S. 475 (1973), states the rule that 
controls here: 
(Wje hold today that when a state 
prisoner is challenging the very 
fact or duration of his physical 
imprisonment, and the relief he 
24 
ease 
seeks is a determination that he is 
entitled to immediate release or a 
from that 
imprisonment, his sole federal 
remedy is a writ of habeas corpus. 
[emphasis added) 
Id. This holding forecloses Appellee's 
§1983 action here. Preiser also teaches 
that "a §1983 is a proper remedy for a 
state prisoner who is making a 
constitutional challenge to the 
conditions of his prison life, but not to 
the fact or length of his custody." Id. 
at 499. The District Court relied on 
Strader v. Troy, 571 F.2d 1263 (4th Cir. 
1978), which incorrectly interprets 
Preiser. The Fourth Circuit held in 
Strader that Preiser stands for the 
proposition that "§1983 may be used to 
challenge parole procedures" only when 
the prisoner seeks "consideration for 
parole." Relying thus on Strader, the 
District Court is then able to conclude 
that Roller's use of §1983 is proper 
25 


PDF PAGE 15
because he "does not say he is entitled 
to parole." 
Roller may not in so many words say he 
is entitled to parole, but that is what 
his lawsuit is all about. However loudly 
Roller may protest to the contrary, he 
must be challenging either the fact or 
the duration of his physical 
imprisonment, and the relief he seeks 
amounts to ‘a determination that he is 
entitled to a speedier release from that 
imprisonment. He has made no allegations 
challenging the fact of his imprisonment, 
so that leaves the duration of his 
imprisonment as the only other 
possibility. (It needs no argument that 
Roller is not challenging the conditions 
of his prison life. That never was an 
issue.) And it does no good for Roller to 
insist that he is only “seeking his 
statutory parole hearing and not his 
26 
release." Appellee is at great pains to 
build his ex post facto argument on the 
major premise that the extra year between 
parole reconsideration hearings increases 
"the severity of his punishment" because 
this extra year, by Roller's own 
assertion, “increases the minimum amount 
of time the prisoner serves 
incarcerated." Whatever Roller wants to 
call it, the undeniable import of his 
argument amounts to nothing less than a 
challenge of the duration of his physical 
imprisonment. 
Roller cannot have it both ways. Either 
he is challenging the increase in the 
duration of his sentence as constituting 
an ex post facto violation when applied 
to him, in which case the action lies 
solely in habeas corpus (Preiser, 411 
U.S. at 500) and must be played out in 
the South Carolina state courts. Or he is 
27 


PDF PAGE 16
not challenging that increase, in which 
case it is a mystery just what he might 
be challenging as the retroactive 
disadvantage supposed to be substantial 
enough to violate the Ex Post Facto 
Clause. 
Assuming Roller is not engaging in 
mystery, it is clear that the core of his 
ex post facto challenge, which attacks 
some retroactively applied decrease in 
the frequency of parole consideration 
hearings, is a challenge to future 
confinement in order to obtain an earlier 
release. To call it anything else is 
semantic subterfuge. The Fourth Circuit 
itself has said as much in two cases 
coming after Strader and interpreting 
Preiser. Section 1983 may not be used in 
place of habeas corpus, as a way of 
getting around the Writ's requirement 
that the Petitioner first exhaust his 
28 
state remedies. Leonard v. Hammond, 804 
F.2d 838 (4th Cir. 1986); Todd v. 
Baskerville, 712 F.2d 70 (4th Cir. 1982). 
Insofar as Strader ignores all this, 
it simply gets Preiser wrong. And in so 
doing, Strader needlessly compounds the 
problem, already "difficult enough," 
Hamlin v. Warren, 664 F.2d 29, 33 (4th 
Cir. 1981) (Winter, C.J. dissenting), 
cert. denied, 455 U.S. 911 (1982), 
confronting state and federal courts 
alike: How to sort through the 
frustrating conflict between the civil 
rights and habeas corpus remedies for 
state prisoners. The heart of this 
conflict and, more important, how we 
finally resolve the conflict, implicates 
our deepest concern for and understanding 
of certain fundamental notions of 
federalism and all that that bedrock 
principle entails in the way of comity 
29 


PDF PAGE 17
between the federal government and the 
several states. To rely on Strader, we 
believe, undermines federalism and upsets 
the delicate balance which comity helps 
to keep. Braden v. 30th Judicial Circuit 
of Kentucky, 410 U.S. 484 (1973) 
(exhaustion of state-court remedies 
ensures comity between federal and state 
courts). For, what Strader suggests is 
that South Carolina's right to exercise 
concurrent jurisdiction with the federal 
courts in deciding questions of federal 
constitutional law need not be accorded 
the full respect that federalism says is 
its due, and may in fact be ignored 
altogether whenever a state prisoner, 
crafty enough to capitalize on the 
confusion now troubling the federal 
courts, renames his’. habeas corpus 
petition a §1983 claim for relief. 
The Court should also recognize that 
30 
there is a further obstacle in the 
limitations on suits against the states 
imposed by the Eleventh Amendment. As 
this Court stated in the leading case of 
Will _v. Michigan Dept. of State Police, 
109 S.Ct. 2304, 2309 (1989), “Section 
1983 provides a federal forum to remedy 
many deprivations of civil liberties, but 
it does not provide a federal forum for 
litigants who seek a remedy against a 
State for alleged deprivations of civil 
liberties." The Eleventh Amendment, as 
the Court put it, "bars such suits unless 
the State has waived its immunity." Id. 
And South Carolina, we would assure the 
Court, has never waived its immunity 
here. Further, although Will suggests 
that an action against a state official 
in his official capacity for prospective 
injunctive relief under §1983 is proper 
because such an action would not be 
31 


PDF PAGE 18
treated as an action against the state, 
the suggestion doesn't fit the case here. 
Id. at 2311, n.10. What Roller is 
challenging is not the official capacity 
action of a state official; what he is 
challenging is the system South Carolina 
has set up for reviewing paroles after a 
rejection. The difficulty in relying on 
Strader is thus complicated. And the 
only way out for the Court, we would 
suggest, is for the Court to overrule the 
Fourth Circuit's reliance on Strader in 
favor of the lucid reasoning expressed in 
Preiser and well followed by the Fourth 
Circuit in Todd and Leonard. 
Finally, it is crucial for the Court 
to remember that South Carolina has in 
place adequate state-law remedies--the 
Uniform Post-Conviction Procedure Act, 
§17-27-10 et seg. and the Tort Claims 
Act, §15-78-10 et seg.--that have always 
32 
been available to Roller. He need only 
have availed himself of those remedies, 
which he has chosen not to do. Instead, 
Roller resorts to the Civil Rights 
statute in order to circumvent the 
exhaustion requirements of the habeas 
corpus. statute. For this Court to 
countenance such highjinks even for a 
moment would return us to the state of 
affairs that existed in 1971, before 
Preiser dealt with the problem. It was 
a state of affairs that led one judge of 
the Second Circuit to lament, "[s)tate 
prisoners are increasingly resorting to 
the Civil Rights statutes in order to 
circumvent the [exhaustion] requirements 
of {the habeas corpus statute])." 
Rodriguez v. McGinnis, 451 F.2d 730 (2d 
Cir. 1971). The reasoning that should 
guide the Court's deliberations here is 
first stated in Preiser. Certainly the 
33 


PDF PAGE 19
Fourth Circuit decision does serious harm 
to federal-state comity. South Carolina 
would therefore request the this Court 
grant its petition for a writ of 
certiorari to consider the federal 
questions that this case has raised.

II. 
THE 1986 STATUTORY AMENDMENT TO THE SOUTH 
CAROLINA CODE, WHICH INCREASES THE TIME 
BETWEEN PAROLE RECONSIDERATION HEARINGS 
FROM ONE TO TWO YEARS FOR OFFENDERS 
CONVICTED OF VIOLENT CRIME, IS _ AN 
INSUBSTANTIAL PROCEDURAL CHANGE. IT IS 
OUTSIDE THE PURVIEW OF THE EX POST FACTO 
CLAUSE, AS THE SUPREME COURT OF SOUTH 
CAROLINA HELD IN GUNTER v. STATE, 378 
S.E.2d 443 (1989). 
The ex post facto question isn't as 
simple as Roller and the Fourth Circuit 
have made it out to be. The Fourth 
Circuit wrongly equates parole 
eligibility in South Carolina with parole 
reconsideration. Once this equation is 
made, it is but a short step to the con- 
clusion that the statutory change in 
35 


PDF PAGE 20
question violates the ex post facto 
prohibition. It is a short step because 
the equation, albeit an erroneous one, 
puts the question squarely under the 
well-established rule that parole 
eligibility is a substantive matter and 
attaches to the punishment. Change 
parole eligibility retroactively and to 
the prisoner's disadvantage, and the 
prohibition against ex post facto laws is 
violated. 
True enough. But the point is wholly 
irrelevant to the question here. In 
South Carolina, a prisoner becomes 
eligible for parole on his sentence only 
once; thereafter, he is reconsidered for 
parole every year (in the case of non- 
violent offenders) or every two years (in 
the case of violent offenders). See S.c. 
Code Ann. §24-21-645 (Supp. 1987). 
Roller's date of eligibility for parole 
36 
has not changed; that date was fixed from 
the start. See S.C. Code Ann. §24-21- 
610 (1976), as amended. What has changed 
is the procedure governing the interval 
between parole hearings after a 
rejection. For Roller that interval used 
to be one year; now it is two years. 
This is all that has been changed. No 
punishment has been added to Roller's 
original sentence after the fact. His 
sentence has not been lengthened. His 
parole eligibility has not been revoked 
or altered in any way. No “gain time" 
credits have been lost retroactively. As 
the Supreme Court of South Carolina found 
in Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 113, 378 
S.E.2d 443 (1989), "the standards 
governing petitioner's parole eligibility 
have not been changed. Instead, only the 
frequency with which petitioner can be 
reconsidered for parole has been 
37 


PDF PAGE 21
altered." The only change in Roller's 
situation is just this and nothing more 
--an insubstantial procedural change 
determining how frequently he will be 
reconsidered for parole. 
South Carolina concedes that this 
change does operate to the prisoner's 
disadvantage, but it most certainly does 
not offend the Ex Post Facto Clause. Not 
all retroactive changes offend, a point
