RESPONDENT’S BRIEF IN OPPOSITION TO PETITION 
FOR WRIT OF CERTIORARI 
The Respondent, Gary Lee Roller, prays that a Writ of Certiorari to review 
the judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit be denied. 
SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENTS 
I. The Circuit Court correctly determined that as the Respondent, Roller, was 
challenging the denial of a pre-existing statutory right to parole reconsideration rather than 
the "fact or duration” of his sentence, 42 U.S.C. §1983 was an appropriate avenue for 
declaratory and injunctive relief. Under the principles of Preiser y. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 
475 (1973), and the prior Circuit Court decision in Strader v. Troy, 571 F.2d 1263 (4th Cir. 
1978), as the Respondent was not seeking release from prison “now or ever” but simply the 
right to a timely hearing, there is no requirement that the action be brought under a writ of 
habeas corpus. 
II. A statutory change in the time period between parole reconsideration 
hearings is legally indistinguishable from an increase in time for initial consideration of 
parole and is subject to ex post facto protection. As the Respondent was statutonly entitled 
to reconsideration for parole on a yearly basis at the time of his crime and at the time of his 
sentencing, a subsequent statutory change to provide for a reconsideration hearing every two 
(2) years should not be applied to the Respondent. The Respondent had a legitimate 
expectation at the time of his sentencing that if denied parole at his initial hearing he would 
be entitled thereafter to review for parole on a yearly basis.

ARGUMENT 
I. THE CIRCUIT COURT CORRECTLY DETERMINED THAT A 
CHALLENGE TO DENIAL OF PAROLE CONSIDERATION MAY BE PROPERLY 
BROUGHT UNDER 42 U.S.C. §1983. 
The Petitioner, State, seeks review of a long standing rule of law in the Fourth 
Circuit that changes in parole eligibility may be challenged under 42 U.S.C. §1983. In 
essence the State bases its jurisdictional challenge upon the premise that the Circuit Court 
erred in deciding Strader v. Troy, 571 F.2d 1263 (4th Cir. 1978). It is argued that Strader 
and this case violate this Court's decision in Preiser vy. Rodriguez. 411 U.S. 475 (1973). 
The State advances no other basis for this Court to grant certiorari under Rule 10. 
Preiser vy. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. at 509, held “when a state prisoner is 
challenging the very fact or duration of his physical imprisonment, and the relief he seeks 
iS a determination that he is entitled to immediate release or a speedier release from that 
imprisonment, his sole federal remedy is a writ of habeas corpus.” If Respondent were 
asking for either an immediate or speedy release from prison, then clearly Preiser would 
mandate that such action be brought pursuant to habeas corpus. Release was not the relief 
sought by Roller; only the opportunity for a timely parole hearing. Roller’s lawsuit 
challenged the State’s action in retrospectively cutting in half his opportunities to seek parole 
in violation of the Constitution’s protection against ex post facto laws. It was never alleged 
that Respondent was unlawfully detained nor that he was entitled to be released on parole, 
now or in the future. “Like the inmate in Strader, Roller does not argue that he is entitled 
to parole.” (Opinion, Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals, Roller vy. Cavanaugh, Petitioner's 
Appendix at p. 9). 
Where the prisoner's challenge is not to the fact of imprisonment or its 
duration, it is inappropriate to bring a habeas corpus action. The appropriate and only 
federal remedy available to a prisoner for declaratory or injunctive relief to matters unrelated 
to the fact of confinement is §1983. The Respondent respectfully requests that the Petition 
for Writ of Certiorari be denied on this issue. 
ll. A STATUTORY RIGHT TO PAROLE ELIGIBILITY AND 
RECONSIDERATION HEARINGS IS PROTECTED FROM SUBSEQUENT 
LEGISLATIVE CHANGES BY THE PROHIBITION OF EX POST FACTO LAWS. 
The State seeks certiorari review of this issue as an earlier decision of the 
South Carolina Supreme Court in Gunter y. State, 298 S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989), 
conflicts with the Fourth Circuit's decision in this case. Gunter held that the increase in ime 
for parole reconsideration hearings was not a violation of the ex post facto clause. 
The State concedes that the change in time between reconsideration hearings 
works to Roller’s disadvantage. The State argues, however, that the disadvantage 1s 
insubstantial and is somehow different in nature from a delay in initial consideration for 
parole. Conceding that delay in initial parole eligibility is violative of the ex post facto 
clauses of the Constitution, the State argues that once Roller reaches his initial parole 
eligibility date he is eligible for parole thereafter and the one year delay in reconsideration

is simply a procedural change “governing the interval between parole hearings after a 
rejection.” (State's Petition for Certiorari, p. 37). 
The State engages in a game of semantics when distinguishing between parole 
“eligibility” and “reconsideration”. The reality of the State's present situation is that once 
parole has been denied, Roller is not again eligible for consideration of parole for two years 
rather than the one year review mandated by law at the time of his crime and sentencing. 
The State makes the somewhat circular argument that Roller is currently eligible for parole 
although he is prevented from engaging in the only procedure by which he may obtain 
parole. As pointed out by the Circuit Court, “[e]ligibility without consideration is cold 
comfort." (Appendix p. 17). 
The State agrees that Georgia violated the ex post facto provision of the 
Constitution when it changed parole reconsideration hearings from every year to every 8 
years. Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d 1558 (11th Cir. 1991). The State is unable to find similar 
fault in its own actions. The only difference between Georgia's and South Carolina's actions 
is the amount of time involved. Certainly Petitioner would not take the position that 
changing the actual sentence for a crime would not violate the ex post facto provision of the 
Constitution if the sentence increased by only | year, as opposed to 8 years. The violation 
either occurs or not and the determination of what constitutes a violation cannot reasonably 
hinge on the magnitude of the violation. The State’s argument that one year was not a 
matter of substance was not accepted by the Circuit Court. “We are not willing to disparage 
the ‘substance’ of a year, especially a year in prison." (Petitioner's Appendix, p. 16). 
5. 
The Circuit Court correctly determined that the impact of the change in 
statutory law worked to the disadvantage of Roller and that the protections of the ex post 
facto clauses of the Constitution extend to statutory changes in time periods for parole

Petition for Writ of Certiorari be denied.
