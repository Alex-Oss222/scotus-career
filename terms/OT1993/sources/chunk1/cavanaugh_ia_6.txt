QUESTIONS PRESENTED FOR REVIEW 
I. May a State prisoner challenging the denial 
of timely parole hearings seek injunctive relief under 42 
U.S.C. §1983, or must relief be sought under 28 U.S.C. 
§2254? 
II. Does the retrospective application of a 
Statute increasing from one to two years the time period for 
parole consideration violate the Ex Post Facto Clause of the 
United States Constitution?

STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
The Respondent/Plaintiff, Gary Lee Roller, 
(hereinafter Roller), was convicted in Chester County, South 
Carolina, Court of General Sessions, of the crimes of 
Voluntary Manslaughter and Grand Larceny, said crimes 
occurring on December 13, 1982. Roller was sentenced on 
March 25, 1983, to thirty (30) years for the crime of 
Voluntary Manslaughter and five (5) years for the crime of 
Grand Larceny. The law in effect at the time of his crimes 
and sentencing required annual reconsideration of parole if 
Roller was denied parole on his original eligibility date. (J.A. 
6). In 1986, the State amended the parole statute to provide 
that violent offenders would be reconsidered for parole every 
two years after initial rejection. (J.A. 7). Roller was initially 
considered and rejected for parole on October 27, 1990. The 
letter of rejection stated that he would be eligible for 
reconsideration on or about October 27, 1992, in accordance 
with the state law passed in 1986. 


PDF PAGE 5
Prior to the administrative determination by the 
Petitioners as to Roller’s reconsideration date, the South 
Carolina Supreme Court decided in Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 
113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989), that the retrospective application 
of the change in the parole law was not in violation of the Ex 
Post Facto Clause. 
On December 28, 1990, Roller filed a Complaint 
under 42 U.S.C. §1983 in the District Court for the District of 
South Carolina seeking injunctive and monetary relief. (J.A. 
2-11). The Honorable Henry M. Herlong, Jr., United States 
District Judge, issued an Order, filed November 8, 1991, 
granting the State’s Motion for Summary Judgment. (J.A. 23- 
26). Roller appealed the District Court’s ruling with regard to 
the denial of prospective injunctive relief. The Fourth Circuit 
Court of Appeals reversed the District Court and granted 
Roller the injunctive relief requested in its opinion of January 
15, 1993. (J.A. 28-36). Thereafter, the State of South 
Carolina petitioned this Court for certiorari as the Fourth 
Circuit ruling conflicted with the South Carolina Supreme 
Court’s ruling in Gunter v. State. Certiorari was granted in 
this Court’s Order of May 24, 1993. 
Since this Court granted certiorari, the South 
Carolina Supreme Court has overruled its decision in Gunter 
v. State. In Griffin v. State, Opin. No. 23909 (July 19, 1993), 
the South Carolina Court held the change in parole 
reconsideration hearings under the Omnibus Crime Act of 
1986, when applied to inmates such as Roller and Griffin, is 
violative of the Ex Post Facto Clause of the Constitution. In 
Griffin, the South Carolina Court adopted the reasoning of the 
Fourth Circuit’s opinion in Roller.

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT 
The first issue raised is jurisdictional in nature 
and calls for this Court to determine whether a prisoner may 
challenge the denial of timely parole hearings under 42 U.S.C. 
§1983. As a hearing seeking parole neither assures Roller of 
release, nor involves any change in the length of his sentence, 
§1983 is a proper avenue for relief. The exclusive remedy 
provisions of Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475 (1973), apply 
only to cases in which immediate release or reduction in length 
of confinement is sought. If the remedy sought relates to 
process rather than outcome, then the provisions of 28 U.S.C. 
§2254 do not preempt jurisdiction under §1983. As a ruling 
in Roller’s favor on the underlying action will not entitle him 
to release, §1983 is a proper procedural alternative to vindicate 
his constitutional rights. 
The second issue requires interpretation of the 
Ex Post Facto Clause of the Constitution. Roller was 
sentenced at a time when the statutory law of South Carolina 
-4- 
provided that upon initial rejection for parole he would be 
considered for parole every twelve months thereafter. In 1986, 
the parole law was changed to provide that the reconsideration 
hearings were to be held every twenty-four months rather than 
every twelve months. The State chose to apply the new statute 
to inmates sentenced prior to 1986. Under the guidelines of 
Calder v. Bull, 3 Dall. 386 (1798), and Collins v. Youngblood, 
497 U.S. 37 (1990), the application of the subsequent statute 
to Roller violates Calder in that it “changes the punishment, 
and inflicts a greater punishment, than the law annexed to the 
crime, when committed." Calder at 390, [emphasis in 
original]. The State’s categorization of the change as 
"procedural" is not relevant to the determination as the 
retrospective application of the statute goes to substantive 
aspects of the sentence imposed upon Roller, and “penal 
provisions accorded by the grace of the legislature” are equally 
entitled to the protection of the Ex Post Facto Clause as the 
actual sentence imposed. Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24, 30 


PDF PAGE 7
(1981). Even though Roller may never be accorded the grace 
of parole during his term of imprisonment, the retrospective 
application of the statute restricting his opportunity to seek 
parole is more harsh than the law at the time of his sentencing 
and therefore “makes more burdensome" the punishment for 
his crime. 
I. 
AS THE RESPONDENT IS NOT 
ATTACKING THE FACT OR LENGTH OF HIS 
CONFINEMENT, NOR SEEKING AN IMMEDIATE OR 
MORE SPEEDY RELEASE, 42 U.S.C. §1983 IS AN 
APPROPRIATE REMEDY TO SEEK INJUNCTIVE 
RELIEF. 
If Roller were seeking his release from custody, 
or a reduction in his sentence in order to gain an earlier 
release, his only avenue to obtain such relief would lie in 
habeas corpus. In the case at hand, Roller seeks injunctive 
relief for the sole purpose of obtaining an annual parole 
reconsideration hearing. Such a hearing would only give 
Roller the opportunity to seek parole which, in no way, is a 
guarantee of release. “That the state holds out the possibility 
of parole provides no more than a mere hope that the benefit 
will be obtained." Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal & 
Corr. Complex, 442 U.S. 1, 11 (1979) (citing Board of 


PDF PAGE 8
Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564, 577 (1982)) [emphasis in 
original]. This "mere hope” of release is the determinant 
factor in deciding whether Roller may proceed under §1983 or 
is limited to habeas corpus relief. 
Under Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475 
(1973), the fact or duration of incarceration may not be 
challenged under 42 U.S.C. §1983. Roller does not challenge 
this general principle. The State, however, extends this 
qualification far beyond Preiser and its progeny. Preiser 
established the "extent to which §1983 is a permissible 
alternative to the traditional remedy of habeas corpus." 411 
U.S. at 500. “When the relief sought is actual or speedier 
release, Preiser restricts the availability of the civil rights 
Statute. “In short, Congress has determined that habeas corpus 
is the appropriate remedy for state prisoners attacking the 
validity of the fact or length of their confinement, and that 
specific determination must override the general terms of 
$1983." 411 U.S. at 490. Similarly, the question here is 
whether the general language of §1983 is preempted by the 
specific provisions of 28 U.S.C. §2254. 
The language of 28 U.S.C. §2254(a) provides in 
The Supreme Court, a justice thereof, a circuit 
judge, or a district court shall entertain an 
application for a writ of habeas corpus... only 
on the ground that he is in custody in violation 
of the Constitution or laws or treaties of the 
United States. 
This language does not indicate that Congress intended this 
remedy to apply to constitutional complaints other than 
improper custody. As such, it does not preempt the general 
language of §1983 allowing a prisoner, challenging prison 
conditions and asserting denial of other constitutional rights, to 
proceed under §1983. Preiser at 498-499. The Preiser Court 
limited its holding to those cases in which plaintiffs did not 
seek damages, but only equitable relief which would result in 
their actual or speedier release. 
This Court’s rulings since have adhered to the 
Preiser parameters. Wolff v. McDonnell, 48 U.S. 539 (1974), 
-9- 


PDF PAGE 9
recognized that §1983 was a proper vehicle to challenge the 
procedures used in taking good time credits, although 
restoration of good time credits under §1983 was foreclosed by 
this Court’s ruling in Preiser. This limitation, however, did 
not deprive the inmates of other relief under §1983. 
[D]etermination of the validity of the procedures 
employed ... as a predicate to a damages award 
would not be barred by Preiser; and because 
under that case only an injunction restoring 
good time improperly taken is foreclosed, 
neither would it preclude a litigant with standing 
from obtaining by way of ancillary relief an 
otherwise proper injunction enjoining the 
prospective enforcement of invalid prison 
regulations. 
Wolff, 418 U.S. at 555. 
In Gerstein v. Pugh, 420 U.S. 103 (1975), 
pretrial detainees filed a §1983 class action suit challenging the 
Florida criminal procedure which allowed for pretrial detention 
without a judicial determination of probable cause. The suit 
sought injunctive relief and did not seek a release from 
custody. 
Respondents did not ask for release from state 
-10- 
custody, even as an alternative remedy. They 
asked only that the state authorities be ordered 
to give them a probable cause determination. ... 
Because release was neither asked nor ordered, 
the lawsuit did not come within the class of 
cases for which habeas corpus is the exclusive 
remedy. Preiser v. Rodriguez, see Wolff v. 
McDonnell. 
Gerstein, 420 U.S. at 107 n.6 [citations omitted]. 
Greenholtz v. Inmates of the Nebraska Penal & 
Corr. Complex, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), dealt with a substantially 
similar issue as that raised by Roller; whether constitutional 
concerns were violated by post-sentencing parole procedures. 
This Court reached the merits of the Greenholtz §1983 claim 
without mention of whether the suit was properly before the 
Court. As pursuing the action under §1983 is a jurisdictional 
matter, this Court would have sua sponte raised the issue and 
refused to consider the merits of the underlying claim. Collins 
v. Youngblood, 497 at 41. 
Under the State’s reasoning, Greenholtz should 
have been dismissed. The basis of the inmates claim was that 
“they had been unconstitutionally denied parole...." 442 U.S. 
a. 


PDF PAGE 10
at 3. As such, the Greenholtz plaintiffs, had they been 
successful, may have had an increased opportunity for release 
on parole. The fault in Petitioner’s argument is that the State 
fails to distinguish between claims focusing on procedure and 
those focusing on the outcome of litigation. Gerstein v. Pugh’s 
holding that pretrial detainees were entitled to fair and 
impartial probable cause determinations could have led to the 
release of some plaintiffs from pretrial detention. This fact did 
not limit the detainees to habeas corpus relief as the remedy 
sought was a fair hearing and not release. “[T]he fact that a 
prisoner’s success in the litigation might increase the chance 
for early release does not, in itself, transform the action into 
one for habeas corpus." Georgevich v. Strauss, 772 F.2d 
1078, 1087 (3rd Cir., en banc 1985) (citing Wright v. Cuyler, 
624 F.2d 455 (3d Cir. 1980)). 
The question is one of process as opposed to 
outcome. When an inmate is challenging the procedures 
applied as violative of the Constitution, and not seeking actual 
-12- 
release, §1983 is an appropriate vehicle. In Georgevich, the 
Circuit Court considered a constitutional challenge to parole 
procedures on equal protection grounds and found the process 
versus Outcome distinction determinative of which claims are 
properly under §1983 and which are restricted to habeas 
corpus. After reviewing this Court’s decisions in Preiser, 
Wolff, Gerstein and Greenholtz, the Third Circuit found “the 
distinction [to be] between process-based claims and those 
focusing on the outcome of a particular decision. Since 
plaintiff's claim relates only to process and not to the actual 
duration of confinement, we hoid that it could properly be 
brought as a §1983 action." Georgevich, 772 F.2d at 1087. 
Roller agrees with the Third Circuit that “analysis of the relief 
sought is necessary to determine whether exhaustion of state 
remedies is required." Seed v. Strauss, 772 F.2d at 
1086. 
Roller does not challenge the legality, fact or 
duration of his confinement. What is challenged is the process 
-13- 


PDF PAGE 11
by which the State has denied Roller hearings to which he was 
formerly entitled. If Roller is successful in obtaining the 
injunctive relief requested, the ultimate effect of yearly 
hearings on his ability to obtain parole is unknown. Obtaining 
yearly as opposed to bi-yearly hearings does not in any way 
guarantee him a speedier release from prison. This Court’s 
decision on the merits of this action will not divest the parole 
board of its sole authority to decide if Roller is to be released. 
Therefore, Roller is not asking this Court to provide him with 
a speedier release, but with an opportunity to be considered for 
release under the circumstances as they existed at the time of 
his crime and sentencing. 
While Roller makes no assertion that he is 
entitled to parole now or at any time in the future, the Fourth 
Circuit Court’s decision makes it clear that he should not 
expect such relief. “Our decision does not, of course, require 
or even suggest that Roller be paroled. South Carolina need 
never parole him, so long as it considers the question every 
-14- 
year.” Roller v. Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d 120, 124 (4th Cir. 
1993). Simply put, release for Roller is “neither asked nor 
ordered” under his §1983 action. Gerstein, 420 U.S. at 107 
n. 7. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals correctly decided 
this issue and Roller’s action is properly brought under §1983. 
Petitioner also argues that Roller should have 
first sought redress in the South Carolina State Courts before 
asking for federal relief. While exhaustion of state remedies 
may be necessary for habeas purposes, the same is not true of 
$1983 actions. Patsy v. Board of Regents, 457 U.S. 496 
(1982).

Il. 
THE RETROSPECTIVE APPLICATION OF 
A STATUTE INCREASING THE TIME PERIOD FOR 
CONSIDERATION FOR PAROLE FROM ONE TO TWO 
YEARS "INFLICTS A GREATER PUNISHMENT" FOR 
A CRIME AND VIOLATES THE EX POST FACTO 
CLAUSE OF THE CONSTITUTION. 
The Fourth Circuit found that "South Carolina 
has undoubtedly applied its new statute to ‘alter the conditions - 
of...[Roller’s}] preexisting parole eligibility." Roller y. 
Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d at 124 (citing Fender v. Thompson, 883 
F.2d 303, 306 (4th Cir. 1989)). Based upon the established 
general rule in the Fourth Circuit that changes in parole 
eligibility after the commission of the crime are in violation of 
-the Ex Post Facto Clause, the Circuit Court found a violation 
of the Constitution in Roller’s case. This decision conforms 
with the long standing rules of this Court as to the ex post 
facto prohibition and is consonant with the tradition of this 
-16- 
venerable precept. 
In Calder v. Bull, 3 Dall. 386 (1798), Justice 
Chase engaged in a lengthy discussion of the differences 
between ex post facto and retrospective laws. 3 Dall. at 391- 
393. While indicating that both Categories are “generally 
unjust; and may be oppressive," Calder at 391, he found that 
only penal laws fall “within the words and the intent of the 
prohibition.” Calder at 390 [emphasis in Original]. The term 
ex post facto was in use “long before the Revolution, and had 
acquired an appropriate meaning, by Legislators, Lawyers, and 
Authors." Calder at 391 [emphasis in original]. Justice Chase 
found this meaning to encompass four categories: 
Ist. Every law that makes an action done before the passing of the law, and which was innocent when done, criminal; and punishes 
such action. 2d. Every law that aggravates a crime, or makes it greater than it was, when committed. 3d. Every law that changes the Punishment, and inflicts a greater Punishment, 
than the law annexed to the crime, when committed. 4th. Every law that alters the legal rules of evidence, and receives less, or different, testimony, than the law required at the time of the commission of the offence, in order 
-17- 


PDF PAGE 13
to convict the offender. 
Calder at 390 [emphasis in original]. 
These precepts were found to be consistent with commentaries 
of Sir William Blackstone who “considers an ex post facto law 
precisely in the same light I have done.” Calder at 391 
{emphasis in original]. While the accuracy of the historical 
discussion in Calder has been debated, this Court has faithfully 
adhered to the general principles espoused by the Justices in 
that case. See, Collins v. Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37, 41 n. 2 
(1990). 
This Court has, on a number of occasions, 
revisited the meaning of the ex post facto provisions. Of these 
cases, this Court has found that Beazell v. Ohio, 269 U.S. 167 
(1925), “is faithful to our best knowledge of the original 
understanding of the Ex Post Facto Clause." Collins v. 
Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37, 43 (1990). In the 127 years 
between Calder and Beazell, this Court, on a number of 
occasions, found retrospective penal laws did not violate the ex 
-18- 
post facto doctrine. In Beazell, Justice Stone reviewed these 
opinions and found that there was substantial precedent for the 
principle but there was no bright line test to determine when a 
procedural change violated the Constitution. 
But it is now well settled that statutory changes 
in the mode of trial or the rules of evidence, 
which do not deprive the accused of a defense 
and which operate only in a limited and 
unsubstantial manner to his disadvantage, are 
not prohibited.... Just what alterations of 
procedure will be held to be of sufficient 
moment to transgress the constitutional 
prohibition cannot be embraced within a formula 
or stated in a general proposition. The 
distinction is one of degree. But the 
constitutional provision was intended to secure 
substantial personal rights against arbitrary and 
oppressive legislation, and not to limit the 
legislative control of remedies and modes of 
procedure which do not affect substantial rights. 
269 U.S. at 170-171 [citations omitted]. 
Examples given of retrospective changes which 
do not violate the prohibition are the qualification of convicted 
felons as witnesses, Hopt v. Utah, 110 U.S. 575 (1884); 
allowing evidence which had been previously held 
inadmissable, Thompson v. Missouri, 171 U.S. 380 (1898); 
-19- 
ey ee 


PDF PAGE 14
changing the place of trial, Gut v. Minnesota, 9 Wall. 35, 19 
L.Ed. 573 (1870); and changing the mode of execution, Malloy 
v. South Carolina, 237 U.S. 180 (1915). 
The degree of the distinction is illustrated by 
comparing the ruling in Malloy with Ex Parte Medley, 134 
U.S. 160 (1890). In both cases the defendant was under a 
valid sentence of death. In Medley, the retrospective statutory 
change added a requirement of solitary confinement prior to 
execution, in Malloy the method of execution was changed 
from hanging to death by electrocution. As this Court found 
that solitary confinement was "an additional punishment of the 
most important and painful character," Medley, 134 U.S. at 
171, his punishment was found to be in violation of the 
Constitutional protection. On the other hand, in Malloy, the 
Court found that the change from hanging to electrocution was, 
if anything, a more humane method of producing death and 
"some of the odious features incident to the old method 
[hanging] were abated." Malloy, 237 U.S. at 185. Therefore, 
the statutory change in Malloy did not violate the prohibition. 
The distinction between the cases turns upon the issue of 
whether the new statute makes more harsh the punishment 
being imposed. If it does, then it violates the Ex Post Facto 
Clause; if it does not then there is no violation. 
This Court revisited the "procedural" question 
in Collins v. Youngblood, supra. 
Several of our cases have described as 
"procedural" those changes which, even though 
they work to the disadvantage «* the accused, 
do not violate the Ex Post Facto Clause. While 
these cases do not explicitly define what they 
mean by the word “procedural,” it is logical to 
think that the term refers to changes in the 
procedures by which a criminal case is 
adjudicated, as opposed to changes in the 
substantive law of crimes. 
497 U.S. at 45 [citations omitted]. 
This Court found an element of confusion had been fostered by 
a number of cases which accorded protection to procedural 
changes which affected "matters of substance” or “arbitrarily 
infring[ed] upon ‘substantial personal rights." Jd. By 
returning to the original concept of Calder, the Court indicated 
-21- 


PDF PAGE 15
that ex post facto violations should be founded upon the 
categories set forth by Justice Chase. By whatever designation 
given a retrospective penal change, if it involves one of the 
four Calder categories, and is detrimental to the defendant, it 
violates the Ex Post Facto Clause. 
We think the best way to make sense out of this 
discussion in the cases is to say that by simply 
labelling a law “procedural,” a legislature does 
not thereby immunize it from scrutiny under the 
Ex Post Facto Clause. Subtle ex post facto 
violations are no more permissible than overt 
ones....But the prohibition which may not be 
evaded is the one defined by the Calder 
categories. 
Collins, 110 S.Ct at 2721 [citations omitted]. 
The question becomes whether an increase from 
one to two years in parole consideration after initial rejection 
is encompassed in the Calder categories. The Petitioner 
ignores this aspect of Collins and focuses an argument to 
whether the change is “substantial.” While Roller maintains 
that the change in his case is substantial, the Respondent 
believes the proper inquiry lies in the nature of the deprivation. 
-22- 
The Petitioner focuses on the "substantial protections” and 
"personal rights” issues in arguing that Roller is not protected 
by the constitutional prohibition. Such inquiry is inconsistent 
with the effort of the Court in Collins to clear up the confusion 
engendered by these phrases and refocus the analysis to the 
Calder categories. Collins, 497 U.S. at 45-46. 
While Roller’s total sentence has not been 
changed, his opportunity to seek his release, pursuant to a 
procedure mandated by the law at the time of his sentence, has 
been altered. While a State has no obligation to employ 
parole, good time, or work credits, once these benefits are 
granted by legislation, they are entitled to ex post facto 
protection. "Thus, even if a statute merely alters penal 
provisions accorded by the grace of the legislature, it violates 
the Clause if it is both retrospective and more onerous than the 
law in effect on the date of the offence.” Weaver v. Graham, 
450 U.S. 24, 30-31 (1981). 
The Petitioner argues that there has been no 
-23- 


PDF PAGE 16
change in the sentence imposed upon Roller. While Roller’s 
thirty-five year sentence has not been changed, this fact does 
not end the inquiry. In Weaver, this Court found that the 
retrospective change in gain time credits to the inmate’s 
detriment, while outside of the sentence imposed, was a change 
in “the quantum of punishment." 450 U.S. at 33 (quoting 
Dobbert v. Florida, 432 U.S. at 293-294). Similarly Roller 
has suffered a change in his punishment, and the retrospective 
application of the statute at issue here falls squarely under the 
Calder categories. 
In Weaver, the issue as to the effect of the 
retrospective change was a close one. The gain time statute 
was amended to reduce the amount of gain time for good 
behavior but increased the opportunity for discretionary gain 
time. Then Justice Rehnquist, concurring in the judgment, 
indicated that he was “persuaded in this case, albeit not without 
Goubt, that the statute is more onerous than the old." 450 U.S. 
at 38. The significance of the resolution of this issue in the 
-24- 
inmate’s favor is that the Petitioner’s argument as to 
“substantial deprivation" misses the point. If there is no 
dispute as to the detrimental effect of the statute, then the 
inquiry ends. 
Roller’s entitlement to annual parole hearings 
after reaching his parole eligibility date was statutory law in 
South Carolina at the time of his crime and sentencing. As 
such, it was a “part of the law annexed to the crime at the 
time” of his offense. Roller v. Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d at 122 
(quoting from Schwartz v. Muncy, 834 F.2d 396, 398 n. 8 (4th 
Cir. 1987)). Changing the law to require Roller to serve at 
least one additional year before parole reconsideration is a 
change in the quantum of punishment. “We are not willing to 
disparage the ‘substance’ of a year, especially a year in 
prison.” Roller at 123. This change, while couched by the 
State as “procedural,” does not affect some procedure 
concerning Roller’s trial nor can it be said that it simply 
changes the manner in which the State is imposing the 
-25- 


PDF PAGE 17
punishment. 
The fact that there is no guarantee that yearly 
reviews would make any difference in Roller’s ultimate release 
date does not diminish the constitutional error of the State’s 
action. In the case of Lindsey v. Washington, 301 U.S. 397 
(1937), the petitioner committed a burglary at a time when 
there was a minimum sentence of six months and a maximum 
sentence of fifteen years. At the time of sentencing the law 
had changed to provide for a mandatory fifteen year sentence. 
This Court found the application of the mandatory sentence to 
Lindsey to be violative of ex post facto. Although Lindsey 
could have been sentenced to fifteen years under the original 
Statute, such a sentence was not mandated. 
It is true that petitioners might have been 
sentenced to fifteen years under the old statute. 
But the ex post facto clause looks to the 
standard of punishment prescribed by a statute, 
rather than to the sentence actually imposed. 
The Constitution forbids the application of any 
new punitive measure to a crime already 
consummated, to the detriment or material 
disadvantage of the wrongdoer... 
We need not inquire whether this is technically 
an increase in the punishment annexed to the 
crime. It is plainly to the substantial 
disadvantage of the petitioners to be deprived of 
all opportunity to receive a sentence which 
would give them freedom from custody and 
control prior to the expiration of the fifteen-year 
sentence. 
Lindsey v. Washington, 301 U.S. at 401-402. 
While Roller may never be paroled, the proper 
test is whether his opportunity to seek release is being 
adversely affected. Without question Roller is being denied all 
opportunity to seek his release for the one year period after the 
anniversary of his parole eligibility. 
Petitioner argues that there is a distinction 
between initial parole eligibility and subsequent hearing dates. 
That somehow once eligibility is reached, changing the dates 
for consideration for release does not disadvantage Roller. The 
Fourth Circuit rejected this argument finding that "[e]ligibility 
without consideration is cold comfort." Roller, 984 F.2d at 
123. Although the two may be technically different, 
Petitioner’s argument calls to mind the Collins’ admonition that 
-27- 


PDF PAGE 18
"[s]ubtle ex post facto violations are no more permissible than 
overt ones." 497 U.S. at 46. This position also assumes that 
the opportunity to secure release is not a matter of substance. 
As a practical matter, no typical inmate could reasonably be 
expected to distinguish between the initial eligibility and 
subsequent concideration for parole. In each situation a 
hearing is the only means by which the inmate may obtain 
release. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held 
that the distinction was irrelevant for the purposes of the ex 
post facto clause. 
To argue that the disadvantage to Roller imposed 
by South Carolina’s Omnibus Crime Bill is insubstantial and 
therefore constitutional flies in the face of the purpose and 
spirit of the ex post facto clause. A retrospective increase in 
the burden of punishment is constitutionally prohibited. 
The retrospective application of the 1986 parole 
provisions to Roller is in violation of the Ex Post Facto Clause 
of the Constitution. The change is penal in nature, is being 
-28- 
applied retrospectively, is contained within the Calder 
categories, and is to the detriment of the Respondent. While 
the states should be encouraged to fashion and implement 
changes in rehabilitative programs to foster improved 
correctional practices, such changes should not be at the 
expense of time honored constitutional principles. There is 
room in our society for both.
