SUPPLEMENT TO THE STATEMENT OF THE CASE 
Parole eligibility has always been set by statute in 
South Carolina, and it is simply not at issue here. What is 
at issue is the frequency of parole reconsideration hear- 
ings after a parole rejection. And this, we believe, makes 
all the difference. 
Prior to June 15, 1981, the frequency between parole 
hearings was determined by policy of the South Carolina 
1 


PDF PAGE 4
Parole Board on the basis of sentence length, rather than 
by any statute. That policy was this: 
sentence length frequency of 
reconsideration 
hearings upon 
parole rejection 
30 yrs or more 24 months 
25 yrs but less than 30 yrs 21 months 
20 yrs but less than 25 yrs 18 months 
15 yrs but less than 20 yrs 15 months 
less than 15 yrs 12 months 
On June 15, 1981, the General Assembly of South 
Carolina enacted legisiation governing the frequency of 
parole reconsideration hearings upon a rejection for 
parole. The 1981 law required parole reconsideration 
hearings annually for all inmates eligible for parole. 
On June 3, 1986, the legislature further amended the 
law regulating the frequency of parole reconsideration 
hearings. The 1986 amendment first defined certain 
crimes as violent. Then it changed the procedure so that 
inmates who had been convicted of a violent crime and 
who were eligible for parole on their sentence would be 
reconsidered for parole after two years following a rejec- 
tion. Inmates serving sentences for non-violent crimes 
would continue to have annual parole reconsideration 
hearings. This 1986 amendment was applied retroactively. 
In 1989, the South Carolina Supreme Court ruled that the 
retroactive application of the law providing for parole 
reconsideration after two years did not violate the Ex Post 
Facto Clause of the Constitution. Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 
113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989). 
On January 15, 1993, the United States Court of 
Appeals for the Fourth Circuit ruled, contrary to Gunter, 
supra, that the retroactive application of the two-year wait 
between parole reconsideration hearings did violate the 
Ex Post Facto Clause. This Court then granted certiorari. 
On July 19, 1993, the South Carolina Supreme Court 
reversed its decision in Gunter, supra, in Griffin v. State, 
Opinion Number 23909 (July 19, 1993) (Appendix to 
Reply Brief 1).

ARGUMENT 
I. The United States Supreme Court's jurisdiction is 
unaffected by the South Carolina Supreme Court's 
decision in Griffin v. State, Op. No. 23909 (July 19, 
1993) because the South Carolina Supreme Court 
based its decision upon federal law as interpreted 
by the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth 
Circuit. 
The decision in Griffin v. State is based upon federal 
law: 
The Fourth Circuit’s analysis is compelling. It is 
difficult to determine where the difference lies 
between a review once every two years and 
once every eight years. This gray area tortures 
the ex post facto analysis between a change in the 
standards for review and a procedural change in 
timing. The Akins Court, [922 F.2d 1558 (11th 
Cir. 1991), cert. denied, 111 S.Ct. 2915 (1991)] was 
faced with a statute that provided for a pro- 
cedural change which effectively changed the 
standards for parole. We must now acknow!l- 
edge that where a procedural rule is so overly 


PDF PAGE 5
intrusive that it substantively effects [sic] the 
review standard, it then becomes an ex post facto 
violation. In adopting the Fourth Circuit’s hold- 
ing in Roller, vre overrule our holding in Gunter, 
298 S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989). 
It is patently clear that the South Carolina Supreme 
Court’s decision is not based on any independent or 
adequate state ground. The Griffin opinion rests primarily 
upon Roller v. Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d 120 (4th Cir. 1993) and 
its reasoning. 
This Court retains jurisdiction to review a state-court 
decision where it appears “that the state court decided 
the case the way it did because it believed that federal 
law required it to do so.” Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032, 
1041 (1983). The Griffin decision does not render a deci- 
sion by this Court moot since the Griffin decision is 
clearly predicated on the decision of the Fourth Circuit 
now before this Court. 
II. Because he seeks a speedier release from prison, 
Roller may not proceed in federal court under 42 
U.S.C. § 1983; he must first exhaust his state-court 
remedies through habeas corpus 
To support his argument that he is entitled to pro- 
ceed under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, Roller claims that his pro- 
cedural due process has been violated. To come within 
reach of § 1983 he denies outright that what he is seeking 
is a speedier release, and he focuses on procedure. But 
the jurisdictional question cannot be answered in the way 
Roller would have this Court ask it. The question is 
whether or not Roller’s challenge of South Carolina’s 
procedure really amounts to an attempt to gain an imme- 
diate or speedier release. Since Roller seeks more fre- 
quent hearings, he seeks a speedier release. The fact that 
he has a hope of release on parole but not a right to 
parole does not alter the nature of his claim. He attacks 
the procedure in order to win a speedier release. He 
should not be permitted, therefore, to pursue his claim 
under § 1983. He must proceed under habeas corpus. 
And he must exhaust his remedies in the state courts of 
South Carolina. Roller cannot avail himself of § 1983 
simply by claiming that he is entitled to parole. The 
Fourth Circuit’s statement to the contrary ignores the fact 
that Roller is challenging a procedure in order to obtain 
speedier release. Roller v. Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d 120 (4th Cir. 
1993). 
III. The retroactive application of the South Carolina 
law requiring two years between parole consider- 
ation hearings does not deprive Roller unconstitu- 
tionally of a meaningful opportunity to be 
considered for parole. 
Roller argues that the frequency of parole consider- 
ation hearings is a part of the punishment. He is wrong 
about this. And his error leads ineluctably to a patent 
absurdity: any tampering with parole procedure amounts 
to a tampering with a substantive right, so that finally no 
state legislature may ever change its parole procedures 
and then apply those procedures after the fact without 
violating the ex post facto prohibition. Under Roller’s 
view, states are left with almost no room to experiment 
with their parole procedures. 


PDF PAGE 6
In support of his position, Roller relies on the Fourth 
Circuit’s statement that “South Carolina has undoubtedly 
applied its new statute to “alter the conditions 
of .. . [Roller’s] preexisting parole eligibility.” Roller v. 
Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d at 124 (citing Fender v. Thompson, 883 
F.2d 303, 306 (4th Cir. 1989). We see precisely here the 
basic flaw in the Fourth Circuit’s reasoning and in 
Roller’s argument: the equation of parole eligibility with 
parole reconsideration. The Fourth Circuit’s and Roller’s 
conclusion that South Carolina has in effect altered its 
laws on parole eligibility is simply wrong. The law that 
determines parole eligibility remains unchanged. In 
South Carolina, parole eligibility annexes at the time of 
the crime. The frequency of parole hearings once an 
inmate becomes eligible for parole and is rejected for 
parole, however, is altogether another matter. To equate 
the frequency of parole hearings in South Carolina with 
parole eligibility misunderstands South Carolina’s law 
and conveniently avoids deciding the issue on its merits. 
Petitioners rely upon Rodriguez v. U.S. Parole Commis- 
sion, 594 F.2d 170 (7th Cir. 1979). This was a habeas 
corpus action challenging a change in the frequency of 
parole consideration hearings. Rodriguez says that once a 
parole system has been created, inmates subject to that 
system are entitled to a meaningful parole hearing. Fail- 
ure to provide that meaningful parole hearing denies a 
federal constitutional right. Under the facts in Rodriguez, 
the change at issue required a parole hearing upon enter- 
ing prison and a second hearing at or near the expiration 
date of the sentence. Prior to the change, a parole hearing 
would be conducted at the one-third point in the 
offender’s sentence. The Rodriguez Court held the parole 
hearing at the one-third point to be meaningful. The 
change in procedure required a hearing at the beginning 
of the sentence and at the end of the sentence, but not at 
the one-third point. The Court held that the hearing at the 
end of the sentence was not meaningful. The Court then 
concluded that the change in the parole reconsideration 
procedure resulted in the denial of a substantial right - 
the right to a meaningful parole hearing during the 
inmate’s active sentence. This change, the court held, 
made the punishment more burdensome after the com- 
mission of the crime. Beazell v. Ohio, 269 U.S. 167 (1925). 
Therefore, the change violated the Ex Post Factc Clause. 
The holding in Rodriguez is consistent with Collins v. 
Youngblood, 497 U.S. 37 (1990), in which the Court held 
that a change in procedure must be of such moment as to 
cause a denial of a substantial personal right, before it 
can be said to create a constitutional problem under the 
Ex Post Facto Clause. 
In Roller’s case, there has been no retroactive denial 
of the opportunity for a meaningful parole hearing before 
the expiration date of his sentence. He is reconsidered for 
parole every two years, instead of every year. This may 
be somewhat of a disadvantage, but it is certainly not so 
great a disadvantage as to deny him a substantial per- 
sonal right. Although the frequency of Roller’s parole 
hearings has in fact been decreased, every such pro- 
cedural change does not violate the Constitution, even if 
it does marginally disadvantage the inmate. Dobbert v. 
Florida, 432 U.S. 282 (1977).

CONCLUSION 
Roller should not be allowed to bring this action 
under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Notwithstanding his attempt to 
frame the question in terms of procedure alone, what he 
seeks is a speedier release, and that fact forecloses federal 
jurisdiction under § 1983. But even if the Court should 
find that federal jurisdiction is properly founded on 
§ 1983, Roller’s Ex Post Facto claim must fail on the 
merits. For his claim is meritless. The changes to the 
South Carolina parole procedure do not deny Roller a 
substantial personal right. The Fourth Circuit would 
therefore be properly reversed in this case. We ask the 
Court to do so.

TOAL, A.J.: The State appeals from an order grant- 
ing Respondent's application for post-conviction relief. 
We AFFIRM. 
Facts 
The Respondent, Griffin, was indicted for murder in 
April 1984. On May 21, 1984, Griffin appeared before the 


PDF PAGE 8
2a 
court and entered a plea of guilty to voluntary man- 
slaughter. Prior to the acceptance of the plea, the trial 
judge conducted a lengthy colloquy to determine the 
voluntariness of the plea. At the conclusion of the judge’s 
questioning, the plea was accepted and Griffin was sen- 
tenced to thirty years imprisonment. Griffin did not 
appeal his guilty plea or sentence. 
On July 1, 1991, Griffin filed an Application for Post- 
Conviction Relief. In his application, Griffin alleged, inter 
alia, that the State’s application of the Omnibus Crime 
Bill, specifically the change from an annual review for 
parole eligibility to a biannual review, violated the ex post 
facto clause of the South Carolina and United States Con- 
stitutions. The PCR judge found that Griffin relied upon 
the annual review for parole eligibility in his decision to 
plead guilty, and that Griffin’s constitutional rights under 
the ex post facto clause were violated. We granted the 
State’s petition for certiorari to review the PCR court’s 
order. 
Law / Analysis 
The State raises two issues on appeal. The first and 
pivotal issue is whether the PCR judge erred in ruling 
that Griffin suffered a violation of the ex post facto clause. 
The second issue is whether the PCR judge erred in 
finding that Griffin’s plea of guilty to voluntary man- 
slaughter was not knowingly, voluntarily, and intel- 
ligently entered. 
We addressed this first issue in Gunter v. State, 298 
S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989). In Gunter, the applicant 
for post-conviction relief raised an ex post facto challenge 
3a 
to the change in parole eligibility review. Id. After exam- 
ining the question, we held that: 
the standards governing petitioner’s parole eli- 
gibility have not . . . changed. Instead, only the 
frequency with which petitioner can be recon- 
sidered for parole has been altered. We find no 
ex post facto violation in the application of the 
questioned statute to petitioner. 
Id. at 115-16, 378 S.E.2d at 444. 
The PCR court distinguished Gunter on the grounds 
that Griffin had pled guilty and that Gunter was con- 
victed at trial. This is not completely correct. While it is 
true Gunter was tried, convicted, and sentenced to thirty 
years for voluntary manslaughter, we reversed the con- 
viction in State v. Gunter, 286 S.C. 556, 335 S.E.2d 445 
(1985). On remand, Gunter pled guilty to voluntary man- 
slaughter and received a thirteen-year sentence. Our PCR 
holding in Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 
(1989), was rendered while Gunter was serving the thir- 
teen year sentence as a result of his plea of guilty. 
Since Gunter, we have held that “[t]he ex post facto 
clause protects against retroactive legislative provisions 
which are disadvantageous to the offender. A mere pro- 
cedural change in law, not increasing punishment or 
changing elements of the offense, does not result in an ex 
post facto violation.” Elmore v. State, 305 S.C. 456, 459, 409 
S.E.2d 397, 399 (1991); see also Miller v. Florida, 482 U.S. 
423, 107 S. Ct. 2446, 96 L. Ed. 2d 351 (1987). 
Respondent cites the recent Fourth Circuit decision in 
Roller v. Cavanaugh, 984 F.2d 120 (1993). In Roller, the 
Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals disregarded our decision 


PDF PAGE 9
4a 
in Gunter, and held that the statutory amendment for 
review of parole eligibility was a violation of the ex post 
facto clause. The Fourth Circuit relied on Akins v. Snow, 
922 F.2d 1558 (11th Cir. 1991), which found an ex post facto 
violation in a Georgia statute that decreased the fre- 
quency of parole hearings from once a year to every eight 
years. Obviously the Georgia statute was an example of 
how a procedural change could be expanded to have a 
substantive effect. 
The Fourth Circuit’s analysis is compelling. It is diffi- 
cult to determine where the difference lies between a 
review once every two years and once every eight years. 
This gray area tortures the ex post facto analysis between a 
change in the standards for review and a procedural 
change in timing. The Akins court was faced with a stat- 
ute that provided for a procedural change which effec- 
tively changed the standards for parole. We must now 
acknowledge that where a procedural rule is so overly 
intrusive that it substantively effects the review standard, 
it then becomes an ex post facto violation. In adopting the 
Fourth Circuit’s holding in Roller, we overrule our hold- 
ing in Gunter, 298 S.C. 113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989). 
In light of our holding today, we need not reach the 
second issue. Accordingly, for the reasons stated, the 
decision of the PCR court is AFFIRMED.
