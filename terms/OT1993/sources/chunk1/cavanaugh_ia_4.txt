This civil rights complaint brought by a state pris- 
oner proceeding pro se is before the undersigned United 
States Magistrate Judge for a report and recommendation 
on the defendants’ summary judgment motion under 
Rule 56 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. 28 U.S.C. 
§ 636(b). 
The Plaintiff, Gary Lee Roller (Roller), is presently 
incarcerated at a facility of the South Carolina Depart- 
ment of Corrections. Roller was convicted in the Chester 
County Court of General Sessions of voluntary man- 
slaughter and grand larceny committed on December 13, 
1982. He was sentenced on March 25, 1983, to serve thirty 
(30) years for voluntary manslaughter and five (5) years 
consecutive for grand larceny. He initially became eligible 
for parole on October 27, 1990, pursuant to the parole 
eligibility statute in force on the date of his offense. 
15 
Roller received a parole hearing on October 31, 1990, 
at which time he was rejected for parole, notified of the 
reasons for the rejection, and informed that his next hear- 
ing would be October 31, 1992. 
Roller brings this civil rights action pursuant to 42 
U.S.C. § 1983 against Michael J. Cavanaugh, Executive 
Director of the South Carolina Department of Probation, 
Parole and Pardon Services, as well as all present mem- 
bers of the Pardon and Parole Board. The defendants are 
being sued in their official and individual capacities for 
compensatory and punitive damages, declaratory relief, 
and a court order enjoining the defendants from applying 
the South Carolina Omnibus Criminal Justice Improve- 
ment Act (Act) of 1986 related to the manner and timing 
of parole reviews to Roller. 
Roller asserts that the defendants’ application of the 
Act to him increasing the time between parole recon- 
sideration hearings from one (1) to two (2) years is viola- 
tive of the prohibition against ex post facto laws and 
denies him due process under the Constitution of the 
United States. 
Section 24-21-645 of the South Carolina Code of Laws 
(Law. Co-op. 1976) made effective June 15, 1981, provided 
that all inmates would be reviewed for parole annually 
following an initial rejection for parole. That section was 
amended effective June 3, 1986, to provide that a violent 
offender would not be rescheduled for another parole 
hearing for a period of two (2) years from the date of his 
rejection.

Roller contends: 
1. The defendants application of the 1986 
laws to Roller increases the punishment he 
must suffer for his crimes and is thus a 
impermissible retroactive application in vio- 
lation of the ex post facto clause. 
2. Application of the 1986 laws to the 
plaintiff denies him a substantial protected 
liberty interest in violation of the due pro- 
cess clause of the fourteenth amendment of 
the United States Constitution. 
3. The application of 1986 laws to the 
plaintiff violates due process and ex post

tion. 
On June 4, 1991, the defendants filed a motion to 
dismiss or for summary judgment. They argue that they 
are immune from liability under the eleventh amendment 
and not amenable to suit under § 1983. Further, they 
assert they are entitled to defense of qualified immunity. 
In addition, they argue that the plaintiff has not 
exhausted any state remedies as required and that the 
changes in parole procedures do not violate the ex post 
facto or due process clauses of the Constitution. 
A copy of the defendants’ motion and supporting 
memorandum was provided Roller on June 13, 1991, 
along with an explanation of dismissal and summary 
judgment procedures with pertinent extracts from Rules 
12 and 56 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure similar 
to that required by Roseboro v. Garrison, 528 F.2d 309 (4th 
Cir. 1975).

In response, Roller filed his opposition to the defen- 
dants’ motion, memorandum in support of the Opposi- 
tion, his own affidavit, a motion for appointment of 
counsel, and an affidavit in support of motion for 
appointment of counsel. Roller’s motion for appointed 
counsel was denied on July 15, 1991. Hence, it appears 
that the defendants’ motion is ready for consideration. 
A review of the record and relevant case law indi- 
cates that the defendants’ summary judgment motion 
should be granted. 
Roller’s claim for damages against the South Caro- 
lina parole officials in their official capacity is barred by 
the eleventh amendment. See, Edelman v. Jordan, 415 US. 
651, 662-63 (1974). Further, the parole officials sued in 
their individual capacities are entitled to absolute immu- 
nity under Pope v. Chew, 521 F.2d 400 (4th Cir. 1975). In 
Pope, the Fourth Circuit held that parole officials involved 
in the quasi-judicial function of considering parole appli- 
cations are entitled absolute personal immunity. 521 F.2d 
at 405-06. The defendants in their individual capacities 
acting as parole officials are protected by the qualified 
immunity under Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 (1982). 
Further since the constitutional question raised by Roller 
is unsettled, qualified immunity is an acceptable alternate 
ground for dismissal. See, Anderson v. Creighton, 107 S.Ct. 
3034 (1987). Nonetheless, a defendant sued in his or her 
capacity as a Parole Board member could be subject to an 
injunction under the Ex Parte: Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908) 
(exception to eleventh amendment immunity). Roller has 
alleged an ongoing violation bringing his claim within 
the purview of the Young exception. Papasan v. Allain, 478 
U.S. 265 (1986), McConnell v. Adams, 829 F.2d 1319 (4th

18 
Cir. 1987), cert. denied, __ U.S. __, 108 S.Ct. 1731 (1988). 
Therefore, only to the extent that Roller seeks injunctive 
relief preventing these officials future conduct, may this 
action proceed. 
The defendants’ argument that Roller’s action is 
designed ultimately to shorten the period of his incarcer- 
ation and thus is subject to the exhaustion requirement of 
28 U.S.C. § 2254(b) is unfounded. While a § 1983 suit is 
not a substitute for a writ of habeas corpus, here Roller 
seeks only consideration for parole, not release or short- 
ening of the period of incarceration. Prieser v. Rodriguez, 
411 U.S. 475 (1973). Under these circumstances, the 
Fourth Circuit has held that § 1983 may be used to 
challenge parole procedures. Strader v. Troy, 571 F.2d 1263 
(4th Cir. 1978). 
Initially, there is no constitutional right to parole 
unless the state creates a protectable liberty interest in the 
establishment of a parole system. See, Greenholtz v. 
Nebraska Penal Inmates, 442 U.S. 1, 99 S.Ct. 2010 (1979). 
Such an interest arises only when a parole statute pro- 
vides that specific conditions mandate release. Damiano v. 
Florida Parole & Probation Commission, 785 F.2d 929 (11th 
Cir. 1986). 
The plaintiff has no right to parole in South Carolina. 
S.C. Const. Art. IV, § 14. Parole is a matter of grace 
bestowed by the government. Bearden v. State, 443 F.2d 
1090 (1971). State law does not mandate parole even if 
certain conditions are met by an inmate. That is, the 
conditions listed in § 24-21-640 must all be satisfied 
before the Board will consider exercising their discretion 
to release an inmate on parole. In the event the Board 
19 
denies parole to an inmate, it is required to give the 
inmate written notice of the reasons that the parole was 
rejected. It is uncontroverted that Roller was provided 
with the reasons for the denial of his parole. His due 
process claims are without merit. 
Next, Roller alleges that the application of the 1986 
Act to him by the defendants violates the ex post facto 
clause of the United States Constitution. In 1982, when he 
committed his crimes, § 24-21-645 provided that the fre- 
quency of parole reconsideration hearings would be 
every twelve (12) months following an initial rejection for 
parole. In 1986, § 24-21-645 was amended to provide that 
parole reconsideration hearings for persons convicted of 
violent crimes as defined in § 16-1-60 also effective June 
3, 1986, would be held every two (2) years following an 
initial rejection. It is uncontested that the defendants 
have applied these code sections retroactively to Roller. 
The United States Constitution, Art. I, § 10 states in 
pertinent fact: “No state . . . shall pass any bill of attainer 
or ex post facto law ...” In Fender v. Thompson, et al, 883 
F.2d 303 (4th Cir. 1989), the Fourth Circuit held that the ex 
post facto clause “forbids . . . the state to enact any law 
which imposes a punishment for an act which was not 
punishable at the time it was committed, or imposes 
additional punishment than that then prescribed.” Weaver 
v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24 (1981). Two critical elements must 
be present for a criminal or penal law to be ex post facto: it 
must be retrospective, that is it must apply to events

ter is considered a violent crime subject to this section.

occurring before its enactment, and it must disadvantage 
the offender effected by it. Id. at 29.

“[T]he prohibition of ex post facto laws does not 
extend to every change of the law that ‘may 
work to the disadvantage of a defendant.’ It is 
intended to secure ‘substantial personal rights’ 
from retroactive deprivation and does not ‘limit 
the legislative control of remedies and modes of 
procedure which do not effect matters of sub- 
stance.’ (quoting Dolbert v. Florida, 432 U.S. 282, 
293 (1977). 
Portley v. Grossman, 444 U.S. 1311, 1312 (1980). 
In Gunter v. State, the South Carolina Supreme Court 
in addressing this issue held: 
On this issue, we find persuasive the holding of 
the California Supreme Court In Re: Jackson, 39 
Cal.3rd 464, 703 Pac.2d 100 (1985). There the 
court held that a statutory amendment prolong- 
ing the time before reconsideration for parole 
was a procedural change outside the purview of 
the ex post facto clause. The court reasoned that 
the amendment did not alter the criteria by 
which parole suitability is determined, but 
rather only changed the frequency with which 
an inmate was given to demonstrate parole suit- 
ability. By the same token, the standards govern- 
ing the petitioner’s parole eligibility have not 
been changed. Instead, only the frequency with 
which petitioner can be reconsidered for parole 
has been altered. We find no ex post facto viola- 
tion in the application of the questioned statute 
to petitioner. 
378 S.E.2d 443 (S.C. 1989). 
21 
No additional post-conviction punishment has been 
imposed because Roller’s sentence has not been length- 
ened, his possibility of parole has not been revoked, and 
the conditions he must meet to be considered for parole 
eligibility remain the same. The only change is a pro- 
cedural one controlling how often Roller’s case is 
reviewed for parole. 
Lastly, Roller’s allegation that application of the Act 
to him is violative of the South Carolina Constitution 
does not provide a basis for a § 1983 suit. Section 1983 
was intended to vindicate only federal rights determined 
under federal substantive law, not claims arising under 
state law. Weller v. Department of Social Services, 901 F.2d 
387 (4th Cir. 1990); Clark v. Link, 855 F.2d 156 (4th Cir. 
1988); See, Paul v. Davis, 424 U.S. 693 (1976); Riccio v. 
County of Fairfax, 907 F.2d 1459 (4th Cir. 1990). 
In summary, inmates in South Carolina have no pro- 
tected liberty interest in parole; lengthening of the time 
between parole reconsideration from one (1) to two (2) 
years does not offend the due process or ex post facto 
protections of the United States Constitution, and § 1983 
is not available to test application of the Act to Roller 
under the South Carolina Constitution.

OPINION 
HALL, Circuit Judge: 
Gary Lee Roller appeals an order of the district court 
granting summary judgment for the defendants in 
Roller’s 42 U.S.C. § 1983 suit challenging the frequency of 
his parole reconsideration hearings. We must decide 
whether a statutory amendment increasing the length of 
time between parole reconsiderations from every year to 
every two years is an unconstitutional ex post facto law if 
applied to prisoners whose crimes were committed before 
the amendment. Concluding that it is, we reverse. 
In early 1983, Gary Roller was convicted of voluntary 
manslaughter and grand larceny in South Carolina state 
court. Both of these crimes occurred on December 13, 
1982. On March 25, 1983, he was sentenced to consecutive 
terms of imprisonment of thirty and five years. At that 
time, a South Carolina statute provided that, within 
ninety days of the prisoner's becoming eligible for parole, 
the Parole Board would review the case and determine 


PDF PAGE 18
30 
whether to grant parole. If the Board decided not to grant 
parole, “the prisoner’s case shall be reviewed every 
twelve months thereafter for the purpose of such deter- 
mination.” Former S.C. Code 24-21-620 (1976 & Supp. 
1981). In 1986, this statute was amended to make recon- 
sideration of parole for violent offenders less frequent - 
every two years. S.C. Code § 24-21-645 (1989 & Supp. 
1991). The date at which a prisoner initially becomes 
eligible for parole, however, was not changed. 
In 1990, Roller became eligible for parole. On October 
31, 1990, the Board rejected parole, and advised Roller 
that his next hearing would be held October 31, 1992.! 
On December 28, 1990, Roller filed this suit under 42 
U.S.C. § 1983 against the members of the Parole Board in 
their official and personal capacities. He sought mone- 
tary, declaratory, and injunctive relief. The gravamen of 
his claim was that application of the less-frequent parole 
review to him violated the federal constitution’s prohibi- 
tion of ex post facto laws. 
The defendants moved for summary judgment. The 
magistrate recommended that the motion be granted. 
Over Roller’s objections, the district court adopted the 
magistrate’s recommendation. 
Roller appeals.

III. 
A. 
The Constitution prohibits both the federal and state 
legislatures from passing ex post facto laws. U.S. Const. art 
1, § 9, cl.3 and § 10 cl. 1. Neither Congress nor the states 
may “enact any law ‘which imposes a punishment for an 
act which was not punishable at the time it was commit- 
ted; or imposes additional punishment to that then 
described.” ” Weaver v. Graham, 450 U.S. 24, 28 (1981) 
(quoting Cummings v. Missouri, 4 Wall. 277, 325-26 (1867)). 
Though there is no constitutional requirement that a state 
permit parole or early release from confinement, Green- 
holtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex, 
442 U.S. 1 (1979), statutes providing for parole are “part 
33 
of the law annexed to the crime at the time of the per- 
son’s offense.” Schwartz v. Muncy, 834 F.2d 396, 398 n.8 
(4th Cir.1987) (quoted in Fender v. Thompson, 883 F.2d 303, 
305 (4th Cir. 1989). 
The ex post facto clauses are a restriction on the power 
of government and operate without regard to the affirma- 
tive “rights” of the individual. Consequently, it is imma- 
terial to an ex post facto challenge to computation of a 
release or parole eligibility date that the prisoner does not 
have a “vested” liberty or property right in parole, “good 
time,” or the like. Weaver, 450 U.S. at 29-30 (criticizing 
state court for rejecting ex post facto claim by finding no 
“vested right” to good time credits). 
The South Carolina Supreme Court has rejected a 
challenge identical to Roller’s. Gunter v. State, 298 S.C. 
113, 378 S.E.2d 443 (1989). 
Four of our sister courts of appeals have directly 
addressed whether a retroactive reduction in the fre- 
quency of parole consideration violates the ex post facto 
clause. Three have held that it does, though the Ninth 
Circuit’s opinion was vacated on other grounds and is 
thus a nullity. Rodriguez v. United States Parole Comm'n, 
594 F.2d 170 97th Cir. 1979) (parole reconsideration 
changed from one-third point in sentence to every eigh- 
teen months; ex post facto clause violated as to a prisoner 
serving only a two-year sentence); Watson v. Estelle, 859 
F.2d 105 (9th Cir. 1988), vacated, 886 F.2d 1093 (1989); 
Akins v. Snow, 922 F.2d 1558 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 111 
S.Ct. 2915 (1991); but see Bailey v. Gardebring, 940 F.2d 1150

(8th Cir. 1991), cert. denied, 112 S.Ct. 1516 (1992) (lead

result; the third dissented). 
In Akins, the Eleventh Circuit considered a Georgia 
statute that decreased the frequency of parole hearings 
from once a year to once every eight years. The court held 
that this change affected the substantive rights of persons

S.C. Code Ann. § 24-21-650 (1976) enacted 
June 3, 1986 (parole hearing rescheduling) 
“Section 24-21-650. The Board shall issue an order 
authorizing the parole which must be signed by at least a 
41 
majority of its members, with terms and conditions, if 
any; provided, that at least two-thirds of the members of 
the Board must sign orders authorizing parole for per- 
sons convicted of a violent crime as defined in Section 
16-1-60. Thereupon the supervisor of parole, or one law- 
fully acting for him, shall issue a parole order, which, if 
accepted by the prisoner, shall provide for his release 
from custody. 
Provided, that upon a negative determination of 
parole, prisoners in confinement for a violent crime as 
defined in Section 16-1-60 must have their cases reviewed 
every two years for the purpose of a determination of 
parole.” 
S.C. Code Ann. § 16-1-60 (1976) enacted 
June 3, 1986 
(Definition of violent crime) 
“Section 16-1-60. For purposes of definition under 
South Carolina law a violent crime includes the offenses 
of murder, criminal sexual conduct in the first and second 
degree, assault and battery with intent to kil!, kidnap- 
ping, voluntary manslaughter, armed robbery, drug traf- 
ficking as defined in Section 44-53-370(e), arson in the 
first degree, burglary in the first degree, and burglary in 
the second degree under Section 16-11-312(B).”
