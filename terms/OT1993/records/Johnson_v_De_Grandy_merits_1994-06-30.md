## Controlling holdings

### 1. The state validation does not preclude the reserved factual claims

**Controlling proposition:** Under 28 U.S.C. §1738, Florida's validation receives its actual state-law preclusive scope, including its express preservation of later factual Section 2 challenges; it therefore does not bar these private claims. The availability of a return to state court does not require exhaustion of that optional procedure before this preserved federal litigation.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt this limited rule. Thomas and Scalia do not join the opinion, and their different statutory disposition supplies no additional published joinder.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respecting a state judgment requires respecting its limits as well as its conclusions. Marrese requires the federal court to begin with the preclusive effect supplied by state law. Allen preserves ordinary preclusion principles without making every federal-rights claim immune from them. Florida's constitutional validation took place on a thirty-day timetable and expressly preserved subsequent factual challenges under Section 2. The private plaintiffs now present the kind of challenge that remained available; their amended complaints concern the new plan. The State's interest in finality cannot convert that limited validation into a completed adjudication of the reserved claims. Growe requires legal effect for an operative state plan, but does not insulate it from a properly pleaded federal defect. Nor does permission to seek further state review make that route mandatory. The Court creates no general Voting Rights Act exception to Section 1738 and decides no preclusion question beyond the judgment's established reservation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373: applies the state-law-first rule for the preclusive effect of a state judgment; no additional federal preclusion is imposed.
- Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90: preserves ordinary preclusion and its qualifications; creates no categorical federal-forum exemption for Voting Rights Act claims.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: follows its requirement to respect the legally operative state plan and distinguishes its obsolete-plan defect; no enlarged party-preclusion rule follows.

### 2. The United States brings an original enforcement suit

**Controlling proposition:** Rooker and Feldman do not bar the United States' independent Section 2 enforcement suit against this plan: the United States was not a party to the state validation and seeks no lower-federal appellate review of a state judgment entered against it. The holding does not decide every nonparty, privity, intervention or intertwined-claim question or permit review of a preclearance determination.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the original-suit distinction on these facts. Thomas and Scalia publish no join in this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this enforcement action falls within original federal jurisdiction. Rooker denies federal district courts appellate authority over a state judicial judgment. Feldman preserves that limit while distinguishing an independent challenge from review of a state court's determination. The United States did not participate in Florida's validation proceeding and has no adverse state judgment of its own rights to overturn. It brings its statutory claim against the enacted electoral arrangement. The fact that success could unsettle a court-approved map does not itself transform that original suit into an appeal. The Government's other federal litigation and preclearance responsibilities did not make it a party to the state proceeding. The Court need not classify every aspect of state plan validation as legislative or judicial, nor reconsider ordinary preclusion. Shaw's separate restriction on review of federal preclearance remains intact; no such review is sought here. Jurisdiction permits adjudication of the claim and does not establish its merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co., 263 U.S. 413: applies the limit on lower-federal appellate review of state judgments; this independent enforcement action does not invoke that authority.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 460 U.S. 462: preserves the appellate/original distinction without deciding every possible relationship between nonparties and a state judgment.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the designated channel and nonreviewability limits for federal preclearance; those limits do not immunize a state plan from an independent statutory claim.

### 3. Necessary voting conditions do not require maximization

**Controlling proposition:** This Section 2 fragmentation claim requires a minority sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a district majority, political cohesion, and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate; the claimant must also establish unequal opportunity to participate and elect chosen representatives under the totality of circumstances, without proving discriminatory purpose. Even when those necessary conditions are satisfied, Section 2 does not require every feasible additional majority-minority district; the disputed first condition is assumed here, leaving citizenship, population-denominator and coalition or influence-claim questions unresolved.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the complete necessary-versus-sufficient distinction and rejection of maximization. Thomas and Scalia's statutory noncoverage position is a different rationale and supplies no join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a possible additional district identifies potential electoral opportunity, not an automatic statutory entitlement. Gingles supplies the conditions connecting the challenged arrangement to the asserted dilution. Growe applies those conditions to single-member fragmentation and requires local voting evidence rather than addition of distinct groups' populations. Voinovich keeps the burden on challengers to establish unequal opportunity and distinguishes state districting choices from judicial relief for a proved violation. Holder likewise separates a workable comparison from proof of injury and from remedy. Those decisions retain the statutory totality inquiry. Here the Court assumes the contested first condition in the challengers' favor and accepts the findings of cohesion within each minority group and polarized voting. Neither those premises nor the feasibility of additional districts dispenses with the remaining inquiry. Congress preserved a results claim while disclaiming proportional officeholding. The Court therefore rejects both an automatic maximization requirement and a new discriminatory-purpose requirement, without selecting a citizenship measure or deciding an influence or coalition entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its causal conditions, special-circumstances qualification and independent totality inquiry; the conditions do not establish liability by themselves.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: preserves local cohesion and majority-bloc proof for single-member fragmentation and the unresolved coalition, influence and population questions.
- Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993: follows the challengers' burden and the distinction between a State's authority to choose districts and a court's power to remedy a proved violation.
- Holder v. Hall, June 30, 1994: applies its separation of coverage, comparison, proof and remedy; a permissible comparison creates no maximization command.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the independent statutory elements; its constitutional injury rule adds no further element to Section 2.

### 4. Proportional opportunity is relevant evidence, not a safe harbor

**Controlling proposition:** The relationship between a group's share of the relevant population and the share of districts in which it has an effective opportunity to elect its preferred candidates is relevant evidence within Section 2's totality inquiry, whose weight depends on the other evidence; it is neither a conclusive safe harbor nor a burden-shifting affirmative defense, and disproportionality alone likewise does not establish liability. The inquiry concerns demonstrated electoral opportunity, not nominal district labels or the race of officeholders; it guarantees no proportional election, racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, or universal geographic or population denominator, and cannot excuse proved local packing, fragmentation or another barrier merely because opportunities exist elsewhere.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices directly adopt this contextual rule and its explanation. Kennedy separately accepts the exact limited propositions that proportionality is relevant, that the necessary conditions alone do not establish liability, and that proportionality supplies no safe harbor; his concurrence does not join Part III's full rationale or Parts IV and V's affirmative applications. No combined rationale under Marks is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual opportunity matters to the statutory question. Gingles requires an appraisal of the local electoral system under the totality, and Voinovich rejects liability inferred solely from a preferred district arrangement. A relationship between effective districts and population may bear substantially on whether an asserted lack of opportunity exists. But district opportunity, election outcomes and officeholding by racial identity are different facts. The statutory permission to consider protected-class electoral success does not guarantee proportional election or make any numerical relationship decisive. History, polarization and particular barriers retain their force when the aggregate appears favorable. Thus a State cannot defend proved local packing or fragmentation simply by pointing to compensating opportunities elsewhere. Conversely, an arithmetical shortfall does not itself prove the statutory wrong. The State's conclusive-defense proposal fails, as does treating statewide measurement as indispensable to that supposed defense. The Court uses the locally tried record without announcing a general geographic rule or resolving unnecessary preservation questions under Rule 8(c).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its contextual inquiry and treatment of electoral success; adopts no conclusive proportional-success presumption.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the need for proof of actual unequal opportunity rather than automatic condemnation or approval of a district form.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves independent totality proof after an adequate comparison; its local institutional comparison supplies neither a quota nor an evidentiary safe harbor.

### 5. The House liability and dependent remedy cannot stand on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the disputed first Gingles condition in the challengers' favor and retaining the supported findings of discrimination and polarized voting, the tried House record does not establish unequal opportunity under the totality beyond the erroneous inference that every feasible additional Hispanic district must be created. The liability judgment and dependent substitution of Modified De Grandy Plan 268 are reversed; no new liability trial, Supreme Court replacement map or numerical district minimum is ordered, and implementation must follow lawful election administration.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports reversal and the mandate under Parts II and VI on the narrower rejection of maximization, without joining Part IV; the House disposition therefore has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the House judgment used the wrong measure of injury. The findings of continuing discriminatory effects and polarization remain serious and accepted. Feasibility is assumed favorably. Nine effective Hispanic districts accompany about half the voting-age population in the eighteen primarily-Dade districts; the distinct twenty-district frame yields nine opportunities against about 47 percent Hispanic voting-age population. Those relationships are evidence, not immunity. The Court also considers testimony about split neighborhoods in District 116, concentration in District 114, and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. No finding establishes inconsistent standards for comparable minority and other neighborhoods; such a finding is not a new prerequisite to liability. The credited testimony, history and voting evidence do not establish the asserted unequal opportunity merely because two more districts are feasible. The liability announcement and insistence on eleven districts instead made that possibility obligatory. Gingles requires respect for local findings under Rule 52(a), but does not insulate the legal error defining the ultimate injury. Voinovich and Holder require a proved wrong before federal relief. The dependent remedy consequently falls without adjudicating its precise boundaries or reopening liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows the totality standard and Rule 52(a)'s protection of supported findings; corrects a legally infected liability determination without creating a ratio presumption or new intent requirement.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: applies the need for an established federal violation before judicial imposition of remedial districts.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: distinguishes a sufficient comparison from the separate proof of unequal opportunity; its established liability and remedy remand do not supply missing liability here.

### 6. The Senate judgment stands because no violation was proved

**Controlling proposition:** On the tried Senate record, the asserted unequal opportunity is not established under the totality merely because an additional Hispanic district and a different additional Black district are each feasible; the judgment leaving the State's Senate plan operative is affirmed for lack of a proved violation, not because a proved violation may remain when proposed remedies conflict. The existing Black electoral opportunity with crossover support is relevant evidence, not a second Black-majority district, proof of general cohesion between Hispanic and Black voters, or a legal entitlement to a crossover district.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part V, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports affirmance under Parts II and VI on the narrower no-maximization ground without joining Part V; the Senate disposition has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Senate result rests on absence of statutory injury. The July 2 judgment found no violation, while the July 17 explanation described violations but competing remedies. The latter reasoning cannot excuse a proved wrong. Gingles requires examination of actual opportunity, and Holder requires lawful relief once injury is established. Here the seven-district area has three effective Hispanic-majority districts and one Black-majority district against respective voting-age shares of 44.8 and 15.8 percent. A further district supplies demonstrated Black electoral opportunity with crossover support. The Court considers those opportunities with the accepted discrimination and polarization findings; no ratio alone controls. The five wholly-Dade districts and broader South Florida account remain distinct. Separate cohesion within the two groups does not establish a joint coalition. The possibility of maximizing each group's districts separately does not first prove two injuries and then create a conflict of federal rights. Voinovich preserves the difference between feasible arrangements and required remedies. No additional Senate district follows, and no separate Black appeal or influence-district entitlement is decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: applies contextual unequal-opportunity proof without inferring liability from separately feasible district increases.
- Growe, February 23, 1993: preserves the need for proved common cohesion before distinct groups can be treated as one electoral coalition.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the unresolved influence-claim question and ties compelled relief to an established violation.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves the obligation to formulate lawful relief for an established wrong; no competing-remedies exception to liability or relief is recognized.

## Holdings

### 1. The state validation does not preclude the reserved factual claims

**Controlling proposition:** Under 28 U.S.C. §1738, Florida's validation receives its actual state-law preclusive scope, including its express preservation of later factual Section 2 challenges; it therefore does not bar these private claims. The availability of a return to state court does not require exhaustion of that optional procedure before this preserved federal litigation.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-A, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt this limited rule. Thomas and Scalia do not join the opinion, and their different statutory disposition supplies no additional published joinder.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that respecting a state judgment requires respecting its limits as well as its conclusions. Marrese requires the federal court to begin with the preclusive effect supplied by state law. Allen preserves ordinary preclusion principles without making every federal-rights claim immune from them. Florida's constitutional validation took place on a thirty-day timetable and expressly preserved subsequent factual challenges under Section 2. The private plaintiffs now present the kind of challenge that remained available; their amended complaints concern the new plan. The State's interest in finality cannot convert that limited validation into a completed adjudication of the reserved claims. Growe requires legal effect for an operative state plan, but does not insulate it from a properly pleaded federal defect. Nor does permission to seek further state review make that route mandatory. The Court creates no general Voting Rights Act exception to Section 1738 and decides no preclusion question beyond the judgment's established reservation.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373: applies the state-law-first rule for the preclusive effect of a state judgment; no additional federal preclusion is imposed.
- Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90: preserves ordinary preclusion and its qualifications; creates no categorical federal-forum exemption for Voting Rights Act claims.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: follows its requirement to respect the legally operative state plan and distinguishes its obsolete-plan defect; no enlarged party-preclusion rule follows.

### 2. The United States brings an original enforcement suit

**Controlling proposition:** Rooker and Feldman do not bar the United States' independent Section 2 enforcement suit against this plan: the United States was not a party to the state validation and seeks no lower-federal appellate review of a state judgment entered against it. The holding does not decide every nonparty, privity, intervention or intertwined-claim question or permit review of a preclearance determination.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part I-B, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the original-suit distinction on these facts. Thomas and Scalia publish no join in this part.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that this enforcement action falls within original federal jurisdiction. Rooker denies federal district courts appellate authority over a state judicial judgment. Feldman preserves that limit while distinguishing an independent challenge from review of a state court's determination. The United States did not participate in Florida's validation proceeding and has no adverse state judgment of its own rights to overturn. It brings its statutory claim against the enacted electoral arrangement. The fact that success could unsettle a court-approved map does not itself transform that original suit into an appeal. The Government's other federal litigation and preclearance responsibilities did not make it a party to the state proceeding. The Court need not classify every aspect of state plan validation as legislative or judicial, nor reconsider ordinary preclusion. Shaw's separate restriction on review of federal preclearance remains intact; no such review is sought here. Jurisdiction permits adjudication of the claim and does not establish its merits.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co., 263 U.S. 413: applies the limit on lower-federal appellate review of state judgments; this independent enforcement action does not invoke that authority.
- District of Columbia Court of Appeals v. Feldman, 460 U.S. 462: preserves the appellate/original distinction without deciding every possible relationship between nonparties and a state judgment.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the designated channel and nonreviewability limits for federal preclearance; those limits do not immunize a state plan from an independent statutory claim.

### 3. Necessary voting conditions do not require maximization

**Controlling proposition:** This Section 2 fragmentation claim requires a minority sufficiently large and geographically compact to form a district majority, political cohesion, and majority bloc voting ordinarily sufficient to defeat its preferred candidate, absent special circumstances such as an unopposed minority candidate; the claimant must also establish unequal opportunity to participate and elect chosen representatives under the totality of circumstances, without proving discriminatory purpose. Even when those necessary conditions are satisfied, Section 2 does not require every feasible additional majority-minority district; the disputed first condition is assumed here, leaving citizenship, population-denominator and coalition or influence-claim questions unresolved.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part II, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy and Ginsburg; seven Justices directly adopt the complete necessary-versus-sufficient distinction and rejection of maximization. Thomas and Scalia's statutory noncoverage position is a different rationale and supplies no join.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that a possible additional district identifies potential electoral opportunity, not an automatic statutory entitlement. Gingles supplies the conditions connecting the challenged arrangement to the asserted dilution. Growe applies those conditions to single-member fragmentation and requires local voting evidence rather than addition of distinct groups' populations. Voinovich keeps the burden on challengers to establish unequal opportunity and distinguishes state districting choices from judicial relief for a proved violation. Holder likewise separates a workable comparison from proof of injury and from remedy. Those decisions retain the statutory totality inquiry. Here the Court assumes the contested first condition in the challengers' favor and accepts the findings of cohesion within each minority group and polarized voting. Neither those premises nor the feasibility of additional districts dispenses with the remaining inquiry. Congress preserved a results claim while disclaiming proportional officeholding. The Court therefore rejects both an automatic maximization requirement and a new discriminatory-purpose requirement, without selecting a citizenship measure or deciding an influence or coalition entitlement.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Thornburg v. Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its causal conditions, special-circumstances qualification and independent totality inquiry; the conditions do not establish liability by themselves.
- Growe v. Emison, February 23, 1993: preserves local cohesion and majority-bloc proof for single-member fragmentation and the unresolved coalition, influence and population questions.
- Voinovich v. Quilter, March 2, 1993: follows the challengers' burden and the distinction between a State's authority to choose districts and a court's power to remedy a proved violation.
- Holder v. Hall, June 30, 1994: applies its separation of coverage, comparison, proof and remedy; a permissible comparison creates no maximization command.
- Shaw v. Reno, June 28, 1993: preserves the independent statutory elements; its constitutional injury rule adds no further element to Section 2.

### 4. Proportional opportunity is relevant evidence, not a safe harbor

**Controlling proposition:** The relationship between a group's share of the relevant population and the share of districts in which it has an effective opportunity to elect its preferred candidates is relevant evidence within Section 2's totality inquiry, whose weight depends on the other evidence; it is neither a conclusive safe harbor nor a burden-shifting affirmative defense, and disproportionality alone likewise does not establish liability. The inquiry concerns demonstrated electoral opportunity, not nominal district labels or the race of officeholders; it guarantees no proportional election, racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, or universal geographic or population denominator, and cannot excuse proved local packing, fragmentation or another barrier merely because opportunities exist elsewhere.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part III, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices directly adopt this contextual rule and its explanation. Kennedy separately accepts the exact limited propositions that proportionality is relevant, that the necessary conditions alone do not establish liability, and that proportionality supplies no safe harbor; his concurrence does not join Part III's full rationale or Parts IV and V's affirmative applications. No combined rationale under Marks is needed.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that actual opportunity matters to the statutory question. Gingles requires an appraisal of the local electoral system under the totality, and Voinovich rejects liability inferred solely from a preferred district arrangement. A relationship between effective districts and population may bear substantially on whether an asserted lack of opportunity exists. But district opportunity, election outcomes and officeholding by racial identity are different facts. The statutory permission to consider protected-class electoral success does not guarantee proportional election or make any numerical relationship decisive. History, polarization and particular barriers retain their force when the aggregate appears favorable. Thus a State cannot defend proved local packing or fragmentation simply by pointing to compensating opportunities elsewhere. Conversely, an arithmetical shortfall does not itself prove the statutory wrong. The State's conclusive-defense proposal fails, as does treating statewide measurement as indispensable to that supposed defense. The Court uses the locally tried record without announcing a general geographic rule or resolving unnecessary preservation questions under Rule 8(c).

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows its contextual inquiry and treatment of electoral success; adopts no conclusive proportional-success presumption.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the need for proof of actual unequal opportunity rather than automatic condemnation or approval of a district form.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves independent totality proof after an adequate comparison; its local institutional comparison supplies neither a quota nor an evidentiary safe harbor.

### 5. The House liability and dependent remedy cannot stand on this record

**Controlling proposition:** Assuming the disputed first Gingles condition in the challengers' favor and retaining the supported findings of discrimination and polarized voting, the tried House record does not establish unequal opportunity under the totality beyond the erroneous inference that every feasible additional Hispanic district must be created. The liability judgment and dependent substitution of Modified De Grandy Plan 268 are reversed; no new liability trial, Supreme Court replacement map or numerical district minimum is ordered, and implementation must follow lawful election administration.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part IV, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports reversal and the mandate under Parts II and VI on the narrower rejection of maximization, without joining Part IV; the House disposition therefore has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the House judgment used the wrong measure of injury. The findings of continuing discriminatory effects and polarization remain serious and accepted. Feasibility is assumed favorably. Nine effective Hispanic districts accompany about half the voting-age population in the eighteen primarily-Dade districts; the distinct twenty-district frame yields nine opportunities against about 47 percent Hispanic voting-age population. Those relationships are evidence, not immunity. The Court also considers testimony about split neighborhoods in District 116, concentration in District 114, and fragmentation involving Districts 102 and 109. No finding establishes inconsistent standards for comparable minority and other neighborhoods; such a finding is not a new prerequisite to liability. The credited testimony, history and voting evidence do not establish the asserted unequal opportunity merely because two more districts are feasible. The liability announcement and insistence on eleven districts instead made that possibility obligatory. Gingles requires respect for local findings under Rule 52(a), but does not insulate the legal error defining the ultimate injury. Voinovich and Holder require a proved wrong before federal relief. The dependent remedy consequently falls without adjudicating its precise boundaries or reopening liability.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: follows the totality standard and Rule 52(a)'s protection of supported findings; corrects a legally infected liability determination without creating a ratio presumption or new intent requirement.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: applies the need for an established federal violation before judicial imposition of remedial districts.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: distinguishes a sufficient comparison from the separate proof of unequal opportunity; its established liability and remedy remand do not supply missing liability here.

### 6. The Senate judgment stands because no violation was proved

**Controlling proposition:** On the tried Senate record, the asserted unequal opportunity is not established under the totality merely because an additional Hispanic district and a different additional Black district are each feasible; the judgment leaving the State's Senate plan operative is affirmed for lack of a proved violation, not because a proved violation may remain when proposed remedies conflict. The existing Black electoral opportunity with crossover support is relevant evidence, not a second Black-majority district, proof of general cohesion between Hispanic and Black voters, or a legal entitlement to a crossover district.

**Authority:** Souter's Opinion of the Court, Part V, joined by Stone-Zsela, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor and Ginsburg; six Justices adopt this full contextual application. Kennedy supports affirmance under Parts II and VI on the narrower no-maximization ground without joining Part V; the Senate disposition has seven votes, against Thomas and Scalia's vacatur-and-dismissal alternative.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court holds that the Senate result rests on absence of statutory injury. The July 2 judgment found no violation, while the July 17 explanation described violations but competing remedies. The latter reasoning cannot excuse a proved wrong. Gingles requires examination of actual opportunity, and Holder requires lawful relief once injury is established. Here the seven-district area has three effective Hispanic-majority districts and one Black-majority district against respective voting-age shares of 44.8 and 15.8 percent. A further district supplies demonstrated Black electoral opportunity with crossover support. The Court considers those opportunities with the accepted discrimination and polarization findings; no ratio alone controls. The five wholly-Dade districts and broader South Florida account remain distinct. Separate cohesion within the two groups does not establish a joint coalition. The possibility of maximizing each group's districts separately does not first prove two injuries and then create a conflict of federal rights. Voinovich preserves the difference between feasible arrangements and required remedies. No additional Senate district follows, and no separate Black appeal or influence-district entitlement is decided.

**Precedent treatment:**

- Gingles, 478 U.S. 30: applies contextual unequal-opportunity proof without inferring liability from separately feasible district increases.
- Growe, February 23, 1993: preserves the need for proved common cohesion before distinct groups can be treated as one electoral coalition.
- Voinovich, March 2, 1993: preserves the unresolved influence-claim question and ties compelled relief to an established violation.
- Holder, June 30, 1994: preserves the obligation to formulate lawful relief for an established wrong; no competing-remedies exception to liability or relief is recognized.

## Precedent Treatment

The holding-specific treatments govern. Gingles's causal conditions, independent totality inquiry and protection of supported local findings remain; the Court rejects neither dilution coverage nor the 1982 results standard. Growe and Voinovich retain state responsibility, local proof requirements, the challengers' burden and the distinction between a permissible state district choice and a judicial remedy for a proved wrong. Holder's coverage, reasonable-comparison, proof and remedy holdings remain distinct; none creates maximization.

Allen and Marrese govern the actual preclusive scope of the state validation. Rooker and Feldman retain their lower-federal appellate limit. Shaw's constitutional boundary and its separate federal-preclearance channel remain unchanged. Presley v. Etowah County Commission, January 27, 1992, preserves the Section 5 voting/governing boundary and independent Section 2 claims; it supplies no nonretrogression defense to these Dade claims. No precedent is overruled. The dissent's contrary statutory construction is noncontrolling.

## Law After Decision

Effective June 30, 1994, the ordinary Section 2 fragmentation predicates remain necessary but do not establish liability without unequal opportunity under the totality. The Court expressly rejects maximization and a conclusive proportionality defense. Relevant proportionality concerns effective electoral opportunity and must be assessed with the complete local evidence; it does not guarantee proportional election, shift the claimant's burden or excuse proved local injury. The full contextual House and Senate applications have six direct adherents; the mandate and the necessary-versus-sufficient rule have seven. No fractured-opinion combination is required.

The disputed first Gingles condition is assumed for decision. Citizenship methods, the population denominator, a universal geographic scale, coalition cognizability, influence claims and adaptation of the majority-size condition remain unresolved. The Court neither decides a general Rule 8(c) preservation rule nor replaces the tried local claim with an untried statewide claim. Section 2's race, color and language-minority coverage and its permissive consideration of protected-class electoral success remain distinct from the disclaimer of proportional officeholding; no question under the language-minority cross-reference is separately decided.

Supported findings of history and polarization remain. Inconsistent treatment of comparable neighborhoods is not made a mandatory element, discriminatory purpose is not required, and no statutory violation may be excused because proposed remedies conflict. Constitutional claims retain their own elements, and preclearance supplies no general immunity. These appeals determine the presented statutory claims, not the legality of every racial district configuration or a future electoral record.

## Separate Writings

### O'Connor's concurrence

O'Connor joins the Court throughout and emphasizes the proper evidentiary role of proportionality. The relationship between effective opportunity districts and population is relevant to the ultimate statutory inquiry and must be considered when the record supplies it. Its relevance does not depend on a presumption that a particular racial ratio defines the right. Gingles's local appraisal remains necessary, and other evidence can show unequal opportunity despite aggregate proportionality. Conversely, a shortfall does not itself establish dilution. Her concurrence creates no affirmative-defense burden shift, district quota, statewide denominator or constitutional approval. It explains why neither ignoring the evidence nor making it conclusive states the Court's rule.

### Kennedy's concurrence in part and in the judgment

Kennedy joins the limited procedural holdings, the necessary-versus-sufficient distinction, the rejection of maximization and the mandate. Voinovich separates a possible districting arrangement from proved injury; Holder's comparison dispute likewise illustrates why a desired alternative does not itself measure the statutory right. The lower court's treatment of feasible additional districts as required supplies his ground for reversing the House judgment and affirming the Senate result.

Kennedy separately accepts that proportionality has limited relevance, that the Gingles conditions do not automatically establish liability and that proportionality cannot provide a safe harbor. He withholds the Court's full affirmative application of proportionality and any suggestion that this statutory disposition constitutionally validates race-conscious districting. His constitutional concern is a reservation consistent with his dissent in Shaw, not the governing rule of that case. Shaw's rejection of the design-only theory remains the Court's law, and no constitutional claim is presented here. His concurrence supplies no racial floor or ceiling, statewide offset, requirement to merge minority groups, or order to create an additional district. The six-Justice full application controls without expanding his joins.

### Thomas's dissent, joined by Scalia

Thomas and Scalia would vacate the appealed judgments and remand with instructions to dismiss the consolidated Section 2 actions for failure to state a covered claim. They maintain the statutory construction stated in their Holder dissent: the Act protects registration, ballot access, casting and proper counting, rather than the allocation of electoral weight through district boundaries. In their view, subsection (b)'s results inquiry operates within the practices covered by subsection (a); it does not create a districting-dilution cause of action. They would preserve a results test for practices they regard as covered, without requiring discriminatory intent for those practices.

Their construction would reject Gingles's contrary dilution rule and the incompatible portions of Growe and Voinovich, despite their earlier joins. They recognize that current majority law takes the other view. The difficulty of selecting a theory of group representation reinforces their objection, but does not turn their broader construction into a join in the Court's no-maximization or proportionality analysis. Nor do they merely affirm the Senate judgment on a different measure of statutory injury: their proposed vacatur and dismissal is a different formal mandate. They adjudicate no constitutional claim and would not revive dismissed claims, reopen congressional proceedings or disturb the Escambia settlement. Their disagreement changes no controlling precedent.

## Procedure After Action

The District Court's House liability judgment and dependent Plan 268 substitution are reversed; its operative Senate judgment is affirmed. Proceedings below must implement that partial reversal and affirmance through lawful election administration. No renewed Section 2 liability trial, additional Senate district, Supreme Court replacement map, special election timetable or retained Supreme Court supervision is ordered. Congressional proceedings, the Escambia settlement, dismissed claims and preclearance determinations remain undisturbed. Supreme Court merits review ends; ordinary mandate procedure applies, with no special issuance date or later filing established.
