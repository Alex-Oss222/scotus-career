## Continuity — published noncontrolling positions

O'Connor's plurality, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia and Ginsburg, identifies the constitutional source before asking whether §1983 provides relief. Graham directs seizure-related harms to the Fourth Amendment, and Collins cautions against converting a state tort into an unidentified substantive guarantee. Albright surrendered, posted bond and faced travel conditions; those restraints cannot be dismissed as harmless simply because they stopped short of prolonged incarceration. The plurality nevertheless rejects the asserted freestanding substantive right to prosecution only on probable cause. It preserves Soldal and James Daniel Good: an independently applicable due-process protection can coexist with a seizure rule. Gerstein distinguishes a restraint requiring probable cause from supervision of the decision to prosecute. Williams's confined federal supervisory protection against a materially distorted grand-jury presentation does not decide this Illinois constitutional damages claim. The plurality adopts no general ban on charging oversight, exclusive criminal due-process methodology, exhaustion rule, immunity revision or Fourth Amendment merits determination. Its four-Justice reasoning is noncontrolling.

Scalia concurs separately, with no joiner, and joins the plurality in full. He emphasizes the need for an identified constitutional guarantee before §1983 liability, rather than deriving a new substantive right from the common-law tort. Graham supports applying the specific seizure protection here; the dismissal before conviction does not supply an adjudicated trial-process injury. His resistance to expansion of substantive due process does not displace the Court's existing independent-protections decisions or the actual Williams duty. He decides no procedural theory that Albright has not presented. This additional explanation has one vote.

Ginsburg concurs separately, with no joiner, and joins the plurality in full. Release on bond need not end constitutionally significant control of a defendant's person. Required appearances and travel permission may continue the restraint, so an appropriately presented Fourth Amendment inquiry must not assume that physical release ends every seizure interest. Graham therefore supplies a meaningful constitutional source, not an assurance that wrongful charging is harmless. But Albright abandoned that theory; her concurrence does not restore it, settle accrual, impose liability for preliminary-hearing testimony or resolve functional immunity. The possible continuing-restraint protection explains her join in the narrow plurality without becoming a Court holding on an unpresented claim.

Kennedy concurs in the judgment, joined by Thomas. They accept Graham for an arrest claim but distinguish the initiation of prosecution. Gerstein and Costello separate restraint and lawful charging procedure from a substantive guarantee against every prosecution lacking probable cause. Actual Williams prevents a universal no-oversight argument: its known-evidence duty and prejudicial-distortion dismissal concern federal supervisory power and expressly leave the state constitutional question unresolved. Their due-process conclusion therefore rests on the absence of the asserted independent substantive guarantee here, not immunity for all charging misconduct. Alternatively, assuming an ordinary due-process interest in freedom from unauthorized malicious prosecution, Parratt and Hudson permit adequate postdeprivation redress for random unauthorized conduct; Illinois supplies a malicious-prosecution remedy whose general adequacy is not challenged. That conditional ground concerns whether the assumed interest was deprived without due process. It neither requires exhaustion nor defeats an independently established substantive or Fourth Amendment violation. They reserve a materially different case without adequate state redress and do not adjudicate a separately pleaded procedural challenge. Their two-Justice opinion is not a join in the plurality.

Souter concurs in the judgment alone. Soldal and James Daniel Good prevent treating the Constitution's coverage of a subject as exhausted by one provision. Collins nevertheless requires care before recognizing a new substantive guarantee. On this complaint, Albright identifies no substantial injury attributable to the mere initiation of prosecution outside the protection ordinarily supplied for the ensuing seizure: the bond restraints, reputational and financial harms and distress do not establish an additional constitutional deprivation. That conclusion does not deem those harms trivial or decide whether Albright would prevail under the abandoned Fourth Amendment theory. Souter reserves an otherwise unprotected substantial deprivation, including a materially different injury arising before a seizure. He neither joins categorical displacement nor relies on the availability of a state tort action to cure a substantive violation.

Stevens dissents, joined by Blackmun. They would permit development of a due-process claim for deliberate, arbitrary misuse of criminal process, rather than make common-law malicious prosecution automatically actionable under §1983. Hurtado's distinction between prescribed forms and protected substance, Winship's meaningful proof requirement and Mooney's rejection of deliberate deception support examining the asserted abuse despite formal procedural steps. Collins concerned a failure to provide workplace safety, not the affirmative misuse alleged here. Soldal and James Daniel Good preserve distinct constitutional protections; Graham's force-during-seizure rule does not answer every injury from accusation. Williams reinforces scrutiny of deliberate charging distortion, while its federal supervisory remedy remains different from this damages claim. They reject treating bond restraints and an accusation's serious consequences as constitutionally irrelevant or counting possible Fourth Amendment compensation as the absence of another right. In their view, Parratt cannot defeat an established substantive violation; Zinermon's distinction also cautions against calling every exercise of delegated charging power random and unauthorized. Reversal would leave causation, actual culpability, immunity and municipal attribution for lawful further proceedings, not award damages or revive every unpresented claim. Their proposed constitutional rule is noncontrolling.

## Separate Writings

O'Connor's plurality, joined by Stone-Zsela, Scalia and Ginsburg, applies Graham's specific-source approach and Collins's requirement of an identified constitutional wrong. The arrest and pretrial restraints belong under the appropriate specific protection; they do not establish the asserted freestanding substantive guarantee. The plurality rejects the lower court's palpable-consequence requirement and preserves independently applicable guarantees under Soldal and James Daniel Good. Gerstein distinguishes restraint from the decision to prosecute. Williams's federal supervisory protection does not establish the state constitutional tort, but prevents a categorical denial of all judicial scrutiny of charging misconduct.

Scalia concurs separately and joins the plurality. He emphasizes the need to identify a constitutional guarantee rather than convert common-law malicious prosecution into a new substantive right. Graham supplies the specific-source approach; his additional reasoning does not displace existing independent protections or decide a procedural claim not presented.

Ginsburg concurs separately and joins the plurality. Release on bond need not end all constitutional restraint: required court appearances and travel permission can continue governmental control. That reservation explains why the Fourth Amendment remains a meaningful source; it does not decide the abandoned claim, accrual, preliminary-hearing liability or immunity.

Kennedy concurs in the judgment with Thomas. They distinguish an arrest claim governed by Graham from initiation of prosecution and find no independent substantive guarantee of the kind asserted here. Gerstein and Costello preserve the distinction between restraint and lawful charging procedures; Williams's actual federal supervisory protection remains separate. Alternatively, assuming an ordinary due-process interest against unauthorized malicious prosecution, Illinois's adequate tort remedy answers the random-unauthorized-deprivation concern under Parratt and Hudson. This is neither exhaustion nor a rule curing an established substantive or Fourth Amendment violation. They leave a materially different absence of adequate redress open and do not join the plurality.

Souter concurs only in the judgment. Soldal and James Daniel Good preserve overlapping constitutional protection, but Collins counsels restraint where no additional substantial deprivation has been alleged. He finds no substantial injury here attributable to mere prosecution outside the protection ordinarily afforded for the ensuing seizure. He does not treat the harms as trivial, decide the Fourth Amendment claim or rule out an otherwise unprotected substantial injury in another case.

Stevens dissents with Blackmun. They would allow development of the asserted deliberate, arbitrary prosecution claim. Hurtado protects substance beyond procedural form; Winship and Mooney show why meaningful proof and freedom from deliberate deception matter. Collins's workplace omission differs from affirmative abuse, and Soldal and James Daniel Good preserve independent rights. Williams reinforces scrutiny of deliberate distortion without itself supplying a state damages cause. They reject the view that a potential Fourth Amendment remedy eliminates an independently protected injury, and regard Parratt as insufficient to defeat an established substantive violation; Zinermon also limits treating delegated official conduct as necessarily random and unauthorized. Their proposed reversal would leave causation, culpability, immunity and municipal attribution unresolved.
