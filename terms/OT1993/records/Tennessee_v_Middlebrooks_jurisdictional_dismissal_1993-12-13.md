## Decision kernel: posture, participation, and entering law

Tennessee seeks review of State v. Middlebrooks, 840 S.W.2d 317 (Tenn. September 8, 1992). The Court heard argument on November 1, 1993. The state court affirmed the felony-murder and aggravated-kidnapping convictions, reversed the death sentence, and ordered a new sentencing hearing. The question presented by the State concerns the Eighth Amendment validity of using the felony-murder aggravator when the conviction rests solely on felony murder. The dispositive threshold question is whether the judgment independently rests on Article I, §16 of the Tennessee Constitution. The federal review route is 28 U.S.C. §1257; the Court does not decide whether the sentencing remand satisfies a Cox exception to finality.

## Operative source findings and material limits

The jury convicted Middlebrooks of first-degree felony murder and aggravated kidnapping, while acquitting him of premeditated murder, armed robbery, and aggravated sexual battery. It found the torture aggravator and the felony-murder aggravator. Part III of the Tennessee majority opinion independently invokes Article I, §16, uses the state's statutory prohibition of duplicative noncapital enhancement as an analogy, and rejects an asserted justification under the Tennessee Constitution. Its rule addresses a defendant convicted of first-degree murder solely on the basis of felony murder; it is not a ban on the felony aggravator in every premeditated-murder case.

The relevant aggravator, Tenn. Code Ann. §39-2-203(i)(7) (1982), reaches murder while committing, being an accomplice in, attempting, or fleeing after committing or attempting listed felonies: first-degree murder, arson, rape, robbery, burglary, larceny, kidnapping, aircraft piracy, or unlawful throwing, placing, or discharging of a destructive device or bomb. Its text is not declared literally identical to the offense definition. The lower court's constitutional assessment of duplication is a judicial holding, not the statutory command itself. The present Court decides neither its federal correctness nor the proper federal narrowing analysis.

The original state majority could not find removal of the invalid aggravator harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, notwithstanding sufficient evidence of torture. It accordingly required resentencing while permitting Tennessee to seek death again on a lawful basis. Howell, decided November 10, 1993, applied the state rule to a different defendant and found harmlessness on that different record. Its Part II.E and majority footnote 7 expressly confirm that Article I, §16 required Middlebrooks's result and that the federal discussion examined an additional ground. That explanation corroborates an original state basis; Reid's separate opinion is not substituted for the majority. Howell's different harmlessness findings, sentence, and directions do not decide Middlebrooks's remedy.

## Controlling-holding kernel

### An independently sufficient Tennessee constitutional ground forecloses federal review

**Holding and operative rule:** The Court lacks jurisdiction to review a state judgment when an adequate state constitutional ground independent of federal law requires the same judgment whatever the answer to the federal question. The Tennessee Supreme Court's original state constitutional analysis in Middlebrooks, expressly confirmed by its subsequent majority opinion in Howell, independently requires this resentencing judgment, so the writ is dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**Authority:** Justice O'Connor's Opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Stone-Zsela and Justices Blackmun, Stevens, Scalia, Kennedy, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg; all nine Justices support the rule and its application. No combination of separate opinions is necessary.

**Controlling explanation:** The Court reviews judgments, not abstract statements of federal law. Herb v. Pitcairn forbids a federal correction that cannot affect the judgment; Coleman v. Thompson recognizes that adequate, independent state grounds impose that jurisdictional limit on direct review. Michigan v. Long respects a clear state ground even when a state court also discusses federal law. Tennessee's original opinion did more than cite its Constitution. It relied on Tennessee's prohibition of duplicative enhancement in noncapital sentencing and independently construed Article I, §16 to prohibit the aggravator's use here. Howell's majority then expressly explained that this state provision required Middlebrooks's result and that the federal discussion considered an additional constitutional ground. The extended Eighth Amendment analysis therefore does not make the state judgment dependent on federal law. Correcting that analysis would leave the resentencing requirement intact. This conclusion rests on the original reasoning and the state court's explicit clarification together, not on counsel's assertion or a rule that any later footnote defeats review. The Court decides neither finality under Cox nor the federal sentencing merits.

## Law After Decision

Federal direct review cannot proceed here because a decision about the Eighth Amendment would leave the state resentencing judgment unchanged. The conclusion rests on the original independent state analysis and the Tennessee Supreme Court's express clarification together; a lawyer's assertion or the mere presence of a later footnote does not automatically defeat review.

The Tennessee rule concerns defendants convicted of first-degree murder solely on the basis of felony murder and remains a state constitutional rule. It does not become a federal prohibition of every aggravator that overlaps an offense element, nor does this decision invalidate that aggravator for every premeditated-murder conviction. Federal capital eligibility, narrowing, harmlessness, guilt, habeas review, and Cox finality remain undecided in this case.
